The Oxide Step has no inhabitants the way a city does. There are no houses, no streets, no temples, no graves. It has residents the way a body has residents: films, glands, fibres, slow walkers, secret hunters, and vast quiet processes that move through it. Life here is not a decoration on the landscape. It is one of the mechanisms by which the landscape continues.

If one were forced to answer, “Who lives there?” the most accurate reply would be: **the living chemistry comes first; everything else is secondary to it.** After that, one finds animals shaped more by heat, dampness, mineral taste, and vibration than by light.

Below are the provisional names that a visitor might give them, though the Step itself has no need for language, and no creature there has been made to answer to it.

---

## 1. The Crusts That Think With the Stone

The oldest and most pervasive inhabitants are not animals in any obvious sense. They are mats, films, crusts, and threads: microbial ecosystems grown directly onto rock, in seams, around vents, on the rims of shallow basins, and within the damp pores of basalt.

These are often called **oxide lace**, though “lace” is too delicate a word for what they can be. In some places they form a rust-red skin over stone; in others, a black slime that smells of sulfur, iron, and hot metal. In fissures where warm moisture rises, pale filaments branch like frost turned inside out. Near mineral-rich water, blue-white bioluminescent threads outline the edges of a basin like a faint nervous system exposed to air.

The crusts do not merely grow. They *process*.

They oxidize iron. They reduce sulfates. They concentrate salts. They create the fine metallic dust that drifts through the air. They make the rock slick, or crusty, or stained in copper-red and black. Some live almost entirely by heat, drawing energy from thermal gradients and mineral reactions rather than from any sun that never reaches them. Others depend on the faintest glow reflected from stone, the chemical radiance of the ground, or the warmth of water itself.

To an observer kneeling and touching one, an oxide lace crust might feel slightly oily, slightly damp, and faintly warm. If left for an hour, a faint scent of metal and smoke would gather in the palm. If disturbed, the crust might release a tiny flicker of blue-white light, not enough to see by, but enough to remind the observer that something alive had been there.

These films are the foundation of nearly everything else.

## 2. The Low, Patient Plant-Things

There are no forests on the Oxide Step, no meadows, no sprawling green cover. Vegetation is sparse, strange, and deeply local. It tends to appear in rings around moisture, in heat shadows beside vents, in crevices where mineral water rises, or on shelves where condensation forms.

Where it exists, it is low and careful. It does not reach upward in search of sunlight, because sunlight is mostly absent. Instead, it reaches sideways toward dampness and downward toward heat.

The most common plant-like forms are:

### Threadgrass

Threadgrass is not truly grass, but it might be given that name by a visitor who lacks better language. It grows in dense tufts of wiry, metallic leaves, each strand no thicker than a needle, stiff as copper wire. The leaves are dark at the base and fade to pale iron-grey near the tip. They do not rustle so much as *tick* against one another when wind moves across them.

Threadgrass survives by drawing moisture through deep, brittle taproots and by concentrating minerals from the substrate. Its tissues carry salts and silica, so that old patches leave white dust when they crumble. It is grazed, nibbled, and dispersed by the movement of larger animals. In dry cycles it contracts into tight coils; during moist breaths of air, it unfurls like a hand opening.

A patch of threadgrass on a warm shelf may look from a distance like a black stain with thin silver threads rising from it. If touched, it feels dry and scratchy, yet the ground beneath it is unexpectedly warm and damp.

### Black Lichen

Black lichen spreads over basalt shelves in fractal patches, so regular at the edges that it seems almost drawn by a careful hand. Its body is thin, dark, and leathery, marked with concentric rings like the cross-section of a very slow tree. Some colonies are older than the animals that graze near them.

It can live almost entirely on mineral moisture and heat. In certain places it becomes so thick that it seals stone against wind, holding dampness beneath it and creating tiny habitats for mites, larvae, and microscopic animals. In other places it is reduced to faint smudges, a ghost of growth on chalky ground.

Black lichen sometimes carries a dull sheen, as if it has been rubbed with oil. In darkness it may not glow at all, or only in scattered points of blue-white, like cold stars embedded in soot.

### Ember-Rosettes

The most striking of the plant-analogs are the **ember-rosettes**. These are low succulent-like organisms with broad, dark leaves arranged in tight rosettes around central cavities. The leaves are deep maroon or blackish-red, veined faintly with gold, and they often look like dried blood stretched over stone.

They do not open to light. They open to warmth.

When a basin receives a warm rain, or when rising geothermal heat makes the air heavy with moisture, the rosette leaves slowly separate. Beneath them is a small cup holding a thick, metallic-tasting fluid: a mixture of water, dissolved salts, and exudates from microbial films. The fluid is not sweet in any ordinary sense, but it has a rich, smoky, almost oily taste that animals seek.

When the air dries or the warmth fades, the rosette closes, sealing its moisture within. Over time, the outer leaves become brittle and blackened, leaving the plant looking like a charred star pressed into stone.

They are most common in hollows and near crevices where vapor rises. A single well-fed rosette can become a local center of life, attracting insects, small skitters, and moisture-riding mites that feed on its outer tissues and carry spores or mineral dust elsewhere.

### Salt-Cups and Chalk Rosettes

In white, calcite-crusted hollows there are paler plant-like organisms: small chalky rosettes or cup-shaped bodies, pale as old bone, with leaves that store salt and water in a gel-like pulp. They appear almost crystalline when dry, then soften during condensation cycles, becoming glossy and faintly translucent.

Some grow in rings around shallow pans, forming a white lacework that cracks and flakes. Others cluster at the edges of vents, where acidic vapor etches the ground and then settles into new mineral deposits. They are brittle, strange, and easily mistaken for mineral formations until one sees them move very slowly, adjusting their orientation toward warmth or damp.

## 3. The Small Shiny Animals

The most numerous residents of the Oxide Step are small, insect-like creatures. Because the atmosphere is dense, many of them do not fly in the usual sense. The air resists motion, and flight would be energetically expensive. Instead, they crawl, glide short distances, or move in slow undulations. Some have wings reduced to vestigial plates or membranes, used not for flight but for thermoregulation, mating displays, or protecting soft body parts.

They are often beautiful in a hard, industrial way.

### Copperbacks

Copperbacks are beetle-like animals with broad, flattened bodies and shells coated in oxidized mineral crusts. Their color is dull: tarnished copper, black iron, deep bronze, or bruised violet. The shells are not polished by accident; they are secreted that way. The animal maintains a thin metallic film over its carapace that repels moisture and resists acidic vapors.

A copperback moving across warm stone can resemble a small piece of metal debris that has learned to walk. It is dry, hard, and faintly warm to the touch, its body temperature closely matched to the ground.

They feed on oxide lace, microbial films, and mineral dust. Some scrape rust from stone with rasping mouthparts. Others bore into soft crusts and consume the living layers beneath. Their larvae are more active, tunneling through warm rock seams and feeding on fungal-like mats of bacteria and mineral residue.

When disturbed, a copperback may press itself flat against the stone and seal its limbs beneath its shell. In that posture it is almost invisible, indistinguishable from a flake of oxidized rock.

### Ash-Moths

Ash-moths are soft-bodied, crawling creatures with broad, dusty wings that lie flat over their backs like soot-colored sails. Their bodies are covered in fine scales that flake away at the slightest touch. Their antennae are long, sensitive, and heat-seeking; they can detect a rise of only a few degrees across a stone.

They do not fly far. They crawl up rock faces, over lichen fields, and into the open centers of ember-rosettes. Inside the rosette they drink mineral fluid with a short, stiff proboscis. They pollinate these plant-analogs by transferring thick, smoky spores from the rosette’s inner tissues to other basins.

Ash-moths are slow, clumsy-looking, and yet exquisitely adapted. Their legs taste temperature. Their wing-scales carry faint bioluminescent specks that glow only when the moth is warmed by a hot stone. A moth on a cold rock appears dull; on a warm surface it can flash a scattered line of amber-blue, like a lamp seen through fog.

### Seam-Crawlers

Seam-crawlers are flattened, multi-legged animals that live in the damp cracks between basalt slabs. They have no visible eyes, only broad heat-sensing pits along the sides of their heads and long sensory palps near their mouths. Their bodies are pale in the upper layers, nearly translucent, but their undersides are dark and slick, adapted to crawling through moisture that never fully dries.

They feed on microbial films, dead larvae, shed skins, and dissolved salts. They are also cleaners and aerators of the stone. Many narrow water channels in the plateau exist because generations of seam-crawlers kept them open with scraping, boring, and molting.

When a seam-crawler leaves its old skin behind, the cast can resemble a thin glass sheet in the rock, so delicate that it breaks with a faint sound. These discarded skins accumulate in crevices and may eventually become incorporated into mineral crusts.

### Oil-Skin Ticks and Breath-Mites

Many larger animals carry smaller passengers. Among the most common are oil-skin ticks and breath-mites.

Oil-skin ticks are tiny, dark, smooth-bodied parasites or quasi-symbionts that live in the folds of hides and shells. They feed on dead skin, salt, glandular secretions, and traces of microbial film. Their bodies secrete a slick, water-repelling oil that prevents them from being washed away by condensation. In large numbers, they can give a grazer’s coat a dull, varnished appearance.

Breath-mites are smaller still. They live around the nostrils, mouths, and open skin of heat-emitting animals, feeding on evaporated salts and microscopic particles carried in exhaled vapor. They can swarm briefly over a warm stone where an animal has pressed its body and left a fading heat shadow.

### Pan-Floaters

In shallow pans, water is often so clear and still that it appears not like liquid but like polished glass. Small aquatic animals move across this surface as though walking on oil.

**Pan-floaters** are tiny crustacean-analogs with broad, high-domed legs and waxy undersides that exploit surface tension. Their bodies are translucent, often revealing faint internal movement: a pulse of dark fluid, a flicker of red pigment, or a tiny organ of heat storage. When disturbed, they scatter across the water surface in bursts of motion, leaving small rings of light where their legs break the tension.

They feed on microbial films and dead plant-analog material. In some pans their excrement makes the water slightly cloudy. In others they remain so few that their presence is known only by the faint tremor they send through the surface when they feed.

### Tremor-Larvae

Tremor-larvae are soft, segmented aquatic larvae that live in the deeper mineral pans. They do not swim elegantly. They crawl along the bottom, wriggling, burrowing, and creating tiny vibrations in the water. These vibrations serve several purposes: stirring microbial films into suspension, signaling presence to nearby larvae, and warning predators or scavengers when conditions change.

When a pan begins to dry, tremor-larvae move in coordinated pulses toward the last damp seams. If the change is gradual, they may abandon the water entirely and enter crevice communities, where they molt and eventually emerge as land-dwelling adults. If it is sudden, they may burrow into salt-mud and enter dormancy, becoming little more than mineral-bound cocoons until the next condensation cycle.

## 4. The Slow Grazers of the Shelves

The most immediately visible large animals are the **shelf-walkers**, broad-backed grazers that move in loose bands across the highland shelves. They are not abundant in the way a herd of familiar livestock might be abundant. On a given day, a traveler might cross many miles of empty stone and see only one distant moving shape, or a faint heat-disturbance through fog, or no sign at all. Yet the plateau as a whole supports them in numbers sufficient to maintain a stable cycle.

Shelf-walkers are built for patience.

Their bodies are wide, low, and heavy through the shoulders. Their hides are dark, thick, and heat-absorbing. In some populations the coat is short and velvety; in others it is longer, coarser, and matted with mineral dust. On the backs of older individuals, the skin may carry small gardens of black lichen, threadgrass, salt-crust, and even the occasional rosette-like plant that has rooted in accumulated moisture and detritus. Such an animal can look, at a distance, like a moving ledge.

Their legs are thick, ending in broad, hoof-like feet with hard mineral rims. These feet strike the ground with a sound that is not loud, but is meaningful: a slow, measured, resonant tap that carries through stone more than through air. The rhythm of a shelf-walker herd can be felt through the ground long before it is heard.

They drink by pressing their tongues into shallow pans. Their tongues are rough, warm, and lined with fine sensory ridges that can detect the difference between pure condensation and mineral water. They lick salt crusts from pale hollows and graze threadgrass from rocky crevices. Their digestion is slow and highly efficient. Inside them, vast chambers of microbial symbionts break down tough plant-analogs and mineral-rich matter. The heat of this digestion contributes to their own warmth; the animals are, in a very real sense, small furnaces moving through the landscape.

A shelf-walker does not hurry. It moves in arcs between moisture basins, salt patches, and wind-sheltered rock shelves. It stops often. At rest, its breath fogs visibly only in the densest dark or in the coldest hollows. Its flank radiates a faint warmth that attracts tiny animals and mites. Its shadow in the low light is broad and strange, more like a stone than a creature.

Their social behavior is subtle. There is no leader in the ordinary sense. A band may move because several animals choose the same warm path, or because a calf follows the heat-shadow of its mother, or because the memory of moisture has accumulated in many bodies at once. Their communication is not vocal in a way a human would easily recognize. They use hoof-taps, shifts in breathing rhythm, chemical marks from skin glands, and faint subsonic tones generated by their chests. These signals can travel through rock and fog without producing obvious noise.

Over generations, shelf-walkers learn the plateau not through maps but through bodily memory: the warmth of a basin, the metallic taste of a certain stone, the vibration that means water is present, the smell of rain in a distant hollow before rain arrives.

If the Oxide Step has any creatures that might be called intelligent without ever building a single artifact, the shelf-walkers are among the strongest candidates. They do not make things. They remember cycles. They know places by heat. They may teach their young to read fog, to taste the coming of a warm drizzle, and to follow the faint pulse of geothermal seams. But there would be no proof of that intelligence in the form of roads, ruins, or tools. Only movement. Only pattern. Only the long, patient shaping of behavior by a landscape that is itself alive.

## 5. The Silent Hunters

Predators exist on the Oxide Step, but they are not dramatic in the way ordinary hunting animals might be. There is no roar, no sudden sprint across open grass, no visible pack chase. The predators of the plateau are thin, low, and quiet. They live in a world where hearing and scent are secondary to warmth, and where killing is a matter of reading thermal signatures.

The most common are called **ash-stalkers**, though they are nothing like ash, unless their movement is what the name describes: a faint, soot-colored passage through the lower air.

Ash-stalkers are slender, long-bodied, and flattened close to the ground. Their hides are dark and oily, absorbing heat rather than reflecting it. Their limbs move with a strange fluid precision, allowing them to pass over basalt shelves without disturbing dust. Their feet are soft-padded, but their claws are fine and curved, designed for gripping warm rock and dragging prey into crevices.

Their eyes are not large. They do not need them. They possess heat-sensing organs: pits along the sides of the head, membranes beneath the jaw, and long sensory whiskers that can detect the tiny thermal wake left by another body. In the dark, an ash-stalker does not see a shelf-walker. It perceives the animal as a broad heat shape moving through a cooler field, a warm pulse accompanied by moisture and mineral scent.

They hunt by waiting. An ash-stalker may lie motionless for hours on a warm stone, or remain pressed against the shadowed underside of a rock, or move with such slowness that its body temperature matches the environment. When it strikes, it does not race. It erupts from concealment in a short, silent burst, aiming for the neck, flank, or joint where heat and vulnerability meet.

They do not roar. Their sounds are limited: a click of teeth, a hiss of breath through flared nostrils, a low thrumming vibration in the throat. These sounds are felt more than heard, especially when traveling through stone.

Ash-stalkers are social, but not openly so. Their groups are defined by thermal familiarity rather than visible rank. They may share kills, coordinate movements, or drive young animals toward basins where escape is difficult. Their young learn to hunt by observing, by being left with weakened prey, and by following the scent-trails and heat-trails of adults across shelves. Over generations, particular hunting routes may become as stable as the migration paths of grazers.

There are also smaller predators in the water and near the crevices.

### Dip-Hunters

Dip-hunters are low, amphibious animals that live in and around the damp pans. They resemble a cross between flattened reptiles, otters, and armored skimmers. Their bodies are long, their tails laterally compressed, their skin slick with oil. They do not swim so much as crawl through shallow mineral water, using webbed toes and sensory barbels around their mouths to detect tremor-larvae, pan-floaters, and disturbed films.

They are often hidden. A dip-hunter may remain beneath a stone lip for hours, waiting for the surface tension of a pan to break under the movement of prey. When it strikes, the water barely ripples. It vanishes into a crevice with the same abrupt quiet with which it disappeared.

Like ash-stalkers, they perceive the world thermally. But where ash-stalkers hunt larger animals, dip-hunters exploit the intimate water systems of the plateau. Their presence turns a harmless-looking pan into a small theater of predation, invisible until one sees a flicker of movement beneath the still, metallic water.

## 6. The Swarms and the Passing of Cycles

There are times when the Oxide Step seems to become crowded, though the crowding is temporary and almost always misread by any observer expecting ordinary seasons.

During certain thermal cycles, when warm moisture rises and the ground begins to cool, huge numbers of small, wingless creatures emerge from crevices and burrow systems. They move across shelves, down ridges, and toward basins that are still holding heat. The effect is like a dark tide crawling over stone. From a distance, the swarm looks like spilled dust with a pattern. From close, it is a slow river of bodies: thousands of tiny hard-backed animals, their carapaces dull and metallic, their legs moving with the patient rhythm of those who have never known a sun.

These are not locusts, not termites, not any ordinary swarm of familiar earth. They are more like the plateau’s own cellular migration.

The swarms serve many purposes. They graze microbial films. They transport spores and mineral dust. They aerate crusts. They molt on warm stone, leaving thin, brittle skins that crackle under foot. They die in crevices, adding phosphorus, sulfur, and metal-rich nutrients to water pans. They are eaten by dip-hunters, ash-stalkers, copperbacks, and even some grazing animals that opportunistically lick swarms off their hides.

After a swarm, the landscape may be subtly changed. A basin that was dry may hold more mineral residue. A rock that was dull may have acquired a thin blue-white trace where swarm-cores decomposed. A path may smell briefly of metal and smoke, as though the ground itself had been struck by a hidden fire.

No observer can predict the precise timing of these swarms without long study. They are governed by cycles that are too large for any animal to map explicitly: shifts in geothermal output, changes in atmospheric moisture, subtle fluctuations in the plateau’s own breathing. The creatures do not know the future; they only respond to tiny thresholds. When temperature, humidity, mineral concentration, and vibration align, they move.

## 7. The Hidden Lives: Scavengers, Parasites, and Symbionts

Death on the Oxide Step is not hidden. The plateau is too quiet, too dry in places, too mineral-rich for a carcass to decay in the ordinary way. Instead, it is picked apart, dried, salted, oxidized, and slowly returned to the stone.

### Copperbones and Rust-Skimmers

Small scavenger-animals called **copperbones** are common near places where grazers die. They are not large. Many are beetle-like or reptilian in appearance, with hardened jaws and acid-tolerant digestive systems. Their shells carry a dull copper sheen from the mineral films they ingest. They feed on dried tissues, skin, tendon, and the salt-crusts that form over remains.

Other scavengers, perhaps better called **rust-skimmers**, crawl over exposed bone and consume microbial films growing on its surface. They do not merely clean a corpse. They incorporate its minerals into the broader chemical system of the plateau.

A dead shelf-walker may become a temporary island of life. Threadgrass will root in its dried flank. Lichen will spread over its hide. Copperbacks will feed on salt-crusted tissue. Oil-skin ticks will colonize the folds. Eventually the skeleton will bleach to pale white, then crack, then sink into the mineral dust until almost nothing remains. The bones may become coated in calcite, blackened by iron, or etched into strange shapes by acidic vapor. They may be mistaken for stone for centuries.

If a visitor saw a bleached rib rising from a dry hollow, it would not look like a relic of civilization. It would look like part of the geology.

### The Parasitic and the Mutual

Much of the Step’s life exists in relationships too subtle to classify easily as parasitism or mutualism.

Oil-skin ticks may irritate a grazer, but they also prevent crusts from blocking skin glands. Breath-mites feed on vapor secretions, but they help regulate moisture around sensitive tissue. Lichen growth on a shelf-walker’s back may offer no direct benefit to the animal, but it creates microhabitats for insects and may help the herd identify individuals through scent or heat patterns altered by the growths.

Many plant-analogs depend on the movement of grazers to disperse spores and minerals. In turn, grazers depend on the microbial films associated with plants to obtain digestible compounds. The line between organism and ecosystem becomes blurred. The Oxide Step is full of relationships that would look like accidental contamination to an outside observer but are in fact ordinary living.

## 8. What They Know, If They Know Anything

The plateau has no need for thought as humans imagine it, but its creatures are far from blind mechanisms. They have memory, orientation, learning, preference, and social transmission.

A shelf-walker knows moisture by taste, temperature by pressure, and danger by vibration. A calf may learn to avoid a particular basin not because its mother says “danger,” but because repeated thermal and chemical signals have shaped the herd’s movements. An ash-stalker may learn that a particular rock shelf warms earlier in the cycle, or that pan-floaters gather near a certain mineral seam, or that certain prey become easier to catch when fog thins.

If their intelligence is measured by artifact, they have none. No tool is held in the hands of these creatures, because there are no hands. No ruin is left behind, because no one builds. No writing marks the stone, because their records are not durable.

Their culture is carried in bodies and patterns.

- A herd’s migration path is a memory.
- A predator’s preferred hunting seam is a lesson.
- A chemical trail is a sentence.
- A hoof-beat rhythm may be a song.
- A thermal route learned through generations may be a road without stone.

If the Step were to have a mind, it would not be a mind of walls and names. It would be a mind of gradients.

## 9. The Inhabitants as Seen by an Observer

If someone were to stand on the Oxide Step, what would they actually see?

For long stretches: nothing.

The ground would seem empty. The shelves would stretch in uncertain darkness. The low fog would sit like spilled milk in the hollows. The stones would gleam faintly, or lie chalk-dry, or show veins of rust so ordinary that they might be mistaken for mineral staining. The air would be warm, too warm for the apparent altitude, and would taste faintly of metal and smoke. The silence would not be total, but it would be patient.

Then a movement.

A shape would cross a distant shelf. It would not look exactly like an animal at first. It might look like a rock slowly changing position, or a dark stain lifting itself from the stone. The observer’s eye would doubt it. The shape would pause. The heat of its body would blur the air for an instant. Then it would move again, or vanish into a seam, leaving only a faint metallic scent.

Perhaps a pan nearby would tremble. The observer would lean forward. In the water’s mirror-surface, a small ring would expand. A copperback might crawl from beneath a stone, its shell dull as old ore. A breath of warm fog would rise from a hollow. Somewhere out of sight, something soft would step through dust, or a tongue would press into salt, or a thin predator would lower itself onto a ledge where the stone was still warm.

The observer would understand very quickly that the Step is not empty. It is only sparse, slow, and unwilling to reveal itself as crowded.

Every sight would be a small event.

A single grazer passing through fog would be an occurrence. A swarm crossing a shelf would be a spectacle. A predator’s shadow moving along a heat-bright stone would feel almost mythical, because there would be so little movement in the visible world. The observer would begin to read the plateau differently: not by landscape, but by disturbance. A ripple on water. A broken crust. A faint warmth where no warmth should remain. A tick from a hoof too distant to see.

## 10. The Essential Truth of Life There

What lives on the Oxide Step is not a collection of animals in a place. It is a place that has learned to metabolize life.

The bacteria and crusts transform stone. The plant-analogs gather heat and moisture. The small animals process mineral films and carry spores. The grazers move nutrients, create basins with their feet, and disperse life across wide shelves. The predators regulate populations and return bodies to salt and stone. The swarms bind many systems together in sudden, temporary abundance. The death of any one creature becomes another phase of the plateau’s chemistry.

There is no single ruler, no dominant species, no ecological king. The true ruler is the cycle itself.

If one asks, “Whoever lives there?” the answer might be: no one, in the sense of a named being with a home. But “whatever lives there?” That answer is everywhere.

A copperback pressing its shell against a warm stone.  
A threadgrass tuft unfolding in humid air.  
A shelf-walker licking dew from a black basin.  
An ash-stalker breathing slowly beneath a basalt lip.  
A pan-floater trembling on the surface tension of metallic water.  
A swarm moving like rust-colored dust across a hidden path.  
A lichen patch spreading one degree toward heat, over one slow season.  
A dead grazer becoming mineral, and mineral becoming life again.

The Oxide Step is alive, but its life is quiet enough to feel like geology. It is populated by creatures that read the plateau through warmth and damp, not through light. It is a place where the biological has been folded into the chemical, and where every living thing is also, in some degree, a participant in the making of the land.

Standing there, one would not feel accompanied by beings that notice them. One would feel accompanied by a world too large, too slow, and too internally occupied to regard the stranger as anything more than a brief disturbance of heat and breath. The plateau would continue. Condensation would form. A rain would begin in one basin and not another. A herd would move through dust without scattering it. A small animal would crawl into a seam and vanish. And the Oxide Step would remain what it has always been: a high, dark, warm place where life exists as process, and where everything that lives is also, in some way, part of the furnace that refines the world.
