The most honest answer begins as a denial: no one lives in the Hollow of Anhydrous Green the way one says a family lives in a house.

The place is too wide, too dry, too woven, too patient. If you ask for its inhabitants, you must begin by naming several kingdoms at once: the beings who walk the mirror-lit streets, the groves that hold the buildings upright, the animals that move through the city without asking permission, the machines that breathe through roots, the old root-roads that remember pressure, the dust that carries pollen and secrets, the light itself when it reaches low enough to be read like weather. The Hollow does not have occupants. It has participants.

Still, the first thing a visitor might notice is the Dry-born.

They are human enough to be unsettling. Their bodies have the familiar architecture of people: two arms, two legs, faces that can frown and laugh and show exhaustion. They speak, barter, argue, hold children, mend cables, curse the angle of a mirror, and stand too long in a light column because it feels like being touched by noon. But they are not water-people. Whether they were once ordinary surface humans is a question the Hollow seems reluctant to answer. They have been amended by the place.

Their skin is not wet with sweat. It is dry, taut, faintly waxed, with the pale sheen of pressed mineral. Along the forearms and necks there are fine oil glands that weep not water but a clear, faintly amber resin; in cool air this resin thickens into a protective skin that keeps the moisture of their internal chemistry from being drawn away by the Hollow’s relentless dryness. Their lips are often chalked with fine salt. Their breath, when they exhale, smells of stone and faint metal, because their lungs are lined with crystalline salts that filter the dust and exchange gases without losing precious liquid. They do not cry water. When grief comes, it produces a tight burning behind the eyes and then a dry crust at the corners, a salt-dust tear that leaves no stain on the skin.

Their blood—if one would dare call it blood—runs thick and copper-green, carrying oxygen dissolved in oils and mineral compounds. They do not bled red when cut. They bleed slowly, in threads of dark amber, and the wound seals almost at once with a paste of resin and plate-like cells that resemble flaky bark. Their body heat is lower than that of ordinary people. They feel cool to the touch, not dead, but like a polished seed kept in a cellar. They move with an economy that might seem sleepy to surface travelers: deliberate steps, wide-handed gestures, heads turning toward sound before their bodies follow. In the Hollow, speed is not virtue. Stability is.

Their eyes are strange in the best light. The irises are pale green or mineral gray, but because they have lived under bright, filtered, reflected illumination for generations, they can look longer into shafts of dry brilliance than outsiders could endure. A thin translucent membrane sometimes flicks across the eye when a mirror-column trembles or a lamp clicks too brightly. At night—or when the shafts are darkened by storms above—they see by heat and motion, and their pupils widen until the eyes look like small, oil-bright pits.

They are quiet physically, too. Their footsteps are not heavy because they know how to stand on a living surface. A child learns before it learns to read that the ground is not passive. It is not mud, not sand, not stone. It is a mat of roots, a skin of felt, a body that responds to weight. The Dry-born have a particular way of stepping: heel first, then the ball of the foot, then the toes pressing lightly as though asking permission. On old root-roadways, trained by centuries of use, they walk quickly and softly. In new rootwards, where the mat is brittle or too young, they move carefully, avoiding hollow sounds. They can tell from the way the ground gives whether it is firm, whether a chamber is forming below, whether the roots are thirsty for mineral vapor, whether something large is moving nearby.

They call themselves, depending on district, the Sifted, the Rootborn, the Chalked, the Grafted, the Dry-Kin, the Mirror-Walkers, the Children of the Green Engine. Some names are affectionate, some are regional, some are old insults that lost their bite. In the industrial rootwards, people may call themselves Oilerfolk or Press-folk. In the nursery districts, Seedwarders or Growers. In the upper shaft villages, Mirror-born. No one claims the whole Hollow by a single name. That would be too much like a map drawn by someone who never walked it.

The Dry-born organize themselves into rootwards.

A rootward is not exactly a neighborhood, though it functions as one. It is a civic organism with a living center: usually a grove, sometimes a single enormous tree, sometimes a complex of engineered plants trained together over generations like a cathedral made of branches. The buildings of a rootward are anchored to this grove. Walls rise from compressed rootboard. Roofs curve over latticed branches. Staircases climb trunks that have been taught to bear load. Balconies extend from living beams that are still growing, still responding, still needing pruning. In the oldest rootwards, no house has ever been torn down completely. It has been absorbed, reshaped, fed, pruned, allowed to become part of the next structure.

The most ordinary homes are small, dry, and bright. They have roofs shaped like closed shells or half-open seed pods. The walls are pale mineral skin over a woven root lattice, polished where hands and shoulders have touched it for decades. Inside, furniture is grown as much as made: chairs with broad, flat seats of compressed fibrous wood, tables whose legs are fused roots, beds suspended in cradles of cured vine-felt. There are no rugs that absorb dampness; there are thin mats of woven dust-filter grass, changed often and burned when overloaded with fine particles. Lamps hold oil, not water, and small crystalline salts that burn with a pale green-white flame. Windows are not glass panes but mica sheets cut thin enough to transmit reflected light. Doors are shell-tough bark plates that hiss softly when moved.

A household may include relatives, friends, apprentices, tools, birds, beetles, cat-kin, and a tree. The tree is especially important. It is not decorative. It may regulate air by exchanging mineral vapors with the root-mat. It may provide oil, or dried leaf, or a structural pillar, or shade from certain mirror-light angles. A child’s name may be linked to the first branch that supports the house where they are born. A family that neglects its tree does not merely invite structural damage. It invites social trouble.

The rootwards have their own character.

In the upper terraces, near the mirror shafts, the streets are pale and wide. The light is clean. The houses are shallow, with rooflines trained to catch columns of daylight and redirect them inward. Here live the mirror-tenders, glass-groovers, cable engineers, and those who watch weather above through dust patterns and wind tones. The air is chill and tastes of copper. The people of these districts are often tall and lean, with eyes that always move upward. They speak little about the surface. They know more about the angle of light than about rain.

In the middle basins, the city becomes denser, though not crowded. Factories, warehouses, grinding halls, drying towers, storage vaults, oil presses, seed bins, and cable stations stand around great engineered groves. The buildings are blunter, more mechanical, built of iron and baked root-timber. Pipes crawl over their sides, carrying air, oil, resin, powdered mineral, seed-dust, and catalytic vapor. They do not drip when damaged. They hiss. The Dry-born have a saying for a broken pipe: “It complains, so it is alive.” In these districts, people smell of warm resin, chalk dust, roasted seed, and hot metal. They walk faster. They sleep near fans that turn in vertical wells, breathing the Hollow’s artificial weather.

In the outer clearings and unfinished districts, the city looks embryonic. Houses have only half-formed roofs. Staircases climb toward missing upper floors. Walls of stone sit waiting to be embraced by trained roots. Here live nursery-builders, root-grafters, quarry-planners, and families too poor or too adventurous to remain in a mature rootward. The ground is uneven. Fresh cracks widen. New chambers open at edges with a sound like knuckles popping. The people here are careful not to celebrate. The Hollow is still being made. To build too confidently is to be reminded that roots and stone are older than plans.

Work in the Hollow has blurred all the usual borders.

A rootwright is not merely a gardener or a mason. A rootwright reads the direction in which a living mat wants to grow, then assists, restrains, channels, feeds, prunes, or relocates structures accordingly. A mirror-setter must climb cable towers so high that the light appears as a dry white shaft in empty air. They adjust polished mica, copper, and grown-glass at precise angles. A single mirror-setter can change the temperature of an entire terrace. The best ones are almost celebrities, though their fame is practical: they are the reason a market square does not go too dim, the reason an assembly hall can be read in, the reason certain factories overheat.

Oil-pressers work in warm, dry rooms where seeds are crushed, resin is separated, and plant oils are refined for lamps, machinery, skin-care, and trade. They have stained fingers that no amount of dust-wiping can clean. Their clothes are thick, waxed, and layered. They know the smell of every oil: bitter, sweet, acrid, waxy, metallic, floral, chalky. A skilled oil-presser can tell by scent whether a grove is stressed, whether a seed batch came from the north shafts or the east orchards, whether a fermentation vault has begun producing catalyst too quickly.

Dust-farmers tend crops of fine mineral-bearing lichens and crystallized moss-analogues that feed on vapor and light. They do not harvest with blades but with soft brushes and suction trays, because the desired product is a powder. Dust-farmers are quiet people. They wear face veils made of root-felt and oil-treated gauze. They move through pale fields like slow ghosts, collecting glittering motes that become pigment, salt, abrasive, fertilizer, or medicine. The upper orchards are partly maintained because of their work; the trees drop seeds into collectors, the seeds produce oil, and the leftover hulls become dust-cakes for grinding rooms.

Cable-runners move between towers and shafts, carrying messages, small parts, seed capsules, and emergency resin. They ride narrow platforms swung on vines and iron. Their job is dangerous in the dry way: there is no river below to catch a fall, only terraces, root shelves, and hard mineral floors. Cable-runners are respected and pitied. They are the nervous system of the city, especially in rootwards where sound travels strangely and mechanical signals are slow.

Nursery-builders are perhaps the strangest profession. They grow rooms.
