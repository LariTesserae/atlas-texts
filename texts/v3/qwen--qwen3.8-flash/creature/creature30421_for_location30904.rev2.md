The inhabitants cannot be listed like species in a field guide, because most of them are not discrete enough for the habit. To live here is to be a temporary arrangement of the same materials that make the basin: mineral, water, fungal lattice, shell, ceramic, vibration, waste, memory. A resident may be a colony of climbing crustaceans, a living wall-node, a person-shaped listener, a herd of filterers standing in brine, or a pattern of standing mist with animal tendencies. The place does not contain organisms so much as it precipitates them.

The first and most numerous inhabitants are the films. They are so common that one almost forgets they are alive. Pale fungal mats spread across wet stone in veils of cream and gray. Networks of filaments, too fine to be seen singly, carry moisture and chemical warnings through pores that look like the grain of ordinary rock. Bacterial slime shimmers on pools, not as dirt but as a living surface tension, a skin over the world’s water. Concentric mineral crusts grow on ledges, hard and shell-like, as though the basin were endlessly making tiny, brittle architectures. These organisms do not need light, or air, or the kind of space a forest would require. They need only a gradient: wet over dry, salt over fresh, warm over cooler, one mineral over another. They feed on the dissolved world and return it as structure. Their bodies become the substrate for other bodies. Their deaths become the mortar of streets, the linings of rooms, the walls of channels. To walk through the basin is to step over thousands of years of living construction, each layer thinner than a fingernail and harder to imagine.

Above and within these films move the shell-white climbers. They look at first like crustaceans, but their shells are not grown in any ordinary animal way; they are secreted in repeated laths of calcium, hardened by vibration until they resemble roof tiles or fragments of porous ceramic. They climb the wet walls in slow processions, broad-footed, each foot tipped with a silica comb that scrapes fungal matter from stone. Their antennae are flattened, almost leaf-like, except that they have no leaves and no green; they are translucent plates of mineral tissue, used for tasting the flow of water and the pressure of air. An old climber’s back is marked with rings, each ring a season of mineral uptake, a season of humidity, a season of sound. When one dies, its shell often becomes a useful object. Other creatures nest in it. Listeners use it as a bowl, a shield, a resonator, or a tile in a wall. The basin recycles its residents into its architecture so casually that one rarely knows whether a wall is built or born.

In shallow channels live the slip-fish, flat and dark, more like wet leather than ordinary scale. They do not need depth. They live by squeezing between layers of slime and stone, by reading currents with lateral lines so broad and sensitive that their bodies seem lined with visible nerves. In the upper districts, near condensation pools and mineral terraces, they are small enough to ride in a finger’s gutter. In the deeper flooded passages, they can be as wide as a table, their eyes reduced, their mouths opening as broad flat valves. They feed on spore-clouds, bacterial films, and the soft inner edges of fungal mats. Schools move through channels not like herds of animals but like a change of texture in the water. When one is startled, the whole school flattens, pressing itself into grooves as though the stone had suddenly become hungry.

Tiny flying inhabitants breed in the droplets. They have the metallic elytra of insects, but no resemblance to beetles familiar from other worlds. Their wing cases are layered like nacre and refract normal light in small sudden sparks, not for beauty but for signaling among the wet dark. They lay eggs in the rims of condensation pools and the pores of salt crusts. The larvae are soft, eyeless, and wormlike until they begin to mineralize; then they develop thin ceramic plates and crawl onto fungal ramps. Their adult bodies are light enough to ride fog currents, yet tough enough to survive being swept through capillary channels. They carry spore-dust across the basin in their wing seams and under their legs. Where they gather, fungal mats bloom thicker. Where their larvae feed, mats thin and expose the stone beneath. Their brief sparks in the mist are among the few light-signs the world has, but they use them sparingly. Most information here is heard, not seen.

Larger animals move through the packed districts with a patience that makes them seem, at rest, like features of the terrain. There are broad-bodied grazers, dome-backed and low-limbed, whose shells have fused into mineral plates the color of wet rust. They crop calcium films and fungal shelves with mouths like grinding stones, slowly, with whole heads that seem half geological. Their dung is not waste but a binding slurry, rich in silica and calcium, and where it settles the walls thicken and repair. Their young are born soft and pale, then dragged to ledges where the air is damp with mineral steam and their shells begin to form. When threatened, they do not flee so much as seal themselves: the shell plates close around the limbs, the body sinks into the channel, and for a time one cannot tell whether it is animal or stone.

There are also the long-necked filterers, standing in brine pools with legs too slender for their bodies, as though balance were a problem they had solved only by never needing to move quickly. Their necks are not simply long; they are flexible, almost hollow, threaded with pumping sacs and fine acoustic filaments. They drink through vibrating siphons, taking in mineral soup and filtering the useful particles through membranes that hum when they work. Their heads are small, shell-covered, and often held almost motionless. When they sense pressure changes, however, their throats open wide and emit a low, sustained pulse that tells the herd which way the water is turning. They cannot live in the highest salt flats, where the condensation is too thin, nor in the lowest acoustic chambers, where the sound is strong enough to stun them. They belong to the middle layers, where moisture and resonance are both abundant.

Near the salt bands and the cooler upper surfaces, there are drifters that migrate according to the rhythm of condensation. They are not birds, and they have no feathers. Their bodies are thin, translucent at the edges, stretched with fine mineral veins. They glide on fog currents through perforated roofs and between acoustic baffles, letting the warm wet air do the work that wings would do elsewhere. During heavy condensation seasons, they pour through openings like dust made coherent, leaving behind faint salt trails. When the air dries even briefly, they settle onto ledges and fold their membranes tight, becoming nearly invisible, only pale scars on the stone until the next moisture returns. Their migrations are not annual in any human sense; they are periodicities of wetness and pressure, the basin breathing and exhaling through them.

There are predators here, though predation is rarely dramatic. In a world that is already loud, most killing is done by misdirection, by pressure, by collapse, by the exploitation of a pocket of relative quiet. Some flat-backed hunters lie under ledges, their bodies armored with dark bronze plates that absorb sound. They can seal their own throats to produce a brief silence around themselves, a false stillness in the roar. Other animals, adapted to hearing the world as a continuous pressure field, find this sudden absence terrifying. They freeze. The hunters take them. There are also drill-slugs, soft-bodied parasites with shells shaped like corkscrews, that enter the joints of climbers and graze animals through the seams. They do not kill immediately. They live inside motion, inside the click of limbs and the creak of shell, until their host begins to respond strangely to the wrong frequencies and the colony rejects it. Then the host dies, or is expelled, or becomes a shelter for something else.

The closest thing the basin has to citizens are the beings that one might call Listeners. If there are people here, they are not the people who would call themselves that elsewhere. Their bodies are shaped for wet acoustics and mineral abundance. They are broad-limbed and low-centerd, with thick torsos and wide ribcages housing resonant chambers. Their skin is not soft, nor is it truly hard; it is a living ceramic membrane, continuously secreted and renewed, slick with a brine that helps them grip wet stone and maintain the salt balance of their bodies. Hair is absent, replaced by ridges, fins, and bony plates along the shoulders, temples, and spine. These plates are hearing organs as much as armor. Their ears, where visible, are flattened and mobile, able to close like shutters when the roar becomes too physical. In deeper places, the outer ear is often gone; hearing is taken in through the bones of the skull and the water-filled cavities behind the eyes. Their eyes are small, dark, and covered by a nictitating film that keeps them moist without fogging. Those that live near salt flats have larger eyes, able to detect subtle shifts in glare and mist; those in the lower city have almost none, relying instead on reverberation and pressure.

Listeners do not breathe in a way that resembles breathing elsewhere. Their lungs are folded membranes kept permanently wet, drawing humid air across thin passages that extract oxygen without drying out. They can seal their nostrils and throat-valves for long stretches underwater, not because they are fish, but because being submerged in the basin is ordinary. Their hands and feet are webbed with tough, translucent skin, and their fingers are unusually long, tipped with flat pads that read the micro-texture of stone: whether a surface is salted, fungal, ceramic, mineral, dead, alive, or becoming. A Listener can stand in a corridor and know, by touch and vibration, whether a wall beyond is growing, cracking, flooded, or inhabited.

Their speech is not merely heard; it is built into the environment. A greeting is a short pulse matched to the resonance of a passage. A warning is a sharp click that carries through water as well as stone. A name is not a word but a timbre, a small chord that can be sung into a surface and will persist as a slight mineral pattern if the conditions are right. Listeners can tell from another’s voice the shape of the chamber it was spoken in, whether the speaker was alone or pressed against others, whether the speaker had recently eaten or was dehydrated. A strange voice sounds wrong, too thin, as though it has not learned the density of the world. Children’s cries are often recorded not as words but as resonances, small mineral stains grown around the place of their first breath. A family’s voice is its architecture; a clan’s voice is the passage in which it can stand without losing itself.

They live in hollows that may or may not have been made by them. Some chambers were formed by repeated dripping, some by mineral deposition, some by the slow expansion of a cavity as a Listener colony grew around it. Many dwellings are grown from a mixture of animal labor and cultivated biology. A family may spit calcium-slime over a seam, place a ceramic nodule where water will flow, and sing to it at intervals until the wall begins to accrete according to that frequency. Over years, the room becomes harder, smoother, more responsive. If the family dies, the room may continue to “remember” their tone for decades, its mineral layers holding the particular pattern of their habits. A stranger sleeping there will dream differently, if a being there dreams at all, and the walls may feel too warm or too tight.

Listeners do not own territory in the way a farmer owns a field. There are no fields. Ownership, to the extent it exists, is relational: one does not possess a channel but maintains a working resonance with it. A group’s claim is its ability to keep a passage clear, to direct the fungal mats along its walls, to prevent it from drying into silence or filling so thick with sound that no body can move through it. If the channel collapses, the claim collapses with it, and the group migrates by taking its songs with it. This is not sentimental. The songs are practical. They carry information about flow rates, load-bearing frequencies, predator pressures, and safe ledges. A Listener can enter an unfamiliar ruin and learn its history by listening for old mineral voices still faintly present in the stone.

They sleep poorly in silence, though silence is rare. Sleep in this world is a kind of submission to the roar, the body allowing itself to be held by pressure. ManyListeners seek out hollows where the sound folds back on itself and becomes almost tactile, a deep thrum through the ribs. They do not find this comforting because they are indifferent to the noise; they have simply learned that noise means water is moving, stone is holding, air is circulating, and the city is not dead. Quiet is suspect. Quiet is a fault.

Among the inhabitants are also the grown ones, the beings that resist the categories animal, machine, fungus, and mineral. They are often embedded in walls or rooted to floors like porous ceramic sculptures, yet they breathe. Their bodies are lattices of fungal filaments guided through acoustic deposition, threaded with capillary channels and hardened by vibration into forms that seem intentional. Some have no head but only a broad resonant plate facing the water. Others have limbs of fused shell and a central cavity where mist moves in rhythmic pulses. They are not born in any simple way. They bud from old nodes during pressure storms, fragment in flooded chambers, or emerge where enough repeated sound has organized mineral growth into a living architecture.

The grown ones maintain much of the city’s impossible technology. They keep standing-wave corridors suspended. They regulate mist rooms. They guide water through capillary systems too fine for ordinary hands to construct. A grown node can listen to a district for years and then, without moving, redirect the flow by emitting a long, low tone that changes the pressure in the surrounding walls. In response, fungal networks thicken or thin, ceramic pores open or close, and water chooses a new path. The Listeners do not command them so much as negotiate with them. Sometimes a grown node dies, or becomes too hard, too dry, too loud. Then it becomes a hazard, a resonant tomb that attracts swarms or collapses entire passages. The city must break it apart and recolonize it with softer life.

Some of these grown beings are so integrated into the infrastructure that it is difficult to say where the machine ends and the organism begins. A bridge might be a mineral deposit, a walkway, and a nervous system all at once. A gutter might be a channel, a throat, and a memory. A pool may be a reservoir, a nursery, and an instrument. The basin does not distinguish sharply between tool and body, house and animal, technology and ecology. To build here is to encourage growth. To grow is to be shaped by use. To be used is to be worn into function. The inhabitants and their constructions are in constant translation with one another.

The ecology among them is not gentle. It is crowded, purposeful, and intense. Every organism produces waste, and every waste becomes substrate. Fungal mats feed on dead climbers; slip-fish filter the resulting spores; elytra insects distribute the spores; broad grazers eat the mats and excrete the cement that thickens walls; wall thickening changes the acoustics; acoustic changes redirect the water; water determines which mats survive. The system is a loop so long and fine that no single creature seems dominant except as a temporary focus. If one species grows too abundant, the noise of its movement may alter a channel, causing a collapse that scatters it. If another declines, the fungal film it depended on may crust over and become brittle, opening a passage for a different kind of life. Balance here is not peace. It is a constantly renewed negotiation of density.

Listeners have learned to read these negotiations as seasons. A condensation season begins when the salt flats sweat and the mist corridors widen. During such times, the city feels full and cooperative. Water runs through new channels. Fungal ramps bloom. Migratory drifters descend through perforated roofs in pale sheets. Children are born in warm pools. A pressure storm follows, and the roar becomes almost unbearable, driving the population into sealed hollows and resonant recesses. In that period, the city tests itself: weak walls fail, dead nodes collapse, and new frequencies are born. When the storm settles, some districts are unrecognizable. A route that existed for generations has become a vertical shaft. A room has become a pool. A passage has become a fungal shelf. The inhabitants do not mourn this as disaster alone; they call it change with a certain exhaustion and reverence. They know that the city has grown too old to remain fixed.

Death here is rarely clean. A body may be consumed by fungal films, broken apart by pressure changes, incorporated into a shell wall, or dissolved in brine until only mineral traces remain. Listeners sometimes allow the dead to rest in pools where the water is warm and the noise is deepest, believing that the strongest resonances carry a person farthest into the stone. Others leave the body in a newly grown chamber so that its salts and acoustic memory may season the room. A few, in certain deep-lineages, choose calcification: they enter a node chamber, hum themselves into the rhythm of its mineral growth, and become a living threshold, neither dead nor fully active, but still able to answer pressure. Their descendants may ask the wall’s tone when they need to know whether the old passage can bear weight. Such answers are slow, uncertain, and often misinterpreted, but they are real enough for those who live in a place where memory is deposited in stone.

There is also a kind of intelligence that does not move. The fungal networks have a distributed awareness, not in the way of thought but in the way of response. They can learn which frequencies encourage mineral hardening, which attract certain animals, which repel parasites, which signal a change in water chemistry. Over generations, entire districts develop habits that resemble intention. A passage may become too quiet and thus be reclaimed by slime. A chamber may resonate dangerously and thus be sealed by overgrowth. The city behaves as though it has preferences, but these preferences are made from the accumulated responses of countless organisms and materials. To live here is to be aware of this quiet agency in the walls. One speaks to stone sometimes. Not to all stone. Only to that which has grown enough to listen.

The beings that live in the high salt bands are different from those below. Their bodies are lighter, more translucent, edged with crusts that catch the ordinary sun. They migrate with the rhythm of evaporation and condensation, their lives timed to the slow drying and wetting of the upper shelves. They have little use for deep echoes; they navigate by glare, salt texture, and air pressure. Some resemble wide-shelled walkers, grazing on calcium blooms. Others are thin and almost weightless, drifting through fog curtains with bodies crystallizing and dissolving according to the dampness. They cannot remain long in the lower city, where the roar would shake their brittle frames apart. They are not lower in dignity, though the deeper inhabitants may say their songs are too high and their memory too short.

The deep city contains the blind clay-skimmers, creatures whose skin is like polished mud and whose bodies are built almost entirely around reverberation. They have no eyes, or only vestigial lenses buried under mineral ridges. Their heads are broad and flattened, their limbs long and jointed like the legs of insects, but covered in ridged skin that can detect pressure changes from a distance. They live in the lowest channels, where light is dim and moisture constant. They herd fungal mats not with teeth or hands but by singing them into place, by maintaining low pulses that cause the mats to spread in desired directions. They sleep in water so warm and loud that the sound feels like weight. They are not predators exactly, nor herdsmen, nor farmers, but participants in an economy of texture. A skimmer dies, and its clay-skin becomes a new mineral layer. A passage changes, and the colony shifts its song. They are among the oldest forms of animal life in the basin, or at least they seem so, because they move as though they have never needed to hurry.

There are no open fields, no grazing plains, no forests, no cities built upon bare ground. The inhabitants do not look up for horizon unless the horizon has been perforated by stone shelves and acoustic baffles. Their worlds are interiors stacked upon interiors. A “neighborhood” may be a vertical sequence of hollows, each with its own tone, its own water path, its own fungal economy. Movement between neighborhoods is not by roads but by resonances. One finds a passage by matching one’s body to the pressure of the air, by feeling the wetness change underfoot, by listening to whether a wall is alive or dead. Children learn to walk before they learn to speak, but walking and speaking are both acoustic skills. To be lost in the basin is not merely to be in the wrong place; it is to be out of phase.

Their arts are not separate from survival. A beautiful chamber is one that carries a voice without distortion, that keeps water moving without flood, that allows sleep without deafness. A good song is one that makes a wall harder or a channel clearer. A gift between listeners may be a small ceramic node tuned to a personal frequency, something that can be embedded in a wall so that the house will remember the giver even if the giver dies. A festival is not a display of color but a temporary alignment of many bodies in a resonant hollow, a shared hum that can be felt in the teeth and ribs. When too many join, the sound can become dangerous; when just enough join, the room seems to breathe more easily. Beauty is not ornament. It is functional harmony.

Their conflicts are usually about flow. Two clans may want the same passage for opposite reasons: one to keep it dry enough for walking, another to widen it for swimming; one to let fungal mats seal a wall, another to scour the wall for mineral harvest. Violence, when it comes, is not usually a war of bodies but a war of frequencies. A hostile group may tune a room so that a rival’s dwelling becomes too resonant to stand. Another may clog a channel with grown debris until the water chooses a new route, leaving the original passage dry and dead. The city itself can be weaponized. If a node is forced to sing the wrong tone, it may begin to erode nearby walls. If a predator’s silence is imitated, panic can collapse an ledge. These are not metaphors. In this place, sound is architecture, and architecture is survival.

Even so, there is a strange generosity among the inhabitants. Waste is not refused. Shelter is not hoarded unless one can maintain it. A dead climber’s shell may be claimed by ten creatures, each using a part until it crumbles into sediment. A Listener family may leave a room open for years because its tone is known to stabilize water. A grown node may direct flow toward a struggling district without being asked, because the city’s old patterns still pull through its filaments. Nothing here wastes a body. Nothing wastes a sound. Even silence is used, briefly, by predators and by those who need to rest. The place is full because it refuses vacancy, not only physically but functionally.

If one asks what lives in the basin, the answer is: almost nothing, in the sense that almost nothing is separate enough to count. Life is distributed. It appears as films, shells, channels, hums, mist corridors, mineral rings, grazing herds, listening bodies, grown nodes, salt migrations, and the slow intelligence of stone that has learned where to be wet. The inhabitants are not a civilization placed upon the land. They are the land’s habit of continuing to be occupied. They are the way this warm, wet, roaring country keeps rebuilding itself: not by choosing one shape and preserving it, but by making every substance do work, every sound do work, every body become material, every material become body, until the whole basin breathes like a city, and the city sweats like a world.
