## The Tenants of the Dry City

No one lives in the Dry City, if “someone” means the kind of person who would have built it. There are no citizens with names, no families keeping houses, no kings with thrones of obsidian, no merchants lining the salt plazas with goods. There is no civilization of intention. The streets were not made for feet, nor the thresholds for doors.

And yet it is wrong to say the city is uninhabited.

What lives there is a sparse, slow, strange ecology, a civic life made of mineral skins, charged growths, pressure-readers, static-feeders, glass-listeners, dust-fliers, burrowed predators, and the long persistence of forms that behave as if they were buildings. The Dry City does not contain life the way a forest contains trees or a river valley contains deer. It is inhabited by the fact that its processes have repeated until repetition looks like order. And in that order, certain bodies have learned to live.

---

### The Skin Before the Animals

The first inhabitants are not animals at all. They are the city’s living surfaces.

Across shaded basalt, along the lower edges of salt terraces, and inside the cool alcoves of pumice ridges, pale crystalline mats spread almost invisibly. At first glance they resemble frost lichen, frost lace, or a thin bloom of mineral mold. But they are not frost exactly, nor lichen, nor mold. They are a lithic skin. They grow from vapor and mineral dust, from dry fog crawling through corridors, from salt needles released by cold nights, from glass spores that drift on the wind like metallic pollen.

These mats feed in the most literal mineral sense: they condense the air’s invisible wealth into solid form. Where the city breathes—where temperature changes, where pressure shifts between shaded stone and sunless open dust—the mats draw crystalline filaments from the air. They thicken. They harden. They pattern the ground into cracked tiles, white films, silver veins, and fine netlike traceries. In time, when the dry fog moves through a colonnade, these mats can leave behind a thin white crust that later becomes a brittle wall, and when that wall breaks, its shards fall into new arrangements and become part of the next district.

The dry-frost mats are both residents and instruments. They do not merely live on the city; they help the city become what it looks like. Their growth fills fissures. Their decay releases spores. Their films alter how wind passes through corridors. Their presence gives dust enough continuity to seem like architecture.

And they have no need for sunlight. They have temperature. They have vapor. They have the cold mineral breath of the plateau.

### The Mineral Gardens

Above the mats, in the more permanent places, there are growths that are not quite plants but are not inert either.

Barite-like stalks rise from fractures in the basalt. They are pale, brittle, straight, and too regular to be accidental. In the dim light they cast almost no shadow. Where the air is drier, they close their needle-thin tips; where fog drifts through a street, they open slowly, like tiny bones unfolding. They do not photosynthesize. They grow by accumulating minerals, static, and crystalline dust. They are not beautiful in a biological way, but in a civic way: they line the city like unfinished balustrades, like the remains of some pale botanical order that never learned to need soil.

Near shallow depressions, sulfur-yellow blooms form in hollows where vapor collects. They are soft-edged, powdery, faintly luminous when the cold turns. They appear suddenly, in small colonies, in places that had been barren the night before. By midday they may look scorched or collapsed. By the next cool cycle they return in slightly altered patterns. Their life is not a single long life but a rhythm: bloom, collapse, condense, re-form. They feed the city’s memory, because the places where they appear are the places where the plateau has, over and over again, decided to open itself.

The strangest of these garden-life forms are the black branching structures that resemble burnt coral. They are made of compacted mineral ash and are often found in the deepest corners of courtyards, beneath the shadowed sides of fallen pumice blocks, and in the places where the ground hums faintly. These ash-corals conduct tiny electrical charges. They do not spark in the way of living nerves, but they accumulate static in their branching bodies. When a glass-walker presses one of its folds against a stalk, a faint shimmer leaves the coral and enters the animal. The coral dims for a moment, then begins gathering charge again from the dust, the fog, and the thermal stress of the stone.

These growths are not animals. They are not plants. They are not machines. They are boundary creatures: forms that have learned to live in the interval between erosion and organization.

### The Salt-Walkers

Among the smallest and most widespread animals are the six-legged walkers.

They are pale, almost colorless, with translucent shells that seem made of salt, frost, and thin glass. Their bodies are small enough to fit in a human palm, and their legs are so fine that they appear not to step so much as to be admitted by the ground. When they move across the salt fields, their weight is distributed so evenly that the salt plates do not ring. If you watch closely, you may not even hear them. They stop suddenly, sometimes in the middle of a path, then resume moving as though they had only paused to listen to pressure.

They have no visible eyes. They navigate by differences in pressure, by the faint flex of salt plates, by the movement of dry fog across their shells, and by the static charge of nearby mineral growths. A six-legged walker can feel the approach of a burrower before it sees anything, because the ground is changing its mind in a place the walker understands.

They feed on microscopic crystal blooms, on the edges of salt fields, on dry-frost mats, and on the powdered glass spores that drift through the lower hollows. Their mouths are not obvious. They appear to scrape, absorb, and draw away the thinnest films from stone. They do not chew. They graze like small living wafers, moving in slow spirals or lines across a plate.

They are rare individually but not socially. They often occupy the same broad polygons of salt as though those plates were neighborhoods. A single plaza may seem empty, but its surface is full of their subtle passages: lines where the dust has been brushed aside, hollows where their young have developed, faint white films left behind after a generation dies.

When startled, they flatten until they are nearly impossible to see. Their shells become the same brightness as the salt. Their legs vanish into the shadows of cracks. To a traveler, the ground appears to have swallowed them. They do not flee in panic. They simply decide, very quickly, to become part of the architecture.

Their young begin as grains of crystallized dust. A tiny walker, newly emerged, is barely distinguishable from a flake of salt. It grows by absorbing mineral films and by moving through the fog paths the adults have learned. It has no childhood in the warm sense; it has only stages of crystallization.

### The Glasswalkers

Larger, rarer, and more difficult to mistake are the glasswalkers.

They are slender animals, long-limbed, smooth, and reflective. Their bodies catch the dim light in broken flashes, so that at a distance they may look like a shard of obsidian, a column of fused glass, or a trick of the sun through haze. Up close, they become unsettlingly animal. Their limbs are too fine for stone, their posture too careful for accident. They stand on their hind legs among the colonnades, motionless for hours, then move through a plaza with a slow, deliberate stride that never looks hurried and never seems wasted.

They feed on the charged mineral growths, though no one can say exactly what they draw from them. Their mouths are not mouths in the ordinary sense. They are narrow folds, almost like lips pressed together by habit, and when a glasswalker places one against a barite stalk or an ash-coral branch, it draws away a faint shimmer. The growth dims slightly. The animal seems to brighten by comparison, as though it has taken something invisible and carried it into its reflective skin.

Glasswalkers do not appear to have social groups. They are solitary, paired, or spaced in patterns too consistent to be chance. In the Basalt Colonnades, one may see a single glasswalker standing beside a standing stone, its body angled not toward the stone but toward the wind. If there are others nearby, they do not crowd. They keep their distances, as though their social life is organized by the geometry of space itself.

They are not blind, exactly. Their vision may be pressure, resonance, thermal reading, or reflected movement. Their skin is sensitive to changes in the air, to the faint static of mineral gardens, and to the low hum of stressed stone. A glasswalker can stand for a long time with its limbs locked and its head lifted, and it may seem dead until the wind shifts a column-tone and its body changes by a fraction of a degree.

The glasswalkers may also be the city’s most architectural animals. They do not merely live among the forms; they seem to complete them. A plaza with a motionless glasswalker at its edge stops feeling empty and begins feeling watched, though not by a predator. A colonnade with a glasswalker standing between two basalt pillars becomes an avenue, not because the animal has made it so, but because it has chosen it. Its presence confirms the city’s idea of itself.

They molt, sometimes. When the temperature shifts and the glass plains tick in soft bursts, an old glasswalker may leave behind a dull reflective husk, like a broken mirror skin. The husk does not rot. It becomes a curved shard, a tiny lens, a fragment in the next street. Thus the animal and the architecture become indistinguishable over long ages.

### The Ash-Moths

The air of the Dry City is mostly empty, but not entirely. The dry fogs are inhabited by ash-moths.

They are not moths. The name is a human concession to shape. They drift through the low fogs on wings made of thin membrane stretched over filaments of dust. Their bodies are too slight to cast a proper shadow. They appear in small numbers, far apart, moving without apparent urgency. They are drawn to places where the glass has recently cracked. When they settle on fragments, their wings flick, not as though nervous, but as though listening.

The ash-moths seem to respond to fracture. They do not need broken glass to eat, as far as anyone can tell. They need the event of breaking. A new crack in a glass plain releases dust, static, and a small shock of rearranged structure. The ash-moths find that place from a distance and settle upon it, as if arriving at a new street corner before the dust has learned its new name.

They carry micro-spores on their wings: glass dust, salt films, mineral fragments, fragments of ash-coral, and sometimes the dust of older creatures. When they move through the fogs, they distribute these particles into fissures, hollows, and shaded terraces. In this way they are civic pollinators. They do not pollinate flowers; they pollinate streets.

Their relationship to time is strange. Embedded fragments of ash-moth wings have been found in salt walls that were dead longer than the surrounding stone. This suggests that the city has a memory, and that the ash-moths are one of its forms. They are made from the same dust that becomes buildings, and when they die, their dust becomes part of the city’s future geometry. Perhaps they are not so much creatures as temporary gestures of the plateau’s memory.

When a deep subterranean tone passes through the plateau, the ash-moths rise all at once and vanish into the haze. No one can say whether they are afraid, whether they are summoned, or whether they are simply reading the ground better than anything else can. In that moment, they seem less like animals than like scattered thoughts returning to the place that produced them.

### The Burrowers

The predators of the Dry City are rare, silent, and unspectacular.

The burrower lives beneath the salt plates. It is pale, eyeless, and segmented in a way that suggests mineral flesh. Its body is too flat to be mistaken for stone when still, and too smooth to be mistaken for frost. It does not build. It waits. It remains beneath a plate for days, sometimes weeks, sensing vibrations through the dry ground.

It does not hear footsteps in the human sense. It feels pressure changes, tiny flexes in the salt plates, the subtle settling of dust, the faint movement of thermal stress. It knows when a walker crosses a line. It knows when a plate is about to lift and settle. It knows when the city is about to make a small gesture, and it is patient enough to place itself beneath the next gesture.

When a six-legged walker crosses near, the burrower strikes by rising. The salt shifts upward in a soft eruption. No jaws snap. No claws slash. The burrower opens beneath the plate like a pale door made of flesh. The walker falls. Then the burrower withdraws. The salt settles. Nothing rushes. Nothing swarms. The surface may appear unchanged for minutes afterward, except for the broken fragment of prey left where the walker had crossed.

The burrower’s violence is not dramatic. It is administrative. It removes the wrong pressure, the misfit body, the temporary excess, and then the city continues. Its remains—shells, husks, and sometimes old shed skins—become nourishment for mats and micro-crystals. Even death here is not a rotting event. It is a return to the surface.

They are also part of the city’s hidden architecture. Their tunnels are not visible, but they open pathways through the salt, allowing vapor to move through the lower layers. They create small hollows where fog collects, where sulfur blooms may open, where ash-corals may begin. The burrower does not intend to build, but it builds nevertheless, because the city is made of consequences and the burrower is one of the oldest ones.

### The Clicking Life of the Obsidian Staircase

The Obsidian Staircase is not empty, but it may be the most difficult place to call inhabited.

If you stand at its edge, you may hear a faint clicking from below. It sounds like someone moving carefully in the dark. But no one is there. The plateau itself clicks. The steps adjust. The glass expands.

And yet there is a living thing in that bowl, if by life one means a system of small actions that behaves as a body.

The black steps are full of microscopic fissures. In each fissure lives or forms or persists a lattice of glass spores, salt needles, dust films, and thermal stress. Together they make a collective that is too diffuse to be one animal and too active to be dead stone. It is the click-choir.

The click-choir is not a species. It has no shape, no limbs, no feeding mouth. It is the combined ticking of the staircase. It is the sound of the glass being aware of itself. When the plateau cools, it expands in a slow chorus. When the fog passes, it holds. When a deeper tone rolls through the ground, it falls silent, not because it is frightened, but because the city has spoken over it.

Those who descend too far may feel that the staircase is counting them. That is not true. The staircase is not aware in the way a mind is aware. But it has a rhythm, and that rhythm is close enough to intention that a traveler might mistake it for hospitality.

Rarely, a six-legged walker is found near the lower steps. It should not be there. The bowl is cold, dry, and too quiet. The walker moves with its legs spread as though testing the absence of air. It does not seem trapped. It seems to be visiting the threshold where the city stops pretending to be a city and begins pretending to be a hole. Then it returns, and no one can say whether it ever truly reached the bottom.

### The High Terraces and the Moving Stones

At the highest levels, the city becomes sparse and strange in a way that is harder to name.

There, life does not gather in large numbers. It gathers in absences. A single stone in the middle of a pale plain may have its shadow fall in two directions during different phases of the cold cycle. Another stone may appear between two others after a night no one watched. The arrangement changes: triangle, line, doorway, wall. This is not merely erosion. It is not merely wind. It is not merely the movement of frost. It is the plateau continuing to think with its stones.

The creatures that live near these high, open spaces are rare and almost invisible. A single glasswalker may stand at the edge of the rim for a full cycle, then leave no trace of having moved. A few ash-moths may drift above a terrace and vanish when the sun dims further. The six-legged walkers do not go here often; the pressure gradients are too subtle, too shifting. The mats grow slowly, almost reluctantly, as though the high air does not offer enough structure to hold them.

The stones here are alive in a non-animal way. They move not because they have will, but because the ground beneath them has cycles. They are repositioned by frost needles, by salt expansion, by pumice drift, by the slow migration of fog. Each new arrangement is a sentence in a language no one speaks. If a triangle becomes a line, if a line becomes a doorway, that is the city drafting itself. The high terraces are not populated by residents. They are populated by possibilities that have not yet settled.

### The Fog as a Migratory Body

Even the dry fogs behave like inhabitants.

The fog moves through the city-like corridors in long, slow passages. It is not water in the ordinary sense, but it carries vapor, dust, mineral film, spores, and the faint charged shimmer left by thermal cracks. It chooses avenues. It avoids some plazas. It returns to the same hollows. It leaves behind white films on basalt columns and salt ridges. In time, those films become crusts, then walls, then streets.

The fog is not a single animal, but it is not weatherless either. It migrates. It settles. It feeds the mats, opens the sulfur blooms, charges the ash-corals, and carries the ash-moths into places they would otherwise never reach. The dry city breathes through its fogs. Its breath is not warm. Its breath is mineral. Its breath is dry. Its breath is patient.

The creatures know it. They do not fear it. They follow its currents. They live in the places where it has left films. They wait for the fog to move so that they can move through corridors that had previously been too dry, too charged, or too open.

### How the Residents Live in an Unreasonable City

The Dry City is strange not only because it looks built, but because it behaves as if it knows what buildings are supposed to do.

Streets terminate against walls that are not walls. Squares widen when entered and contract when crossed. Distances depend on tone, pressure, and rhythm. A corridor may be shorter if the basalt columns hum. A gap may remain wide for a full cold night. The city respects architecture enough to alter space according to architecture.

The inhabitants do not struggle with this. They have adapted to it.

A six-legged walker does not cross a plaza by judging its distance in steps. It crosses when the pressure of the salt plates, the angle of the fog, and the vibration of the ground agree. A glasswalker does not go to a column because it sees one. It goes because the column is humming, or because the threshold is ready, or because its reflective body needs the broken light that column casts. An ash-moth does not seek the broken glass as a resource; it seeks the event of breaking. A burrower does not hunt according to map; it hunts according to the rhythm of plates.

They do not inhabit the city as humans inhabit cities. They inhabit it as symptoms inhabit a body: not by choice, but by alignment.

---

### Death Without Decay

No corpse rots in the Dry City.

A six-legged walker dies and becomes a thin white film. Its shell becomes micro-crystal. Its body returns to the salt. A glasswalker dies and leaves a dull reflective husk that cracks into a shard, which later becomes part of a street. An ash-moth dies and settles into dust, and that dust may become the next generation’s wings. A burrower dies beneath a plate and slowly presses itself into the structure below, leaving a hollow that later fills with fog. Even the mineral gardens collapse into powder and begin again elsewhere.

Death here is not decomposition. It is reassignment.

The city feeds on its own inhabitants, but gently. There are no rotting things, no flies, no carrion, no smell of corruption. There is only the quiet transformation of body into surface. The animals do not seem mourned, because mourning is a warm, wet, living thing, and the plateau has no room for such things. But neither do they vanish. They are incorporated.

In a city made of processes too slow to be called building, the residents are the small, temporary accelerations of those processes. They are the parts of the city that move before the city moves. When they die, the city keeps them as streets.

### The City as Its Own Inhabitant

If one must answer the question plainly, the inhabitants of the Dry City are these:

The pale six-legged walkers that read the pressure of salt.  
The tall glasswalkers that stand motionless among the columns.  
The ash-moths that drift through dry fog and listen to cracked glass.  
The burrowers that wait beneath the plates and strike by becoming ground.  
The crystalline mats that spread across stone like frost-lace memory.  
The barite stalks and sulfur blooms and black ash-corals that grow without soil.  
The fogs that migrate through avenues.  
The hum that gathers where salt meets glass.  
The stones that move, though no one moves them.  
The walls that decide whether to be walls.  
The plazas that widen and narrow without apology.  
The Obsidian Staircase and its clicking, which sounds like presence but is only adjustment.

And beneath all these, the city itself.

The Dry City lives.

Not as a person. Not as an animal. Not as a god. It lives as a city lives when it is no longer merely stone and space, but a pattern that continues. It lives by repetition. It lives by rhythm. It lives by the slow fact that erosion, vapor, salt, glass, and resonance have arranged themselves into streets so many times that the arrangement has become a kind of intention.

Its inhabitants are not its owners. They are its gestures.

They move through its avenues and make those avenues real. They stop at thresholds and make the threshold mean something. They feed on charged stalks and make the stalks more than mineral accidents. They die and make new streets. They are not enough to explain the city. The city was never explained by them. But they complete it.

A traveler standing in a large empty plaza may feel watched. The feeling is false, and also not entirely false. There are no eyes. There may be a glasswalker a kilometer away, perfectly still, its body catching the dim light like a broken shard of a column. There may be a burrower under the next plate, sensing the traveler’s warmth as an error in pressure. There may be a handful of six-legged walkers frozen into invisibility. There may be ash-moths in the fog, listening to the traveler’s boots as a strange new rhythm.

But the place is not hostile. It is not welcoming either. It is simply established. It has learned to hold contradictions: streets without builders, walls that may decide to be columns, distances that shorten in hum, stones that form doorways overnight, life that survives by temperature and static. The residents do not disturb the city. They are made of the same logic: persistence shaped into form.

So if you ask what lives in the Dry City, the correct answer is both simple and impossible:

A city lives there.

And around it, in the salt, the glass, the fog, and the humming stone, a few patient animals live in the city’s grammar. They are the salt-walkers, the glasswalkers, the ash-moths, the burrowers, the mats, the stalks, the blooms, the corals, and the faint click-choir of the deep bowl. They have no names. They need none. The city names them by making room for them.
