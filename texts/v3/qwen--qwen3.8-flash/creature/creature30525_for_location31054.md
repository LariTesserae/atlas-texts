That clinging is not parasitism, or not only parasitism. It is more like maintenance. A smaller body settles on a larger one because the larger body has already made the surface wet in the right way, already holds a pocket of still air, already carries a charge or a temperature gradient that the smaller thing cannot manufacture for itself. In return, the smaller thing cleans a hinge, tastes a drop of brine, polishes a lens of frost, or carries a microscopic record of another surface to the place where it is needed. The result is not a food chain. It is a system of duties.

If names matter, the inhabitants do not have names in any human sense. They are not species with lineages. They are stations. A surface becomes available, a condition begins to gather, and some life fills the gap as though the gap had always been waiting for it. What lives in the Condensary is not a community of creatures. It is a community of functions wearing creature-shapes.

At the lowest level, what first appears as dirt or corrosion is alive. The silver crusts around drains are colonies of tiny, flat-bodied organisms so compressed against the metal that they seem to be mineral deposits. They have no eyes, no limbs visible at first, no obvious mouth. They grow in concentric rings, like salt, and move by a slow swelling and contraction of the whole film. They feed on machine dust, oxide, and the thin acidic film that cold metal produces when it has been wet for too long. Their excretion is chalky and dry-feeling, though it never becomes dry, because every grain of it is held in a skin of condensation. The smell of the place—brine, chilled enamel, old rain on tin—begins here, in the labor of these nearly invisible creatures.

The thread-mosses are animals too. Or perhaps that is too narrow. They are colonial things, neither moss nor lichen nor plant, but a soft architecture of filamentous bodies anchored in microscopic pits in the alloy. Each pale tuft is a family. Each thread is a body joined to others at the tips and bases, drawing water along channels too small to see. They do not photosynthesize. They feed by absorbing the mineral film that forms on wet metal, and by holding static charges from the air. When one touches them, they are neither wet nor dry but cold and oddly resistant, like hair left too long in a freezer. Where they grow thickest, the surface seems almost alive because the surface is being kept alive: the filaments pull water away from rivet-heads, prevent pooling, and give the plates the false appearance of having been worn by centuries of weather.

Above this living film are the larger inhabitants, the ones that first seem to violate the place.

The foil-winged moth-things are the most immediately wrong, the most impossible. From a distance, they look like flakes of varnish or torn pieces of insulation. Up close, their wings are too finely edged to be accidental. They rest with the wings folded tight against their backs, but they are not resting in the ordinary sense. Their wings are instruments. Each surface is covered with microscopic cells that hold droplets in long, trembling lenses. The material is not quite paper and not quite metal, but something between: a thin, oily membrane that resists wetting in some places and encourages it in others. When the moth-thing shivers, static runs along the wing and the droplets do not fall. They gather, stretch, and break apart into smaller drops, which are flung along hidden threads of moisture into nearby channels. The moth-things are portable condensation. They carry fog from one plate to another. They move the rain that the room keeps refusing to let fall.

They feed, if feeding is the word, on charged particles suspended in the air. Their proboscides are too delicate to draw water directly; instead, they taste the electric film on a condenser’s lip, or sip the brine gathered in the hollow of another creature’s wing. When one settles on a surface, it does so with the deliberation of a thing placing itself exactly where a measurement is needed. If you lift it, it comes away cold and slightly reluctant, as though the surface had been its bed for a season. Its legs leave no marks, but its belly may take a pattern with it—a fine print of the terraces, the channels, the frost-flowers, all copied onto the underside in scales so small they look like dust.

The beetle-creatures are different. They are heavy, patient, and slow in a way that feels architectural. Their black carapaces are studded not with mere texture but with small, useful forms: glass lenses that catch the faint light, rivet-shaped reservoirs that hold water, fluted edges that guide films along their backs.
