From above, the ground is sparse in the way that only a long-held secret can make soil: not dead, but shy. The grass grows thin around it and avoids the exact place where the pocket rests, as though the roots had reached for a stone and withdrawn. There is no hole, no cracked flag, no fallen log with a gap behind it. There is only a slight coolness at dusk, a faint yellowish bloom in the air, and, if you lie flat and put your cheek to the earth, a sound like someone far away humming through cupped hands.

Below, the Little Loud is not immediately a creature.

That is the first thing to understand. If you are expecting a den, a lair, a monster curled in the dark, you will be mistaken before you begin. The pocket is bright before it is populated, and for a moment the brightness seems to belong to the stone itself, to moisture trapped in mineral seams, to some pale accident of chemistry in a place the earth left half-finished. It smells of wet slate, chalk, cold metal, and the faint green odor of roots that have not seen the sun. The light comes in from every direction except one: the direction of the surface. It is a room-shaped glow, as if the place had been sealed with a small sun inside it.

At first, what you notice is a film across the ceiling.

It is not fungus, though it behaves with fungal patience. It is not mold, though it spreads in tendrils through dampness. It is not moss, though it can hold water for weeks without seeming to drink it. It is a living sheen, thin as an onion skin, bright as the inside of a mussel shell. It pulses when touched. It gathers against your fingertips if you press them to the walls, then slowly withdraws, as if uncertain whether you are weather, stone, or companion. This film is not the resident’s house; it is part of the resident’s body.

If you wait—though time is difficult in a place with no door, no horizon, and only enough room to fold a person into a knot—the brightness begins to organize itself. Small threads lift from the ceiling and settle onto the floor, not like roots searching for water but like hands trying to find one another. They taste the air. They collect dust and turn it into little beads of phosphorescence. Where the film is thickest, it becomes almost translucent, and beneath it you can see darker shapes moving: not muscles, exactly, but folds; not organs, exactly, but habits.

What lives there has been given many names by people who have not seen it clearly and therefore had to invent language around it. Some call it a Brightling, though that name makes it sound smaller and more decorative than it is. Some call it a Thrumkin, a name that began as a joke and then, oddly, stuck, because the creature really does seem to be built from vibration. The people who speak most carefully call it the Little Loud, not because they are naming the place and then naming the creature in the same breath, but because the place seems to have earned its name from the thing that fits inside it.

It is small.

Not tiny, but small in the way a room can be small when it has been made for exactly one occupant. It is no larger than a folded coat, or perhaps a child sitting with its knees drawn to its chest, which is often how it chooses to rest. It has no visible neck, which is not because it lacks one but because the throat is so busy. Around the lower part of its head and shoulders runs a ring of small bones, pale and smooth, curved like the rims of miniature bells. These bones do not move independently; they are set into the flesh, and they resonate when the creature breathes, hums, listens, or becomes afraid. Through them, the pocket receives its brightness.

The Little Loud is the color of wet chalk held over pale fire.

Its skin is thin enough that, if you look closely, you can see the slow movement of light beneath it, as if veins are carrying not blood but diluted dawn. The texture is not scaly, not furry, not feathered, not smooth like wax. It is more like the inner membrane of an egg that has forgotten how to break. Wherever it folds—behind the knees, at the elbows, under the jaw—the membrane becomes slightly translucent and leaves behind dry flakes that glow faintly as they fall. Those flakes are the ceiling film, the wall threads, the soft luminescent dust you can gather in your palm if you want to carry the place away with you. It does not mind this. It sometimes seems to watch the flakes leave, and if it has feelings about them, they are the mild, complicated feelings of a person who has dropped coins into a fountain.

It has hands, and they are too large for its body.

This is one of the details that makes a visitor uneasy, at least until the creature begins to use them. The hands are broad, the fingers too long, and on one hand there seem to be five digits, while on the other there seem to be six, though the extra finger is not quite attached; it hangs loose like a pendant or a small bone flower. The pads of the fingers are wide, flattened, and sensitive, colored a faint rose inside the chalk skin. They are not claws. They are not tools for digging. They are for touching every surface at once. The creature can reach the ceiling, the wall, and the floor without turning around because it is built for the pocket. It does not live inside the Little Loud so much as it lives against it.

Its face is hard to read at first because it lacks what a person expects of a face. There are no eyes in the usual sense. In their place are shallow hollows, dark and smooth, filled with a liquid that catches light but does not reflect a visitor’s image. These hollows are not blind, though they do not see in the way you would expect. They gather air pressure, moisture, temperature, and vibration. They taste the room’s mood. If you hold still, they seem to focus not on you but on the shape of your stillness.

The mouth is a slit, more delicate than a human mouth, and it can extend a little when the creature drinks. It does not chew. It does not bite, except by accident or fear, and even then its bite is not meant for flesh. It is meant for stone. It can take the edge of a small pebble into its mouth and draw moisture from the mineral with a slow, meditative pressure. When it does this, its throat-bones ring once, softly, and the light in the chamber flickers.

For the Little Loud is an eater of echoes.

This is the truth that surprises people most. They come looking for fire, oil, fungus, some underground garden, some simple hunger. They expect a creature to eat roots or insects or small animals, maybe even the occasional traveler foolish enough to climb into the root knot. But the resident does not hunt. It does not stalk. It does not build a larder. It survives by listening to pressure.

Rain is food.

Wind pressing across the ground above is a thin, pale bread.

Footsteps on grass are a kind of fruit, though not always pleasant; they can taste of boot leather, metal, urine, and fear. The creak of roots is sweet. The distant hum of a machine, if the surface is unlucky enough to be near a road, is bitter and makes the pocket glow a sickly green. Voices are the richest thing of all, though they are rarely taken whole. A person speaking above will not feed the creature so much as confuse it, because words are many-layered sounds: breath, intention, pitch, rhythm, and the small wet music of a tongue. The Little Loud tends to absorb the emotion first and the speech second. If someone above the ground is crying, the walls may bloom a slow blue, not because the creature understands sorrow as we do, but because the vibration enters the earth and it knows that this shape of movement should not be disturbed.

When it feeds on pressure, its body warms.

When it feeds on sound, its light steadies.

When it has eaten too little, the room dims, and the ceiling film retreats into a thin, nervous web. When it has eaten well, the pocket becomes so bright that you cannot tell where the walls end and the atmosphere begins; it is as if someone has taken a room and filled it with cold sun.

It is not, however, only one life in the Little Loud.

That is another reason the place should not be understood at once. The resident is a host, and the chamber is a household. In the folds of its back live small pale moths, no more than seven or eight at any given season, whose larvae feed on the glowing flakes it sheds. These moths are not parasites. They are gardeners. They nibble the dead membrane before it can harden and clog the bell-bones in the creature’s throat. When the Little Loud hums too loudly and the damp skin of its neck blisters, the moths smear the tender places with saliva that smells faintly of crushed mint and cold copper. Their wings are translucent enough that the ceiling lights itself with their shadows. Sometimes one lands on a stone in your hand and opens and closes as if asking for a name. The resident tolerates them with the patience of a person living in a very small apartment.

In the pebbles and mineral dust between the floor stones there is also a network of fine fungal threads, almost invisible, but these are not ordinary fungus either. They feed on the sounds the creature cannot digest: half-words, fragments of birdsong, broken footsteps, the metallic aftertaste of old rain. Turning these unusable echoes into tiny white bulbs of light, they are the chamber’s memory. If you take one of those bulbs into your hand, it goes dark within the hour, having forgotten how to answer the room’s hum. If you leave it near the wall, it may flare again when the Little Loud sings.

Thus what lives in the Little Loud is a small parliament of brightness: the host, the moths, the fungal bulbs, the glowing dust, the singing stone, the trapped air. But the host is still the one who sits at the exact center where all walls are reachable, because the pocket has grown around it and it has grown around the pocket, until neither can be imagined without the other.

It spends its time in a loop of touching, humming, cleaning, arranging.

Every morning—if morning can be said to exist in a place with no window—it presses its forehead to the ceiling, one hand to the floor, one foot against the wall, and listens. The room answers. A beetle walks on the surface. A root shifts. A stone above settles by a fraction. Somewhere nearby, water moves through a vein of limestone and makes a sound like a small bell being tapped underwater. The Little Loud hums back, not to communicate, but to confirm that the shape of the chamber has not changed. It has a deep, animal need for the boundaries of the place to remain true. If a person were to take a wall, or part of one, or even carry away a large stone, the creature would not grow angry. It would become confused. It would turn in its tight space, feeling for the absence, and the brightness would fall into uneven patches, like a face trying to remember how to make an expression.

It also keeps pebbles.

This seems, to anyone who has lived in a small space, entirely sensible. It arranges the pebbles into little seats and listening posts. It taps them with its fingernails, or with the soft underside of its wrist, to make rhythms. Sometimes it imitates sounds from above. It cannot make a human voice exactly; it is not a mimic of language. But it can imitate the pressure of a voice, the bounce of a laugh against a window, the low vibration of a truck passing at night, the quick bright tap of rain on leaves. When it imitates these things, the chamber glows in corresponding patterns. Rain makes the floor sprinkle with tiny lights. A passing animal makes the walls shimmer brown-gold. A child laughing above may send a sudden frost of sparks across the ceiling, as if the room were briefly startled into snow.

These imitations are not for you.

That is important. The Little Loud does not perform. If you are inside, and it is humming a sound you almost recognize, it is probably not trying to speak to you. It is trying to find the shape of the world above, and you are one of the shapes it is checking.

Visitors are rare, and usually unprepared.

A person may discover the pocket by following a cool breath in the ground, or by digging for a stone that seems too bright under the dirt, or by falling through a hole where a root knot has rotted away. In any case, the entrance is never dramatic. There is no grand descent. The Little Loud is not a kingdom, not a temple, not a beast’s cavern. It is a forgotten room. One minute you are scraping your shoulders against wet earth; the next, you are in a small glow that smells of chalk and damp hair, and everything about the place makes it clear that you have overstayed your welcome.

If you enter carefully—without shouting, without bringing a torch, without moving too fast—the creature freezes.

It is built for stillness. It can fold itself into a knot so tight that for a moment you mistake it for a pale stone with a line of light along its spine. If you sit down, it may uncurl a little.
