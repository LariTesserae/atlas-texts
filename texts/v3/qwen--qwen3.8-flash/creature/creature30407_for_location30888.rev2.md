To call this place uninhabited is the first mistake. It is not a ruin. It is not an empty bowl of stone. It is a city whose citizens are mostly invisible, whose architecture is made of bodies, and whose ordinary activity has lasted so long that it has begun to look like intention.

If you walk across it, you are walking through a population. Every surface is claimed. Every crevice is a room. Every shallow pool is a crowd. The smallest organisms are not rare here; they are the reason there is a “here” at all. What appears as mineral, water, salt, peat, and stone is also a living tissue, slowly turning itself into landscape.

The most numerous inhabitants are not animals in any familiar sense. They are microbial films: mats of bacteria, cyanobacteria, diatoms, algae, and the countless microscopic particles of life that form a thin, slippery world between water and stone. In the black mirrors of shallow basins, the water is not merely water. It is a suspension, a broth, a breathing medium. It glows faintly when light passes through it, not because the light is strange, but because there is so much life suspended in it. The organisms do not build towers, but they build surfaces. They secrete sticky substances that catch dust and mineral grains. They trap calcium, silica, and salts in their surroundings. Over time, their mucous skins become walls. Their dead layers become floors. The pale crusts on the terraces are not merely deposits; they are cemeteries and workshops at once.

Cyanobacterial filaments spread in dark green, olive, and rust-colored films over the wet margins of basins. They take up water, light, and the thin chemistry of the stone, and they return it changed. In cold high air, they grow slowly. They do not need warmth in the way a forest needs summer. They need wetness. They wait through frost, through dryness, through the brittle season of salt, and then, when a film of moisture returns, they wake inside it. Their cells stretch across the surfaces like hair. They form threads, sheets, knots, and ropes. They make the ground feel sticky and soft. When a patch of terrace has recently dried, it may still look bare and chalky, but if you put your mouth near it, or let it sit in a vial of clean water, something will begin to turn green again. The city is full of dormant life.

Diatoms live among them, each one a small glass house made of silica. Their bodies are not separate from the landscape. When they die, their glass shells settle into the sediment, accumulate, bind, and contribute to the pale, hard floors that make the basin seem paved. Some of the thin blades that stand in dense clusters along the waterline may begin as something alive: delicate, mineralized forms left behind by organisms that once floated in pools too small to be called water. Their remains harden, crack, and rebuild. They do not quarrel with the stone; they become its skin.

In the salt-rimmed edges, halophilic microbes live in brines so concentrated that larger animals would die in them. The salt appears white and empty from a distance, but the crystals are not empty. Their surfaces are coated with bacteria and archaea, tiny cells that can survive when moisture vanishes into brine inclusions inside the crust. The white crust is not only a mineral deposit; it is a habitat, a vault, a sealed community. When the sun evaporates the shallow water, the salt rims brighten. When the fog returns, the salt begins to darken, soften, and exhale a faint mineral smell. Life in those rims does not move from room to room as animals do. Life enters dormancy, survives, and wakes again. Its migrations are not across the landscape, but across states of being.

Beneath the black peat benches, the inhabitants are not absent either. The peat looks inert, even dead, but it is a layered archive of former growth. It holds fungi in a dense tangle, bacterial colonies in the dark spaces between fibers, root molds full of trapped air, and countless microscopic animals moving through channels too narrow for a human eye to follow. There are nematodes, worms thinner than a hair, grazing through the pores. There are springtails, tiny six-legged creatures that live in the water film among decomposing moss, eating fungi, algae, and spores. There are mites, some feeding on other small animals, some feeding on decaying material, some crawling through the peat like dust given legs. There are rotifers, barely visible to the unaided eye, sweeping microorganisms into their mouths with beating crowns of cilia. These animals are not large, not loud, not obvious, but they are the population that keeps the dead matter alive as a living substance.

The peat itself is built from the bodies of sedges, mosses, and the microscopic organisms that break them down and then refuse to let them disappear. A black bench at the waterline may look like a simple dark cushion, but it is also a slow furnace. In the upper layers, oxygen is present, fungi grow, and tiny animals feed. In the lower layers, water seals the system from air. There, microbes break down dead matter more slowly, leaving behind gas pockets, sour chemical changes, and the strange, sweet smell of decay that has almost stopped. When a foot breaks the crust, the peat gives and exhales cold moisture. That breath is the sound of the city being disturbed.

If you crouch near a pool and let the surface stillness settle, the water reveals more than a reflection. It is full of motion. Ostracods crawl over the thin bottom film, enclosed in bivalve shells no larger than a grain of dust. Water fleas drift in the middle layers, their antennae rowing in short, urgent strokes. Tiny worms burrow into the sediment and leave their tails exposed, waving as they feed. Some larvae remain anchored beneath stones, drawing in dissolved oxygen or scraping biofilms from mineral walls. Their bodies are pale, transparent, or tinged with faint red, brown, or olive, because they live inside the materials around them. The larvae of midges are a quiet abundance in this landscape, small red-thread creatures in the dark margins of pools, feeding on detritus and mineral films, waiting to rise. Empty egg cases cling to submerged stems. Shed skins hang in the water like translucent ghosts. These are not remains of death alone; they are the residues of transformation.

There are small water beetles in some channels, but they are not obvious, because their lives are slow and guarded. They move under surface tension, or among submerged stems, or in the dark underside of a wet stone. Their bodies are streamlined, their legs fringed for pushing through thin films. Where the current is strongest, they cling. Where the pool is deepest, they rest among the dead leaves. Some larvae hunt smaller animals with jaws adapted for piercing, while adults may feed on detritus, algae, or the remains of other creatures. Their presence does not disturb the silence; it adds another layer to it. A beetle crawling over a biofilm makes a sound like a drop of water shifting inside a shell.

The pools are also homes for protozoans: amoebae changing shape in the water films, ciliates sweeping through the currents, flagellates darting among the suspended dust. They are not separate from the city’s structure. They eat bacteria. Bacteria eat dissolved carbon. Diatoms photosynthesize. Fungi decompose. The water itself carries particles. To stand at the edge of a basin here is to stand beside a vast, thin ecosystem that would fit inside a handful of moss. Yet this handful, when distributed over a plateau, becomes an ecology the size of a province.

Tardigrades live here, too, though only a microscope would show them. They crawl among moss and lichen when water films make movement possible. They feed by piercing cells. They enter torpor when drying becomes absolute, becoming nearly invisible packets of endurance. In this place, survival is not dramatic. It is structural. A tardigrade may be suspended in a leaf for months, then revive from a drop of dew. A rotifer may be trapped in salt, then swim again when the salt dissolves. A bacterial spore may rest in peat, then become active in the next thaw. The city is not always awake, but it is never fully dead.

The mosses are not many, but they are central. They form low, dark cushions over wet stone, not because the stone has soil upon it, but because moss does not need the kind of soil a forest does. It holds water against its surface like a sponge. It protects its own tiny world. In the center of a moss cushion, there may be rotifers, springtails, nematodes, and mites living in a habitat no larger than a clenched fist. The moss is both organism and neighborhood. It absorbs dew. It traps dust. It shelters spores. It dies at the surface while producing new growth below, layer by layer, building a dark bench over the mineral floor. The moss does not dominate the landscape, but it softens the stone and prepares it for other things.

Lichens are another form of resident that refuses easy classification. They are fungi partnered with algae or cyanobacteria, neither one nor the other but a compound of lives. They spread across pale terraces as maps, cracks, dots, and flakes. Some form yellow stains. Some look like fine gray lace. Some press themselves into mineral pores so tightly that they become part of the stone’s appearance. Lichens live by waiting. They tolerate drying. They resume photosynthesis with dew. They erode the surface of rock chemically, trapping particles and becoming soil without ever needing to become forest. They colonize new terraces from dust carried by wind. Their growth is so slow that a human lifetime would reveal no obvious change, but over centuries they bind mineral dust into biological crusts. They are citizens of the rims and crevices, where moisture is thin but never absent.

Fungal threads are the city’s hidden infrastructure. They run through peat, moss, dead stems, root channels, and mineral crusts. Some fungi feed on decay. Some live in partnership with plant roots. Some break down tough organic matter that bacteria cannot handle. Their networks are not a single mind, but they create a kind of continuous tissue throughout the landscape. When a sedge dies, fungi enter. They pull its fibers apart, fill the hollows, stabilize the decaying matter, and return its minerals to the water. In the pale mineral rims, fungal threads may bind salt, calcium carbonate, silica, and dust into thin skins. These skins crack in dry weather and swell in wet weather, but they are not inert. They are repaired by growth. The city’s walls are partly living.

The vascular plants are sparse but real. Sedges are among the most important residents because they are engineers. Their roots do not merely grow into peat; they alter it. A sedge root, submerged in oxygen-poor black peat, can release a faint flow of oxygen into the surrounding soil, creating a thin halo of different chemistry around itself. In that halo, microbial communities change. Tiny worms move toward it. Fungal threads follow it. Dead roots leave channels, and living roots maintain them. The plant is not just a thing standing in the ground; it is a moving source of conditions. When it dies, its hollow stem may be filled by water, salts, spores, and larvae. It becomes a vertical cistern, a tube, a pillar. Its decay adds to the architecture.

Short, reedlike tufts of sedge lean into the wind, but their apparent frailty hides their persistence. They are adapted to a world that is cold, wet, mineral-rich, and difficult. They store energy in underground rhizomes. They send new shoots from the same network. They do not compete as a lush meadow. They survive as individual clumps, sometimes forming lines where water follows a long path. A line of sedges may mark an old channel the way a row of buildings marks a street. But no one chose the street. The plants found it, or the street found the plants, or both were produced by the same movement of water. The sedge clumps trap dust, slow the current, and stabilize the banks. They are residents and also builders.

Salt-tolerant rosettes are more solitary. They grow where the mineral crust is thin enough to hold a little moisture. Their leaves are thickened, gray-green, waxy, and pressed close to the ground. They do not flourish so much as withhold. Their form is a strategy for scarcity disguised as abundance. Water sits in their centers after rain, or condenses there at night. In those tiny cups, algae and bacteria may live for brief periods. The rosette itself does not need to be a pond, but in this landscape, almost no surface is without a community. Even a droplet held in a leaf becomes a temporary city.

Dwarf shrubs, if they exist here, are fewer still. They grow only in places where peat is deeper, where salts are washed a little thinner, where wind is interrupted by a terrace edge. They are stunted, silvered with dust, bent by frost, covered in lichens. Their roots are shallow and wide. Their branches are short and tough. They do not make a forest. They are more like sentinels placed at intervals, marking the limits of what vegetation can do in this wet, cold, mineral world. Around their bases, spores fall, mosses start, small insects shelter, and the ground smells of stone and slow growth.

The animals that appear larger are not the city’s permanent citizens. A wader crossing a basin is a visitor, perhaps a seasonal resident, perhaps a passer-through, but it belongs to the open sky more than to the wet stone. It steps carefully because the ground is not dead. Its feet feel the tremor of water under salt crusts, the soft resistance of peat, the hidden channels beneath the surface. It feeds on larvae and small crustaceans in the shallows. Its eggs, if laid here, are not the product of settled life but of borrowed life: the wader uses the margin without owning it. A hawk above the plateau is more distant still. It may hunt small animals that occur along the edges, but the city below does not require it. The hawk lives in air, thermals, and silence, not in mineral skin.

Bats appear at dusk, and in that hour they become part of the city’s night shift. They cross the open basin in quick, irregular lines, hunting midges and tiny moths that rise from the pools. Their flight is not noisy, but it is one of the rare sounds of movement in the landscape. They are not residents of the basin, but they are residents of its edges, its crevices, its nearby roosts. They use the basin as a larder. The insects they eat are born from the water films and peat channels below, so the bat is connected to the city even when it is not walking through it. Its hunting changes the rhythm of the insect population, and the rhythm of the population changes the life of the pools. The bat is an extension of the city’s metabolism carried into the sky.

If there are mammals at all, they are rare and peripheral. A vole-sized creature may tunnel where peat is deep, where roots have made hollows, where frost can be escaped beneath a crust. But it is not a citizen of the main city. It lives in the margins, where the landscape has softened enough to be entered. Its burrow may collapse under mineral pressure, become filled with water, and then serve as a channel for larvae. Its dead body may feed beetles, fungi, and the slow microbial appetite of the peat. Even a large animal, here, is eventually reduced to building material.

The birds are mostly visitors, too. But there are exceptions, small life histories embedded in the dry rims. Some ground-nesting birds may come for a short season to scrape a shallow hollow into peat or mineral dust. They use the openness as protection because predators are few and visibility is wide. Their eggs may lie in bare bowls lined with sedge fragments and lichen crumbs. Their chicks survive by stillness. They are not the city’s builders, but they are its temporary tenants. Their nests are accidental rooms, made in a place where architecture has not yet learned roofs.

The real inhabitants do not have roofs. They have films, pores, cracks, bubbles, crusts, and dew. The microbial mat is the floor of the city. The salt rim is its wall. The peat bench is its storage. The sedge clump is its tower. The pool is its reservoir and its bloodstream. The lichen map is its street pattern. The fungal thread is its wiring. The larva is its moving citizen. The egg case is its archive.

If you think of a city as a system of movement, the movement here is clear. Water enters. Life follows. Sediment accumulates. Organisms trap particles. Dead matter becomes mold. Fungi bind mold. Salt forms where water evaporates. Minerals fill voids. Plants find the stable places. Animals come to eat the organisms. Wind carries spores. Frost cracks open spaces. Water refills them. This is not a single organism, but it is not a random scatter of things either. It is a living system in which every body is both resident and residue.

The pools are especially alive in the early morning. Frost has thinned on the rims, and the air begins to warm only enough to loosen the ice skins. Water rises through capillary action in porous stone. Dew falls onto salt. Microscopic life begins to move in the surface film. A droplet trembling on a lichen may contain bacteria, algae, protozoa, and dormant cells. A blade of sedge, when bent to a pool, releases a faint scent of wet stone. In the water, tiny crustaceans stir from the sediment. Midges rise in thin columns when they emerge, hover for a moment in the still air, and then disappear. The pool’s surface breaks and reforms because small bodies are passing through it.

At midday, the city looks brightest and most deceptive. The light is clean and sharp, the wet stone shining, the salt rims white. Everything seems static, because the scale of activity is too slow for casual sight. But in the biofilms coating the channels, diatoms and cyanobacteria are photosynthesizing, producing oxygen in small bubbles that cling to surfaces. Bacteria consume and release. Fungi extend thread by thread. Nematodes navigate through water films. A sedge root is slowly oxygenating the black peat around it. A salt crystal is growing. A dead stem is collapsing into a mold. A new fringe of mineral crust is forming along the terrace edge. Nothing is idle. The city appears frozen, but it is working.

At night, the residents shift. The pools exhale different gases as photosynthesis stops and respiration continues. In the peat, decomposition warms by fractions of a degree. In the black mirrors, stars reflect and tremble. If you place a hand near the surface, the film may stiffen and then crack from a slight current or from a bubble released from below. That is not an unseen animal tapping the water. It is usually life itself: respiration, decay, dissolved gases, temperature changes, or the movement of larvae beneath the surface. The invisible city keeps touching the visible one.

Bats arrive when the water begins to release its airborne population. Midge adults rise in short, silent bursts. Moths may drift from somewhere beyond the plateau, but the true abundance remains small: midges, flies, tiny beetles, aphid-like insects, mites blown from moss cushions. The bats take what they can from this thin night harvest. The sky over the basin is not empty; it is a corridor of feeding, breeding, and returning. The city below produces the insects, and the insects produce the night’s larger movement. The bats are therefore linked to the city even though they do not touch its floors.

If one were to name the inhabitants collectively, the names would be misleading. “Microbes” is too small. “Insects” too narrow. “Plants” too green. “Animals” too warm. “Fungi” too strange. None of these words alone captures the place. The life of this basin is better described as a stratified community of surfaces, films, threads, and bodies. It has no dominant species in the way a forest has canopy trees. Its dominants are processes that wear bodies: wetting, drying, trapping, binding, feeding, dying, and recrystallizing. But these processes are not abstract. They occur in living cells, inside animal bodies, within plant tissues, and along the edges of stone.

The city’s most important citizens may be the ones that leave no obvious traces. A bacterial film can alter the chemistry of water around it. A cyanobacterial mat can encourage mineral precipitation. A fungal thread can hold a crack together. A diatom can contribute glass to a wall. A springtail can redistribute spores from one moss cushion to another. A midge larva can aerate sediment with its movement. A sedge root can change the oxygen profile of the peat beneath it. These acts are not grand, but they are cumulative. In a landscape with no fires, no flood-plain forests, no deep rivers, no large mammals, small organisms become the architects. Their labor is slow enough to look geology, but it is life labor.

There is also a strange economy here: the dead are never fully separated from the living. A shell is an architectural fragment. A larval skin is a record of molting. A dead stem is a mold for new crust. A peat bench is a compressed population. A salt crust may contain dormant cells from generations of pools that evaporated before humans noticed this place. The city does not have a boundary between building and inhabitant. The wall is partly a former organism. The organism is partly a wall. The pool is a living medium, and the living medium is a pool.

This is why, from a distance, the place looks empty. There is no herd, no canopy, no fire, no road, no tower. There are few birds, and the mammals, if present, are hidden. The visible landscape is dominated by stone, water, salt, peat, and sparse tufts of plants. But if you lower your perspective to the scale of the moss cushion, you find a jungle. If you examine a drop of pool water, you find a crowd. If you split open a dead sedge stem, you find a tunnel system. If you look at a salt rim under magnification, you find a desert city of microscopic life. The emptiness is only an optical illusion created by human scale.

The residents also do not behave like animals in a wild place. They do not roar, hunt in packs, build nests of sticks, or defend territories in visible ways. Their lives are mostly chemical, structural, and slow. They respond to wetness, mineral concentration, temperature, light, and decay. They migrate by spores, eggs, drift, or dormancy. They compete for surfaces. They colonize cracks. They trap particles. They become fossils while still functioning as habitats for others. Their conflicts are not dramatic because they happen within the scale of a biofilm. A fungus may suppress bacteria. A ciliate may eat a bacterium. A springtail may graze a fungal thread. A larva may feed on detritus. A water beetle may hunt another larva. All of this occurs quietly, often beneath a surface that looks like stone.

The sparse plants are not merely residents; they are anchors for other residents. The rosette holds a droplet. The droplet hosts algae. The algae feeds protozoa. The protozoa become food for rotifers. The rotifers move through the peat. The peat holds the rosette. The dead rosette becomes mold. The mold becomes channel. The channel becomes salt crust. The salt crust becomes habitat for bacteria. The bacteria become part of the mineral rim. The rim becomes terrace. The terrace becomes street. The street becomes city. Nothing is outside the system. Nothing is merely scenery.

If a human walked for miles through this place, the first impression would be solitude. The second impression, after kneeling and watching, would be population. The third impression, after returning, might be awe—not because the place is magical, but because it is biologically dense in ways human senses are not built to see. The city is not hidden behind rocks; it is in the rocks. It is not waiting in the pools; it is the pools. It is not beneath the ground; it is the ground’s living condition.

There are no rulers here, no central authority, no planning organism. The city grows by feedback. A place becomes wet; life settles; life traps mineral; mineral holds water; more life settles. A crack forms in a terrace; dust enters; spores arrive; fungi thread through the dust; water carries salts into the crack; the crack hardens; lichens spread across the new face. A sedge clump slows water near a channel; peat accumulates; roots deepen; insects lay eggs; larvae feed; adults emerge; birds come; bats come; dead insects and dead larvae become sediment. The city is built by consequences.

The animals that are most visible—wader, hawk, bat—are not the city’s core. They are part of its upper atmosphere. The true residents of the landscape are smaller: microbes, larvae, spores, hyphae, moss, lichen, sedges, rosettes, tardigrades, rotifers, springtails, nematodes, beetles, midges, worms, shell-bearing crustaceans, dormant seeds, dead bodies, and the films that make life possible. They are not all equal in size, but they are equal in relevance. The city cannot be understood by looking for the largest beings.

At dawn, the most abundant movement is condensation. The water does not fall in rain so much as appear on surfaces. It collects in pores and becomes a film. In that film, life becomes mobile. Tardigrades crawl. Rotifers pump. Bacteria divide. Spores drift. A blade of silica, if it has formed overnight, may be the result of a long biological history, but it becomes visible only when the first light catches it. The dawn city is not waking in the way a human city wakes. It is wetting, releasing gases, crystallizing, softening, and beginning its slow metabolism.

At noon, the city looks bleached. Salt rims flare. Moss cushions dry at their surfaces while retaining water inside. Lichens go dormant, their chemistry slowing. In the shallow pools, life continues in the cooler water. In the peat, temperature is steady beneath the crust. In the root channels of sedges, small creatures move through wet tunnels. The midday city is a place of waiting, but waiting is active here. Dormancy is not death. It is a way of living without the need to be seen.

At dusk, the pool surfaces tremble with emergence. Midges rise. Water beetles move to less still places. A bat passes overhead. The air cools, and the first moisture begins to collect again. The city’s visible animal life increases briefly, because the larger beings are sensitive to the change. But this is only the outermost layer. Beneath it, the microbial city is also responding: changing oxygen levels, shifting gas production, consuming or releasing compounds as the light fails. The evening city is both a biological exchange and a physical one.

At night, the wet terraces become black mirrors, and the city’s reflection is inverted in the sky. The salt rims glow. Frost forms where the air can find a thin point of contact with cold stone. In the deeper pools, life moves without light, feeding on chemistry, on detritus, on the remains of daytime photosynthesis. In the shallower pools, ice skins crack and reform. Somewhere, a larva burrows. Somewhere, a spore germinates. Somewhere, a filament lengthens. Somewhere, a bubble from the peat rises and breaks the surface. The night city does not sleep. It is cold, slow, wet, and full of invisible movement.

If one asks what kind of society lives here, the answer is unsettling to human expectations. It is a society without individuals in the way human cities are made of people. It is a society of surfaces, films, and processes. The smallest beings are not “lower life” in any moral sense; they are the city’s foundation. They build, repair, recycle, and sustain. They are not separate from the stone. They are not separate from the water. They are not separate from the city. They are the city’s living matter.

The larger animals, when present, are not inhabitants so much as extensions of the city’s reach. A wader touches the water and leaves. A hawk circles and passes. A bat hunts and returns elsewhere. These animals are part of the basin’s ecology because the basin feeds them, shelters them, and is changed by them. Their droppings add nutrients. Their footfalls disturb crusts. Their wings carry seeds and spores. Their eggs and dead bodies, if they fall here, become part of the accumulation. Even the visitor is eventually incorporated.

There is a strange intimacy to the way life occupies this place. Nothing wastes a surface. Every film has a purpose. Every crevice holds potential. The dead are used. The empty shells are used. The root holes are used. The peat layers are used. The salt crusts are used. The city’s residents do not merely live in the place; they are continuously transforming it into the conditions that allow them to live. The pool makes microbes. The microbes make films. The films trap minerals. The minerals form terraces. The terraces hold pools. The cycle is not a circle drawn in the air; it is a working system built from bodies.

If a visitor were to press a finger into the pale tufa, it would feel chalky and cool. The dust would cling to skin. The smell would be mineral, faintly salty, not rich or green. That dust would contain fragments of former organisms. It would contain diatom walls, fungal threads, bacterial remains, spores, silt, salt, and traces of plants that died slowly and became part of the architecture. The finger would not touch a dead surface. It would touch a living archive.

If the visitor were to lift a peat bench, it would be heavy with water. Inside it would be a dark weave of roots, fungi, fibers, tiny insects, mites, and dormant life. It would feel soft, almost muscular. When returned to the ground, it would settle with a sigh of moisture. That sigh is not metaphor. The peat is saturated with life. Its compression is a history of slow decay and renewal.

If the visitor were to place a hand in a shallow pool, it would feel cool and slippery. The water would be alive with suspended particles. As it moved over the skin, a tiny crustacean might bump the finger. A rotifer might pass like a microscopic bell. A film might cling to the hand. The pool would not be clear even if it looked clear. Its clarity may be a trick of stillness and light. Under magnification, it is a crowd.

If the visitor were to walk far enough, the silence would feel thick enough to hear. That silence is not absence. It is the accumulation of too many small sounds happening at too low a volume for human attention. It is the drip, the tick, the hiss, the faint crackle of salt, the small collapse of a droplet, the movement of water through capillary stone, the breath of frost forming, the emergence of a midge from a pool. The city speaks in whispers too small to be named.

If the visitor were to return at a different season, the place might appear unchanged. The same terraces, the same wet seams, the same sparse tufts, the same salt rims. But beneath the apparent sameness, the city has moved. A new crust has formed. A pool has deepened. A channel has shifted. A root mold has filled. A shell has cracked. A spore has germinated. A film has thickened. A population has bloomed and died. A larva has become an adult. A bat has fed elsewhere. The city’s identity is not fixed. It is maintained by continuous correction.

This is the most important thing about the inhabitants: they are not stable residents. They are processes that sometimes become visible as beings. A bacterium may become part of a wall. A fungus may become part of a soil crust. A larva may become part of the food for a bat. A sedge may become part of the peat. The city does not hold them still. It consumes them, incorporates them, and uses them again.

The plants are the most obvious living things, but they are not the most abundant. The animals are the most dramatic when present, but they are not the most continuous. The true residents are the smallest, the slowest, and the most embedded in the landscape. They are the films, threads, spores, eggs, skins, shells, and dormant cells. They are the organisms that make the stone wet in ways that are biological, not merely physical. They are the organisms that give the “city” its name without ever recognizing themselves as builders.

In the end, the place is not uninhabited, nor is it inhabited by one kind of life. It is inhabited by gradients of life. The high stone has lichens and bacteria. The wet peat has fungi and nematodes. The shallow pool has algae, protozoans, larvae, and small crustaceans. The sedge root has microbes and worms. The salt rim has dormant cells. The air carries spores. The night carries bats. The dawn carries moisture and awakening. The city has no single population. It has strata of populations, one within another.

If you must name them, you can name them this way:

They are the cyanobacterial mats that stain the basins green and gold.
They are the diatoms that make glass and leave it behind as stone.
They are the bacteria that live inside salt and breathe through water films.
They are the fungi that knit peat, root, and dust into walls.
They are the lichens that map the terraces with slow color.
They are the mosses that hold the smallest animals in their cushions.
They are the sedges that exhale oxygen into black soil.
They are the rosettes that withhold water until life can begin in a drop.
They are the tardigrades that walk through dew like tiny bears through a forest of lichen.
They are the rotifers that sweep microscopic bacteria into transparent mouths.
They are the nematodes that thread through peat and dead stems.
They are the springtails that feed on fungal threads.
They are the water fleas and ostracods that swim or crawl in shallow pools.
They are the midge larvae that live in the dark sediment.
They are the worms that move through mineral-saturated water without breaking the surface.
They are the small beetles that hunt or scavenge in thin channels.
They are the shells and egg cases that become part of the architecture.
They are the bats that come for the rising insects.
They are the waders that step across living floors.
They are the hawks that watch from a distance.
They are the wind-carried spores that decide, for a time, which surface will be colonized next.

And beneath all of these names, there is a simpler fact: the inhabitants are not separate from the landscape. They are the landscape in its living phase. The city is not built out of stone that happens to have life on it. It is built out of life that has become stone, salt, peat, channel, terrace, and pool. To describe whoever or whatever lives there, you must stop trying to see them as visitors. They are the place itself, moving very slowly, with wet hands, making itself into a city.
