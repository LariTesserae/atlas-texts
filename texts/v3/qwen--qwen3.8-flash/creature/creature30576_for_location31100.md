What lives there is not a herd, a swarm, a garden, or a community in any ordinary sense. There is no one who owns the place, no tribe that moves through it, no burrow that betrays habitation. Instead, life exists there as a set of quiet, marginal, mineralized continuations of the land itself. The inhabitants are so fused to salt, water, stone, cold light, and sound that a visitor might walk for days and believe the place empty until something almost invisible makes a motion. Then they will find that emptiness has been the least accurate word available.

The first and most pervasive inhabitant is the **brine-lace**, a pale film that lies over wet shelves like weak tea stained with chalk. At first glance it seems to be a crust, a bloom, a chemical stain, or a thin skin of condensation. But if water retreats and returns around it, it thickens in places, thins in others, cracks along particular lines, and holds droplets with a strange, deliberate geometry. It is not a plant. It is not moss, lichen, algae, or mold. It is closer to a living mineral surface: a network of microscopic threads that draw moisture from stone and give back salts, calcium, silica, and tiny crystalline seeds. It has no color beyond mineral gray, faint yellow, and the dull sheen of wet skin. It has no visible growth toward light, because light is everywhere and too fierce to organize desire. It simply persists where its chemistry is possible.

The brine-lace may be the true first life of the place, though even that word feels too warm for it. It does not eat in any way a human would recognize. It does not hunt, graze, or root. It metabolizes gradients: moisture and dryness, salt and water, heat and cold, mineral-rich flow and stillness. In a sense, it consumes the boundary conditions of the land. When a film breaks, it does not die; it becomes fragments, flakes, dust, and new stains. When two films meet, they may merge or remain separate by a hairline gap, as if each has its own chemistry to preserve. This life is so simple, so reduced, that it is nearly indistinguishable from geology. It is also nearly everywhere.

Above that film live the small **salt-walkers**, creatures like insects and crustaceans combined, though neither. They have six slender legs and bodies like translucent shards of glass. Their shells are clear enough that the interior looks hollow, or as if the animal were made of frozen air. Their eyes are absent, too small, or buried beneath crystal plates; they do not need ordinary sight in a land where everything glares equally. Instead, they perceive through pressure, salinity, vibration, and surface tension. Their legs end in tiny pads of crystalline material, broad enough to keep them from piercing the thin membranes of water lenses, sharp enough to grip salt needles and glass spines. They stand motionless on mineral ridges for long periods, not sleeping, not waiting for prey, but reading the land through the standing wave of sound.

A salt-walker does not look like something that could suffer. Its body is cold, dry-looking, and precise. It has the appearance of a creature made from fragments of the plateau itself: one leg like a splinter of limestone, its shell like broken ice, its thorax like a bead of calcium. When it moves, it moves without hurry, each step seeming calculated by some slow chemical patience. It does not leap, crawl, flee, or search. It drifts from crystal to crystal as if following an invisible path. It scrapes microscopic flakes of brine-lace with fine plates near its mouth, not chewing, but wearing them away with slow mineral teeth. Its waste is not dung; it excretes tiny lenses, beads, grains, and salt films that join the surrounding crust. In this way, even its body is a continuation of the landscape’s habit of covering itself with more surface.

The salt-walkers are rare, but their rarity is not tragic. They do not need many others because they do not form societies. They are not lonely because they are not social. Their existence is more like a punctuation mark than a conversation. A single walker on a ridge may be the first evidence that a place that looks like a dead mineral sheet is actually populated by something too subtle to announce itself. If one follows it long enough, it may move into the shadow of a lens, touch the edge of a basin, pause at a weeping rock band, or simply stand while a droplet forms on its back. It will not hide. There is no point. The land already makes it indistinguishable.

In the shallow basins live the **curled ones**, pale segmented creatures like worms made of salt and thin bone. They lie in dry-looking hollows, coiled so tightly that they resemble rolled flakes of calcium or small fossil spirals. They are not dead. Their bodies are slow, their chemistry so patient that movement between one pose and the next may take hours. Each has a segmented shell of translucent white plates, and along the underside runs a groove where moisture gathers. They breathe condensation, drink the film on stone, and absorb dissolved minerals through their undersides. They do not actively hunt. They lie where water is likely to appear, where salt crust is thin, where brine-lace spreads. If a droplet falls across their bodies, they may unroll slightly, not quickly, as if waking from a season.

The curled ones seem to be waiting for a rhythm only they understand. The roaring chord never changes, yet they do. They fold when the crust becomes dry enough to threaten them, expose themselves when moisture arrives, and sometimes extend fine threadlike feelers into a thin pool. They are blind to image, but sensitive to the shape of wetness. A basin that looks empty may contain one, curled in a perfect spiral at the center of a cracked salt plate. It might look like a bead, a seed, a fragment of shell, or a piece of debris. Only when its body adjusts by a hairline does it become animal again.

Their death is not a rotting event. In that cold, sealed brightness, they do not decay as a deer might decay in grass or a fish in a river. When one ends, its plates separate slowly, its moisture evaporates, and its shell becomes part of the crust. Salt-walkers later scrape flakes from it. Brine-lace creeps into the seams. The body is reintegrated into mineral logic. There is no carrion, no scavenger gathering, no fly, no smell. Death is only a redistribution of surface.

Within the hollow stone tubes and vent-like columns live the **pipe-dwellers**, not exactly creatures but perhaps not colonies either. These are pale, porous bodies fused to the interior walls of former hot-water channels. They look like soft stone, mineral coral, or wet sand turned to glass. Tiny holes in their surfaces open and close by a slow change in tension, not like mouths, but like pores adjusting to moisture. They feed on vapor, dissolved gases, mineral films, and the faint chemical breath rising from below. They grow by adding thin layers of calcium, silica, or salt over themselves, becoming indistinguishable from the vent walls. In time, a pipe-dweller may be only a slightly different color, a slightly smoother texture, a place where condensation beads too neatly.

They are shaped by the roar. The constant vibration that presses through the hollows becomes, for them, a form of circulation. The sound does not disturb their bodies; it helps move fluids through their pores. Some have inner membranes, translucent as onion skin, that tremble in response to particular frequencies. They do not sing, because they cannot change the chord, but they tune themselves to it. If the roar were silence to us, it might be breath to them. If the plateau were an instrument, they would be its internal dampers, living things that have learned to exist inside the tone.

On the shallow water lenses live another kind of inhabitant: the **mirror-discs**, tiny transparent organisms that ride just beneath the mineral film covering the pools. They look like drops, beads, bubbles, or splinters of glass until they drift. They are barely animals. Their bodies are flat discs of clear membrane with a dark center, not an eye but a chemical core, perhaps a pigment, perhaps a nucleus, perhaps a place where moisture is stored. They move by changing the surface tension around them, creeping or pooling into new shapes. When a water lens breaks, some split; when films connect, others merge. They are almost impossible to see without moving. They are so clear that the land’s glare uses them as mirrors. A visitor may mistake them for bubbles, reflections, or flaws in the stone until one slides beneath another.

The mirror-discs may be the most delicate life in the place. They depend on films, not depth. Too much water and they become lost; too little and they dry into flakes. They live in the exact boundary where liquid remains only as a trembling surface. They may feed by absorbing brine-lace through their undersides. Their only visible activity is slow motion, the merging of two drops, the breaking of one disc into three beads, the sudden stop when light becomes too sharp. Their existence suggests a life more like chemistry than ecology, a life that has abandoned the need to become large, fast, or hungry.

There are also **glass-hermits**, rare and solitary creatures that use fragments of the land as partial shells. They resemble salt-walkers, but with a softer, open underside that accumulates mineral layers. A hermit may carry a curved shard of limestone, a lens of salt, a pane of cracked glass, or a plate of crystalline crust over its back. This is not built, not constructed, not a house in any meaningful sense. It is borrowed shape. The creature secretes calcium around the edge of the fragment until the shard becomes part of its shell. Over time, the shell thickens, layers with new glass, and turns the animal into a walking piece of architecture that is not architecture. Its motion sounds faintly underfoot, a small chime swallowed instantly by the roar. If it stops in a basin, it becomes indistinguishable from a shard lying where it fell.

The hermit does not flee. Its shell is already camouflage, a piece of the plateau grown into a body. It moves only when a chemical gradient changes: when salt thickens underfoot, when water beads on a vertical face, when the wind shifts a thread of brine-lace, when the roar vibrates a particular ridge at a frequency that makes moisture tremble. It may pause for hours in the middle of a flat, its body invisible beneath a broken plate. To an observer, that is merely a shard. To the hermit, that is shelter, skin, and history.

What does not live there is anything like a food web. There are no predators chasing prey across the flats. No birds stoop on lenses. No insects swarm. No lizards dart over salt. No wolves pass through ridges. No scavengers gather. No herd moves. The fauna, if one can call it that, does not interact through violence in any visible way. A salt-walker may scrape a film that a curled one would later consume; a curled one’s dead shell may become a seedbed for brine-lace; a mirror-disc may drift through the remains of a broken salt-walker; a pipe-dweller may incorporate the shell of a hermit into a vent wall. But none of this resembles appetite. It resembles recycling, but slower than time usually permits. It resembles ecology, but without motion, urgency, or hunger.

These creatures communicate without voice. The roar makes vocalization impossible and unnecessary. Their “language” is made of tiny changes in wetness, tension, salt concentration, and pressure. A salt-walker’s leg touching a water lens can send a ripple. A ripple may alter the film’s shape. That altered film may signal to a mirror-disc whether water is safe, salty, cooling, or breaking. A pipe-dweller may shift the way moisture condenses on its pores, leaving a faint ring of salt for a walker to read. A curled one may expose a groove of body-fluid into dry air, changing the nearby mineral color. These are not signs, not words, not messages. They are subtle states, read by other slow beings. Communication here is not speech; it is chemistry adjusting to chemistry.

Their intelligence, if it exists, is not the intelligence of curiosity. They do not explore. They do not collect. They do not seem to remember in any way a human could recognize, though perhaps they do, because their bodies are shaped by long exposure to patterns. If one watched a salt-walker for a season, it might seem to take the same route, but the route may be the only one the minerals make possible. If one watched a hermit, it might seem to avoid certain basins, but those basins may simply be too dry or too wet for its chemistry.
