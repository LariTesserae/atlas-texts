## The Inhabitants of the Hollow of Keth-Salt

The first answer is not the people.

Before the broad steps, before the doorways cut from the old slope, before the granaries on short legs and the low rounded roofs, the Hollow of Keth-Salt was already inhabited by what lives across surfaces: a pale, slow, mineral life that might not be called life at all except by those who have watched it move.

The true natives are skins.

They spread over damp ledges in rust-colored films and white lacy mats, chalky blooms and salt frosts. They creep across walls that have sweat through the night, over the edges of brine pans, along the underbellies of stone shelves, in the thin damp seams where a vein of gypsum meets a band of shale. They do not rise like plants. They do not flower like flowers. They advance, retreat, dry into flakes, dissolve into slime, return in a different form when the stone gives back a few hours of hidden water. They are food. They are shelter. They are the first field of the hollow.

The people have names for them that sound almost like weather: wall-milk, lace-ash, crust-bread, the white tongue of the floor. They scrape them, encourage them, burn or brush dead layers from stone, salt them, wash them with dilute brine, press them into cakes, fold them into hides, soak them until they release a faint smell like cold yeast and iron. A prepared wall is not decoration. It is a larder. A family may know a particular crust as another people might know a vineyard, a meadow, a fishing ground. The best walls are low, cool, open to the fault-light, and crossed by capillary damp. The worst are too dry, too hot with geothermal breath, too deep in shadow where something else can grow.

Under and among these skins live the small walkers.

Pale crabs go through brine channels with translucent shells, so delicate that their internal salt glands look like folded moons pressed under glass. They push grit ahead of them in tiny tidal waves and clean the bottom of shallow pans, leaving behind patterns too regular to be chance. The people do not chase them, but they know where they feed. When the crabs emerge from dry cracks, something wet is coming.

Beetles with glassy carapaces climb salt walls and scratch faint trails into the dust. Their paths are like narrow maps of moisture: curved where the air hung heavier, straight where the stone was bare and dry. They feed on old lace and dried crust, sometimes on dead things no larger than a thumbprint. Their shells are used by children for counting and by adults for marking thresholds; a beetle casing embedded in plaster is a sign that the wall has been read.

In wet crevices, blind worms coil and swallow mineral paste, then expel it as fine white ribbons that line the tunnels behind them. This worm-lace is prized. It absorbs damp, binds cracks, and stiffens when exposed to salt dust. The people collect it, wash it, press it, and use it to seal seams between stone and hide where no plant fiber could be twisted. A house whose joints are caulked with worm-lace will not shout against the air; it will only breathe when the hollow breathes.

Shelled slug-animals rest beneath stone shelves when condensation comes, moving only when the water draws back. They leave silver tracks that dry into chalky lines, and they carry spores and films from one damp niche to another. Some are eaten. Some are tolerated. Most are considered signs. If a silver track appears across the entrance to a granary, the granary is watched. If a slug sleeps too long on a sleeping platform, the platform is lifted and aired.

Then there are the bright drifters.

They are winged things without a place among birds or true insects: soft-bodied fliers with wide, thin membranes that catch the mineral light without quite becoming visible. They drift through the brightest streets in sudden silent spirals, drawn to damp crystal, to the smell of fungal blooms, to the chemical warmth of surfaces that have just begun to dry. They settle on lintels like living dust. They do not chirp. They do not hum. When they are disturbed, they fold upward and vanish into the glare.

The Keth say they carry the hollow’s scent. Whether this means pollen, or spores, or mineral dust, or simply the memory of water moving somewhere no one can see, they do not argue. They leave fliers on the roof beams. They brush them gently from salt shelves. They do not crush them unless they gather too thickly in a granary, where they can spoil stored lace by wetting it with their own moisture.

There are predators too, but the Hollow of Keth-Salt does not produce loud ones.

Chert-foxes, if they may be called foxes, are low-slung and silent, with flat heads, broad salt-slick feet, and pale eyes that reflect fault-light in a narrow white streak. They hunt along spill-ways and behind stacked walls, taking crabs, young beetles, fliers, and at times the calves of the broad-backed animals. They are not hated. They are not welcomed. They are read as signs of balance: if chert-foxes avoid a terrace too long, something beneath the terrace has gone wrong. If a flier cluster grows restless in a court, the fox may already be there, waiting with its head flat against the stone.

Among all creatures, though, the broadbacks are the hinge of life.

They are the grazers of Keth-Salt, domestic or half-wild, kept by the hollow-dwellers without being truly owned. They are broad-backed, short-necked animals with pale eyes, thick salt-stained hides, and hooves wide enough to stand on damp grit without sinking. Their mouths are made for rasping fungal mats from rock, and they lick prepared walls the way other herds would graze a meadow. They eat crust-skins, salt films, mineral pastes, the dried remnants of lace, and when a brine pan overflows too fast, they drink until their flanks ripple.

They are not loud. A herd passing through an empty court makes no sound but a slow scrape of hooves and a dry crackling of dust. They lie down in sheltered places when the air turns cold. They crowd beneath lintels when a warm pulse is rising. If a broadback refuses to lick a certain wall, the wall is suspect. If a broadback stands too long in one spot, sniffing the ground with its blunt mouth, a hidden channel may be close to opening.

Their young are raised in dry courts, away from sudden condensation showers, because calf hides cannot yet resist salt damp. The people do not fence the calves away from the herd for long; they arrange the emptiness so that the animals move without confusion. A mother knows a court by scent, by chalk mark, by the angle of the fault-light above it, and by the taste of the water in the pan.

The broadbacks give more than meat.

Their dung, mixed with salt dust and mineral ash, becomes plaster. Their hair, pulled out at the right season when the roots loosen with warmth from below, can be twisted into thread. Their bones are tools, weights, door-fastenings, handles, pins, and polished wedges. Their fat is rendered into lamps. Their hides are scraped, salted, stretched, and layered into clothing and coverings. A single animal can give material to a house for years.

Some people milk them too, but not as if milking were ordinary. The Keth take milk only when the animal offers calm and only when a pulse has loosened the herd into gentleness. The milk is thick, faintly mineral, and used sparingly in fermented crust-pastes or in the feeding of sick children. To milk a broadback too often would be to disturb it from reading the stone, and a disturbed herd is a loss greater than one day’s drink.

The people of the hollow are the Keth.

They are not a single race by the measure of the dry plain above them, but generations of living in Keth-Salt have worn them into a shape that belongs to the place. They are compact and broad through the chest and hips, with short thick necks, strong shoulders, and wide feet that seem almost made for salt-slick terraces. Their hands are large, soft-palmed, and callused in odd places—on the knees from squatting by walls, on the palms from scraping crust, on the fingers from feeling damp before the eye can see it. Their skin is pale to greyish brown, always dusted with salt or chalk, and their faces have a chalky shine in the fault-light. Their noses are broad and short, their lips often cracked, their teeth worn from grit but white-banded with mineral salt. They have little body hair except at the scalp, where their hair is coarse and sometimes felted with animal fat and chalk. Their eyelashes are dense. Their eyes catch the light strangely, often a clear grey-blue or pale amber, with pupils that stay narrow even in the cool brightness.

They move as if the hollow is asking them not to hurry.

This is not because they are lazy. It is because haste is a mistake in Keth-Salt. A too-strong push on a damp wall can break a crystal bloom before the water has been read. A too-heavy footfall can crack a salt shelf and reveal a hole beneath. A too-loud voice can make children cry without seeming to carry any message at all. The Keth do not walk as if they are late. They walk as if they are being watched by the floor.

They are human, and they remain human enough to be recognizable: they carry infants at the breast or on the hip, they mend each other’s clothing, they joke with faces that barely move, they hold grudges over water, and they sometimes sit in a quiet court for hours without speaking, simply because the place needs to be listened to again. But their humanity has been narrowed by the hollow into a different patience.

Their origin is uncertain, and the Keth do not like being asked too closely.

There is a story that says they were once plain-folk above, driven down when the dryness hardened the old river paths. Another says they came for copper and salt and forgot the surface while still alive. The most common household version is older and simpler: the steps were already there, and the people were already in them. Keth-Salt does not ask for a founding myth with a beginning. It is too old for that. To be Keth is not to descend into a cave and survive; it is to belong to a place that has learned to shine through broken stone.

Their houses are not boxes, but readings.

A Keth dwelling is usually a court: a low wall of stacked stone, a shelf cut from the hollow’s own slope, a sleeping platform on short legs, a pan for condensation, a crust garden behind the lintel, and a space for animals if the household tends a small herd. The roof is lashed with hide and fungal cord, sometimes covered with thin sheets of gypsum that hold the fault-light and shed droplets gently. The forms are blunt and rounded because sharp angles invite trouble: salt crust can catch in a seam, brine can hide under a straight line, and crystal growth will pry anything rigid until it fails.

They build with gaps.

A door may be a natural wall on one side and a hand-cut lintel on the other, because if the stone lifts the lintel later, there will be room for it. A roof frame may have spaces between lashed cord and the walls so that condensation can bead and run without soaking. Granaries sit on short legs so the floor stays above sudden damp, and the legs themselves are cut from stone with rounded bases so salt swelling will not tilt them. A street may be the old groove of vanished water, widened only enough to pass without disturbing the film inside it.

The settlement is sparse, and the Keth are not troubled by the distance between homes.

To a visitor, the emptiness can feel lonely. To the Keth, emptiness is part of the house. A court with no roof is a court that listens to the ceiling-sweat. A street with no footprints for an hour is a street the hollow has chosen to keep for itself. They do not say, “That is where someone lives” as if life could only mean a doorway. They say, “That is a place water is thinking,” or “That ledge remembers,” or “Those animals are moving through.” A home is not always a structure. It can be a shelf of prepared wall, a dry cleft where a broadback rests, a cistern pan ringed by footprints, a terrace where the condensation falls just enough for two households to share without crowding.

Their tools are few, but every object knows its material.

Basalt hammerstones are chosen by voice: struck against a hard wall, they will answer with the right tone. Chert chisels are split by hand until their edge follows the desired curve. Polished bone picks wear down and are reshaped rather than replaced. Copper needles are drawn from native ore, annealed in warm fissures when the hollow allows it, and never left in salt too long unless their owner wants them pale-green and brittle with age. Awls of hardened tooth are used for hide. Cord from gut and sinew is twisted over the thigh, rubbed with fungal powder, and hung in coils from lintels where the air keeps it dry. Baskets are woven from flexible fungal strips, stitched with bone needles and waxed with rendered fat and mineral resin.

They do not have wood, so they do not think in wood.

A Keth does not imagine a beam. A Keth imagines a load path: a shelf under the stone, a spread of stacked wall, a hide lashed and pulled, a gypsum sheet bent over a corded frame. Their engineering is tactile, patient, and almost bodily. They learn to feel the difference between a wall that is merely damp and a wall that is preparing to give up a vein of water. They learn which basalt will crack and which will take a knock without complaint. They learn that a good floor may look ruined because it has been left soft enough to receive movement.

Their work is mostly small and repeated.

In a household day, a person may scrape dead crust from a wall, wet a section with a handful of condensation water, rub a salt-ash paste into the stone, move weights on a granary leg, clear a brine channel with a bone hook, stretch a hide over a roof frame, sort fungal strips, twist a cord, cut chalk marks along a threshold, guide a herd toward a court, gather crabs from a shallow pan, render fat in a stone saucer, press a cake of lace-dough into a mold, and sit for a moment on a dry ledge doing nothing at all.

The doing nothing is not wasted. It is listening.

A skilled Keth can tell the difference between the slow hiss of dust moving through a groove and the faint tick of salt lifting a slab. They can tell when a crystal bloom is merely growing and when it is pushing something loose. They can place a palm flat against a wall and know by the temperature, the damp, the faintest movement of grit, that a hidden vein will give water within an hour. This is not magic. It is the ordinary result of living in a place where every surface is a page and the water is the hand.

Their children learn before they are old enough to speak.

An infant is held against the breast or hip and carried through the dry courts, but the first real lesson comes when a child’s palm is pressed to a sweating wall. The wall must not be too warm or too sharp. It is usually chosen because it will give a single drop in a known place. The child feels the drop gather. Then it runs. The child is not asked to understand the fault-light, the old currents, or the chemistry of salt. They are asked simply not to pull away.

By the time they are young enough to herd, children can already read moisture by the look of dust. They know which beetle trails mean water is coming, which chert-fox signs mean not to pass, and which flier spirals mean the air is turning. They are given short chalk sticks and small weights made of bone or polished stone. They mark thresholds. They count pulses by adding a bead to a cord. They learn not to step on the last white ribbon left by a worm, because that ribbon may be the beginning of a caulk.

They are loud children by other standards, but Keth-Salt suppresses their noise. They laugh by shaking their ribs and letting the sound die almost immediately. They call to each other with hand signs or by striking a short, flat knock on stone. The knock is not heard as an echo; it is felt in the floor. If a child calls too loudly, an adult does not scold them from a distance. The adult comes, takes the child’s face gently in a salt-coarse hand, and waits until the voice goes small.

Their language is low, full of soft breath, and made of words that seem to begin and end against the teeth.

There are many words for wet. There are fewer words for hot, because heat is rare and unpredictable. There are words for the look of salt when it is dry and the look of salt when it is about to weep. There are names for surfaces that have been touched by the fault-light, surfaces that have been sheltered too long, surfaces that have become too sharp. There are no words for leaves or blossoms, except as borrowed from the skins: “lace,” “crust,” “bloom,” “frost,” “white tongue.” There is no word for sky in the ordinary sense. The closest is a phrase that means “the shining wound that gives cool light,” and the Keth say it without reverence, the way other people might name the roof.

Because the sound fades so quickly, much of their speech is visual.

A household may communicate across a court by chalk. A single line means “I am here.” A circle means “dry.” A broken circle means “not safe.” A thumbprint pressed into salt means “I have passed and left this mark.” A hand-cut arrow along a channel means “do not fill this.” A small pile of flier-membrane on a threshold is a question: “what moves?” A stone set on a threshold means “do not enter.” A bone needle left on a sleeping platform means “someone needs help but cannot shout.”

They also knot cords.

A gut cord with a bead of copper and a twist of fungal thread may carry a message between distant households. One knot can mean “brine is rising.” Two knots can mean “the animals know.” A bead of bone can mean “wait.” A bead of salt can mean “the stone has given.” If the recipient understands, they do not reply. They add their own mark to the next cord. This keeps distances alive without forcing them to be crowded.

Their law is not written on walls, because walls change.

A law marker must be portable, repeatable, and cut into a place where people will find it even if the settlement rearranges itself. The Keth therefore make law a practice of stone-reading. There is no single judge. There are those who are better at waiting.

The most respected are not the loud or the strong, but the listeners. These are often older people who can sleep with one palm against the floor, or who will sit at the mouth of a channel for hours, watching a salt film grow. They know the difference between a house that is merely uncomfortable and a house that is about to fail. They know when a herd is nervous without understanding why. They know when the warm pulse has come too deep and the air is beginning to sour with mineral breath.

Disputes are mostly about water, salt paths, animal shelter, or a wall someone has scraped too greedily. Because no one can afford to build a great new house quickly, and no one can afford to poison a channel out of anger, conflicts are resolved by following the water. If two households claim the same groove, both may sit at different points and wait for the next condensation pulse. Whichever path the water takes is accepted. If neither path survives, a new marker may be cut between them. If a household is found guilty of blocking a channel out of spite, it is not beaten. It is asked to move.

To be asked to move is severe.

In Keth-Salt, moving does not mean walking a few yards. It means choosing a new set of relationships with stone, salt, animals, and light. It may mean learning new grooves before the first pulse. It may mean sleeping in dry places where the air is thin. Exile is not banishment to a distant kingdom; it is banishment to emptiness without the knowledge of how emptiness is used.

Their religion is not a temple.

A temple would be a fixed thing, and Keth-Salt cannot be fixed. Yet the Keth behave as if the stone is listening, because in every practical sense, it is. Every footprint is a memory. Every dropped weight is a pressure. Every channel cut or filled is a sentence the hollow will repeat in its own way. They do not pray to the fault-light. They attend to it. They do not worship the water. They feed it. They do not fear the deep places so much as the hidden places. The deep places are only empty. The hidden places are where salt can trap a limb, where a thin crust can hide a hole, where a crystal bloom can grow too fast.

If there is a sacred principle, it is shallowness.

To remain near the surface of things is to remain readable. To dig too deep is to break the old veins. To cut a line too sharply is to fight the hollow, and the hollow will win by filling the cut with something you cannot predict. A good house, a good path, a good meal, a good animal, a good child—all are shallow, attentive, and easy to move with. A bad one is hard, hidden, and proud.

They celebrate pulses, though they would not call celebration that.

When the warm breath rises and the fault-light seems to sharpen, the Keth do not gather in one place. There is no need for a crowd. The hollow itself becomes a gathering. People move quickly: pulling down canvas roofs, covering stored crusts, guiding herds toward sheltered courts, closing the mouths of channels with damp clay so the water will not wash through too fast. Children are held or set in dry corners. Fliers lift from lintels. Crabs emerge. Lace walls darken as they drink.

Then, when the pulse fades, the town becomes bright in another way. The water film catches the mineral light from a thousand angles. Dust glitters. Salt shelves shine. The broadbacks stand in the courts with their pale eyes catching the glare, chewing slowly as if tasting the change. The people come out again, not in a rush, but with their fingers on the walls, reading what has moved. A new thread of brine may have appeared between two stones. A crystal may have cracked open with a sound almost like a sigh. A granary floor may have given a wet creak, lifting by a hair’s breadth.

This is not fear. It is conversation.

In the nameless hour, the Keth do not name the time aloud. They have many small names for its phases: “the stone drinks,” “the walls weep,” “the light sharpens,” “the dust lifts,” “the fliers wake,” “the animals move,” “the salt gives.” But they do not give it one fixed name, because to name it fully would be to say it has a place in the hollow, and the hollow has taught them that some things become more dangerous when they are named as if they will come back in the same shape.

Their food is strange to the plain people.

They eat crust-cakes made from scraped lace, salt, mineral paste, and sometimes rendered fat. They eat fermented brine-paste stored in clay pots, where the sourness is not vinegary but cold and chalky. They eat crabs lightly pounded in salt, beetles roasted on stone plates, worm-lace softened in dilute water, flier membranes dried and crumbled into seasoning for animal meat. They eat broadback flesh sparingly, because a live animal is worth more than a meal, and because the herds are read as weather. But when an animal is too old to lick well, or too tired to move with the condensation, or has become dangerous in a channel, it may be taken by a household and its parts divided into many uses.

Their bodies are made for this diet.

They eat little plant food, so they have learned to tolerate fungal compounds that would trouble a stranger. Their tongues are wide, their lips strong, their teeth worn but solid. Their stomachs are said to be hard, their hands hard, their eyes hard, their breath cool. They can survive long stretches of dry crust and salt, provided hidden water is somewhere within walking. They are not fragile, but they are not careless. The old ones know which walls are safe to lick and which ones carry too much copper or too much iron. They know when a salt pan is sweet enough and when it is only poisoning the air.

They drink from stoppered gut flasks filled with condensation water, not because brine cannot be drunk when separated, but because the body remembers the difference. A person who drinks too much hidden salt water becomes thirsty in a way that cold fault-light cannot fix. The Keth do not spit when they taste bitterness. They stop walking, touch the ground, and find something better.

Their clothing is layered and pale.

They wear hides scraped thin by salt, fungal felts pressed over inner skins, and broad wraps dyed faintly with mineral pigments: rust, chalk, copper-green, grey, and the white that cannot be dyed because it is already everywhere. Shoes are wide, flat, and stitched with bone needles, often lined with worm-lace and dried animal hair. Gloves are common at the hands because walls are rough and crystal blooms cut. Caps and hoods are lashed to protect hair from condensation showers, which are not rain but behave like rain when they are trying to ruin a child’s crust-cake.

They smell of salt, dust, animal fat, fungal paste, cold stone, and the faint metallic tang of fault-light. A stranger notices that they also smell clean, or at least not dirty. Salt does much in Keth-Salt. The people wash hands in brine, rub dead skin from their palms with pumice-like stone, and scrape dust from their bodies after a dry walk. They do not bathe as in a river. They stand under a condensation thread when it appears and let the water run over them for the brief time it remembers them.

Their grief is quiet because the hollow makes noise fragile.

When a person dies, there is no great lament across the basin. A child’s crying is carried from mouth to mouth in soft breath. An adult may press their face to the dead person’s salt-caked hands and make a small sound that stops too soon. The body is washed in brine, wrapped in fungal cloth, and placed where the family can watch it without fear that water will destroy it too quickly. Sometimes the body is laid on a low shelf of porous stone. Sometimes it is placed in a dry cleft where the lace-skins will spread slowly over it. Sometimes, if the person lived far from a house and no shelter can be reached before the pulse, the body is covered with chalk, salted, and marked so that later, if the stone allows, it may be read back into the settlement.

The Keth do not bury deep. They say the ground has too many hidden mouths.

Over weeks or months, depending on how the hollow is moving, salt blooms over the body. A hand may become a white negative against the stone. A face may become a smooth chalk ridge. After the bloom has finished, the household cuts new marks into a nearby stone: not a name only, but a gesture. A bent line for a channel. A small circle for a court. A pair of scratched marks for a herd. The dead are not made into monuments. They become readable.

They believe this is enough.

If the stone remembers every movement, then a life leaves a shape even after the shape dissolves. The Keth may not speak of this as faith. They speak of it as craft. A body is a movement. A mark is a movement. A wall is a movement that has become almost stone. To be remembered is not to remain unchanged. It is to have left a groove someone else can follow.

They are not primitive in the way visitors mistake.

A person from the dry plain might look at a Keth dwelling and think it crude: rounded stone, stretched hide, no beams, no carved facades, no grand plaza, no market, no army, no written laws. But that judgment is wrong in the most interesting way. The Keth have not failed to build a surface city. They have built a different kind of city, one that cannot be judged by the rules of a place that assumes stability.

In Keth-Salt, permanence is a lie told by dry stone to the wet.

If you build too solid, the hollow will pry you apart with salt. If you make water too straight, it will choose a new path around you. If you make a roof too tight, condensation will find the seam. If you shout, the sound dies. If you crowd, the emptiness becomes dangerous. The Keth’s skill is not in resisting these truths. It is in using them. Their walls are rough because roughness holds dust. Their streets are wide because a narrow street becomes a channel trap. Their granaries are high because the damp must be given room to move. Their houses are sparse because density is a luxury of places that know what tomorrow’s water will do.

They are also, without saying so, ecologists.

They encourage crusts that feed crabs and beetles, and those feed fliers and young predators. They keep channels open not only for water but for animals that aerate brine and spread salt. They let worm-lace line tunnels because it makes the stone more flexible. They let broadbacks move across courts because their rasping tongues keep walls from growing too thick, too foul, or too brittle. They let chert-foxes hunt in certain places because a court without predators becomes a court too crowded with small things.

The hollow is not a tame world, but it is not untamed either. It is attended.

If someone from above comes down the broad rounded steps, the Keth do not hide. They may watch from a distance, reading the stranger’s footprints as if the feet are telling them how much weight the stranger expects the floor to bear. Visitors often speak too loudly. This is not because they are rude but because they are frightened. The near-silence feels wrong. Voices vanish before they are finished, and strangers think they have been ignored.

The Keth answer by waiting.

They may let a visitor sit in a court until the visitor understands that no one will explain the hollow in words. They may offer a cup of condensation water, a piece of crust-cake, and a warning: do not touch the white wall, do not step on the cracked salt pan, do not disturb the animals until they have read your breath. If the visitor becomes impatient, the Keth will simply move away. If the visitor is quiet, they may be taken to a place where the fault-light is sharp and a new brine channel is appearing, and the visitor may see how the hollow chooses its own direction.

The trade, when it happens, is small.

The Keth do not need much from above. They have salt, gypsum, copper from native deposits, bone, hide, fungal strips, and animal fiber. From the plain, if a trader ever reaches the first terrace, they might want something harder to find: dried plant material, surface grain, resin, or goods that smell of sun and leaves. The Keth often consider such things unnecessary, even wasteful. They may trade a gypsum sheet for a copper ingot, or a basket of worm-lace for a handful of surface flint. They do not send caravans. They do not climb often. The fault-light is enough sky.

Some Keth become surface-readers.

These are people who walk the upper slope often enough to know the wind before it changes the hollow’s air. They watch dust in the shaft-light. They notice when the fault lenses dim because of a dry storm above, or when condensation increases because unseen rain has soaked the plain. They are not prophets. They are observers. They know the hollow is not cut off from the surface, only quiet about it. When they return, they report by marks: a line on a wall, a chalk circle at the threshold, a knot in a cord.

Among themselves, the Keth are not cold.

They are restrained, yes. They are slow. They are careful with touch. They do not flood a court with bodies. But when they meet after a long absence, they press their palms to each other’s salt-dusted hands and hold there while the other breathes. They lean against each other on low walls to share warmth from a stone shelf. They divide a crust-cake with a small bone knife, never cutting more than can be finished. They carry a tired child on the hip without complaint. They leave a cup of sweet water at a threshold when they wish to say “I remember” without shouting the person’s name across empty air.

A Keth court can feel abandoned until a pulse comes.

Then suddenly, from hidden places, life appears.

A flier drops onto a lintel. A crab emerges from a crack. A beetle crawls across the wall-milk. A broadback turns its blunt head toward the condensation. A woman crosses from one shelf to another with a bundle of chalk sticks. A child kneels to touch the new wet line on the floor. An old person places a palm on the granary leg and finds that the salt has lifted it by less than the width of a fingernail, and says nothing, but moves a stone weight to the next place. The whole court brightens as if the air itself has become aware of water.

This is the hour outsiders remember most, though they often cannot say why.

The hollow feels inhabited then not because the people have emerged, but because the place has answered them. The people do not create the pulse. They join it. They are not separate from the biology of the skins, the animals, the fliers, the brine, the crystal. They are one part of a larger metabolism. The Keth know this in their bones. A child who is afraid of the sudden shining wetness learns that fear is only an incorrect movement, too tight, too fast, too much like fighting. The correct movement is to place one hand on the wall, one hand near the child’s back, and let the water do what the water must do.

Their animals know it too.

The broadbacks are not domesticated in the sense of being obedient. They are domesticated in the sense of being understood. A herd will not stand still because a person commands. A herd will stand if the condensation tells it to stand, and if the person has read the condensation well enough to be there first. The bond is not ownership but agreement. A Keth herder may sleep with a broadback in the same court, not because it is affection alone, but because the animal’s breathing tells the human whether the air is turning warm or cold. The human’s chalk mark tells the animal where water will be found. The animal’s hoofprints tell the human which stones are about to shift.

Their children inherit this attention.

Some are slow and become channel-readers, able to sit for hours at the mouth of a spill-way. Others are quick and become animal-tenders, moving through herds with a small tap of bone on stone. Some have sensitive skin and become crust-gardeners, scraping, salting, and moistening walls with care. Some have hard teeth and can taste a wall’s safety better than an adult. Some cannot read the hollow and become the ones who repair the human parts: lashing, mending, carrying water, cutting hide. No one is shamed for it. A person who cannot hear water may still hear salt cracking in a hidden joint.

They do not fear darkness so much as hiddenness.

In the hollow, darkness is not death. Darkness is stillness, shelter, waiting. But hidden places can become traps. A salt film may look like a dry floor until a person steps through it into a brine channel. A crystal bloom may seal a gap so firmly that it looks like solid stone until someone tries to pry it and the whole thing shatters into wet shards. A channel beneath a granary may appear to be an ordinary groove, but if the film over it is too thin and someone kneels where they should stand, they can sink through one hand to the wrist in cold brine. The Keth teach children not to lean on anything before testing it with a bone needle. They teach them not to look for danger in the obvious dark, but in the subtle bright.

Their fear of the surface is not simple.

Some say the plain is too hot. Some say it is too open. Some say the wind up there carries nothing back to the stone, and therefore has no memory. The Keth have stories of ancestors who climbed and came down with sunburned faces and cracked lips, unable to breathe the thin dry air. They also have stories of ancestors who climbed because the hollow was too patient and they wanted a life that shouted. These stories are not forbidden. They are just not told at night. They are told in the dry seasons of the body, when a person feels the air too close and the fault-light too flat.

There are those who leave.

Not many. The distance is not just steps. The old slope is broad and pale, but it leads to a world where water does not come as a gift from stone. If a person climbs out, the Keth may mark the threshold with chalk and then wait. If the person returns, the mark is touched but not erased. The hollow has remembered them going and coming. If they never return, the mark becomes dry and is eventually covered by lace.

What lives in Keth-Salt, then, is layered and continuous.

It is the skins on the wall, the crabs in the channels, the beetles in the dust, the worms in the crevices, the fliers in the light, the broadbacks in the courts, the chert-foxes along the spill-ways. It is the Keth moving slowly through all of it, reading the hollow not as a thing outside them but as a thing that includes them. It is the pulse itself, a visitor and a resident, because the place is not merely the stone and the animals and the people but the rhythm that brings them together.

To live in Keth-Salt is to be always being read.

A wall is read by a hand. A channel is read by a foot. A herd is read by a mother’s breath. A house is read by salt lifting its floor. A street is read by the direction of dust. Even the fault-light is read: too bright on one surface means evaporation; too sharp on another means condensation; too dim means the plain above is troubled. Nothing is stable enough to be taken for granted, and nothing is empty enough to be ignored.

And the people themselves are read too.

A Keth is known not by loudness, not by decoration, not by the size of a house, but by how they leave marks. Does their chalk follow a water path or fight it? Does their roof let condensation run without soaking? Do their granaries lift gently when salt swells, or do they crack? Do their animals calm when they enter a court? Do children seek their hands after a pulse? Do old people listen when they speak of a wall?

These are the measures.

In the Hollow of Keth-Salt, whoever lives there has learned to live without domination. The people do not impose a plan on the hollow. They enter into a slow negotiation with it. Their tools are borrowed. Their food is grown from films and brine. Their homes are arranged into erosion. Their law follows water. Their grief follows salt. Their joy is not fireworks but recognition: a new channel where none was seen, a wall that gives water at the right hour, a broadback standing in a dry court because the animal knows the pulse is coming, a child placing a palm on the stone and waiting until it sweats.

The hollow is full of presences separated by shining emptiness, but those presences are not alone.

They answer to one another. The skins are scraped. The channels are cleared. The animals are guided. The streets are re-marked. The roofs are lowered before the water can ruin them. The dead are left to bloom. The living listen. And when the fault-light sharpens and the nameless hour begins, all of them move together: crabs pushing wet grit, fliers circling in the brightness, beetles climbing salt walls, worms tightening their white lace, broadbacks grazing the damp crust, children standing close to adults, hands on stone, breath lingering in the cool air.

This is who lives there.

Not one people, not one creature, not even one city. The Hollow of Keth-Salt is inhabited by a web of patience, salt, mineral light, condensation, and quiet movement. The Keth are the speaking part of it. The skins are its first memory. The small walkers are its fingers. The broadbacks are its breath. The fliers are its dust. The pulse is its mind. And the stone, beneath all of them, is the listening shape that keeps them honest.
