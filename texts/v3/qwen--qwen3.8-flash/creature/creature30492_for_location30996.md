What lives in the Still-Roar Cirque is not a population, exactly, but an ecology of presences. It is a slow society of conditions—dampness, cold, pressure, sound, patience—each one having endured so long that it has thickened into something almost personal. There are no herds, no nests, no visible burrows. The cirque’s inhabitants do not crowd or compete in the ordinary way. They keep to themselves, spaced by miles of gravel and silence, separated by ridges that curve away into abstraction. Yet the cirque is full. It is full of the kind of fullness that feels like absence until you stand within it too long.

The black pools are the first and most obvious of these beings, though they are not pools in the way a valley would understand them. Each dark, still surface is an organ. The water is not merely collected water; it is water that has learned to maintain a condition. Beneath each pool lies pale sand and crushed feldspar, and those grains move, though no current moves them. They shift with the slowness of geology compressed into an inch. If you kneel beside one of these pools and watch for hours, you may see the pale bottom rearrange itself into faint threads, like words written in a language that refuses to be finished. The cirque’s people call them Feldspar Sentences, though that name is only a human approximation. Each sentence is begun, suspended, and never completed.

The Feldspar Sentences feed on equilibrium. They take in capillary moisture from surrounding cracks, draw pressure out of the still air, and metabolize light that has been so thinned by elevation that it feels less like illumination than like the memory of illumination. They give off no vapor, no rime, no visible breath. When a hand touches one, the pool receives the contact as a disturbance and then refuses to include it in its history. The water remains cold, wet, and perfectly indifferent. It knows the hand is there only as long as the hand is there. Afterward, nothing remains but dryness, as if the hand had been made of smoke.

These pool-beings are tended by the Dampness Keepers.

The Dampness Keepers are not creatures in a conventional sense. They live as seams. They are long, shining strands beneath overhangs, dark stains on rock, patches of moss too saturated to look real, hollows where moisture arrives by capillary force and stops at the edge of the stone with the hesitation of a living thing. They have no fixed bodies, only temporary expressions of wetness. When they appear, they look like a thin thread of glass pulled from a furnace and allowed to cool into the shape of a slow animal. They move by wetting ahead of themselves, not by walking. A Keeper may spend decades transferring a single bead of dampness from one crevice to another, and in doing so it maintains the cirque’s impossible balance. It prevents the water from becoming abundance. It prevents the dryness from becoming desolation.
