What lives there cannot be counted as tenants in a building, because the building and the tenants are one argument. If you ask who, the place answers with motion: a reed bending where no wind touches it, a tile lifting by its own edge, a thread tightening itself around a joint that was tied only a moment before. If you ask what, it answers with the sound of something too crowded to be still: drip, tap, hiss, scrape, click, the soft creak of wet clay deciding that it will hold.

There are no people in it, not as they left the place behind them. There are no bodies seated by fires, no children learning the names of stones, no old hands resting on tools. But there are hands. Or rather, there are the habits of hands, the aftertaste of intention, the unfinished pressure of fingers that no longer require flesh. This is the first resident, if a resident is a thing that answers to the name of a thing. It has no face, but it has many faces. A thumbprint in wet clay. A half-knot that will not loosen. A flint flake that turns, ever so slightly, toward a gap that needs filling. A root pulled around a pin as though guided by a patient, invisible thumb. If one must speak of it as a who, call it the Continuing Hand. Do not imagine fingers, or wrists, or arms. The fingers are drains. The palm is a terrace. The knuckles are stairs. The breath is steam.
