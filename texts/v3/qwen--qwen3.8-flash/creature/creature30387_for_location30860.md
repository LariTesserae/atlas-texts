The town does not have streets so much as it has currents; its districts are named for the way the water moves through them: the Still Lip, the Green Seam, the Small Loop, the Warm Hollow, the Terrace of Listening Rain. To move through it is to travel in a series of gentle lifts and falls, as if the shelf were turning in sleep.

The people who live there are called, in their own tongue, the Suur. The word is not a name for a species or a tribe, exactly. It is closer to the sound made when a hand moves through still water: a soft parting, a faint catch of breath. Travelers from the lower world, when there are lower-world travelers to make mistakes of translation, have called them rain-folk, water-people, shell-born, drowned saints, or the Warm Ones. The Suur prefer the simple sound because it does not pretend to separate them from the medium that holds them.

They are people, if one can accept that a person may be more river than body. Their bones are light and porous, shot through with tiny chambers like pumice, and the chambers are filled not with marrow alone but with a warm fluid and small pockets of gas. Their skulls are broad, their brows low, their faces long in the middle, with nostrils that can close behind flaps of thin muscle. Their skin is pale green-gold, translucent enough that the movement of fluids beneath it can be seen when they are still, and it is covered in microscopic cilia that tremble when currents change. In the lower world, they would look wet, sickly, almost too permeable to be alive. Here, in the warm flood, they look merely home.

Their breathing is a shared secret between gill and lung. Along the ribs, behind the ears, and in delicate folds at the wrists and ankles, the Suur carry frills of tissue, pale as inner shells, veined with fine threads of blood. In water these frills open like petals and drink oxygen from the current; in the humid air above the basin lines they fold inward, retaining moisture and allowing the lungs to work without the panic of dryness. Their chests move slowly, almost imperceptibly, and when they rest suspended in a basin the frills flutter with each breath, so that a sleeping person can look from above like a flower bed stirred by a passing wind.

Their eyes are adapted to a world without a single clear boundary. They are large, set wide, with a second lid that slides sideways like a ripple. The irises are green-gold in youth, deepening with age to amber and copper, and their pupils widen until almost all the eye is dark. They do not see the flood as a blur. To a Suur, the warm water has textures, colors, voices. They can notice a faint mineral cloud from a dozen paces away, the way a lowlander might see a column of smoke. They can tell if the current above a terrace is fresh rain or older, recirculated water by the way the light bends around the suspended particles. They do not need dryness to see clearly; they need the absence of movement.

Their fingers are long and jointed in extra places, each tipped with a flattened pad that can feel a current as finely as a tongue feels a word. The webbing between them is never more than a faint crest, not enough to turn the hand into a flipper, but enough to catch water in the right way when swimming. Their feet are broad and slightly curved, good for pressing against wet stone without slipping, and their legs move with a strange, liquid patience. They do not run. If the water allows, they drift; if the air allows, they wade; if the stone allows, they climb by wedging their feet into fractures and pressing their bodies to the current.

They are not, however, the only inhabitants of the shelf.

The Suur say, often, that a town is not what stands, but what answers. A channel answers the rain. A basin answers the current. A house answers the family. The green seams answer the stone. The shelf is full of things that answer, and some of them are not animals at all. The most important of these are the living seams themselves: thin films of mineral-organism that grow along fractures in basalt and pumice, leaving bright lines of green, ochre, copper, and pale gold where water has passed and then been encouraged to pass again. The Suur do not build with them so much as converse with them. They rub nutrient pastes made from rain-filtered protein and crushed shell into cracks. They shade a seam from too much heat. They introduce a current into a place where it has grown too thick. Over years, the seams widen into ridges, ridges into walls, walls into the rounded curves of rooms.

A house on the Shelf of Warm Rain is therefore never truly new. It is a collaboration. The stone has already chosen a shape. The water has already chosen a direction.
