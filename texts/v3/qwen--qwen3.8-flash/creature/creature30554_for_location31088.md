No one lives there in the sense of having a name for it.

There is no tribe, no hermit, no abandoned village, no forgotten road, no broken hand-tool sinking into the clay. Nothing has decided to stay there as a person would decide to dwell somewhere, building walls, keeping paths, marking ownership, leaving traces. The basin is not a place that has been inhabited. It is a place that is inhabited through and through, as a body is inhabited by organs, by cells, by chemical cycles, by thousands of small agencies none of which can be pointed to alone.

If one insists on asking who lives there, the answer is not one life but a living whole.

The first residents are too small to see, and without them nothing else would exist.

At the surface of every pool there is a skin of life: bacterial films and algae so thin they seem like a trick of light. Some of them are pale green, some ochre, some dark as tea, some faintly iridescent in the way that oily scum is iridescent, except that this oil is made of living cells. Cyanobacteria braid themselves into slime. Purple sulfur bacteria bloom in the chemically stratified shallows, feeding at the boundary between water and air, between light and darkness, between oxygen and the black anaerobic depths. Diatoms settle into the biofilm in minute glass cases, making their own little houses out of silica without building anything in the human sense.

Beneath this visible skin lies the greater population: bacteria and archaea working in the mud without light. They break down fallen leaves. They transform sugars and tannins into simpler acids. They make gas. They release it as faint bubbles rising through black water. The smell of sulfur that sometimes rises from a disturbed pool is not decay alone; it is the breath of their metabolism. They turn one world into another. They are the first machines here, though no machine was built.

These microscopic residents form mats on stones and roots, and the mats themselves become a habitat. In damp clay, their dead remains and secreted substances accumulate, binding particles together, forming crusts that may dry and crack for a few hours before rain or fog swells them again. In shallower pools, their minerals precipitate into pale films: calcium, iron, silica, manganese, salt, humic stains. The crusts over the mud are not paint. They are a record of what the water and its organisms have done.

Above the microbes live the fungi, and they are among the basin’s true engineers, though they do not engineer consciously.

They spread through leaf litter, peat, root mats, and fallen branches in networks too fine to distinguish with the naked eye. White mycelium creeps over the underside of a rotting log like frost. Black fungal threads lace through the dark soil. Some fungi digest wood. Some digest leaves. Some digest insects. Some digest other fungi. Some form partnerships with the roots of trees, extending them a thousandfold, exchanging mineral nutrients for plant sugars in a quiet economy that does not require ledgers.

Fruiting bodies push up after rain: small caps, cups, shelves, puffballs, threads, crusts. They are temporary cities, built for spores rather than for people. They swell in the wet air, mature, darken, collapse, release their dust of spores, and dissolve into the ground. No one maintains them. They are simply part of the cycle of wetness, growth, and return.

Inside a fallen tree, fungi and insects create a world so busy it almost resembles civilization. The wood becomes a gallery of tunnels, but not architecture. The tunnels are made by eating, by boring, by growing around obstacles, by chemical softening. Beetle larvae live in the swollen, rotting interior. Ants enter through cracks that were never intentional. Mite colonies ride on larger insects. Mold gardens form in abandoned pupal cases. A single log may contain hundreds of species, many of them never leaving the volume of wood from birth to death, never seeing the sky except when the log finally splits and collapses into the pool below.

Then come the plants, and they live as industrially as anything.

The broad basin is dominated by wet woodland. There are palms with long crowns and fibrous trunks standing over black mud. Their roots are not merely anchors; they are sponges, stabilizers, condensers. The palm roots rise from the peat like curved ribs, trapping debris, holding sediment, creating hollows where water lingers. Their dead fronds fall slowly, becoming rafts for moss and shelters for snails and tiny frogs. Their living fronds catch rain and send it down in streams, some of it running into tree hollows that have filled with leaf litter and water, becoming small suspended ecosystems.

There are fig-like trees with wide, dark leaves and buttress roots spreading over the ground. They lean into openings, drop aerial roots toward the mud, and slowly knit the terrain together with living wood. Some stand over pools, their submerged roots turning the water into a shaded lattice of surfaces. Algae grow on those roots. Snails scrape the algae. Shrimp-like creatures hide among them. Small fish rest in the shadow. The root is a plant part, a wall, a nursery, and a food source all at once.

Other trees are less distinct, but no less occupied. Their bark is rough with moss, liverwort, lichen, and fungal crusts. Water moves through their tissues with the patience of hydraulic pumps. In the heat of the day, the forest exhales. At night, the leaves cool and pull moisture from the air, condensing it into beads that fall into the understory. Every leaf is a condenser, every root a pipe, every trunk a reservoir and exchanger.

At ground level, the vegetation is dense enough to seem like a single organism. Ferns unfurl in dark pockets where moisture pools in soil depressions. Sedges and rushes grow along channel margins, their roots threading the mud until the bank becomes a woven rope of living fiber. Mosses spread across logs, stones, tree bases, and the sides of shallow ditches. Liverworts form mats that look like tiny green scales. Damp herbs rise in clusters, thick-leaved, swollen with water, shaded so deeply that their green is almost black.

Epiphytes live in the canopy, not rooted in the soil but in the folds and branch crotches of trees. Bromeliad-like plants collect water and leaf litter into their centers, forming miniature pools above the ground. Those pools become homes for larvae, springtails, midges, nematodes, tiny crustaceans, and frog eggs. Some mosses grow so thickly on bark that they hold enough water to support flatworms and mites. A tree, therefore, is not one plant but a vertical region: a community of roots, fungi, mosses, epiphytes, insects, spiders, and the animals that feed on them.

The true inhabitants of the water are many, and most of them are small.

The shallow pools are not empty mirrors. Beneath the black tea surface are layers of temperature, oxygen, tannin, and life. In the oxygenated upper water swim small fish with eyes adapted to dim light. They are not flashy. Their bodies are pale brown, mud-colored, or dark on top with lighter undersides, making them hard to see against fallen leaves and roots. They rest in the shadows of submerged wood, barely distinguishable from the bottom until they turn or flicker.

Some of them have mouths set low for picking biofilms from logs. Some have high-set eyes for watching the surface for falling insects. Some have lateral lines sensitive enough to feel the movement of another body in water so clear it appears stained. Some can tolerate drying pools and low oxygen, moving through wet mud when the water contracts, or breathing air through modified tissues. Their lives are slow and seasonal. In wet times, they spawn among root hairs. In dry times, they hide in deeper sloughs, or in the wet pores of peat, or in the black hollows beneath buried logs.

In the shallower margins, aquatic insects dominate. Mosquito larvae hang beneath the surface, wriggling with their breathing tubes raised to air. Midge larvae swarm through the organic soup, sometimes forming visible clouds of movement in the water. Dragonfly nymphs wait under leaves, camouflaged in sediment, their ambush speed sudden and precise. Damselfly nymphs, slender and green or brown, grasp stems and submerged twigs. Water beetles and their larvae hunt among the root tangles, some carrying an air bubble beneath their wings, some diving in bursts of motion. Caddisfly larvae drag portable shelters made of grit, fragments of leaf, or tiny sticks—built not by design but by instinct and secretion, so wet and provisional that they look like natural debris rather than construction.

Snails move over everything. Some scrape algae from wood with rasping tongues. Some graze the fungal growth on submerged leaves. Some leave silvery threads across black mud that vanish as soon as the surface dries. They feed, excrete, shed, die, and their shells become mineral deposits, hiding places, and substrates for other life.

Armored crustaceans pick their way through the silt. Crabs and crab-like animals emerge at night, their claws working through mud and leaf fragments. They are not grand creatures. They are small, damp, quick, and constantly in motion, turning up detritus, disturbing the surface of the mud, creating tracks that fill with water and become temporary pools. In their bodies, minerals from the water are concentrated into shells, and when they die, those shells return to the soil in slow fragments.

The damp mud itself is an underworld of bodies.

Under every leaf lies a population. Mites scurry over the surface of the litter, some feeding on fungi, some on algae, some on other mites. Springtails leap when disturbed, launching themselves from the damp dark into the air for a brief moment before vanishing into moss or water. Nematodes move through films of water so thin they could not be seen, feeding on bacteria and each other. Millipedes grind leaf fragments into paste, their numerous legs moving under the ground like a slow pulse. Earthworm-like creatures or their ecological equivalents thread through the mud, opening channels, mixing layers, leaving behind castes of fine soil. Isopods—small, segmented, damp-backed—roll into hollows when the ground dries too quickly.

None of these animals dominates the landscape. They are too small to become symbols. But they are everywhere. The ground beneathfoot is alive not as a metaphor but as density. A handful of mud may contain hundreds or thousands of individuals. A rotting leaf may be a battlefield, a banquet, a nursery, and a highway system for mites. A fallen fruit, before it has even collapsed, becomes the center of a temporary city.

Among the invertebrate residents, ants are especially pervasive. They move along root channels, up tree trunks, over mossy logs, and into the upper canopy. Some are ground-dwellers, weaving nests of mud and leaf fragments in places that will be dissolved by the next rain. Others live inside dead twigs or hollow stems. Arboreal ants move in trails through the leaves, tending aphids or scale insects or simply harvesting the sugar that drips from plant wounds and fallen fruit. Their presence is chemical and tactile: a leaf may look empty, but a trail across its underside can be crowded with bodies.

Spiders are among the quietest residents of the basin, and therefore among the most successful.

In the understory, sheet-weavers and wolf spiders move over damp leaves, sensing vibrations with fine hairs on their legs. In the moss, tiny crab spiders wait on flower-like growths or pale fungal caps. Between branches, orb weavers spin silk in places that seem empty but are not. The webs are not artifacts in the human sense. They are secreted, temporary, chemically precise. A spider’s web is a kind of net made from its own body, stretched in humid air that would ruin a human net quickly. In this place, the dampness makes silk heavy with condensation, but also makes movement invisible: prey cannot see the threads in the dark, and predators cannot see the spider until the strike.

At night, the spiders become a significant part of the basin’s economy. Moths collide with silk. Midges are trapped by the dozens. The spider’s vibrations are faint, but the killing is efficient. In the morning, many webs are gone, eaten or broken, dissolved back into the cycle of silk and rot.

The air is populated too.

It is not empty above the mud. Tiny organisms drift upward: spores, pollen, fragments of insects, microscopic aquatic larvae carried on water films, nematodes blown upward by convection. At dusk, when the heat begins to release its stored weight from the ground, insects rise in a haze. Some are attracted to light leaks in the canopy, some simply seek cooler air, some mate in clouds. Spiders sometimes release silk and are carried by wind over short distances. The air seems crowded even when it looks still.

The larger flying inhabitants are mostly nocturnal.

Bats live in the folds of the forest. They roost in hollows, behind loose bark, in dense leaf crowns, in the spaces between roots and rotting wood. Some are small and almost silent, hunting moths and beetles over the canopy. Others fly lower, skimming the pools where midges gather. Their roosts are not built chambers; they are chosen, hidden, and maintained only by cleanliness of body and behavior. In the morning, the heat drives them into deeper shade, into pockets where the forest breathes slightly cooler air.

At night, when insects move, bats move with them. Their calls are mostly too high for a human ear to separate from the general quiet. But if one stands in the basin and listens carefully, one can hear them: faint clicks, the dry flutter of wings, the occasional sudden silence as a moth folds its flight against the dark. They are not dramatic. They are part of the basin’s quiet industry.

Birds live there, too, though they do not define it.

Some perch in dense tangles where the light barely reaches. They eat fruit, seeds, insects, and snails. Their calls are not a morning chorus in any open sense; they are scattered and muffled by leaves. In the deep night, larger birds may settle in sheltered branches, motionless, hidden, indistinguishable from knots and broken wood until a faint shift of feathers gives them away. There are nightjars or nightjar-like birds with eyes like wet seeds. There are owls or owl-like hunters with silent flight, moving through the black upper air without disturbing the insects below. There are small frugivores that swallow fruit and disperse seeds far from the parent tree, making their excrement a nursery for future growth.

But the birds are guests in a larger economy. They eat fruit, they eat insects, they carry parasites and spores in their feathers and feet. They may move through the basin daily or seasonally, but the basin does not belong to them. They are part of its movement, not its owners.

There may be small mammals: opossum-like climbers, shrews, rodents, tree-dwellers with large eyes and wet feet. They move along root ridges and through the tangle of fallen branches. Their nests, if they have nests, are not structures of intention but accumulations of leaf, moss, and hair, pressed into hollows by sleep and instinct. They are rarely seen. The heat makes daytime movement costly, so they wait in shaded burrows, tree holes, or dense vegetation. At night, their small bodies become warm moving masses in the dark, leaving faint trails through moss.

Reptiles and amphibians fill the spaces between air and water.

Frogs are among the most characteristic voices of the basin, though their voices are subdued. In the hot dark, they sit half-hidden in wet folds of grass, on mossy logs, on leaves above water. Their skin is slick, almost like a second layer of leaf. Some are green-black, some brown with darker mottling like wet mud, some translucent enough in certain places to hint at the body beneath. They breathe through skin and lungs and mouth lining; in this saturated air, that is essential.

Their calls are not loud, but they are numerous. A single pool may hold dozens of frogs, calling faintly from among roots. Tadpoles graze algae and fungal films in the shallows, their mouths rasping the underside of leaves. They are not merely young animals; they are processors of plant matter, filtering microscopic life from the water. As they mature, they climb into the damp understory, leaving their discarded skins as food for ants, mites, and beetles.

Some frogs are tree frogs, with sticky toe pads that let them move through vertical wet surfaces. Others live in moss so thickly that they are part of the green mass. A few are tiny, fitting into rolled leaves or bromeliad cups. They feed on mites and springtails, while larger frogs feed on beetles, crickets, small fish, or even other frogs. Their lives are tied to water not as a destination but as a condition: dryness is the enemy; dampness is home.
