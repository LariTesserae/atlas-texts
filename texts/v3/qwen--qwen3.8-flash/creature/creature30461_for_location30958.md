What lives there is not a bestiary. The animals of that place have been caught at the first lesson of living: how to remain on the threshold of becoming without crossing it. They are inhabitants of a pocket that is neither dead nor alive, neither stone nor water, neither sign nor meaning. They live the way a bead lives at the lip of a ledge—swollen, trembling, ready, and never falling.

The largest resident is the chamber itself.

If it has a name, it might be called the Not-Yet Spring. It is not the water alone, nor the warm breath of damp air, nor the stone that weeps. It is the agreement among them. It has no single body. The shallow film over the floor is its skin. The tiny mirror-places are its mouths. The mineral needles are its hair. The warm veins in the rock are its blood. The rare click of a salt flower settling is the pulse in its temple. It wants to be a spring and, having wanted this so long, it has learned to live as the spring’s intention rather than as its action. It does not flow. It does not emerge. It holds the idea of emergence in the same way a person might hold a word at the edge of memory, almost saying it forever.

The Not-Yet Spring has senses, though none of them are eyes. It knows a kneeling visitor by the disturbance of its skin, by the breath made warm and close against its water. When a finger dips into one of the mirror-places, it does not remember the finger as an event; it remembers the shape of interruption as a kind of weather. It lets the ripple go. It lets the water close. It lets the place become itself again. This is why the chamber feels alive without behaving like an animal. It is a living surface, a wet stone mind, a small country of mineral nearness that thinks in beads and salts and the faint heat rising from below.

Around this slow resident there live others: small, blind, pale, and patient.

There is a grub in a crack between two mineral ridges. It is the color of milk left in darkness, segmented and soft, curled so tightly that it looks like a comma in a sentence begun by the stone and never finished. It is not waiting for season. It is waiting for change itself. Its skin knows dampness. Its tail knows pressure. Its mouth, if it has one, is a pale curve barely distinguishable from the mud. It does not crawl so much as adjust, the way a drop adjusts when the air moves. If you hold a finger near it, the chamber becomes aware of the warmth and the grub may shift its head by the width of a hair. If you withdraw, it settles back into the shape it held before you came.

The grub is not alone, though others of its kind are hidden. They live where the stone has pores, where the mineral mud keeps a film of water, where the black threads cross the damp like the veins of a leaf pressed from the underside of soil. Their exuviae are there too—translucent skins, thin as paper, half-collapsed in cracks. These shed skins do not rot. They are absorbed slowly into the crust of the walls, becoming part of the white salt lace, part of the pale flakes, part of the chamber’s endless inventory of almost-things.

There is a moth.

It is small, eyeless, and colored like ash that has forgotten fire. It comes once, or seems to come once, though “once” is difficult in a place where time does not insist. It enters near the mouth with the last breath of outside air, then finds the cool damp spot near the ceiling where the wall will hold it. It lands. Its wings are folded flat, not as if sleeping, but as if waiting for permission to become alive. They are pale at the center and darkened toward the edges, as though someone had breathed smoke on them in a dream. If one watches closely, the wings do not beat, but they move slightly with the pulse beneath the insect’s thorax—or with the pressure of its own almost-heart.

The moth does not flee. It does not starve in any ordinary way, nor decay, nor leave behind a visible death. If touched, it trembles, and then it stops trembling, and then it remains. This is not gentleness. It is not tameness. It is simply the way of the chamber: everything is allowed to be, but nothing is allowed to become something else.

Some of the dust on its wings is not dust. Under breath or heat, it becomes fine salt grains. Some of the black dots along its wing-edges are fruiting bodies from the sparse lichens near the ledges, carried inward by a current so faint it might have been the memory of wind. The moth is a visitor and a resident at once. It brings the outside into the chamber and then agrees, without knowing it has agreed, to remain at the inside of that agreement.

There are smaller things than the moth.

A bead turns out to be an insect’s eye.

It is black, smooth, and impossibly still. When you think it is only a mineral nodule, it is not. When you realize it is watching, you cannot tell whether it has begun to move or whether the whole chamber has shifted around it. This may be a springtail, one of the tiny armored things that live on water films and salt flowers. It has no name that belongs to it, because names require continuity, and continuity is almost absent here. It hops, or tries to hop. The moisture makes it heavy. It crawls instead, along the surface of a shallow mirror-place, leaving no trail, making no sound. It feeds on salt bloom, on the powdery dust of mineral needles, on the black lace of threads. Its body is segmented like a comma made harder than the grub’s body, and its antennae do not wave. They simply are, as if the act of sensing in this place must be silent to be allowed.

When disturbed, these tiny inhabitants do not scatter. They stop. That is their defense. The bead becomes a grain. The grain becomes salt. The springtail becomes a black spot, a fragment of ash, a pore in the stone. When the disturbance passes—when the breath moves away, when the hand withdraws—they resume their place, though “resume” is too active a word for what they do. They are simply there again, in the exact location the chamber has set for them.

The black threads live there too.

They are not fungus, not root, not vein. They cross the floor in layered veils, thin as hair, dark as iron water. At first you think they are shadows. Then, if the air is warm, you see that they are not quite solid and not quite liquid. They may be colonies of microbial life, or mineral fibers, or something between the two. They grow, or they do not grow. They spread into a surface and then stop, as if the chamber has said: here is enough. They are the place’s quiet nervous system. They connect the wet mud to the salt crust, the lichen flake to the reed peg, the broken clay cup to the water that will not evaporate. They do not decompose what is dead, because nothing is allowed to die in the ordinary way. Instead, they conduct a slow chemistry of almost-aliveness. They make the chamber feel as though it has been touched by intention, even if there is no one who intended anything.

There is lichen on the warmest ledges.

It is sparse—only a few flakes, grey-green, dry to the eye but damp to the touch. Each flake has its own architecture. Its edges are raised like the rim of a tiny cup, and its surface is dotted with black fruiting bodies that have never released spores, or have released them too slowly for human patience to count. These lichens have made a bargain with the chamber. They do not seek light, because light is not here. They do not spread toward the seep, because spreading would be change. They grow only to the thickness of a breath, only to the edge of their own stone, only as far as they must in order to remain what they are. They are plants, or plant-like things, or mineral memories of plants. Their green is not hopeful. It is persistent. It is the color of something that has decided to live without making any promises about tomorrow.

There is moss near the back, at the edge of a seep.

It is small as a coin, a single green thing that might have been placed there by accident, or by an animal that carried it inward without knowing. Its fibers are stiff with moisture. Its tiny leaves curve inward, holding beads at their tips. It does not grow toward the water. It grows toward the idea of water. It is the chamber’s only green mouth, a soft place in a hard world, a miniature forest that has no sky. If you lean close, you may think it breathes. This is probably false. But in a place where air is warm and close and damp without rain, false breath may be as real as anything else.

Near the moss stands a pale shoot.

It is no longer than a finger, thin, straight, and colorless. It might be a blade of grass. It might be a fungus. It might be a crystal formation that has learned to imitate a plant so closely that the difference no longer matters. It does not lean. It does not wilt. It does not bloom. It does not rot. It simply exists at the edge between living and shaped, like a word that has almost become a thing. It may be one of the chamber’s children, though it is not born in any way we would recognize. It may be the chamber’s own idea of plant life, a draft made from mineral water and heat and darkness. It has no root visible, but it may drink through its sides. It has no eye, but it may notice the moth. It has no clock, but it may keep time by refusing to change.
