Nothing with a face lives there, and yet the hollow is not vacant. The bestiary begins where the word animal ends. What you encounter are not organisms standing inside a world, but processes that have become so intimate with architecture, weather, and stone that they have acquired the manners of citizens.

Water is the first resident, and perhaps the only one that would recognize a name. It is not scenery here; it is an administrator. It moves according to gradients the walls have learned, gathering in basins, withdrawing through capillary wells, rising in thermal pulses, condensing on ledges, then falling again as a brief local rain. It has three humors: the clear citizen that carries minerals; the milky one that precipitates paste; the copper-green one that smells of iron and travels with a slow, metallic sheen. It touches every surface, enters cracks, dissolves salts, seals wounds, carries spores and bacteria, and is itself shaped by channels it did not make. It breathes because the hollow breathes. Its body is the floor plan.

Beneath the water are the skins. Bacterial and algal mats line the wet stone like green-black velvet. In the shallower basins they form slippery membranes that shimmer faintly under microbial light. They are not pretty decorations; they are the metabolic borderlands of the place. They feed on iron, sulfur, dissolved carbon, calcium, and the faint glow of biofilms. They grow as thin skins, then thicken into mats, then are consumed from below by hyphae or pushed aside by newer growth. They release acids and enzymes that etch the stone, and they leave behind mineral pastes that the walls use to repair themselves. They are the first settlers and the last recyclers.

The mosses are the closest thing the place has to a populace. They cover the bases of towers, the mouths of pipes, the ledges of former walkways, the rims of cisterns. They are thick, damp, trembling cushions, almost black-green in the dimness until a bead of water magnifies their cells and turns them electric. They grow not as random carpets but as civic vegetation: along cracks, over acoustic channels, at the joints where humidity concentrates, in bands that obey the old infrastructure. They drink mist, trap silt, anchor mineral crusts, and hold moisture longer than bare stone can. When a thicket dies, it collapses into wet pulp, stains the ground dark, and is later colonized by pale filaments that draw out its nutrients. Nothing grazes them. Nothing nests in them. They do not need animals; their predators are pH, drought, overgrowth, and the patient digestion of fungi.

The fungi are the eyes-free wolves of this ecology. Pale hyphae thread through dead moss, through bacterial mats, through the wet pulp of broken plants. They do not hunt with teeth but with chemistry. They dissolve, redistribute, and reconstitute. They colonize the black metal lattice, not eating it exactly, but working with it, sealing themselves into the mineral veins. Some filaments seem to know where water will move before it moves, because they follow the chemical hints in the air, the faint taste of minerals, the sound of a channel opening. They are the hollow’s quiet predators, and because there is no animal predation, their predation is invisible: a stain becoming a bloom, a mat thinning, a dead cushion softening into dark mud.

The algal curtains hang from lintels and low beams where water drips. They sway with the hollow’s breath, not because something disturbs them but because the building itself inhales and exhales through thermal differentials. They form living screens between platforms, filtering mist, catching dust, glowing faintly where microbial films have colonized them. They are also recorders. Their rings, if examined with enough patience, would show mineral pulses, temperature shifts, acoustic events, and the slow chemical seasons of the underground city.

Yet the most alien inhabitants are not plant or microbial. They are structural. The dark fibrous lattice threaded through the stone behaves like a body. It is not wire. It is a skeleton of programmable matter. When touched, it contracts after a long pause. It is colonized by minerals and hyphae. It is probably an old technology, but its old function has dissolved into biology. It regulates capillary flow, opens nanopores, seals cracks, stores patterns of moisture and pressure. The walls are not passive. They are residents. They sweat at their joints because the humidity is being managed. They weep because they are breathing. They repair because something, somewhere in the stone, is keeping an account of damage.

The towers are bodies too, though they do not walk. Each tower is an atmospheric organ. Its inner chambers are lined with microbial films and moss, with channels that condense mist and then release it through pores. Some emit harmonic overtones after the sub-bass thrum passes through the floor, like bells remembering being struck. Others serve as reservoirs of frequency, pressure, and mineral memory. Inside a tower, if you look closely, you may find no room at all—only nested surfaces, narrow vertical runnels, ledges where black-green moss grows sideways, cisterns of warm water reflecting no sky. It is inhabited by gradients, not beings. Its residents are the conditions it keeps.

Sound is one of the clearest inhabitants. Drip by drip, the hollow composes a weather system. Every bead has a path and a note. The water in channels clacks over unweathered ridges. Air moving through apertures produces a continuous whistle. A low sub-bass thrum lives below perception, in teeth and sternum, a pressure from pumps or perhaps from metabolism itself. This sound is not merely a consequence of the place; it shapes it. Moss trembles in response. Algal curtains lean. Water rises or retreats at pulses. Cracks open and seal according to the city’s resonance. Sound is the courier, the clock, and the communal bell.
