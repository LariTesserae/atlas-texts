No permanent lungs live there. No people make homes inside the cold metal, no children grow up between the floodlights, no families pass their nights curled in a crevice beneath a pipe. Human beings exist for this place, but they exist the way weather exists for a forest: as an absence that nonetheless shapes everything. They keep the lamps lit from some remote outside world. They feed the pumps with fuel, data, maintenance, and purpose. If a diver, a submersible, or an engineering pod descends, the water opens around it in a sudden disturbance, and the animals scatter from its bubbles, its noise, its unfamiliar outline. Then, after a while, the place fills itself again.

The inhabitants are mostly those things that ordinary seas produce when heat, current, shelter, and food appear in unfamiliar shapes. They are the first wave of an accidental ocean: fish, crabs, squid, eels, worms, microscopic films, drifting larvae, and the slow mechanical organisms of the construction itself. None of them is native to the architecture, because the architecture is too young for true natives. But all of them have begun to live there as though it has always been there.

The most persistent residents are not visible as individuals. They are the films.

A biofilm grows on the warmest surfaces, on the underside of ceramic tiles, along the shoulder of a weld bead, in the shade of a bolt head, on the outer face of a polymer-lined conduit where water moves too slowly to scour it clean. At first there is nothing there. Then there is a change in sheen: a faint greasiness, a soft blur where water breaks into a thin film. The material of the place is too new for thick growth, too washed by flow for heavy colonization. So life begins as a whisper. Bacteria and microbes settle in the first micro-roughness. Diatoms catch. Algal spores drift in from somewhere and adhere. Fungal threads find the tiny pores in concrete, the gaps in sealant, the hairline shadows at material junctions. These things form a living glaze over what was only recently installed.

That living glaze is what the rest of the ecology begins to eat.

The algae do not make meadows. They are not forests. They grow as thin green and olive smears along warm outflow channels, along the lower sides of pipes, along the edges of service trenches where nutrient-rich water lingers before being swept away. In some places they are only a color change on the surface, visible when a lamp strikes it at an angle. In others, near the slowest discharge grates, they become soft and visible: short strands, pale fuzz, tufts like moss left on stone by a receding tide. They are pioneers, not because they are brave, but because they are small. They do not demand soil. They take what the machine leaks: heat, dissolved minerals, suspended waste, the faint chemical traces of lubricants and filters, the nutrients that rise from some distant surface world and fall into the engineered dark.

The vegetation never fully wins. The systems move too much water. Currents reverse. Maintenance cycles send pressure through conduits. Filters flush. Grates scrape or vibrate. Just as a green film thickens, it is torn loose and pulled toward a basin or a drain. And yet it always returns, because the machine keeps making conditions for it. There are always warm edges. There are always calm pockets. There are always surfaces where light falls and water slows for a few seconds before the current takes it again.

The smallest animals live in that return.

They are not glamorous. They are copepods, larvae, amphipods, tiny shrimp, ciliated microbes, worms no wider than a thread, and unnamed drifting things that pass through open chambers in almost invisible numbers. They feed on the biofilms, on suspended particles, on the remains of other lives, on the nutrient plumes that rise from outflow grates. They are swept through trenching, caught briefly in corners, released by cleaning pulses, and carried upward into wider spaces. They are the living dust of the place.

Then come the fish.

Not fish as scenery, not a vague school moving past a window, but fish as the true inhabitants of the hollow water. They use the empty volumes the way other fish use reefs, shorelines, or river mouths: as rooms, corridors, hunting lanes, and meeting grounds. The place has no bottom in the familiar sense, but its voids are full of meaning to a fish. A column of water between two concrete plates is a street. A broad basin is a square. A vertical shaft is a cliff face. A current from a valve is a river that appears and disappears according to a mechanical will. A lamp is a sun that does not rise or set but changes on schedule.

The most common are slender silver fish, not one species perhaps, but several species that behave in similar ways in this environment. They move in fast, dense clouds through the open chambers. A school may pour past a floodlight like a sheet of metal being torn and re-formed. It turns all at once when a pressure wave arrives from a pump cycle. It scatters when a predator cuts in, then gathers again a few meters away, as though nothing had happened except a correction to a pattern. These fish live in the middle water, above and below any imagined seabed. They do not need sand. They need space, current, light, and the faint drift of food that the machine supplies.

They read the structure through pressure more than sight. Their lateral lines are tuned to the place. They feel the start of a turbine before they see it. They sense the surge of a cleaning cycle coming through a shaft and know to move to the edge of turbulence. They recognize a valve opening not as an event, but as a season: now danger, now food, now water moving too fast to stay in the center of the chamber. Some have learned to wait at the warm side of discharge grates because the plume carries small organisms upward. Others ride the cold edges because predators are less active there. To them, the machine is not a machine. It is simply a climate that pulses.

There are larger predatory fish too. They do not build anything, and they do not care what the place is. They use its geometry. A broad overhang of ceramic plate becomes a cave. A row of anchor points becomes a hunting reef. A shadowed trench beneath a service ladder becomes an ambush lane. These predators have bodies suited to quick, violent movement: compressed heads, long jaws, sharp eyes, and lateral lines that catch the flicker of baitfish against concrete. They patrol the boundaries between light and dark, between warm and cold, between the roar of a pump hall and the deeper quiet of an open shaft. They do not wait behind coral. They wait behind flanges.

When a baitfish school slams against the side of a basin in panic, the predator has already learned how to read the rebound. The silver cloud strikes the matte concrete, scatters in a white flash, then pulls apart into ribbons. The larger fish takes one or two from the chaos and moves on. Nothing remains of the event except a small disturbance in the water, a few stunned fragments, and the renewed motion of the surviving school.

The crabs are among the most adapted residents.

They are pale-bodied, with limbs shaped to grip smooth surfaces that would be impossible in a natural environment without some texture. The construction has plenty of texture: bolt threads, weld beads, seams, ceramic joints, polymer ridges, railings, anchor points, drainage gratings, and the sharp edges of plates. To a crab, every material is a cliff face if it offers a hold. Steel is not better than stone. Ceramic is not strange if it has a crevice. Polymer is not foreign if a current presses it into a wall. The crabs climb vertical surfaces that would defeat a natural tide pool. They crawl along pipes, using the cylinders as roads and the joints as shelters. They press themselves into the shadow under a service bracket and remain there while a lamp cycles on and off.

They live at the edge of the emptiness. In the open water there is nowhere to hide, so they choose surfaces. They know the welds. They know where panels meet and leave a small gap. They know where concrete changes to steel and makes a dark line that can hold a body. They know that a cleaning surge will sweep through a shallow trench and send them clinging to the sides with their legs spread. They know that when the pumps change speed, the water will briefly thicken with food, and they will move to the mouths of drainage channels before the larger fish arrive.

Some crabs are scavengers first. A dead fish drifting into a service basin becomes a communal event. At first it hangs motionless in the cool current, then a crab touches it with one pale leg, then another. Within minutes, claws begin to work at the soft spots. The body is carried into the crevice of a seam. The water around it darkens slightly with suspended particles. Predators come. The crabs scatter or defend, depending on size and hunger. The scavenging can be brief, brutal, and mechanical in its own way. Nothing natural is wasted, but nothing is sentimental either.

The eels and other slender burrowers live in the narrower parts of the place. They go where crabs and fish cannot. They thread through polymer-lined trenches, crawl along conduits, wind between pipe supports, and enter filter housings when the flow reverses. Some are true eels, their bodies made of muscle and patience, moving with a serpentine awareness of pressure. Others are less familiar: snakefish, blennies, wormlike predators, or juvenile forms of larger species. They are the residents of the hidden channels. Their bodies are built for the small spaces, the gaps, the dark places beneath plates. They hide from predators by entering maintenance voids too narrow for pursuit. They come out only when the machine’s own motions create advantage.

A filter cycle can be an opportunity. When a reverse flush sends sediment and stunned prey through a side chamber, eels surge into the outflow mouth and swallow what the machine brings. When the current calms, they retreat into a joint between polymer panels. When the lights shift, they remain still, almost unnoticed, because their bodies do not belong to the geometry. They are the exception to the place’s clean lines: wet, curving, ancient shapes moving through new-made corridors.

The squid are the hunters of the boundary.

They live where industrial light meets the water’s darkness. Near the rim of a floodlit chamber, where the beams become diffuse and then fail, they hover with their arms drawn in and their large eyes reflecting the lamps. They are not afraid of the light itself; they are afraid of losing the advantage the light gives them. They know that fish are drawn to it. They know that predators gather beyond it. They know when a cleaning cycle brightens a chamber and when a maintenance routine throws a section into blue shadow. They use the changes.

A squid may rise silently from the darkness behind a broad flange and jet into the edge of a school. Its tentacles flare. A few fish disappear in a flash. It retreats before the larger predator arrives. In the open water of a basin, with almost nothing near except concrete, glass, cables, and drifting particles, a squid can look like a thought moving through the place: brief, intelligent-looking, and gone.

Some of them have taken to using the structure as ambush architecture. They wait at the warm boundary of an outflow grate, where fish are held briefly by the temperature change. They rest in the shadow cast by a service ladder. They press against a ceramic panel while the current moves past, then push off suddenly and scatter the fish from below. Their skin shifts color not to hide among weeds, because there are few weeds, but to match the materials around them: pale gray like concrete, speckled like alloy, dark like polymer, faintly translucent near glass. The place teaches them camouflage that a natural reef would not.

There may be octopuses too. Not many, perhaps, but enough to matter. An octopus is one of the few creatures that could seem almost aware of the artificiality of this place without being sentimental about it. It curls into spaces that were made for sensors, probes, or cable junctions. It changes its skin to resemble ceramic sheen, rusted metal, or the mottled grain of concrete. It explores maintenance voids with one arm while its body remains hidden in a pipe shadow. It unscrews or manipulates loose fittings by accident, or not by accident. It learns the cycles. It watches a cleaning surge from behind a flange and returns when the water calms. It may catch a crab with a sudden grip or lose an arm to a predator’s teeth. It is not a guardian of the machine. It is simply a resident that finds it more interesting than most environments.

The machines also live there, in a way.

They are not alive, but they are not absent either. The pumps, pipes, cables, grates, valves, and light housings form a kind of body. They take in water. They move it. They heat it. They cool it. They filter it. They discharge plumes like breath. They click, pulse, hiss, roar, and fall silent. They have rhythms that the animals treat as natural law. A turbine hall is not a building to a fish; it is a season. A valve is not a mechanism; it is a weather front. A filter is not a machine part; it is a food source or a hazard. The maintenance arms move on schedule, and certain crabs learn to shelter when their shadows cross the plates.

Some of the maintenance systems may be small mechanical grazers: devices that crawl along ceramic surfaces, brushing away biofilm, testing seals, resetting sensors, and cleaning panels according to a program. If one imagines them, they are not creatures, but they behave with enough regularity to enter the ecology. They move like slow metal beetles. They follow predetermined paths. They leave clean strips behind them, and the algae regrow in the warm current after they pass. The animals do not care that they are not alive. They care only that the machines move, stop, return, and remove growth from surfaces that would otherwise become habitats.

The place also has its larger outsiders. There are things that pass through the open shafts without claiming the structure as home: passing fish schools moving through a corridor of engineered flow, predatory animals from the wider water, marine mammals whose calls reach the chambers as low vibrations, and perhaps occasional sharks circling the outer boundary where the construction ends and the original sea resumes. They are not inhabitants in the full sense. They are travelers, invaders, or predators who enter the made space and leave it again. But their presence matters. A shark near an intake may become part of the rhythm if the school of baitfish learned to avoid that shadow. A whale call rolling through the water may change nothing except the pressure of the world.

No one in the place knows it is artificial.

The fish do not wonder about the lack of sand beneath them. The crabs do not miss reefs, because there are edges enough. The squid do not grieve for natural light, because the lamps provide the same function. The eels do not question why a conduit leads into a vault wide enough to swallow a city. They simply enter where their bodies can pass. The algae do not care that they are growing on alloy instead of rock. The biofilm does not care that its substrate is ceramic rather than shell. To all of them, the place is ocean because it has current, temperature, pressure, light, and prey. The construction is too recent for reverence, too sharp for nostalgia. It is simply the world they are inside.

The balance is not the balance of a forest or reef. It is the balance of a system that runs by schedule. Some animals appear only when the lights are bright. Others retreat when a high-pressure cleaning cycle sends water roaring through a chamber. Some feed at the warm edges of discharge plumes. Others wait in colder shadows where the turbulence is less. If a pump fails, some life scatters and some life dies. If a maintenance routine changes the current, the fish and crabs adapt within days. If the place is kept lit and working, life thickens. If the machine stopped entirely, this world would not become a ruin at once. It would become something else, slower and stranger, and other life would take over the surfaces while the water grew still. But that has not happened here.

The inhabitants are therefore not a simple population. They are a layering of lives arranged by function.

The films are the foundation: microbial coatings, algae, diatoms, protozoa, the first living sheen over cold metal. Above them are the tiny drifting animals: larvae, copepods, amphipods, ciliates, and the invisible particles of food that drift through chambers and sink into basins. Above them are the fish: schooling baitfish, ambush predators, and larger hunters who follow the warm and cold edges. Along the surfaces are the crabs, climbing and feeding, molting, hiding, scavenging, and clicking their claws on plates that were never meant for claws. In the narrow channels live the eels, wormfish, and burrowers, the animals of gaps and maintenance voids. At the edge of light live the squid and octopuses, using the dark and the manufactured illumination as if they were natural weather. The machines themselves act as another layer, not breathing but cycling, not eating but moving, not living but making the conditions under which life gathers.

The most characteristic resident is not one species but the synchronized school. A school in this place is not merely a group of fish. It is a living response to the machine. It brightens and darkens as it turns. It expands when current slows and contracts when a valve opens. It moves around a central column as if orbiting a black planet made of ceramic and steel. When the lamp overhead changes from white to amber during a maintenance routine, the school shifts toward warmth or away from turbulence. When a pump starts, it can hear the change before the motion becomes visible: a pressure wave traveling through the hollow chamber, a vibration that runs through water faster than sound in air, a mechanical heartbeat returning to the made environment.

When that heartbeat begins, the place wakes without ever having slept.

The current shifts through the basins. Algae films ripple where they cling to warm grates. Crabs tighten their grip on sharp edges. Fish rise in long ribbons from the bottom of nowhere and disappear into the upper shadows. Squid pull back from the light boundary, then return as the turbulence settles. The eels retreat into the conduit seams. The mechanical cleaners begin their slow crawl along the panels. Somewhere beyond the visible walls, a valve opens, a pump accelerates, a filter reverses, and the ecology obeys.

So the inhabitants of this place are not mysterious beings. They are ordinary sea life rearranged by an artificial architecture. They are not gods of the machine, nor monsters of the deep, nor remnants of an old reef drowned beneath new steel. They are fish, crabs, squid, eels, worms, algae, microbes, and the slow processes of a living film; and they are also the mechanical organism that keeps their ocean moving. None of them made the place. None of them understand it as a made thing. They simply live inside it, feeding, hiding, hunting, drifting, climbing, and following the rhythm of the lights, until the artificial world becomes habitable enough to be called home.
