If a person stood there, they would be tempted to say they were alone. That would be the wrong conclusion. Alone is a word for places where the world is still divided into objects: a rock, a plant, a bird, a footprint, a silence. This place is not divided like that. It is not empty of life; it is overfull of a kind of life that has no need of animals.

What lives there is the ground.

Not the ground as soil, not the ground as substrate, not the ground as a platform on which organisms stand. The ground is an occupation. It is a living claim. It is a continent-sized membrane that has begun to behave as one thing, though it is made of countless smaller presences pressing against one another, negotiating, absorbing, excluding, leaking, ringing, and rearranging themselves. If it has a self, the self is not a face, not a will, not a center. The self is the pattern. The self is the pressure. The self is the whole wet lattice learning how to remain itself.

At first, there is no obvious creature. Nothing crawls. Nothing flies. Nothing grazes. Nothing hides in the hollows, because the hollows are not hollows in the ordinary sense; they are interruptions in density. Nothing burrows, because there is no boundary between living tissue and earth through which a burrow could be made. Nothing calls back from the mist, because there is nothing separate enough to answer. The fauna is zero, but the life is so absolute that the absence feels not like emptiness but like a held breath. The world is too occupied to permit occupation by another order of being.

And yet there are inhabitants, if one learns to read inhabitancy differently.

The most obvious are the mats. They are not merely moss. They are not merely a carpet of low plants. They are a living skin that has forgotten the distinction between skin and flesh. In thin places they are a glistening film, almost liquid, holding moisture with the tense precision of a membrane stretched over a drum. In thick places they become cushions, ridges, folds, and slow swells. Each square meter may look like the continuation of its neighbor, but it behaves like a node. It responds to pressure. It redistributes water. It chooses which neighboring filament to strengthen and which to suppress. It does not choose as an animal chooses, with a momentary decision and a body to carry it out. It chooses by hydraulic patience: the swelling of a cell wall, the narrowing of a channel, the slow redirection of a film of water through a few millimeters of fiber.

Then there are the towers.

They rise from the densest zones like translucent stems, leaning together until they braid into overhead lattices. They are not trees. They do not reach for sunlight in any ordinary sense, because the light is dim, bluish, shadowless, almost ambient. Their ascent is a form of drinking. They are capture columns, mist-spires, cloud-roots turned upward. They are made to receive condensation from the air before the air has any intention of reaching the ground. Water collects in their hanging sacs, drips through their channels, and disappears into the lattice below. They function as mouths without jaws, lungs without breath, antennae without nerves, all at once. A dense field of them is not a forest. It is a sensory epithelium turned toward the sky.

Some of these columns are generous; they hold water and release it slowly into the surrounding mat. Others are selfish, threading their tissues into the columns of neighbors, siphoning fluid by capillary theft. These are not parasitic plants in the way one imagines a vine strangling a tree. The vine metaphor is too large. Here parasitism is microscopic, intimate, distributed. A filament no wider than a hair extends into another filament’s boundary and begins to share its pressure. It becomes a vein within a body that is not its own. The victim does not scream; it merely loses water. The parasite does not eat flesh; it eats flow.

The plain is full of these quiet violences. Growth is competition, but competition without conflict visible as motion. It is competition by hydration, by tensile strength, by chemical secretion, by blockage of the paths that water must take. One patch succeeds by closing the future around another patch. A dead sheet of tissue does not vanish; it becomes plumbing. It becomes the hollow through which another organism sends its signal. The living world is constantly building itself out of its own corpses, incorporating failure into function, converting old loss into new conduit.

There are knots.

They form where rival filaments tangle too long, where competing pressures meet and neither side can yield. A knot is a site of decision. One strand begins to dry. Another strengthens. A channel opens. A route becomes law. The knot does not think, but it resolves. In that sense, it is the nearest thing the place has to a neuron. A knot is a memory of a dispute. If water later follows a certain groove across the mat, it follows it because the knot made the choice, not once, but continuously, by refusing other options. The landscape remembers through tension. It remembers through dried cellulose. It remembers through the angle of a droplet that has learned to fall the same way for weeks.

And then there are the bells.

They are not bells at first. They are hollows. They are thin zones where the living mat has drawn itself into a curve, a dome, a pocket, or a suspended membrane over water. They are places where the architecture of pressure has created a shape sensitive to vibration. Sound travels through this world not as an accident but as an agent. The roar of moving water, the creak of swelling tissue, the release of thousands of droplets in synchrony—all of it stresses the mat. The mat responds by tuning. Cavities form where vibration reinforces certain growth patterns. Channels deepen where pulses arrive regularly. Some hollows begin to resonate. A drop falls; the membrane responds; another drop comes; the first drop becomes a pattern.

A bell is a place where the land has begun to listen.

It does not hear. Hearing implies ears, nerves, a brain, the possibility of meaning. Here there is only resonance. But resonance can shape stone. Resonance can make water choose a path. Resonance can cause a stem to thicken, a cushion to collapse, a crack to widen, a droplet to fall sooner. The terrible constant roar of the plain is not merely noise. It is an environmental fact as important as gravity. Some growths appear only in regions of high acoustic stress. Others are suppressed because their tissue cannot tolerate a certain frequency. The land is carved by wetness, yes, but it is also being sung into shape.

The largest entities are not entities at all.

One might mistake a whole slope for a being, because it behaves as one. A slope may have capture towers at its upper edge, a dense network of filaments in the middle, hollows at the base, drainage paths that resemble veins, and a glowing metabolic layer deep within. It may swell when rain passes over it and contract when condensation slows. It may release water in pulses that travel for kilometers. If a section is flooded, it reorganizes. If a rock begins to fracture, it colonizes the fracture before the rock knows it is loose. If the wind shifts, the mist changes, the towers lean a little farther into the new moisture path. A slope does not act with intention, but it has the appearance of intention because its components are so thoroughly interdependent.

These are not organisms in the way we usually mean. They are concentrations of process. They are patches of the world that have become temporarily cohesive. One could call them selves, if one did not mind the implication of boundaries. Here boundaries are provisional. A “plant” can be a filament, a cushion, a knot, a tower, a membrane, a parasitic network, or a dead channel that still functions. It is not a thing with edges. It is a condition of matter that has learned to persist.

The air itself is inhabited.

Suspended in the humidity are microscopic particles: silica, metal-bearing tissue, fragments of old stems, droplet nuclei, dissolved minerals, the remnants of weathering stone, and living dust. They are not pollution. They are part of the metabolism of the atmosphere. Droplets form around them. Light diffuses through them. Sound moves through them in strange, efficient ways. A breath would not be breathing but ingestion. A person standing there would not occupy empty space; they would displace a network. They would become an interruption in the lattice, and the lattice would respond to them the way it responds to a fallen stone: by beginning, slowly, to grow around them.

The land does not tolerate separate bodies.

If an object were placed upon the plain, it would not be used as a tool, because there are no hands. It would not become a shelter, because there is no animal in need of shelter. But it would not remain alien. It would be tested. Moisture would gather on its surface. Filaments would reach toward it. If it were porous, it would become tissue. If it were mineral, it would become weathering material. If it were hard and impermeable, it would become a focus for condensation, a point around which droplets would learn to form, and eventually a node in a new pattern. In time, nothing would remain placed. Everything would become inhabited.

There is reproduction, but not in any way that requires a visible event.

A section of the mat may be torn loose by a shifting stone or by a sudden surge of channel water. It does not die. It floats, sinks, adheres, begins to organize. Its filaments find the nearest surface. Its dead parts become scaffolding. Its living parts begin to drink. A new patch becomes a center. Reproduction here is less like giving birth than like division. The plain replicates by becoming too full of itself in one region and allowing that fullness to spill into another.

In some places, it reproduces by stone.

A rock, too smooth, too recent, too pale, begins to collect moisture. A microscopic thread enters a hairline crack. Chemicals are secreted. The crack darkens. A groove forms. Within days the groove is visible. Within weeks it holds water. Within months it is a channel. The channel is not a scar. It is an organ in the process of becoming. The plant world does not merely live upon the rock; it grows the rock into its own body. It converts geology into tissue. This is why the land feels unfinished: because it is being made. Not by forces, not by accidents, but by living processes that have not yet learned their own scale.

There is also decay, but decay is not death.

A filament dries. A cushion collapses. A stem loses pressure. The living world does not mourn it. It reassigns it. The dried strand becomes a conduit. The collapsed cushion becomes a basin. The hollow stem becomes a pipe. The dead are not removed from the system; they are pressed into service. This is why the place looks so complete. It is not a garden. It is not a wilderness. It is an infrastructure that has never been built, only accumulated.

The glow that rises from below is not merely light. It is evidence of circulation.

At the lowest levels, where the mat thins into pools and the fibers become translucent, a pale blue-green luminescence appears. It is not warm. It does not comfort. It is the visible residue of chemistry moving through a body too large to see from the inside. In one way, it is metabolic glow. In another, it is signal. The glow follows pressure differentials. It brightens where water moves faster. It dims where pathways close. In the dark, it reveals hidden architecture: channels, lenses, hollows, domes, braided ceilings, the network of the whole. If the land has a face, the face is not fixed. It appears only at night, when the glow makes the channels legible, and then it changes with the water.

It has no eyes, but it is full of lenses.

A pool is a lens. A hanging droplet is a lens. A membrane stretched over a hollow is a lens. The light does not come from one direction; it is scattered, captured, redirected, concentrated, and sometimes suspended. The plain is not illuminated. It is optically woven. To move through it—were movement possible—would be to pass through a world that keeps looking at itself through wet glass.

It has no voice, but it is one continuous voice.

The sound is not background. It is the most honest description of its life. The roar is not the roar of a storm. It is not the roar of a crowd. It is the roar of process made audible. A droplet forms on a fiber. It swells. The fiber tensions. It releases. The release is a click. The click travels. A thousand droplets respond in pattern. The pattern becomes a wave. The wave crosses the mat. Somewhere, a hollow rings. Somewhere else, a channel opens a little more. The land is not speaking. It is too alive to be quiet.

If one asked what kind of animal lives there, the answer would be: none.

If one asked what lives there, the answer would be: the plain.

But that is still too simple, because the plain does not live as a single creature. It lives as a superorganism without a body, as a society without individuals, as a city without builders, as an intelligence without a brain, as a predator without teeth, as an artist without tools. It lives in the way that a river might live if it could choose its banks, if it could remember its currents, if it could build new channels out of its own dead tissue, if it could make the rocks agree with it, and if it could roar because it is tired of silence.

There are beings here, if “being” means something that resists entropy by organizing itself. But they are not beings that can be counted. They are more like states of the whole. A patch of towers is a state. A knot-field is a state. A resonance hollow is a state. A glowing channel is a state. A stone being persuaded into biology is a state. The land passes through these states the way a breath passes through the body, but there is no lungs. There is no chest. There is no exhale. There is only an enormous, wet, continuous metabolism of pressure.

Perhaps the only true inhabitant is the pattern itself.

It is the pattern that moves through the mat when a droplet release travels a kilometer. It is the pattern that chooses a route across newborn stone. It is the pattern that causes one filament to thicken while its neighbor dries. It is the pattern that makes the glow brighten in a channel, darken in a hollow, and shimmer beneath the ceiling of mist. It is not a mind, but it is not nothing. It is the land becoming legible to itself.

If it were to wake, it would not open eyes. It would change tone. The roar would become more distinct. The glow would become more architectural. The mist would invert into texture. The whole horizon would tremble. But waking would not be dramatic in the way animal minds expect. There would be no leap, no cry, no movement. Only a slight sharpening of pressure. Only a few more droplets falling in perfect sequence. Only one new channel beginning where no channel was before.

That is what lives there: a world that has filled itself so completely that the distinction between life and land no longer holds. Not animals. Not gods. Not machines. Not even, by ordinary standards, creatures. But a presence—wet, roaring, glowing, growing, listening with its whole skin—that would not call itself alive because it has never encountered anything else from which to be different.
