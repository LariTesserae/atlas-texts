What lives there cannot be counted cleanly, because the boundary between body and landscape is still being negotiated. If a human stood at the rim and tried to list the inhabitants, the list would keep slipping into description: creatures that are also weather, structures that are also breathing, sounds that are also predation, water that is also memory. The basin is full of life, but it is not a population in the ordinary sense. It is more like a sentence that has learned to move.

The smallest and most numerous residents are the ones that would barely be noticed if not for the way they change the stone. They are the film-eaters, the salt-scrapers, the tuning-fork-mouthed insects that crawl across wet mineral skins and scrape the precipitated salts from puddle edges. They do not have clear names, because each one seems incomplete alone. Many of them are dark and slick, jointed and faintly metallic, with backs like polished river pebbles and legs that tap at speeds too fast to hear. When several gather at the edge of a warm puddle, their scraping becomes a single sound, high and crystalline, like tiny needles dragged over glass. They are not merely eating. They are pruning the landscape. Where they feed, the mineral crust becomes thinner, and new channels form. Where they do not, salt thickens into ridges, and the stone begins to hold the next creature’s body more rigidly.

Inside the warm depressions live the folded whites. They are not fish, not grubs, not larvae in any way a person would feel safe naming them. Their bodies are pale and doubled, almost like cupped hands, almost like closed eyelids, almost like pages of a book that have swollen with moisture. They live beneath the water’s skin, or within the tiny bubbles released by the spongy ground, or inside the mineral foam where wet sediment has not yet hardened into anything with a definite meaning. Sometimes they lie still for hours, or days, or only long enough for a pulse of air to move across the basin. When they move, they do not swim so much as rearrange themselves. One moment there is a pale shape in a puddle; the next it has become a thread stretched beneath the surface, or a knot in a bubble, or a faint brightness in the wet dust. They are born when water, gas, and vibration happen to agree, and they disappear when the agreement breaks. To observe them is to watch a body decide to exist and then decide, casually, to stop.

In the warmer air just above the stone drift the ribbon creatures. They have no eyes that a visitor could point to, no faces, no wings in the familiar sense. Their bodies are translucent, flattened, and soft, like membranes filled with warm breath and colored by the copper twilight. Their lobes beat slowly, not against gravity so much as against pressure. They are adapted to this thick, moist air, and they move through it as fish move through a current that has forgotten water. When one passes low over a puddle, its edge touches the surface and leaves a small ring that remains for an unnatural moment, as if the water were remembering the contact. They travel in loose schools, sometimes spiraling around the tower’s vapor, sometimes threading between the unfinished cliffs. They feed on suspended salt, mineral mist, and the faint sweet films that condense on their own bodies. A dead one does not fall. It thins, tears into strips, and becomes part of the wet air, which later deposits a little of its sweetness on a traveler’s skin.

The larger inhabitants are the stonebacks. They stand along the rim where the air is moving, low and broad-backed, with six legs set wide apart as if designed to accept the ground’s indecision. Their hides are rough and dark, like basalt that has been wetted but never warmed. They have no visible eyes; instead, the sides of their heads and necks are lined with shallow moist pits that open and close slightly, tasting pressure. They do not graze on plants. There is not enough plant matter here to sustain them. Instead, they draw sustenance from the wet air itself, from the salt films, from vibrations rising through their feet, from the faint heat given off by the mineral crust. Their tongues are strange: pale, slow threads that extend over stones, puddle edges, and even the flanks of other creatures, gathering film as much as food. When a stoneback lifts its head and its back plates rise a little, the air around it becomes denser. If another stoneback stands too close, the two may begin to hum at frequencies felt more in the chest than heard. The sound travels through the basin and causes the ribbon creatures to spiral and the folded whites to surface, which means the stonebacks are not only feeding. They are calling the next meal to where they want it.

The pavilions are not empty, though that is not the same as saying they are occupied. They seem too incomplete for permanent use. The walls stop where the ground becomes wetter. Doors lead to narrow gaps that would catch a body. Some interiors are only a few inches tall, and yet small creatures gather there as if the space were meaningful. Others contain black glass supports that hum faintly when touched. In the evenings—if the word evening means anything under that constant copper twilight—the larger stonebacks sometimes stand beneath a pavilion with only their backs exposed to the air. The pavilion does not protect them from water. It protects them from something rhythmic. When the cliffs begin to sing in higher registers, the basin becomes dangerous in a way that is not physical. A person would not be crushed or cut. They would be reinterpreted. Standing near a low pavilion during such a pitch shift feels like standing inside a sentence that is about to change its subject. The stoneback, however, remains perfectly still. Its back plates flatten against its sides. The moist pits along its flanks seal. If the pavilion is tall enough, the stoneback’s body may be entirely inside the architecture, as if the building were a temporary shell. If it is not, the animal may die without falling, its body drying into salt and black glass. Sometimes the dead stoneback becomes a new support in a later structure, not because the place is cruel, but because construction and erosion are the same verb here.

Predation is also a kind of editing. The comb-callers are predators that live near the pools and shallow wet hollows. They are thin, pale, and jointed like instruments, and they hunt by resonance. They hover or crouch at the edge of water and emit tones too low or too subtle for ordinary ears. The water answers them. Its surface rises into little combs, ridged crests spaced by frequency. Where those combs meet, the folded whites surge up. Where they do not, nothing happens. The comb-callers do not chase prey. They do not need to. They position themselves in the pattern the water has been made to show. When a pale white thing rises at an intersection, the predator strikes by being already there. Sometimes it does not bite. It simply becomes the place where the victim can no longer hold its shape. The victim collapses into a film of wet mineral and disappears into the stoneback’s waiting tongue, or into the air, or into the puddle’s future memory.

The tower is the strangest inhabitant, and perhaps the central one. It is not inhabited in the way a house is inhabited. It is more like the basin’s organ of attention. It stands on polished stone in the most sheltered part of the hollow, its ribbed panels breathing through thousands of tiny holes. From those holes rises vapor that does not behave as smoke should; it spreads sideways before it climbs, forming a low ceiling of moisture around the structure. The ground near the tower is drier, not because water flees from it, but because the air is being altered. The tower inhales atmosphere. It changes the local rules of condensation. Where it breathes, the stonebacks stand still. Where it breathes, the ribbon creatures gather and turn in patterns too regular for wind alone. Where it breathes, the cliffs emit softer tones. The tower has no visible door, no window for a person, no sign of a maker who might return. Yet its geometry is unsettling because it is exact in places where everything else is approximate. Its corners are not decorative. They are instructions.

If one watches the tower during a full pulse, one may realize that it is not the only thing doing the breathing. The entire basin is one respiration cycle. Water weeps. Air thickens. The cliffs hum. Small creatures click. The stonebacks lower their heads. The tower exhales sideways. For a moment, everything becomes synchronized. The sound is not heard so much as assembled from the inside. The teeth ache. The eyes sting. The dim copper light seems to thicken, as if the twilight were a substance. During these moments, the creatures move less like individuals and more like parts of a distributed body. The film-eaters scrape in rhythm. The comb-callers raise and lower their limbs. The folded whites surface in waves that match the pulse. The ribbon creatures spiral inward. The stonebacks hum from the ground, and the pavilions resonate like dry ribs. The basin is not merely loud. It is coherent.

That coherence suggests what lives there: a process. Not a single animal, not a collective spirit, not a machine, though all three comparisons are partly correct. The inhabitants are agents in a landscape that is learning to become itself. They eat the mineral films that the cliffs produce. They carry microbes between puddles. They scatter salt and water and vibration. They alter the ground enough to make new ground possible. They die and are incorporated into the structures and films of the place. Their bodies are made from the same materials the basin is using to build itself. To ask whether they live in the place is almost to ask whether cells live in a body. They do. And they are also the body.

There is, however, something else. The absence of soil, the lack of ordinary vegetation, the presence of half-ruined and half-built structures, all of it points to a former intention that is not quite gone. Some of the pavilions are older than the basin’s current state. Some of the black glass supports are not geologic. Some of the polished stones near the tower have been worn by patterns that no longer exist. The place feels as if a civilization tried to become a terrain, or a terrain tried to remember being a civilization, and neither project finished. The animals now live in that unfinished middle ground. They are not remnants of a people, though they might be descendants, or accidents, or consequences. They are not machines, though they respond to the tower as if it were still calling them. They are not gods, though a visitor might mistake the whole basin’s pulse for prayer.

The rules are felt before they are understood. Do not stand where water moves backward; that is a place where the landscape is changing its mind, and a person may become included in that change. Do not touch the faint lines of light; those are the unfinished outlines of what the place is considering. If the cliffs hum at a high pitch, do not run, because the sound may be following movement, or pressure, or the electrical warmth of a body that does not belong yet. Seek a pavilion, but do not stand in its center. The pavilions are not shelters against weather; they are shelters against construction. If a pulse comes too strongly, the wrong body may be mistaken for material.

A human who lives there too long becomes strange, not because the place is cursed, but because the place is patient. At first the traveler feels only discomfort: too much warmth, too little sky, too much closeness in the air. The smell of heated mineral and wet iron clings to the skin. The ground seems to remember footfalls. Then, if the person remains, they begin to notice that their thoughts feel wetter, slower, more granular. They start hearing the tones before the creatures do. They begin to avoid puddles that have no visible cause. They find themselves standing under the tower, breathing with it. They notice that when they touch the spongy substrate and it exhales, the breath smells faintly of themselves. They do not become animals. They become punctuation. They become a pause in the sentence. If they die, the basin will decide whether to carry them into salt, into gas, into black glass, or into the pale film that the scrapers feed on. If they remain alive long enough, they may become a temporary landmark. Other creatures will move around them, not because they are dangerous, but because their outline has begun to affect the pattern.

What lives there, then, is abundance without stability. It is life that cannot be called flourishing, because flourishing implies a settled form. It is life that is being built, eroded, rebuilt, and heard. The cliffs are new and already inhabited. The water is shallow and already meaningful. The animals are small or large, simple or uncanny, and all of them are temporary organs of a larger becoming. If a traveler looks down from the rim at the whole basin, the motion appears chaotic: rings in puddles, drifting membranes, flicks of jointed legs, slow threads from stoneback tongues, sideways vapor, dust lifting in pulses. But the longer one watches, the more the motion resolves into something like grammar. The scrapers are conjunctions. The ribbon creatures are breath. The stonebacks are nouns that have not committed to themselves. The comb-callers are punctuation. The tower is the thing speaking. The basin is the half-formed sentence still trying to become a place.
