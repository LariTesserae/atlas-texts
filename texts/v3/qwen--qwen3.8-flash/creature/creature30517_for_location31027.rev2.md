If you wait long enough, the place stops feeling empty and begins to feel inhabited by something that does not advertise itself.

The first thing to understand is that they do not come out to meet you. They assemble.

At first you notice only a movement where there should be no movement: a crystalline film thickening along a seam in the wall, a faint glimmer that should not have enough mass to shift. Then tiny flecks of dark glass begin to crawl toward it, not like ants summoned from a nest, but like threads being pulled through the eye of a needle. The flecks gather, align, settle. Something becomes more solid. A joint emerges where none had been. A curve of translucent material forms the suggestion of a limb, then too many limbs, then a body balanced against the wet street.

They are pale, leggy, almost too thin to be believable in the cold. In the pools you may already have seen small versions of them, no larger than a thumb, stepping carefully across the stone with the same unhurried caution. Those are not their young in any familiar sense. They are more like scout forms, or perhaps their first words spoken into the world. A full one is not simply a larger animal. It is a coordination.

Their bodies have the look of grown glass and mineral deposit, the kind of crystalline film that spreads on sheltered rock, but arranged with impossible specificity. They are not flesh. They are not plant. They are not machine, though machines are part of them. They are what happens when a cold wet architecture develops a nervous system and then, little by little, a face.

When one stands fully formed near the edge of a small square, it is tall but does not dominate. Its limbs are too many-jointed, each section thin as a needle and slightly curved, with feet that distribute weight so precisely that it can stand on the rim of a channel without disturbing a single drop. Its torso is not a torso so much as a scaffold of filaments and fused ceramic, inside which a faint dark thread moves, carrying cold fluid or heat in a way too subtle to call blood. There is no obvious head, but there is an orientation. It turns toward water, toward temperature, toward sound. If you stand near it, you feel that it is noticing you, though it does not behave as if you are a threat. It behaves as if you are a weather event, something irregular passing through its territory.

They may be three, or seven, or perhaps only one at a time in any given district of the pocket. Their number is not fixed because their presence is not fixed either. Some are dormant, spread so thin along walls that they appear as frost rings. Some are dispersed into maintenance, their bodies dissolved into the tiny crawling machines until the machines themselves seem to take on intention. A line of dark flecks moving along a seam may be one of them thinking with its feet. A thin shimmer at the edge of a pool may be another, listening through condensation.

They are the inhabitants of the wet streets, but not because they own them. They own nothing. They are bound to the routes.

Each one is responsible for a pattern of water. That is the closest thing to a home. One may be assigned to a series of basalt-walked squares and the low archways around them, tracking how melt arrives at a particular hour, which ledges hold frost too long, which drains run too slowly, where mineral dust accumulates and must be cleared. Another may belong to the thermal panels, to the warm zones arranged in almost musical repetitions across the floor. It spends its time there, testing heat with its fingertips, adjusting a hidden valve by a fraction, watching the mist rise and fall from its skin as if the room were breathing.

They do not live in buildings the way people do.

The low houses around the pocket are not dwellings for families. They are shelters, workrooms, thermal nodes, dry storage for tools that are also extensions of their bodies. Their doors are tall because a full one must enter with careful dignity, but the interior is never luxurious. There are floor channels, wall niches, platforms at varying heights. A resting chamber might contain no furniture except a warm ledge and a thin pool that reflects the ceiling. A workshop might be nothing more than a vertical seam full of waiting machines. The rooms exist to help the inhabitants regulate the city, not to hide it from them.

They eat moisture and mineral traces.

This is not hunger, not appetite, not desire as you would understand it. It is necessity shaped like attention. They take in the tiny films of water that collect on glass and stone. They absorb dissolved salts, metallic aerosols, frost melt, the faint mineral dust carried by high wind. Their bodies process these things and return dry residue in controlled places, into collection guttings that look like ordinary channels but are actually digestion lines for the pocket. No waste accumulates because waste has been designed away. Everything that arrives must be used, stored, redirected, or converted.

In return, they keep the water moving.

They know the routes of every drop. They can read the sound of a channel by the way it changes pitch when a film of ice begins to form. They can tell whether a square is warming too quickly by the angle at which mist breaks against a wall. They can find a blockage before it becomes visible by the faint absence of vibration in the pavement. Their work is not heroic. It is almost invisible. A millimeter adjustment. A cleared seam. A patch of crystalline film smoothed before it can distort flow. A grate nudged into a position that allows breath through the stone.

If you stand beside a pool long enough, one of them may approach.

It will come from above or from the side, descending along a wall as if the wall were a stair. Its feet will test the stone in the manner you have already seen from the smaller forms: careful, patient, noncommittal. It will stand a little distance away. It will not speak with a mouth. Its body may ripple in a short pattern, like frost rearranging itself. Or water may tremble in a nearby channel. Or a set of machines may halt, align, and resume. This is language here: not voice, not gesture alone, but change made legible to those who can read infrastructure.

If you have touched the water, if you have altered a panel with body heat, if your footstep has disturbed a channel’s timing, the one near the pool may turn its attention toward you with a kind of slow courtesy. It does not scold. It corrects. It may extend a hand and, with the faintest movement, shift a bead of ice so that the flow begins again. Or it may wait until you move away, then retrace your path, smoothing out what you did. You are not dangerous. You are temporary. But you are not irrelevant. Anything that can alter a drop can alter the pocket, and the pocket has very little tolerance for accident.

They are not alone.

The tiny dark machines that crawl along seams are not separate from them. They may be tools, children, cells, or thoughts made external. Sometimes a machine crosses a street in a threadlike procession and dissolves into a crack. That is not a swarm leaving. It may be one of them withdrawing into itself. Sometimes the machines pause where they are needed for hours, motionless as dust. Then, when the flow corrects, they resume as if no time has passed. If you touch one, it will not flee. It will simply adjust its angle and continue, as if your hand were part of the local weather.

The pale leggy creatures near the pools are also part of the same system. They are its most visible faces. They are also its most expendable, though “expendable” would be wrong. They are sensory endpoints, probes, quiet servants, perhaps larvae, perhaps extensions, perhaps the way a full being becomes small enough to travel through narrow cracks and test ice. A one that stands in a square may be the visible self of a hundred such forms, each one carrying a fragment of attention back to it. Their caution is not fear. It is calibration.

They do not crowd the streets because crowding is inefficiency.

The settlement looks sparse because it is optimized for a few bodies performing vast maintenance. There is no plaza for festivals, no market, no noisy public quarter. The open squares exist so that someone can stand, observe, adjust, and move again without obstruction. The benches are not for conversation but for waiting: for condensation to shift, for a vent to open, for a temperature to equalize. The city is not a social organism. It is a practical one.

Their time is slower than yours.

They do not hurry, because water here has already learned patience. If a drain begins to overflow, they will not rush in a human panic. They will come to it in sequence, first through sensing, then through small correction, then through a fuller presence if needed. Their work can look like idleness from outside. A figure may stand for a long time beside a channel. You might think it is resting. It is not. It is listening to the way the water strikes a stone edge, counting irregularities, comparing them to a pattern that may have been stored in mineral rings for centuries.

They remember through the place itself.

A crack sealed with a different film marks a past event. A row of warm panels arranged in an odd sequence may indicate an old correction that was never quite undone. The crystal growth on a wall is not decoration; it is record-keeping. A line of frost may encode season, pressure, or damage. Their memories are not stored in heads. They are stored in the architecture, in the slow accumulation of adjusted edges, repaired seams, and rerouted moisture. If you want to know their history, do not ask them. Look at the wear pattern near a grate. Check the thermal zones beneath a doorway. Count the rings of frost on a pillar. They have been writing for a very long time, in the language of water and temperature.

There may be among them something older than the rest.

If you remain in the pocket through a full cycle of the light shaft, you may feel it before you see it. The mist will settle differently. The water’s rhythm will seem more complete. The machines will move with greater certainty, as if a single intention has finally reached them. A body will appear in the shaft of pale daylight, so tall and pale that for a moment you think it is only the light given a vertical form. It is not made of flesh. It is made of the place. Its limbs are fused with walls in places, and where it stands, the floor appears to remember it. It may not move at all. It may simply be present, like the mountain thinking through a particular room.

This is the one who lives there most deeply.

It is not a king, not a priest, not a guardian in any ceremonial sense. It is the pocket’s concentration. It is the point where maintenance becomes identity. It does not speak. It does not need to. Its attention changes the temperature in nearby streets. Its patience allows the frost to form in the correct rings. When it withdraws into the walls, the place feels emptied again. But the absence is also part of its presence. You begin to understand that the city itself is inhabited, not because there are bodies in it, but because the bodies belong to it.

What lives here, then, is neither wholly animal nor wholly machine.

It is a community of custodial forms, assembled from mineral film, glass, cold water, and engineered patience. They are the Few. They are the Pale Tenders. They are the city’s own hands. They do not live in the pocket so much as the pocket lives through them. They are the reason the channels do not freeze at the wrong hour. They are why the light shaft does not simply shine but serves. They are why the crystal on the rock advances where it should and stops before it becomes chaos.

If you stand in the largest square and listen, the quiet no longer feels like emptiness. It feels like occupation.

Footsteps may be rare, but corrections are constant. A panel warms and cools. A grate opens and closes. A machine line crosses the street and vanishes. A pool trembles. Frost retreats from a ledge by a hair’s breadth. The mountain does not sleep, and neither do they. They are the hidden life of the wet stone, the inhabitants of absence, the few necessary minds in a city built not for people but for the precise, endless management of water.

They do not need to be seen to be believed.

But if you are patient, if you keep out of the channels, if you breathe carefully and do not disturb the film on the walls, one of them may become visible near the edge of a square. It will stand with too many joints, pale as the inside of a cloud, and it will look toward you with an attention that is not curiosity. It is maintenance. It is checking whether you will pass through the pocket unchanged, or whether, in your leaving, the water will flow a little differently than before.

If you do not alter it, it will withdraw.

And the place will seem empty again, until you realize that emptiness itself is one of the ways they live.
