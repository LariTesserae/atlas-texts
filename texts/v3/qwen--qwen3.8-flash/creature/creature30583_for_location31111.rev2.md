What lives in that place is not someone, and not any living thing that walks, swims, hunts, sleeps, or waits. There are no citizens of flesh there, no hidden colony of small bodies, no ancestral family of drowned beings, no monsters curling in the darker seams. The pocket is not haunted by animals. It is not waiting for them. It has already found other tenants.

It is lived in by pressure.

The pressure has moods. In the upper lens, just beneath the sea surface, it is alert and thin, pulsing with the breath of tides that cannot be seen directly but only felt as a slow lifting of the entire water volume. Deeper, in the channels between mineral towers, pressure becomes heavier, more patient. It leans against the sides of the current. It supports suspended sheets of water. It holds droplets in curtains that are not rain and not cloud but a kind of weather trapped inside architecture. When pressure shifts, the whole town leans with it, the way a crowd leans when a rumor passes through it.

The currents are the most active inhabitants. They move like residents who know every alley and every shortcut. Some are swift and bright-edged, running along the faster streets of the basin as if they have somewhere urgent to be. Others are slow, dense, almost thoughtful, sliding through wider chambers with a viscous deliberateness, carrying warmth that is barely warmth and light that is barely light—just the faint difference between one water-layer and another. These currents do not merely pass through the pocket; they use it. They pass through the mineral towers as if the towers were lungs. They bend around the cable-thin bridges as if avoiding shoulders in a market. They gather at certain junctions, argue, separate, reunite. When several currents meet, the water becomes a conversation. When one current wins, the pocket sounds like a street suddenly emptied of everyone except its own footsteps.

The mineral towers are not dead objects. They are bodies grown by necessity. They rise from the basin floor pale and segmented, their walls threaded with tiny capillaries of flowing water. Their surfaces are dense with fibers, transparent strands, ridges, pores, and seams so fine they seem almost nervous. If you look closely enough, you can see where they have reinforced themselves in response to pressure. A corner that receives a hard current becomes thicker. An inner channel that carries repeated flow becomes smoother. A brittle shelf that has been struck too often sprouts a lattice of mineral strands, a scar made of structure. These towers are not built for bodies, but they behave as though they have bodies: they flex, clog, clear, leak, harden, soften, and sometimes collapse into new shapes. They are inhabitants because they persist through change, and they are change because their persistence takes the form of growth.

Sound lives there too.

Sound is not incidental in that pocket; it is one of its main populations. The whole basin is a crowd of noises. There is the low hum of water moving through a column. There is the sharp, granular crackle of cavitation where pressure thins and tiny bubbles form and break. There is the deep, bodily groan of a channel carrying more force than can be easily heard. There is the whisper of droplets sliding across suspended membranes. There is the metallic chime of fresh water striking newly split basalt. There is the muffled, almost architectural thud when a pressure sheet loses support and falls into a wider chamber. The sound does not come from speakers or machines. It comes from the city moving. It comes from the town breathing, shifting, settling, and resettling.

This is why the pocket feels occupied even without animals. The noise is too organized, too responsive, too much like a crowd. It has the sense of many small actions happening at once. A current strikes a rib; the rib rings. The ringing shakes a curtain of droplets; the curtain trembles. The tremble scatters light; the light flickers over a pale membrane. The membrane flexes; the flex alters a passage; the passage changes the flow of the entire nearby channel. One thing answers another. No one is present, and yet the place behaves as if it is full of attention.

The light is another resident, though not a being. Ordinary sunlight enters through the narrow shaft and becomes part of the town. It is not used only to reveal. It participates. It bends around denser water layers. It catches suspended filaments and turns them into threadlike crowds. It glints on the sharp glass edges of young basalt. It pours into hollow structures and makes their interiors appear suddenly inhabited by moving brilliance. At certain depths, the light feels almost solid, a pale material pushed into the water by the sea above. In other places, it is thin and nervous, trembling because the water through which it travels is trembling. The light does not illuminate the town from outside. It moves through the town like a visitor who cannot be ignored.

There are plant-like things there, though they are not enough to call it vegetation in any ordinary sense. They are sparse, stubborn, angular, colored in muted greens, grays, silver-whites, and faint rust-red where mineral-rich flows have touched them. Glassy tufts cling to ledges where currents slow enough to allow them a foothold. Copper-veined mosses grow only in wet seams so narrow that one might mistake them for stained stone. Thin filamentous curtains hang from pressure ridges, swaying not because they choose to sway, but because the town is always moving. Occasional translucent fronds anchor themselves to mineral stalks with a patience that is not calm but desperate. These plant-like inhabitants do not own the pocket. They are tenants in margins. They live in the gaps the currents cannot fully occupy, and when a current changes course, they are torn away without warning.

Their absence is not failure. It is part of the ecology. The pocket does not need them to make it alive. They are simply one of many modes of residence. A wall of mineral can shelter them. A current can destroy them. A pressure layer can suspend their broken fragments for hours, making them seem still present even after death. The pocket is full of such temporary presences: shards of basalt suspended in faster bands, grains of mineral glowing in sunlight, droplets held apart from one another by pressure until they vanish, filaments drifting in a slow lens long after their parent growth has dissolved.

The basalt itself is alive in the sense that it changes and reacts. Freshly broken edges gleam black. Fractures open like mouths. Shards fall, strike, chip, and become new conduits. Where water flows across a newly exposed face, the stone darkens, slickens, and begins to wear a thin skin of mineral deposit. Then another crack forms. The water learns a new path. The old path is abandoned or narrowed. A seam becomes a channel. A channel becomes a wall. A wall becomes rubble. The rock is not ancient and patient. It is young, impatient, reactive. It breaks easily and heals slowly, but its healing is not a healing; it is replacement. A new lattice forms where a shard was lost. A mineral strand bridges a fault that existed yesterday and did not exist the day before. The basalt is an inhabitant because the town grows upon it, and it grows upon the town.

What lives there is the town itself.

The town is not a human place pretending to be under water. It is not a drowned ruin. It is a construction whose nature is submersion. Its streets are currents. Its buildings are pressure structures. Its plazas are zones where flows argue and disperse. Its doorways are narrowings of density. Its walls are membranes tuned by water. Its parks are small failures of pressure, places where the packed density loosens long enough for a tuft to take root. Its squares are intersections of moving ocean. Its infrastructure is not separate from its life; its life is its infrastructure. The town is not occupied by people because the town itself occupies the space.

It has habits.

It gathers emptiness. If a gap opens in the mineral lattice, water moves into it. If the gap becomes too wide, mineral strands begin to weave across it. If filaments can survive the current, they colonize its rim. If pressure shifts, the gap fills with a suspended lens of denser water, and the town treats that lens as though it were a room. If a new crack appears in the basalt, a current finds it before the day is over. If a structure collapses, the rubble becomes support. If a shard is carried into a flow, it can become the nucleus of a new column. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is left as mere absence. Absence is only a form the town is about to fill.

It also forgets.

Structures unbuilt themselves. Mineral coatings dissolve when currents change. Bridges weaken where flow softens. Towers thin at the edges. A wall may stand for a season and then, when the current finds a new route, become a lattice of empty holes. Growth does not mean permanence here. Life means continuity of process. A place can be built, inhabited, abandoned, and rebuilt in the same pattern until the pattern becomes a kind of memory. The town remembers itself by pressure. It remembers by current. It remembers by the way certain ledges wear smoother, certain channels deepen, certain columns thicken, and certain noises always arrive first at the same points in the basin.

If you entered, you would not be attacked. Nothing would take you. Nothing would notice you as a predator notices prey. But the pocket would still act upon you. Water would move around you, carrying you with it, pressing against your sides, sliding over your skin, filling every space your body displaced. Mineral particles would brush against you. Light would break across you and vanish. Sound would come through you and around you, not just through your ears but through your chest, your teeth, the hollow of your skull. Your presence would be an interruption. The town would adjust around your body the way a crowd adjusts around a stranger who does not move quickly enough. Then, if you lingered too long, the density of the place would make you feel as though you were not walking through it but being inhabited by it.

That is the strange final answer to what lives there.

It lives in the same way that a bell lives when a hand touches it, or a harbor lives when the tide changes. Not with breath. Not with intention. Not with a heart that beats in sequence. It lives by response. Current answers pressure. Mineral answers flow. Light answers density. Sound answers structure. The whole pocket is a system of answers so complete that it feels like presence. There are no bodies there, yet everything behaves as though bodies are passing. There is no society there, yet the water moves with the rhythm of a crowd. There is no person there, yet if one listens long enough, the town sounds like it is speaking in a language made of pressure, glass, light, salt, and falling stone.

Whoever lives there, no animal has ever lived there. And yet the place is not vacant. It is occupied by a city that metabolizes water, remembers currents, grows in the shape of pressure, and fills every emptiness until the emptiness becomes a street.
