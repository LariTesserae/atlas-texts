What lives in the Underfield is not a single species, not a single order, not a single image. It is a condition of contact: water meeting stone, mineral meeting flesh, breath meeting surface, body pressing against body until the boundary between “floor” and “life” becomes uncertain.

The lowest inhabitants are not easily seen as living at all. They begin as taste and smell before they begin as shape. In the first thin film of moisture on a wall, a faint iron-and-yeast scent may gather where none has been for hours. A chalky bloom appears, then crystallizes into slow ridges like the fingerprint of something large pressing itself out of the stone. These are not mosses. They are not lichens in any ordinary sense. They are mineral-soft skins, bacterial veils, fungal guesses, calcite weavings. Some spread in branching lattices, catching dissolved metals, storing water in microscopic hollows, exhaling a dryness that is itself a form of growth. Others behave like skin: they bead when wet, tighten when dry, crack at the edges, then seal again from the inside. If a pale hand were pressed against one, the hand might feel a faint warmth, not because it is alive with blood, but because it is alive with chemistry.

This living crust is the ground of everything.

Above it come the small bodies.

Blind crustaceans move in broad waves over the wetter floors. They are not all one kind. Some are low and broad-backed, their shells so thin that the white of their legs shows through. Others have flanges like broken plates, useful for deflecting falling beads of water. Many carry tiny mineral grains in the folds of their backs, and when they crawl too slowly across dry powder, the grains make a whisper like sugar poured through cloth. They do not flee a disturbance; there are too many of them for fear to make sense. A shadow crossing them only shifts their direction a little. If pressed, they scatter sideways, then return, because the floor beneath them is food, shelter, passage, and meeting-place at once.

Some shell-skinned things are crablike, but not crabs. Their backs are hollowed into cups, and in those cups they carry water. They seem to be moving treasures from one place to another, though they are only following moisture as it appears and recedes. When a fresh dampness forms on a wall, they turn toward it with their antennae twitching. When a chamber begins to dry, they settle into the crevices, drawing the cup of water down around themselves. In a hot season—if such a thing can be called hot—they become still objects, almost stones, until the next breath of condensation wakes them.

The snails are more beautiful, and therefore more unsettling. Their shells are translucent amber, thin enough to show the slow blue-gray pulse inside. They glide along condensation tracks and leave shining lines that cloud over almost at once. They do not merely crawl; they taste the surface ahead of them, extending a pale tongue over mineral films and shell powder. Their grazing leaves delicate ridges on walls. Sometimes those ridges align with the slow crystalline patterns of the chalky mats, as if the snails were completing sentences begun by the stone.

From the dust, if the dust is watched long enough, things emerge.

Small many-legged burrowers come up as though the powder itself were breeding them. They are no larger than a thumbnail at first, then twice that size, then pale and quick-moving, carrying grains in their jaws and excreting fine white pellets. They dig not through rock, but through the unfinished layer of mineral sand that coats half the Underfield. Their burrows collapse almost as fast as they are made. They spend their lives in a soft, granular twilight, emerging only when the air thickens and the sand takes on weight. When many emerge together, the dust seems to shiver. When they fall back into it, the floor looks as though it has swallowed them by memory.

In the shallow trickles, where water has not yet decided whether it will remain, eel-things pass in sudden silver clusters. They have flat heads and too many fins, little fins arranged along their bodies like the flanges of old coins. They do not swim so much as flow, pressing against each other in narrow channels until their bodies become a single moving rope of silver. They eat bacterial films and the soft bodies of smaller crawlers. When the trickle vanishes, they lie in wet powder for hours, curled into rings, until the water finds them again. Their stillness is not death; it is patience. In the Underfield, patience is a survival strategy, because nothing is stable long enough for panic to be useful.

The birds are the most obvious evidence that this place is not simply an animal market, but a place with its own strange weather of life.

They hang from ledges in pale clusters, their feet hooked into the mineral shelves with an ease that suggests those shelves were made for them. They may not be birds. They have feathers, or something that behaves like feathers: brittle, dry, calcium-flecked strands that catch the diffuse light and throw it away again. Their wings are short. They do not soar. They hop sideways, climb, launch themselves into a clumsy glide, then strike the wall with a wet clatter. Their eyes are milky, but they seem to use them only when the air is thick enough to carry the faintest shine of water. More often, they read the chamber by sound.

They do not sing. They knock.

Their beaks strike stone in short rhythms, as if testing whether the wall has dried, softened, or begun to grow. Sometimes they knock for a minute at one spot, then move to another. Sometimes they knock until a thin mineral ledge flutters and cracks. Sometimes they knock because they are angry with one another over a warm crevice. Sometimes they knock because the sound is useful: in the echo, a bird can tell whether the chamber beyond is full of bodies, full of water, or nearly empty. Their young, if that word is allowed, hatch in shallow niches where condensation gathers. They are born naked, chalky, and almost silent, then grow brittle feather-spines and begin to strike the stone before they can leave the ledge.

And beneath the birds, beneath the crustaceans, beneath the snails and burrowers, beneath the bacterial films and mineral skins, the floor itself is busy.

It has layers. The smallest creatures crawl through the interstices of larger ones, as if the bodies of the larger ones were part of the terrain. Medium creatures form bridges across puddles with their own backs: a line of soft-shell crawlers links one island of stone to another, and when the water rises, the bridge bends, but does not break. Large, slow animals stand in open hollows like boulders. They are not stones. They have shells that have become so thick that the whole creature looks like a piece of architecture fallen from an earlier age. Their legs are columnar, their faces hidden under hoods of plate. They move rarely, and when they do, the smaller animals part around them in dark, shining streams. Their shells harbor films of living crust. Their footprints collect water. Their fallen scales become shelter for creatures no longer than a finger.

This is the life that fills the Underfield from wall to wall, floor to ceiling, drip to powder.

Now, whoever built the huts belongs to this, but is not fully of it.

They are rare, and that rarity is essential. If they were many, the caves would have become something else. If they were many, their bodies might have been seen piled in corners, their tools scattered by the hundreds, their shelters spreading into villages. But no such thing exists. The builders are few—bands of three, five, seven, perhaps sometimes more if a season is unusually wet and the food thickens in the basins. They do not crowd. They drift. They occupy. They leave.

They are upright enough to build, which is why one might call them people, though that word is too generous. They are adapted to this wet mineral darkness in ways a person would not be. Their bodies are squat but strong. Their legs are short relative to their arms, and their feet are broad and three-toed, with thick pads and curved claws that lock against stone. They can stand on a nearly vertical ledge for hours if the ledge is warm, because heat from the ground is one of the few comforts this place gives. Their arms are long, with large hands made for prying, scraping, and holding. Each hand has a short opposing thumb and four long, shell-tipped fingers. The fingernails grow as hard mineral plates, useful for digging into porous crust, for cracking the backs of stony animals, for shaping bone awls, for carving notches into ledges where no tool is available.

Their skin is pale, but not smooth. It has a chalky, damp texture, like a stone that has begun to shed its surface. Over the shoulders, hips, and forearms, it hardens into thin flakes that must be rubbed off every few days, or they crack and bleed beneath. Some of these flakes are kept. Some are stacked in small heaps outside shelters. Some are chewed. Their bodies require minerals in quantities that would make a surface-dweller ill; they eat them as readily as food. A young builder may be seen gnawing a corner of dried mineral plate with the patient pleasure of someone eating bread.

Their faces are broad and flattened, not ugly, but shaped by a world without distance. There is no horizon here, so the eyes have no long work to do. They are small, pale, and often clouded, yet not blind. They see motion, brightness, wetness, and the faint glitter of mineral films better than they see shape. They cannot look far, but they can tell at a glance whether a wall is drying, whether a pool is forming in a chamber behind a ridge, whether a shell is fresh or empty. Their noses are wide, almost like slits beneath a ridge of bone, lined with a mucus that traps mineral dust and carries scent deep into the skull. They smell iron, yeast, salt, rot, wet bone, and the particular metallic odor of water that has touched a young column. They taste the air before they trust a chamber.

Their ears are perhaps the most important part of their bodies.

The Underfield is loud, and a builder learns to listen the way a surface person learns to read a map. Water dripping from different heights sounds different. Wet sand collapsing sounds different from dry powder sliding. A chamber full of crawling bodies hisses with a different texture than a chamber full of birds. A crack in a new column makes a high, dry note, but a crack in an old mineral shelf is duller, almost like bone. Builders tap the floor, the wall, the edge of a shelter, not merely to test it, but to understand where they are. They know their region by sound: the rhythm of a particular seep, the echo of a particular grotto, the repeated knock of a bird-roost against the ledge beside a sleeping place.

They speak, if speech is the word. It is not fluent. It is not soft. Their language is percussive and tactile. They produce short, sharp clicks by pressing the tongue against the palate. They strike pebbles against stone to send messages across a chamber. They tap a rhythm onto another’s shoulder, onto a wall, onto the floor. They touch hands in a particular order when the noise of the water makes voice useless. Their names, if they have names, are not names given by parents but sounds adopted from the place: the click of a shell on a particular ledge, the tap of a bill against the lintel of a hut, the rhythm of a drip that has continued for many years. They are unlikely to call one another “mother,” “friend,” or “stranger” in the way a person might. More important is the sound a body makes while moving through a wet chamber.

They are not numerous because the place does not make it easy to be many.

There are no fields. There are no grains. There is no herding of grazing animals, because there is no grazing. Food is everywhere, but it is thin, scattered, and unreliable. A band may find a chamber where the crustaceans have gathered in a thick wave, and for days they will scrape them from ledges, crack their shells, and eat them raw or heated. The next day, the crustaceans may be gone, carried away by a shift in the floor’s dampness, and the band will move on to a place where chalky mats have spread into edible ridges. They scrape the mats with shell tools, mix them with rendered water, and swallow them in paste. Some of it tastes of iron.
