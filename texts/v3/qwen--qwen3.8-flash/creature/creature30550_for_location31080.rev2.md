## What Lives in the Hollow of Anhydrous Green

The Hollow is not simply occupied. It is inhabited in the broadest sense of that word: not by people alone, but by a household made of bodies, roots, engines, dust, salt, breath, and slow negotiations between what is animal, vegetable, mineral, civic, and unfinished.

If one insists on calling the dominant inhabitants “people,” then begin with the Dryborn.

## The Dryborn

They are human enough to be mistaken for refugees from the surface, or perhaps descendants of those who came down, dug in, and gradually learned to become part of the cavern rather than remain guests in it. Over generations, they have adapted so completely to the dry, cool, bright-underground world that they now look and smell and move as though the Hollow grew around them as much as they grew within it.

Their skin is pale as chalk or cured bark, often faintly silver-green along the shoulders, forearms, and temples, where microscopic light-dwellers live in the pores. They do not shine with moisture. They shine with oil. Their hands are dry, dusted, sometimes lacquered with resin, and their fingernails are filed blunt so they will not catch on root-cables or mirror-frames. Their hair is coarse, pale, often bound into root-braids with copper wire or dried vine-thread. Their eyes are wide and quiet, lids lined with salt-oil, pupils ringed with faint reflective flecks because they have lived long beneath controlled light rather than wild sun. Their teeth are worn flat as millstones, not from age, but from a lifetime of seed flour, hard lichen, roasted fruit-pods, mineral salts, and dry cakes made from ground beetles or oil-soaked grasses.

They do not sweat. They bloom in mineral scent: resin, dried herbs, warm stone, oil, and faint chalk. If they are thirsty, they do not seek puddles; they carry sealed flasks of pressed plant oil, salted sap paste, and small tablets of crystallized electrolyte. Their bodies are flasks in themselves, holding moisture as an inner secret, sealed from the air, never leaking. If a Dryborn worker cuts a hand, the wound is powder-dusted and sealed before it can betray dampness. They do not weep in the old way. Grief and irritation leave behind only fine salt at the inner corners of the eyes, or a dry cough, or a tendency to breathe through the teeth.

Their voices are low. The Hollow absorbs sharpness; there is no wet air to carry shouting, no liquid to slap or drip. So the Dryborn speak in near-whispers and ordinary tones, with dry consonants and long vowels. They listen more than they command. A person from elsewhere might think them calm, polite, or even sleepy, but they are not. They are tuned to root-creak, cable-hum, fan-breath, and the subtle pressure changes that mean a shaft above is opening or closing its diurnal throat.

They wear split-soled boots, not to leave prints, but to feel the living ground. The Dryborn can tell by the give of a root-road whether a building is old, whether a street has been stitched recently, whether the substrate below is warm with fermentation, or whether a grove is preparing to exhale dust. Their clothing is root-cloth, bark-fiber, and lacquered mineral hide, pale green, silver-gray, ochre, or the color of dried sage. They wear gloves in the factories, hoods in the seed-drifts, and goggles of oiled mica when the mirrors are being adjusted.

They are not primitive. They are not savages of the cave. They are citizens of a working world, and their lives are arranged around dry industry, civic biology, and the careful maintenance of light.

## The Children and the Rites of the Rootwards

A Dryborn child is not born into wetness. The midwives work in root-cradles padded with salted filaments and dry moss that gives beneath the fingers without becoming soft. The first act is not washing but sealing: powdered talc, resin paste, mineral dust, and a few drops of oil rubbed into the baby’s shoulders to teach the skin its new element. The child’s first cry is dry and startling because the lungs must learn to fill with cool, filtered air.

Children are named in light-columns. When the mirror shafts open to a new day, the child is carried to the rootward’s clearing and watched for the place where dust lifts most brightly in the falling beam. A chalk mark is made there. The family’s chosen seed-box is planted in the nursery yard, and the child’s name is bound to it. Some names are short and dry: *Tass, Nym, Corl, Orel, Vane, Slat.* Some are grove-names: *Lantern-Root, Salt-Pulse, Copper-Leaf, Second Pollen, Quiet Beam.* A child may be called by two names: one spoken, one grown.

They learn to walk in root-lanes, not on bare stone. The ground teaches balance before the mother does. Children chase powder-moths through late light, roll hard seed-pods down dry channels, beat fallen fruit with reed rods, and listen to crickets for the sound of shift changes. They climb young root-arches that are still tender and not yet load-bearing. They are warned not to sleep beneath mirror-bases unless a Keeper has approved the angle, because a column of light at the wrong hour can burn dust into a child’s lungs and make them taste of metal for days.

Marriage among the Dryborn is not a wet sacrament. Two people enter the grove of their chosen rootward, exchange small sealed vials: one of oil, one of salt dust. They bind a cable of vine and copper around each other’s wrists and touch their foreheads to a warm trunk, letting its mineral breath pass over them. The grove’s pressure changes underfoot for a moment; if it does, the match is said to have “rooted.” If not, they are gently advised to wait until the substrate accepts them. This is not superstition. The Dryborn have lived so long with the green floor that they believe, and are not entirely wrong, that a healthy root-mat responds to human habit as surely as a road responds to wheels.

Death, too, is dry. The Hollow has no water burial. The dead are laid in upper niches to desiccate, then returned to the substrate in a manner determined by their trade. Mirror-Keeppers’ ashes may be worked into glassy mineral paste for new lenses. Root-Stitchers are folded into the living edges of streets. Factory workers may be pressed into resin-board and used as door cores for new nursery-boxes. The living floor accepts them. The ancestors are not ghosts beneath the ground. They are the ground’s civic memory, slowly compressed into root and wall.

## The Working Body of the City

The Dryborn do not merely live in the Hollow. They maintain it, negotiate with it, and are maintained by it.

Most belong to a rootward, but a rootward is not a neighborhood in the ordinary sense. It is a household of human and green interests, a civic unit made of houses, workshops, cable-stations, drying yards, mineral beds, and a founding grove. A rootward has a name that is partly human and partly chemical: the rootward may be called *Salt-Arbor,* *Fan-Root,* *Lace-Terrace,* or *Copper-Thread,* depending on what grows there and what the air does when the wind changes above.

Each rootward has its essential citizens:

**The Mirror-Keeppers** climb cable-wells and shaft galleries to adjust polished mica, copper plates, and grown-glass lenses. They know the angle of surface day from the tremor of light on terraces. They wear oiled goggles and carry long brass rods tipped with dry resin so they can nudge a mirror back into its proper tilt without scratching it. If they fail, the rootward dims; the air grows too cool; the plants close their pores; the Dryborn begin to cough dryly in their sleep.

**The Root-Stitchers** train the living floor into firmness. They compress filaments, braid tendrils across streets, seal tears in the substrate with resin cord, and teach the green where not to thicken. They are scarred on the palms, because root-mat is brittle in dry air and can cut like thin wire. A good street is one the root-Stitchers have persuaded rather than forced: the filaments become dense underfoot, then lace outward into pale, breathing mesh.

**The Grove-Wardens** are the city’s interpreters. They read root-pressure, oil-flow, pore-openings, and the subtle vibrations that pass through the substrate when the air shifts. They decide whether a tree may be tapped for oil, whether a vine may be trained over a beam, whether a plaza should be narrowed because the lace-mat beneath is starving, or whether an unfinished district should be allowed to swallow an old quarry wall. They do not command nature. They bargain with it using salts, heat, pressure, pruning, and patient waiting.

**The Air-Engineers** manage fans, pistons, pneumatic lines, cooling shafts, and thermal wells. They listen to the Hollow’s breath with their whole bodies. They can taste the difference between factory warmth and raw stone cold. They maintain vertical currents that lift seed-dust, regulate resin vapor, and keep fermentation vaults from becoming too dry or too brittle. Their gloves are thick, their faces often powdered, and their language is full of clicks and hand signs because fan noise can drown ordinary speech.

**The Seed-Divers** work when the upper shafts release dry spirals of pods at dusk. They wear mesh hoods and carry long poles with catch-nets made of pale root-lace. They sort, crack, winnow, and store seed in vast resin-sealed bins. Their work determines food, oil, planting material, and the future shape of new nursery-buildings. They are often young, or old enough to move with patience, because seed-diving is less labor than rhythm: standing in a warm updraft, letting the pods fall into nets, hearing the dry knock of fruit as it strikes and splits without wetness.

**The Dry Fermenters** coax plant chemistry into new materials without liquid baths. Their vaults are warm, quiet, and faintly metallic. They cultivate catalytic cultures in oil, powder, and mineral vapor. They produce resins, lubricants, pressed root-board, pigments, medicines, and building pastes that cure in air. They smell of chalk and burnt herb. They are careful about open mouths and bare skin, not because fermentation is wet, but because too much dry culture in the lungs can crystallize.

**The Herders and Beast-Tenders** manage grazing clearings, roost cables, beetle-hoppers, and cricket-resonance tubes. They do not drive animals with shouts. They use fans, whistles, salt blocks, root-mat signals, and the movement of light to guide creatures toward feeding grounds or away from mirror-bases. In industrial rootwards, the factory vents become part of the herding rhythm: when warm air shifts, the beasts move; when seed-pods fall, the scavengers gather; when the thread-cats settle high, the humans know the upper orchard is entering its quiet phase.

## The Animals Who Hold Civic Roles

Animal life in the Hollow is not decorative. It is functional, familiar, strange, and tolerated. The creatures have learned the city’s habits, and the city has learned theirs.

Most famous are the **Dust-Herds**, pale grazing animals like deer, but wrong in the mouth and wrong in the feet. Their coats are fine and dry, almost mineralized at the tips. Their hooves split into soft, wide pads that make no sound on the root-mat, useful for moving quietly through human districts. Their antlers are not bone alone; they lace with salt and crystal, becoming fragile crowns of mineral architecture. They have oil glands at the throat that make their hides valuable for boots, book covers, and mirror-rags. They are not tamed into obedience; they are managed by pressure, warmth, seed supply, and the rhythms of the mirror-light. In good seasons they drift through outer terraces in broad silent herds, and the Dryborn walk beside them without touching them, reading their movements as one might read weather.

Above them roost the **Mica-Swallows**, birds with pale feathers and salt-crusted beaks. Their plumage is dry and faintly glittering, as though powdered with chalk. They do not sing loud songs; they click, whistle, and hum in the high cables, preferring places where the mirror-light is steady. Mirror-Keeppers watch them closely because the birds shift their roosts before a light column changes angle. A Dryborn child can look at a cable full of birds and tell the time of day by their position. Their droppings are white mineral powder, collected carefully for polishing lens-frames and sealing small cracks in stone.

In the upper beams moves the **Thread-Cat**, slender, long-limbed, and many-eyed. Its eyes are not arranged like a mammal’s; they seem set in dry reflective plates around its narrow skull, catching light so that when it turns, it leaves the impression of scattered black coins. It has no wet paw-prints. It moves across cables and vine-arches with the silence of a shadow, hunting moths, beetle-larvae, and the small felt-voles that gnaw at seed-stores. It is not loved in the way of house cats, but it is respected. The Dryborn say it holds the upper rootward in peace. Its fur sheds as fine dry tufts, and those tufts are used to make brushes for mirror dusting.

There is a larger, stranger predator in the outer shafts, sometimes called the **Rasping Owl**, though it is no true owl. It hunts by listening to wingbeats. It has no down, no soft feathers, only thin dry scales and stiff vanes. Its cry is like a stone dragged across slate. Moths and birds know its voice from a great distance. People do not fear it much, because it does not hunt where the root-mat sends the warning signal of tools and engines, but children are taught to walk in pairs when the mirrors begin to dim and the shaft winds shift into dusk-tone.

Below, in the factory margins and seed-drifts, run the **Saltbacks**. They are pack scavengers, low and lean, with salt-encrusted teeth, dry fur, and a habit of following the migratory drift of fallen fruit-pods. They are not vicious because the Hollow is vicious. They are merely efficient. They eat what machines discard: cracked seed shells, resin clippings, broken timber, animal offal, and the powdered remains of failed buildings. They are fed, watched, and sometimes herded away from granary vents. Their hides are used for gloves. Their teeth become tools. Their bones are pressed into handles and pins. The Dryborn do not regard them as noble companions or as pests. They are public sanitation, with claws.

The smallest animals are citizens in ways humans rarely acknowledge.

**Slate Beetles** move through the waste-hoppers with shells like polished stone. They do not crawl through rot; they do not wallow. They grind dead fruit into powder. They are kept in dry bins beneath granaries and in sealed rooms behind factory walls. Their frass, fine and mineral-rich, feeds groves that return oil to the presses. Their shells, when collected and polished, become buttons, inlays, and protective plates for mirror-hinges.

**Powder-Moths** drift through upper orchards, wings dusted with pale mineral scales. They are pollinators of the light-fed canopy plants that drop seeds into the city’s collecting fans. Their dust is harvested with care, brushed from wing-frames that have shed or died, and used in pigments, medicines, and the sealing paste that protects young buildings. At dusk, when the light columns slide low, the moths move through the beams like silver soot, and the rootward becomes briefly full of glittering wings.

**Cricket-Kin** pulse their calls in long, dry intervals. They are not merely insects; they are timekeepers. They are kept in resonance tubes mounted in workshops, granaries, and school-boxes. Their songs change slightly with temperature and air pressure, so workers know whether a ventilation shaft is opening, whether a factory is overheating, or whether a new dust tide is descending. A child learns to read a cricket’s pulse before learning to read a clock-face.

**Felt-Voles** and root-shrews live under buildings, between root shelves, and inside cable housings. They have large ears, dry fur, and teeth built for grinding. They are not pests if one has the right agreements with the grove. They warn of spoiled seed by scratching at store-boxes in particular rhythms. They detect dry rot, overheating resin, and the presence of larger predators. Some are allowed into granary lofts as living alarms. Others are excluded from mirror-bases, where their dust can ruin a lens.

## The Green That Governs

The truest inhabitant of the Hollow is not the Dryborn person, nor the beast, nor the machine. It is the green.

The root-mat is not passive soil. It is the living plate upon which all life balances. It has the habits of a creature without having the form of one. It thickens where roads are wanted. It thins where plazas are intended. It brightens where feet press regularly. It darkens where air pools in hollows. It sends vibrations through the floor when roots split stone, when engines warm nearby soil, when mirrors shift too fast, and when predators approach. The Dryborn do not claim to hear language in it. They are too practical for that. But they do call it *the floor’s consent.*

The trees are not ornaments. They are architecture, infrastructure, and civic partners. Some are tapped for oil like wells. Some are trained into supports, their branches becoming roofs. Some exhale dust that seals cracks. Some open pores at set hours, altering air temperature and mineral vapor. The largest of them, in the industrial districts, are enormous engineered organisms, trunks wider than houses, roots driven into stone like piles. They have names. They have wardens. They have schedules. They are fed factory vent-heat and mineral exhaust, and they return materials that the next factory needs. In this way, the city’s metabolism is circular without ever becoming wet.

There are plants that resemble grasses but cut like brittle reeds; moss-like plates that hold no damp but give a faint chalky spring; fungi whose caps are hard as dried leather and release crystalline spore-dust. There are shrubs with leaves that ring when struck, useful for signaling between distant rootwards. There are pale translucent stalks filled with oil, refracting light like lanterns. There are dry creepers that weave themselves around lamp-posts and cables, not by accident but by a patience so consistent it seems social. The Dryborn believe that if a plant begins growing around a tool too often, the tool should be left there. The city learns which shapes the green wants.

The plants have customs. A certain salt-root is forbidden near mirror-bases because it exudes vapor that clouds glass. A certain lace-fern is allowed in plazas because it thins traffic and lets mineral beds breathe. A certain oil-shrub is encouraged in worker districts because it deters saltbacks. A certain brittle lichen is cleared from old quarry walls because it attracts beetles that loosen stone before the root-stitchers have sealed it. These are not laws in the human sense. They are long habits, repeated until they look like etiquette.

The Hollow’s ecology has become urbanized, but the city has also become ecological. The two are not separate categories anymore. A street is a plant. A foundation is a grove. A factory’s exhaust is a fertilizer. A predator’s route is a boundary marker. A plaza’s root-lace is a civic assembly. To live there is to understand that the ground is watching with a thousand blind, patient senses, and that one’s actions are remembered as growth.

## The Invisible Citizens

If the Dryborn, beasts, and plants are visible, the smaller inhabitants are still more numerous.

The walls bloom with **salt-mold**, pale as frost but dry as flour. It consumes dead fiber, leaves mineral residue, and smells faintly of chalk and resin. The factories feed it intentionally in certain districts; elsewhere it is scraped away with dry brushes because it can alter lens angles or thicken cables.

There are **oil-bacteria** and **dry yeasts** cultivated in sealed rooms, on bark-boards, and inside fermentation vaults. They do not bubble. They do not slop. They work in vapor, powder, and heat. They transform crushed seed into paste, resin into lubricant, mineral dust into binding paste, dead matter into the crystalline compost that feeds the groves. Every Dryborn household keeps a small culture jar by the door, because even the air must be seasoned.

The human skin itself hosts a garden. The pale light-dwellers along the shoulders, the salt-oil microbes around the lips, the fine dry flora in the hairline: these are not disease. They are citizenship. A Dryborn body is never truly private; it carries the Hollow in its pores. A stranger’s skin may become irritated in the first seasons, peeling with the air, until it learns to join the civic microbiology. Some newcomers never adjust. They leave, or they die, or they live in the uppermost rootwards near the freshest shafts, wrapped in oilcloth, breathing from bellows lined with filtered mineral pads.

Below the lowest terraces, where neither root nor machine has reached, there are still other pioneers: stone-splitting microbes, mineral fungi, and dry bacterial mats that work on raw rock with chemical patience. These are not urban citizens, not yet. They are the future inhabitants. They are the Hollow being made.

## Those Who Pass Through

The Hollow is not closed off from all outside worlds. There are shafts that reach the far surface, cable-lines that connect distant basins, and trade routes that bring iron, rare crystals, old books, and the occasional wet traveler.

**The Surface Traders** are often uneasy in the Dryborn city. They bring stories of rain, rivers, dew, and swamps, and the Hollowfolk listen with the polite discomfort one gives to tales of impossible weather. A surface child may ask why there are no puddles. A Dryborn child may ask why surface people are not afraid of being filled from the outside.

**The Unfinished Settlers** live in the new districts where buildings germinate in nursery yards and raw stone still glitters pale at the edges. They are poorer, more adventurous, more tolerant of dust, and more accustomed to living with walls that have not yet decided what they will be. Their doors may lead into rooms that will not have ceilings for another year. Their stairs may curve into air. They do not consider this haunting. They consider it civic hope.

**The Outliers** are the ones who live at the margins: mirror climbers, seed divers, predator watchers, salvage crews, and rootward exiles who prefer the brittle edge where the root-mat thins and the dry stone shows. They know the Hollow’s unfinished places better than its polished plazas. They speak in compressed breath because they work near open shafts. They carry small fans and oil flasks. They know that if a raw crack begins to smell too warm, the green below is moving faster than the city above.

## What It Looks Like at Dusk

At evening, the light columns slide low across the terraces. The mirror shafts tilt. Dust lifts visibly and begins to fall in slow spirals from the upper growths. The city takes on the look of a dry tide moving through stone.

Children return home carrying seed-pods tapped lightly for the sound inside. Workers brush powder from their shoulders. Factory fans change direction with a deep groan, not a shout. Crickets pulse their first evening notes. Thread-cats shift onto the highest cables, eyes catching the last bright beams like tiny black mirrors. Dust-herds move away from the warm industrial basins toward cooler outer grasses. Saltbacks gather near vents that have stopped blowing mineral air and begun smelling of roasted shell and rendered oil.

The Dryborn do not light lamps so much as invite the city to breathe light. Mirrors are adjusted by hand. Resin lamps flicker with dry green flame. Cables hum. The root-mat creaks softly as temperature drops. The air tastes of mineral cleanliness, not dampness. In the rootwards, houses settle into the floor as if agreeing to another night. A grove releases a faint exhalation of spores, and the light catches them for a moment, so that a plaza seems briefly full of suspended chalk fireflies.

An elder sits near a mirror-base, dusting the lens-frame with a beetle-shell brush. A moth lands on their wrist, and they let it stay, then lift their hand gently so the powder-moth can join the evening drift. A child beside them carries a cricket-tube under the arm, listening to it count the dark.

If you ask them who lives here, they may answer in the plural because there are too many kinds of life to count:

“Rootfolk.”
“Mirrorfolk.”
“Seedfolk.”
“Dustfolk.”
“The green.”
“The beasts that hold the edges.”
“The machines that breathe for us.”
“The unfinished rooms still waiting to be grown.”

But if you press further, they will point to the ground beneath their feet.

The Hollow is not a place containing a city. It is a living system containing a civilization. The people are its speakers. The root-mat is its floor and memory. The trees are its scaffolding. The animals are its neighbors, workers, warnings, and wild partners. The factories are its metabolism. The mirrors are its eyes. The dust is its breath.

And the Dryborn, walking softly through that dust, are only one part of a much older, stranger household: a city that has learned to live not beside life, not over life, not beneath life, but inside the slow, dry, green body of the world.
