## What Lives in the Underweft

The first thing living in the Underweft is not a creature, not a machine, not even a person. It is a habit: the habit of being careful.

Everything there has learned, one way or another, not to be too loud, not to be too dry, not to be too fixed. The moss spreads without ceremony. The water moves without announcing itself. The animals keep to the folds and seams. The people speak in lowered voices, walk softly, touch things gently, and understand that a sudden tap on the wrong wall can reveal a hollow behind stone, and that a hollow behind stone can mean failure. The whole basin has become a habitat not for the bold, but for the attentive.

Whoever lives there lives among gaps.

### The Moss-Wrights and the Children of Drips

The human residents of the Underweft do not think of themselves as owners so much as tenants with repair duties. A family does not “own” a house in the clean, legal sense. It has responsibility for a section of wall, roof, pipe, gutter, or condensation channel, and that responsibility is the family’s place in the world. If you keep the water where it should go, if you prevent the salt bloom from eating the tile, if you clear the moss bed before it traps too much weight, you have earned your dwelling for another season. If you do not, you are not punished in a dramatic way. You are simply relocated, quietly, to a fold that needs more attention and can pay you less comfort.

This makes the city feel less like a place of property and more like a living organism with organs. Some districts are lungs, breathing vapor in and out through the condenser towers. Some are blood vessels, carrying water under pressure through ceramic-lined channels. Some are stomachs, breaking down mineral paste and feeding the walls. Some are skin, furred with moss and lichen that regulates temperature and moisture. The people live in all of these at once, and many do not even notice that the city is more metabolic than architectural.

In the residential folds, the households are dense and layered. A mother might sleep in an alcove behind a pipe that warms her during the coldest hours, while her children occupy a narrow shelf above a stream that she must prune of root growth before it backs up beneath their floor. The room smells of wet stone, wool oil, mineral dust, and faint bread. There is no one clean boundary between inside and outside. The walls sweat. The ceilings catch. Water appears in corners not because of failure, but because the city has not yet learned to be entirely sealed and has not entirely tried.

People have nicknames and names that shift according to their work. In the upper terraces, a woman might be known by the seam she keeps: Sela-of-the-Nine-Drips, or Rho of the Cold Lattice. In the lower workshops, names become shorter, blunter, tied to tools: Tull the felt-cutter, Bess of the ceramic pins, old Oren whose fingers were so good at reading pipe joints that young apprentices were sent to him like children to a grandmother. These names are not formal. They are descriptions that have hardened into identity, the way mineral paste hardens into brick.

The children are born into maintenance.

A baby is carried against a parent’s side under a hood of oiled cloth, close enough to be warmed but not smothered by the damp. Infants learn early that the air has a temperature that changes on their skin. Their first sounds are not cries so much as thin, clear protests that are quickly quieted by a palm pressed to the mouth. This is not cruelty. In the Underweft, loudness is not merely impolite; it is dangerous. A shout can carry too well across the open bowl of stone. It can startle a herd, loosen a loose tile, alarm a sleeping worker, reveal weakness to someone else in the fold. Children are taught before they can run that their voices must be placed somewhere useful.

They grow up knowing the difference between a drip and a leak, between a damp wall and a saturated one, between a crack that merely frightens and a crack that means the ground is trying to change shape.

Their games involve water. One of the simplest is called *seam-running*: a child is given a shallow ceramic dish holding a measured amount of condensation collected from a tower, and must carry it through the folds to another child without spilling more than a few drops. Another is called *moss-reading*: two children stand near a wall and listen to the tiny sounds of moisture moving through lichen and root mat, trying to guess where the wettest part of the structure lies. In summer-like weeks, when the air is almost warm enough to exhale and the condensation falls in fine sheets along the buildings, they race along the pipes, collecting the drips that follow them.

They are clever kids, pale and steady-handed, with chapped lips and eyes that seem too large for their faces because they have spent so long looking at small things.

### The Bodies and Habits of the People

To live in the Underweft is to live with cold that is not dramatic but persistent. The people are not snow-hardened mountaineers. They are not frozen peasants or storm-bound sailors. They are damp, layered, patient humans whose bodies have adapted less to the temperature than to the moisture.

Their skin is often pale, not from lack of light, but because they are mostly indoors, in shadowed folds where daylight comes in filtered and thin. Their hands are dry in strange ways, cracked at the knuckles from mineral salts, while their feet are soft from constant near-wetness. They smell less of smoke than of metal, felt, lichen, rendered fat, and the faint briny odor of evaporated water. Their clothes are a second skin of insulation: wool linings, waxed cloth, ceramic toggles, cork-lined boots, and hats with ear flaps that catch condensation and drip it away from the face.

They are careful about heat. Not because they are wealthy, but because heat in the Underweft is a managed resource. Small stoves do not crackle openly. They are housed in ceramic pits, surrounded by felt and ash, radiating a low, even warmth that keeps a room from becoming a pocket of ice. The richer households have dry rooms, or at least drier rooms, with vents and catchments and moss filters. The poorer ones live where the walls breathe too strongly, where the floor takes longer to dry, where the bed is warm only near the engine pipe and cold near the door.

Food is shaped by this too.

The Underweft eats cold things, wet things, salty things, and things preserved with fat. They have learned to crave salt because the damp air and mineral water pull it from the body. Their commonest meal is a thick broth or mash, served in ceramic bowls held with both hands. The broth may be made from tubers grown in shallow beds of mineral paste, sedge roots boiled until sweet, dried algae, a scrape of salt from the condensation bloom, and a spoonful of eel fat or grazer milk curd. Bread is often not baked in ovens so much as dried on warm racks near engines: flat, dense, slightly sour, made from a grain-like sedge or a paste of powdered lichen. Fermented foods are everywhere, because in a place where rot and mildew are constant enemies, sourness becomes a friend. Small jars of fermented reed shoots, beet paste, or fish bits line many shelves.

They do not waste heat.

A family cooking will often cook beneath the floor of a workshop, or beside the engine pit of a drying room, or in a small alcove warmed by the exhalation of a condenser tower. Their kitchens are not bright places with roaring fires. They are low, tucked spaces where steam curls under stone lids and pots are set into insulated niches. Cooking smells are muted. The cold takes too much from the air for anything to linger loudly.

The people are not thin, nor heavy, nor uniform. Some are broad and compact, built for the damp and the long maintenance hours. Some are lean from work that keeps them moving through the folds. Some are pale and cough-prone, living too near the lower channels where the air never fully clears. Some have strong hands and crooked fingers from years of tightening bolts and scraping mineral bloom. They are people shaped by an environment that does not torture them dramatically but never lets them forget that they are not fully separate from the stone.

### The Animals of the Seams

The fauna of the Underweft is small because smallness is the best survival strategy in a place of tight spaces, thin moisture, and unpredictable stone. Most animals here live in the seams: between roof tiles, beneath engine housings, in the joints where pipe meets wall, in the dark hollows where water seeps out and vanishes again.

The voles are everywhere. They are not pests in the simple sense, because the residents know that without them the moss beds would mat and rot, that the root networks would clog, that the tiny underground chambers where springtails and larvae live would stagnate. The voles tunnel, aerate, scatter seeds, and disturb the damp just enough to keep the ground unsettled. They are pale below and brown-gray above, with short ears and quick, uncertain movements. People call them *floor-warmers* because they sometimes burrow near engine pits and carry heat into the moss beds. Children are fond of them, but adults discourage taming, because a tame vole is a vole that stops doing its work.

Beetles climb the condenser towers and feed on the mineral algae that grows in the cooler parts of the metal. They are rounded, hard-shelled, and slow in the cold, so people often find them resting on tiles or along pipe joints, where they look like tiny stones with eyes. They are valuable. The best beetles scrape the algae away from surfaces too fragile for brushing and leave behind a polished, salt-resistant sheen. Some residents collect their shells, which are used in mosaics and small tools. A shell polished just so looks like a dark green tile with silver in its heart.

Birds live in the architecture rather than in trees, since there are no real trees. They nest behind louvered panels, under condenser roofs, in the gaps between beams, and in the abandoned hollows of old factory walls. Their feathers are dense and dark, often edged with pale gray that matches the light. They do not sing in long phrases. Their calls are short: a tick, a rattle, a dry whistle that stops before it becomes too obvious. They move like small, efficient workers, darting from pipe to beam, then vanishing into a gap before anyone notices they were there. They are not beloved for song. They are valued as watchers. A pipe-wren that suddenly goes silent can mean water pressure has shifted in a hidden line, or that a condenser has begun to leak too much mineral mist into the open.

The aquatic creatures of the Underweft are more strange to outsiders than the voles. In the channels, small eels move like silver threads, their bodies adapted to pass through cracks no wider than a thumb. They are caught at dawn in shallow traps made of moss-lined clay. Pale salamanders, slow and translucent in the thin light, rest in the undersides of stepping stones and in the cool mouths of pipes. They are delicate indicators: if the salamanders retreat deep into the channels, something in the water has gone wrong—too much salt, too much oil, too little cold. Crayfish with blue-tinged shells scuttle through the gravel beds, pinching dead matter and cleaning the undersides. They are not numerous in a showy way, but they are constant, like the city’s small custodians.

The largest residents are the grazers. They stand at the edges of the basin on tough grass and reed beds, thick-necked and shaggy, with wool that holds moisture without collapsing. Their hooves are wrapped in woven root-fiber shoes, changed and cleaned by the herders as often as people change boots. They move without hurry because hurry costs heat they cannot spare. When they are startled, they do not flee wildly. They simply stop, watch, and shift their weight. Their milk is thick, almost curdled before it reaches the bucket, and their meat is reserved for important occasions because they are too valuable alive. Their dung is gathered and worked into moss beds. Their wool is carded into felt. Their horns are sawn into handles and pins. They are not companions, exactly. They are partners, large and indifferent, standing in the cold and giving the city enough to continue.

There are also the lesser creatures, the ones people notice only when they are gone. Springtails swarm through damp basements and eat mold. Silverfish live in paper-stored ledgers and old felt linings, feeding on fibers and dead skin. Tiny mites live in the moss beds and keep them from becoming too dense. Some insects are pests, some are useful, and most are simply there, a faint rustle in the corners that tells a person this place has not died.
