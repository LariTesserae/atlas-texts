If by *whoever* you mean persons, then almost no one lives there. The hollows in the stones have been emptied so long that they no longer look like places where people once stood; they look like places where water once stood, had there been water. No lamps burn in windows. No voices braid themselves into the air. No market calls, no quarrels, no laughter, no footsteps except your own if you are foolish enough to push through the fault in the pale cliffs.

But *whatever* lives there is not absent.

What lives in the city lives elsewhere—in the intervals.

It does not live in the broad avenues or the central plazas, where the sound presses against the chest and turns the body into a sounding board. It does not live beneath the open towers or along the dry shafts, where the air rises warm and endless. It lives in the pockets the noise misses, in the folds of architecture where the roar cancels itself and becomes something almost like hush. Life has gathered there because life has learned the city’s grammar: too much sound means nothing can flourish; too little silence means nothing can begin.

So the population of the place is strange and sparse, built more from rhythm than from numbers.

The most obvious inhabitants are the gardens.

They are not gardens in the ordinary sense—no soft soil, no wet roots, no patient gardeners moving through beds with watering cans. They are more like resonant organisms, a dense and mineral bloom, growing only where the acoustics permit them to remain. Broad leaves shine without dew. Thick stems ring when touched. Parallel veins run through the leaves like strings, or like lines on a hand. Their surfaces are dry, sometimes waxy, sometimes faintly metallic, sometimes flushed with a bluish brightness that does not look as if it comes from any visible pigment. They seem, instead, to be lit from inside by the same trapped light that fills the city.

These plants do not drink. They filter. They stand upright in ash-powder and porous dust, drawing in the dry warm exhalation from below, taking in the mineral breath that has no name, using the quartz-bright illumination from above, and—most importantly—using vibration as a kind of force of life. Their hollow stems are not accidents. Many are almost instrumental: thin-walled, precisely tapered, sized by centuries of growth to respond to certain tones. Some hum when the air moves through them. Some remain silent until a particular frequency passes overhead, then they tremble, then they open.

The flowers are dry. They open like folded shells, like parchment cups, like little bells that have never known rain. When a chord rolls through the stone and the garden is caught in its correct frequency, the flowers expand in bursts. They do not smell sweet. They smell of heated mineral, of powder, of something almost like resin but utterly dry. And when they open, they make a tiny sound, a sound that belongs more to insects than to blossoms: a scattering of glass, a whisper of beads, a secret percussion. Outside the courtyard, this sound would be nothing. Inside, it is one of the city’s few remaining intimacies.

Fallen leaves do not rot. They desiccate, curl, crack, and become part of the pale gritty mulch that covers the ground. When you walk through a quiet courtyard, the fallen pieces shift and ring underfoot. Dust lifts in little bursts. The plants lean around your movement, not because they see you, but because you disturb the standing pressure of the air. The whole garden knows when a foreign body enters it.

Among the plants move smaller creatures, so delicate and few that at first they seem like fragments of the landscape that have decided, briefly, to animate themselves.

There are insects that could almost be made of quartz. Their shells are translucent, their edges pale and sharp, their legs thin as wires. They climb the stems and wait. Other insects have darker carapaces that ring faintly when they move. They do not navigate by sight so much as by vibration; they feel the city through their feet and their backs. When the sound lowers, they climb higher, spreading across the broad leaves like living scales. When the high frequencies pierce through the stone, they bury themselves beneath the dust, sealing their legs around themselves and going still. Their stillness is not rest. It is survival.

They are likely the garden’s most numerous citizens, though “numerous” here is a relative word. In a normal city, a place so rich in plant life would swarm with them. Here there are only enough. It is as if the ecosystem has been deliberately kept thin, trimmed back by acoustics so that nothing becomes loud enough to compete with the city itself.

They eat dry pollen. They eat mineral crust. They may eat each other, though that is hard to tell, because they leave so few remains. Their deaths join the pale mulch. Their bodies become part of the dust that the moving air sorts into patterns. In the gardens, nothing is wasted; nothing is even permitted to become wetly, visibly wasted. Decay here has no smell of rot. It has only the slow transformation of one dry substance into another.

The largest visible animal is the copper-furred quadruped.

It is low, compact, broad-skulled, with tiny ears sealed almost entirely by plates of bone. At first you might not know whether it is alive or merely another object in the courtyard. Its coat is the color of oxidized copper, its fur short and dry, never matted. Its feet are wide and quiet. Its eyes are pale, too wide, too reflective, suited to brightness with no shadow. It does not look blind, but it looks as if it has forgotten the need to blink.

This creature does not flee when it sees you. Fleeing is useless in a place where sound can become a wall and where the body can become a mistake in the architecture. Instead, if startled, it presses itself into a recess—a niche, a curved wall, a low doorway, a place where the acoustics flatten—and then it is gone, not by magic but by alignment. It becomes part of the geometry. Its body matches the shape of the silence. Its ears, or what remains of them, are closed against the city. Its fur blends with the rust veins in the stone. Its stillness is so total that the eye forgets it.

It likely lives alone, or sometimes in pairs, in the quietest parts of the courtyards. It does not hunt in the way of fast animals. It is not a stalker of open ground. It moves through gardens in bursts of careful placement, pausing to press its muzzle against a hollow stem, then continuing. It eats dry leaves, seed pods, the crystalline edges of older flowers, perhaps the bodies of the quartz insects. If there are bones here, they are not bones of meat in the wet sense, but of hard mineral growths, of dense cartilage, of the kind of substance the city itself seems to have learned from.

When it drinks—if that word can be used here—it does not lap water. There is none to lap. It may absorb vapor from the warm dust, or lick mineral efflorescence from stone, or take in whatever the city’s dry breath leaves on the surfaces of leaves. Its body would be strange to any wet-blooded creature. It sweats no cooling moisture. Its blood, if it can be called blood, would be more like a slow, salt-heavy fluid, perhaps not even red, but copper-dark or clear, adapted to a place where thirst is not ordinary thirst but a condition of total chemical absence.

Young of this species are rarely seen, perhaps because they are born small and quiet and hidden. They probably learn by imitation, pressing into recesses before they know why. Their first education is the shape of silence. To grow up in this place is to learn the geometry of absence.

Deeper below the courtyards, where the dry shafts fall into warm dark and the air moves upward in slow thermal currents, there is something older and stranger still.

It is the rarest thing anyone might see here: a slow, pale, many-limbed creature, living beneath the floor. It is not frightening. The description of it as frightening would be too simple. It is unsettling in a different way. It makes the emptiness feel not empty, but watched. It gives the impression that the city has not simply been abandoned. It has been continued.
