If you ask who lives there, the place will not answer with names. It answers first with a shiver of surfaces, then with a crackle, then with a dry roar that has no center. There is no hermit in the hollow, no lord of the salt, no beast that owns the basin. The Underbloom, if it must be named, is populated the way a lung is populated, or a swarm, or a fever. It is crowded not by individuals arranged into a society but by processes given temporary bodies.

To describe what lives here is to describe a single continuous fabric torn apart into thousands of moving edges.

### The dust is already alive

The smallest residents are not separate from the ground.

At the beginning, before the valley has shape, there is the skin of it: a pale crust of salt-glass lichen, gypsum bloom, powdered basalt, and white thread-fungus that is not fungus in any familiar sense. It is a living film that grows by accretion and friction, by heat and crystal pressure. It spreads over raw clay and mineral faces the way frost spreads over stone, except that frost does not feed, does not reproduce, does not sense a footfall and close over it within hours.

This film is called dust until you look closely.

It moves in slow granular waves. It is made of crystalline colonies that bind to one another through microscopic salt bridges. Each colony is small enough to pass between your teeth, large enough to build a ridge. Where they meet, they form a crust that cracks under pressure, then repairs itself by drawing in powder from the air. They do not drink, they do not soften. They react at their surfaces, and those surfaces are where all living chemistry in the valley happens.

In this dust live creatures too small to see singly.

They are dry mites and shell-flecks, six-legged and angular, covered in ridges that catch light like flakes of salt. They travel in clouds so fine they look like haze. When the wind lifts, they rise with it. They are not passengers in the dust; they are its workers. They polish mineral plates, grind old shells into fresh powder, carry spores from stalk to stalk, and leave behind excretions that harden into tiny beads. These beads become the nuclei of new crystals.

The valley uses them the way a wet forest might use bacteria, except here nothing is wet, nothing rots, nothing turns to slime. The mites are recyclers of dryness itself. When something dies, they strip it into fragments, carry the fragments away, and the fragments become the beginning of a wall or a ridge or a new root-mat.

There are also the spore-lice, which are not lice but pale segmented forms with needle legs and crystalline backs. They ride the air currents in long columns. You can see them at midday when a wind slips down the throat and strikes a shaft of light: the air fills with a shimmer that looks like powdered gold or salt-snow, but it is life. They land on stalks and flowers and salt plates and remain motionless until disturbed. When disturbed, they do not leap away. They split themselves.

Yes.

One appears to split. It is not dramatic. There is no wetness, no recoil. The shell simply opens along a dry seam and leaves behind half itself, which then straightens and moves. The other half hardens and becomes a husk. The husk may remain for a day and then be eaten by powder-worms.

In this place, reproduction can look like fracture.

### The Underbloom

The true resident, the one that fills the valley from floor to rim, is the Underbloom.

The Underbloom is not a plant, not a forest, not a mat, not a root system. Those are words borrowed from a wet world and pressed onto something that does not behave like them. If forced into a name, the Underbloom is the dry living mass that is always assembling itself from mineral fragments, resin, shell, powder, and heat. It is the valley’s body and also its environment. It is the thing that grows the valley.

Its lowest layer is the root-mat.

The root-mat is a white, pale gray, ochre, and copper weave of threadlike organs that are not roots. They do not suck moisture from soil. There is none. They act as cables, braces, conduits for fine dry powder, anchors, and sensors. They move with a slowness that is not quite vegetable and not quite animal. When a stone shifts, they tighten. When a wind lifts powder from one terrace to another, they receive it and redistribute it through internal channels of resin and silica.

To press your foot into the root-mat is to feel the valley give.

Not as damp earth gives, but as a dry membrane over a living thing gives. It resists after a moment, as if it remembers that your weight should not be there. The roots contract around the edges of your footprint and close it over. If you press your hand into a fresh patch of root-thatch, the mark remains for a few breaths, then disappears.

Above the root-mat grow the first plants: salt rosettes, gypsum scales, pumice mosses, and thread-vines.

The salt rosettes are flat and wide, like shells opened to the light. Their edges are serrated with crystalline teeth. They grow in overlapping circles, covering terraces of ochre clay, and their color is chalk-white until iron-rich dust stains them red-brown. They do not wilt. If you break one, it snaps cleanly, revealing a pale fibrous core and a cavity filled with dry seed-powder. The powder spills out and scatters. Within minutes, nearby filaments crawl over it, incorporating it.

The gypsum scales look like fragments of broken pottery grown across the walls. They are thin and hard, sometimes translucent, sometimes opaque as bone. They overlap in curtains that sound like leather when the wind moves through them. Where they are newly formed, their edges are sharp enough to leave scratches on a hand. The wind polishes those edges. The dust polishes them. The animals polishes them. Nothing remains sharp forever here, because nothing is allowed to remain anything forever.

The pumice mosses are pale gray cushions of porous tissue. They are not soft. If you press them, they crackle and compress, then expand back into their former shape. They trap heat from the mineral floor and give it back slowly. They are living blankets that keep the valley at the temperature of breath.

The thread-vines are coppery and green-yellow, dry as wire. They climb over stalks, bind arches, and seal cracks in walls. Some are so fine they appear to be mere discolorations in the root-mat. When they bloom, the bloom is not a flower but a cluster of tiny resin beads that harden into grains before falling.

These are the small builders.

But they are not alone.

### Stalks, columns, and living walls

Taller than the rosettes and scales are the stalks.

The stalks are hollow, brittle, fibrous, and surprisingly strong. Some are no thicker than a finger and rise waist-high. Others are as thick as a tree trunk, but no tree-trunk has ever been so dry. They are made of concentric layers: outer mineral bark, inner resinous wood-fiber, inner core of pale powder. The core does not carry liquid. It carries movement. The dry powder shifts within the stalk when the wind blows, when the temperature changes, when animals strike it, when heat expands the columns. The stalks use that movement to distribute nutrients, spores, and mineral charges.

Some stalks ring when struck.

The sound is bright, ceramic, almost like a clay pipe tapped with a stone hammer. Those are the oldest columns, their outer shells fused with glassy mineral veins. Others make a dull dry thud. Others sound like hollow reeds full of seeds. If you walk through a grove of stalks and brush past them, the grove will begin to speak in that language: click, thud, chime, crack, whisper.

The living walls are composed of overlapping fronds and mineral barks.

They are not walls because someone walled the valley. They are walls because the stalks have bent against one another, fused, split, grown over one another, died in sections, and rebuilt those sections. A wall in the Underbloom may have archways in it that look like mouths. These mouths open and close slowly over days. Sometimes they are merely hollows where animals shelter. Sometimes they become tunnels when the wall thickens behind them. Sometimes a tunnel collapses and is instantly filled by the same material that built it.

The arches above paths are living columns that lean toward one another and, if left alone for years, grow until they touch. Once touched, they fuse at the upper point, forming a roof. A corridor may exist for one season and become a cave in the next. A cave may become a pillar when its roof collapses inward and the broken shell becomes a foundation.

There are pod-thickets where the stalks bear enormous fruit-hulls.

The hulls are dry shells, some as large as a human torso, some small enough to fit in a palm. They are ridged like the surface of the moon, colored ash-gray, pale green, ochre, or red where iron dust stains them. They do not fall. They remain attached until the wind shakes them or the stalk reclaims their shell. When a pod opens, it bursts with a dry click. The inside is not wet. It is full of folded seed-lamellae, tiny mineral plates, powdery filaments, and sometimes baby animals that had sheltered inside and are now released into the air like a dry exhalation.

There are resin columns, tall and translucent at their tops, glowing faintly golden in sunlight. They exude beads of amber-like substance that hardens into spheres. These spheres fall and are carried away by beetles, or they remain and become part of the wall.

There are black basalt trees, if trees can be called such.

They are not tall like oaks. They are squat, dark, and jagged, with branches that snap like glass rods. Their bark is crushed basalt fused by mineral secretions. They grow where heat rises more strongly, where the root-mat is thin, where the ground is raw and unfinished. They are ugly and brilliant, and their branches hold swarms of seed-crunchers, moths, and tiny lizards.

Every plant form in the Underbloom has the same quality: it is not growing into space. It is growing against the other things around it. It touches, presses, fuses, cracks, seals, and builds.

### Small dry bodies

The smallest animals are so numerous that they change the color of surfaces.

The seed-crunchers are six-legged, hard-shelled, and flat. They live in sheets over the lower root-mats, covering the ground until the ground appears to be a moving mosaic. Their bodies are colored to match the dry world: salt-white, ochre, pale copper, charcoal, or red-brown. They look like seed-pods with legs. Many are seed-pods with legs, because the stalks build their shells into their backs.

Their mandibles are strong and dry. They break open fruit-hulls and click them into fragments. The sound of a swarm of seed-crunchers feeding is like a roof being rained upon by pebbles, except there is no rain. They are eating. Their clicking is not warning or speech. It is the result of mass action.
