To say what lives in such a place, one must first abandon the usual idea that life merely sits on top of ground. Here, life is the ground’s slow, persistent refusal to remain dead matter. The country is not populated in the ordinary sense; it is metabolized into being. What walks, swims, skims, burrows, calls, and glimmers there are not separate inhabitants placed upon a stage, but mobile organs of an ancient body trying to stay coherent in a landscape whose rules have softened.

There are no roads, camps, fences, towers, or shelters because nothing here has learned to live by building apart from itself. The residents are not guests of the plateau. They are part of its working.

## The Membrane

The most dominant resident is not a single animal, though it is undeniably alive.

Across the broken tableland lies a vast, low, green-gray membrane: moss, lichen, peat, fibrous rootwood, fungal threads, damp mineral crusts, translucent stalks, floating skins of plant-matter, and pale films of growth that creep over stone. This is not merely vegetation. It behaves like one continuous organism stretched across a million square spaces. It filters, pumps, condenses, stores, exhales, and repairs.

Where the membrane tears, there is no ordinary soil beneath. There are pale threads like exposed sinew. There are lattices of root carrying fluid. There are pockets of wet absence where something almost biological glows in the dimness. The membrane closes the wounds by secreting a fibrous paste that hardens into new green skin, then leaves behind scars like frost or bone.

The horizontal trees are part of this membrane. They are not individual trees but long, knotted ribs of wood that lie along the ground and fuse with each other, with stone, with living moss, with the shallow channels of water beneath them. They act as supports, valves, reservoirs, pumps, and anchors. Some appear dead, but if pressed, a faint pulse may move through the bark, slower than a heart, steady as a glacier’s patience.

The membrane is the primary native of the country. It is the country’s skin, and most of the other inhabitants are either symbionts upon it, organs of it, or creatures that have learned to live inside its moods.

## Skein-Grazers

On the broader plains and the domes of peat move the skein-grazers: herds of thin-limbed, cold-adapted animals with pale frayed coats and long, delicate legs. They are not deer in the familiar sense. Their bodies are built like woven frameworks: broad ribcages, hollow-feeling bones, joints that seem slightly too numerous. Their hooves are wide and porous, lined with fine fibers that trap a cushion of air or water between hoof and ground.

A skein-grazer can stand in shallow water without breaking the surface. Its weight is distributed through strange pads and tension-sensitive hooves, so the trembling skin of a pond supports it rather than swallows it. When a herd crosses a saturated plain, the animals seem to drift over the ground, their legs pressing without puncturing, their bodies moving with the slow deliberation of large, patient machines.

Their coats are matted with green fibers. Lichen grows in the hollows of their backs. Tiny mosses take root in the coarse hair around their shoulders. Pale seed-filaments hang from their bellies. If one stands still for too long, it becomes difficult to tell where the animal ends and the land begins. A motionless skein-grazer can look like a mound, a ridge of peat, a low dome exhaling mist.

They do not graze by destroying. Their tongues are broad and rasping, but they crop only the outer edges of the membrane, removing aged or saturated growth and stimulating newer tissue to spread. Their saliva is rich in chemical compounds that cause the mat to heal behind them. When they urinate into the runnels, the water darkens with mineral salts and spores. When they defecate, they scatter pale lichen fragments, fungal packets, and root seeds. A skein-grazer moving slowly across a basin may be leaving behind a trail of the next century.

Their horns are not simple antlers. They are pale, mineralized branches, porous and resonant, often grown in shapes that echo the ridges and fissures of the surrounding plateau. The animals use them to strike stones, to probe frozen plant creases, to open small water channels, and perhaps to communicate by vibration. A herd can make a low, clicking chorus as it moves, each animal tapping the ground or touching stone with a soft, deliberate knock.

Skein-grazers migrate in broad, wavering routes. They do not follow obvious valleys or open paths; they follow hidden gradients of warmth, vapor, mineral taste, and pressure. Their migrations correspond to thermal vents, erosion scars, porous ridges, and places where the air is slightly less thin. They can remain in a single region for months, grazing in slow circuits, then vanish as though the land has finished speaking to them.

When they stand still, their shadows may point elsewhere. This is not a trick of light alone. In this country, shadows can lag or lead, and a herd may respond not to what is present but to what its shadows remember. A skein-grazer can stop mid-step because its shadow has touched a stone that has not yet appeared. Another may lie down because its reflection, in a black basin, has already moved away.

## Undermaters

Beneath the living fabric, in tunnels that are part root, part soil, part intestine, live the undermaters.

They are small, pale, segmented burrowers. Some are only a finger’s length; others are longer, heavier, and almost too soft-looking to be called grubs. Their bodies are translucent enough to reveal green cords inside, dark mineral grains caught in their guts, or pale filaments of symbiotic fungi growing along their segments. Many have too many eyes: tiny wet points arranged in circles or clusters. A few have none at all, sensing instead through broad, flattened antennae and pressure-sensitive rings around their mouths.

The undermaters are the maintenance life of the plateau. They chew through dead rootwood, mineral crusts, ice-crystal fibers, old peat, fungal cords, and anything partially decayed that the membrane has abandoned. They excrete a white binding paste that reinforces the ground and closes small tears. Their tunnels become capillaries for water. Their jaws create the faint grinding sound that, when multiplied across millions of bodies, gives the region its mechanical texture.

Some undermaters generate heat. Their larvae feed inside frozen wood or saturated stems, slowly raising the temperature around their burrows until ice cracks and drips. The adult forms emerge with steaming shells and pale, iron-colored wing cases, crawling briefly over the mat before falling into hidden channels again.

Larger forms exist as well: bellworms, or piston-grubs. These are rarely seen. They live in saturated cavities deep beneath peat and root lattices, bodies swollen and muscular, rings of pale tissue contracting in slow rhythm. Each contraction drives water upward through porous stone and hollow root channels. From the surface, the result is a distant hammering, a rhythmic press, a sound that seems too regular to be animal and too wet to be stone. This is the plateau pumping its own fluids.

The undermaters are not safe from the world above. Birds pull them from hollow stems. Skein-grazers crush them in their wide hooves, then spread their remains in dung. Fish eat those that fall through thin water layers. Striders snatch pale bodies from crevices in the peat. Yet their numbers are so vast that predation only sharpens their cycles. They rise and collapse in waves, responding to moisture, temperature, spore density, and sounds arriving before their causes.

Sometimes an entire patch of ground will begin to tremble softly because beneath it a thousand undermaters are moving at once, following the memory of a vibration from a place they have not yet reached.

## Glass-Skimmers

On the shallow waters and wet plant skins move the glass-skimmers.

They are long-legged insects, their bodies almost invisible until they cluster. Their legs spread weight across the surface tension in delicate triangles. When they land, they leave tiny rings. When they move, the rings multiply. When they swarm, the surface of a black pond becomes a shifting geometry of circles, arcs, and lattice-like patterns.

The rings are not waste. They are signals. A glass-skimmer creates surface waves that other skimmers interpret as food, danger, temperature, or chemical richness. A swarm can become a temporary organism, each individual responding to pressure and light, forming shapes too orderly for wind. From a distance, a glass-skimmer swarm may look like a stain moving across a leaf, or like dust rising from a dry ridge though the ridge is wet.

They feed on microbial films, spore clouds, floating plant oils, and the chemical residue left by skein-grazers. Their legs are covered in fine hairs that trap droplets, and those droplets roll into specialized sacs where minerals are concentrated and ingested. Their bodies can carry a faint electrical charge. When they lift from the water, the surface trembles afterward, as though the liquid has been briefly persuaded to remember being disturbed.

In cold mornings, glass-skimmers walk on water films that are beginning to stiffen but have not frozen. The surface clicks beneath their legs. If the water becomes too rigid, they lay eggs into the tension itself, sealing them inside tiny bubbles caught between plant fibers and ice. The larvae hatch into the creases of matting, feeding on frost-crystals and the microscopic green films growing there.

They are essential to the water-membrane relationship. They open pores in stagnant surfaces. They carry spores between black ponds. Their excretions feed pale lichens. Their swarms warn other creatures of pressure changes: when the skimmers rise all at once and form a long, shimmering line across the air, something beneath the ground is shifting, and the more cautious residents know to move.

## Mirrorfish

In the black basins, flooded channels, and pale runnels live the mirrorfish.

They are small, blind, and silver. Their bodies are covered in reflective cells, not to be seen, but to gather and redirect faint light in a country where sunlight is often absent or wrong. Their eyes are gone, or only exist as pale stones beneath the skin. In their place are lateral lines branching over their entire bodies, sensitive to pressure, mineral taste, temperature, and the faint electrical signatures of living tissue.

A school of mirrorfish is not a collection of separate animals. It is a distributed sense organ. Each fish samples water, each turn communicates, and together the school becomes a single fluid awareness moving through the flooded channels beneath the mat.

The channels often seem to have no openings. Water runs through porous stone, root tubes, hollow stems, and saturated seams in the peat, but there may be no visible way in or out. The mirrorfish do not mind. They pass through capillaries of liquid, squeeze into hollow reeds, and travel where ordinary fish would be trapped. In deep black ponds, their scales can reveal themselves all at once as a sudden sheet of moving silver, then vanish into the dark below.

They feed on undermaters, glass-skimmer larvae, and fallen spore packets. They also help regulate the chemistry of the basins. A black pond full of stagnant reflection may be the sign of missing mirrorfish. Their movement keeps the water lightly oxygenated, stirs mineral layers, and prevents certain acidic blooms from dominating.

Their spawning is strange and tied to the region’s unreliable light. They gather in pools where the water reflects clouds that are not overhead, as though the fish respond not to the sky but to its memory. They release eggs onto floating leaves or into the hollows of water-filled stems. The eggs attach to tiny ice crystals, glowing faintly, and hatch when the membrane around them pulses with warmth. The fry emerge already knowing how to swim through channels that do not appear to exist.

Some species live part of their lives inside plant tissues. They enter hollow stalks when the water rises, then leave when the stalk begins to flood too completely, slipping back into underground runnels. In this way they are not merely aquatic animals but circulating fluids within the land’s anatomy.

## Fog-Lines

Above the matting and the low steam basins fly the fog-lines.

They are birds, but they do not look entirely like familiar birds. Their wings are broad and heavy, stiff in the dim light. Their feathers are coated with spore-derived compounds that make them rigid, faintly reflective, and difficult to see against the gray-green air. They fly in noisy, low-slung lines, their calls distorted by humidity and distance until the sounds seem to come from below or from inside the ground.

Fog-lines feed on spores, insects, tiny fish, and chemical foam collected from seepage stones. Their throats contain filtering membranes that prevent acidic fog from damaging their lungs. They breathe in bursts, then pause, then resume, as if the air itself were not entirely dependable.

They nest in vertical root chimneys, steam basins, and hollows where undermaters have softened the ground with warmth. Their eggs are laid in wet peat that must be heated by fungal activity, not by the parent bird. A fog-line may sit near its nest for hours, then leave, returning when the temperature rises from below.

The birds are among the most sensitive residents to the plateau’s spatial unreliability. They can fly toward a feature not yet visible, or land on a stone that is not present, their feet sinking briefly into fog before the ground settles beneath them or before they lift again with irritated cries. A flock may pass through a horizontal tree trunk as though the wood were wet air, scattering no branches, leaving no wound. This is not illusion. It is the same thinness that allows the mat to tear and heal too quickly, only expressed in animals whose bodies have learned not to resist the country’s errors.

Fog-lines are also stitchers. Their droppings carry fibers and binding agents. They carry small pieces of lichen and rootwood in their beaks and feet, depositing them in tears of the membrane. Where a flock roosts, new growth may appear along bare stone within days.

They are not safe. Their calls may reach a predator before the bird itself does. Their reflections may be taken by striders. Their eggs may be eaten by heat-larvae. Yet they are abundant in a scattered way, appearing as noisy lines in low fog, vanishing into cloud below the observer’s eye, then reappearing as tiny marks against the pale ceiling of the sky.

## Image-Striders

Beside the black ponds and trembling basins stand the image-striders.

They are thin, long-necked, and pale, with bodies built like bundles of reeds. Their legs are tall and jointed in several places, allowing them to stand in shallow water or across a mat without disturbing it. Their bills are narrow, hollow, and mineralized, like shards of white stone grown from bone. They do not seem to hunt the way ordinary predators hunt.

An image-strider may stand for hours beside a pool, perfectly still, its neck angled toward the water. It is not watching for fish. It is watching for mismatch: a reflection that does not belong, a shadow pointing away from the object casting it, a ripple where no wind moved the surface, a cloud in the pond that is not overhead.

When it strikes, it strikes the reflection.

Sometimes the real creature in the pond shivers as though touched. Sometimes a skein-grazer on the far shore loses a patch of shadow for a few seconds. Sometimes the water cracks with a sound like glass, and the strider withdraws its bill carrying nothing visible but a faint, cold light inside its throat. What it has consumed is not entirely flesh. It is error. It is the part of a thing that has become too distant from its own image.

This does not mean the striders are purely mystical. They also eat insects, larvae, small fish, and the pale flesh of injured undermaters. But their hunting behavior seems shaped as much by correction as by hunger. In a place where water may rise in columns, shadows may move independently, and objects may briefly forget where they are, predators have evolved to attack not just bodies but inconsistencies.

Their bodies are slow and strange. If touched, a strider does not flutter or leap away. Its neck bends without visible muscle. Its skin may feel like wet bark. At night, faint green light moves through the veins beneath its feathers—or what pass for feathers. Their eggs are laid in still water and must be incubated by reflection, not by body heat. If the water surface is disturbed too often, the eggs die. Therefore striders choose only pools that are dark enough, calm enough, and strange enough to hold an image longer than expected.

## Echo-Hounds

The echo-hounds are pale, fox-like creatures with narrow bodies, long legs, and enormous cup-shaped ears that can fold inward against fog or rain. Their snouts are elongated, and their nostrils are lined with mineral-rich tissue that can detect chemical changes in the air. But smell is secondary. They hunt by hearing.

They chase sounds.

Not always the source of the sound. In this country, a call may arrive before the animal making it, or after the animal has left, or from a direction where no creature exists. The echo-hound runs toward that misplaced sound, paws spreading across saturated ground, body low, eyes half-closed. When it catches up, it often catches nothing. But then it bites into the air anyway.

Sometimes, after the snap of its jaws, the sound stops.

A far-off bird call may vanish. A grinding stone may go silent. A herd’s clicking chorus may lose one voice, not because an animal died, but because the echo-hound has eaten the sound that carried it too early or too late. Whether this is literal predation on temporal error or a biological ability to suppress the source through resonance is unclear. The echo-hounds themselves seem not to know. They are satisfied, or exhausted, or confused by such successes.

They are solitary, though pairs may form when they find each other near a warm seep. Their dens are not built but chosen: porous ridges, hollow stones, dry-looking slopes that breathe slightly warm air from below. Their pups are born nearly blind, with ears too large for their bodies. They learn to follow vibrations in the mat, then learn to listen for sounds that arrive before the creatures producing them. By maturity, they can distinguish a real footprint from an echo of one that may or may not have happened.

Echo-hounds are feared by skein-grazers because they may drive herds into or out of repeated ridges simply by stealing a sound. A grazing herd that has heard a call too early may move toward a basin that has not yet formed. If the echo-hound then captures that call, the basin may fail to fill for days, and the herd will stand in confusion over dry moss that is beginning to smell of frost.

## Ridge-Gazers

At the low domes and high margins, where the air feels metallic and the plant-mat tightens into silver-green spirals, live the ridge-gazers.

They are sparse, slow, and built like stones covered in wire. Their fleece is tough and pale, containing fibers that reflect fog and cold. Their heads are broad, their horns flattened and ridged like cooled slag. Their hooves are flat and resonant; when they step on stone, they tap to test whether it is solid, hollow, or about to move.

Ridge-gazers feed on the high-altitude membrane: tight spiral mosses, mineral lichens, pale crusts, and the hard growths that form on ridges where moisture condenses and freezes before it can fully wet the ground. Their saliva is rich in acids, softening the mineral layers they eat and leaving behind a dark, glassy residue on stones.

They store vapor in nasal sacs. When they exhale in the coldest hours, tiny ice crystals can form and fall upward, caught in the strange vertical currents above the ridges. Their breath may smell of iron and green growth. Their bodies generate little heat; instead, they survive by becoming nearly inert, standing for hours in the wind while frost blooms in the hollows of their coats.

Their herds are small. A ridge-gazer may stand apart from its herd for days, mistaken for a pale boulder, until the wind shifts and it lifts its broad head. Their migration is less tied to food than to pressure. They move when the air feels too heavy or too thin, climbing higher when the sky lowers and descending when the plateau becomes saturated with fog.

They know the rolling stones. Long before a stone begins its centuries-long pause, ridge-gazers move out of its path in a pattern so consistent that it seems learned across generations. They do not panic. They simply step aside, touch their horns to nearby stones in warning, and wait until the grinding crash has passed through the mat.

## Stem-Hearts

Not all inhabitants walk or swim. Some live inside other living things.

Throughout the horizontal trees, the floating plants, and the pale reed stems, there are stem-hearts: fungal organisms that grow inside living tissue without killing it. They occupy the space between root and bark, between plant membrane and fluid channel. When the dim light fades, these internal organisms may glow faintly, not brightly, but enough to reveal a slow chemical pulse moving through the veins of the landscape.

A stem-heart does not behave like a parasite. It is more like a resident organ. It regulates the rhythm of the plant it inhabits, opening and closing fluid channels, producing heat in cold periods, absorbing mineral water, and releasing spores when conditions change. Its presence can be detected only by touch or by the slow, delayed pulse of a stem responding to pressure somewhere far away.

Undermaters feed on old stem-hearts. Birds pluck the fungal packets from wet bark. Fish eat the tiny spore-larvae that fall into the water. When a stem-heart dies, the plant it inhabited may collapse into memory, becoming part of the white fiber beneath the mat.

In this way, the plateau is inhabited not only by animals but by internal tenants, quiet colonies, and slow organisms that blur the distinction between individual and host.

## The Ecology as a Single Operation

The abundance here is not lush in the warm, green sense. There are no thickets, no tall canopies, no obvious forests, no easy trails, no obvious herds gathered in obvious places. The animals are scattered, hidden, camouflaged, and distributed across a large wet country. But their total number is enormous. The land hums with them.

A distant herd may be standing motionless for hours, appearing as a line of peat. A black pond may seem empty until a school of mirrorfish turns all at once beneath the surface. A swarm of glass-skimmers may look like a stain on a leaf, then lift into a shimmering cloud. A fog-line flock may be invisible until the calls of the birds reveal that the sky itself is occupied. An image-strider may stand beside a basin, looking like a broken reed, until it strikes something that was not quite there.

This is an ecology built around functions more than forms. The skein-grazers spread and prune. The undermaters burrow, repair, pump, and heat. The glass-skimmers manage the surface films. The mirrorfish regulate water and carry nutrients through hidden channels. The fog-lines stitch, pollinate, alert, and roost. The striders and echo-hounds remove misplaced shadows, sounds, reflections, and echoes. The ridge-gazers stabilize the high margins. The stem-hearts regulate the internal rhythms of the mat.

Nothing is peaceful in the ordinary sense. There is predation, decay, competition, migration, chemical warfare, and cold pressure everywhere. Some ponds are acidic and lethal, yet lined with creatures that feed on corrosion. Some plants grow faster when damaged. Some insects live inside warm tunnels of ice-cracked wood. Some birds carry fibers that cause the membrane to fuse where it has been wounded. The country is working constantly, and its inhabitants are both the workers and the machines.

If humans pass through, they do not truly live there. They are disturbances. A person might leave a footprint, and the footprint might fail to fill for hours, or fill too soon, or become ringed with a pale mineral crust that should not form so fast. A person’s shadow might remain after the person has moved. A spoken word might echo in the wrong direction and attract an echo-hound before the speaker has finished turning around. But there is no settlement because the place does not recognize the logic of settlement. It has its own shelters, pumps, roads, and memories. A person without those is not resident.

To live in such a country is to become an organ of its uncertainty. The animals here are not merely adapted to cold, wetness, and sparse food. They are adapted to a landscape that is worn almost to the edge of meaning, where cause and effect have loosened, where water may pool before its basin forms, where a reflection may need to be hunted, and where a sound may arrive from a valley that is not yet visible.

What lives there, then, is not a population. It is a physiology.

The land breathes through fog-lines and steam. It pumps through undermaters and bellworms. It grazes and seeds itself through the skein-grazers. It keeps its skin alive through the membrane. It circulates minerals through mirrorfish and glass-skimmers. It corrects its errors through striders and echo-hounds. It stabilizes its highest bones through ridge-gazers and stem-hearts.

And beneath it all, in the dark damp beneath the mat, something still turns, still repairs, still listens, still works. The country is old enough to be tired, wet enough to be patient, and thin enough to let life move through places where life should not move at all.
