No one lives there, and almost everything does.

There is no person in the Saltloom. There is no face that recognizes its own reflection in a black basalt pool, because there are no pools. There is no hand that shapes a stone for a purpose, because no hand would find enough purchase, no need, no idea of a tool. There is no voice raised above the thin warm air, because the air carries almost nothing, and nothing in this dry chemistry has learned to shout. If you stand at the edge of the bowl and ask who lives in the town, the honest answer is: no one. The town is alive around them, under them, over them, through them, but there is no ruling consciousness, no architect, no chief of the warm wells, no keeper of the salt paths.

And yet something lives there.

It lives in the packed salt streets. It lives in the resin seams that take generations to thicken. It lives in the soft shift of grubs under a fungal shelf, the slow tilt of fur-crowned heads toward exhaled gas, the tiny polished trails of grazers across a mineral mat, the careful movement of crabs into the shadow of a hand. It lives in the whole arrangement of bodies, secretions, deaths, shells, oils, hyphae, and warm breath until the place resembles a city, not because the city was planned, but because every creature, in being what it is, has made every other creature’s life possible.

What lives in the Saltloom is a quiet commonwealth of organisms too interwoven to be separated without violence. They do not know they are inhabitants. They know only what it is to be warm enough, dry enough, tight enough, sheltered enough, rich enough in oil or vapor or mineral taste to continue. They do not build. They are the building. They do not maintain the city. They are the maintenance. If an outside eye insists on naming them, it will have to invent names that belong to no one, and then let those names blur together.

The amberbacks are among the first to betray the illusion of empty ground.

They are small, broad-shelled, with ridges carved across their backs like channels in a dry riverbed, except that here there has never been a river to carve anything. They move with a patient, rolling gait, their soft velvet wings folded beneath shells the color of salt-crust and old bone. Their legs end in pads that seem almost blind until you notice how they press, pause, withdraw, and then settle more fully where heat rises. They taste the ground with their feet. Not taste as a wet mouth might taste: rather, they read the mineral breath of the salt, the oil-sweetness of resin, the faint sulfur warmth of a vent below. They are attracted to cracks that exhale slowly, to ledges that hold heat, to places where a shelf fungus has begun to harden into a lip or a tube-builder has left a ringed pillar.

An amberback carries beads of pale resin in the ridges along its back. It does not know it is carrying them for anything. The resin comes from brushing too long against a wax-leaf shrub, from sitting where an oil gland has wept under the sun, from moving through a chamber where a living membrane has secreted a film onto its shell. As it walks, a bead may loosen. It may lodge in a crack, a joint, a seam between salt terraces. If the weather is warm, the bead softens slightly and hardens again into a bridge. If many amberbacks have passed that way for seasons enough, the cracks fill with small supports. A wall gains a spine. A floor gains a skin. A doorway that might have collapsed holds because a beetle’s body, for no reason any creature would understand, made the same route thousands of times.

The amberbacks breed in the middle levels, where the air is neither too hot nor too still. They press into the soft undersides of salt shelves and lay eggs that look like tiny grains of polished mineral. The eggs are not wet; they are sealed with resin, as though the mother’s body had remembered a dryness older than rain. In the warm periods, the eggs absorb vapor and soften just enough for the young to emerge, but they do not drink. They taste heat. They crawl through narrow channels packed with metallic grains and eat the thin film of oil that condenses on chamber walls. If a chamber grows too cool, the larvae cluster and become a pale mound of shivering bodies. If the vent breathes warm and wetness is absent, they separate and move toward the scent of resin, toward the soft places, toward the edges of fungal shelves where decay is beginning to become paste.

The tube-wrights do not appear as animals unless one knows where to look.

They are pale, ringed, worm-things, narrower than a finger, dwelling in vertical fissures or in the joints between basalt columns. Each secretes a tube of calcified paste, a thin cylinder of calcium and hardened organic residue. One lives inside it. Another rests against it. A third, after the first dies, enlarges the tube by adding a ring of its own making. This is not construction in any conscious sense. The tubes begin where the air is right. They thicken where heat makes the secretions flow just enough to set. They arch where a creature has passed repeatedly through the same warm cleft. Over generations, the arches become doorframes. The tubes become columns. The column becomes a pillar. The pillar becomes the support of a shelf, the shelf becomes the roof of a chamber, the chamber becomes the nursery of another species.

An outside observer may look at a row of pale, bone-white cylinders holding up a roof and see architecture. What actually lives there is a lineage of bodies that have died into a shape and a lineage of descendants who continue that shape because it suits them. The first tube-builder has no intention. The tenth has no memory of the first. The hundredth is simply warm and safe. The city grows because safety leaves fossils, and fossils become homes, and homes become walls.

The tube-wrights are not alone. They share the pillars with shelf-fungi, with mite-cleaners, with pale grubs that feed on dead hyphae and excrete a paste that binds loose salt into terraces. The fungi themselves are neither plants nor animals, though the Saltloom does not care for such distinctions. They grow in pale overlapping plates, each one stiff as parchment, each one furred with spores on the underside, each one threaded through with hyphae so fine they can be mistaken for white filaments in old bone. A shelf may begin as a stain on basalt and end as a staircase, because generations of creatures have eaten its lower edge, excreted, died, and been layered over by new growth.

The grubs are the most unsettling residents if you touch one.

They lie in the hollow centers of fungal shelves in dense, motionless packets. If you lift the shelf, you might think it empty. A moment later, the whole mass of white bodies shifts. Not like water. Like dried seeds in a cup when the cup is moved. They are blind, soft, segmented, almost translucent, and they possess tiny jaws that scrape dead skin from the walls of a chamber. They consume nothing fast. They take the dried husk of a shed creature, the fallen scale of a warm-tilter, the crumb of an old resin seal, the powder of a beetle wing worn to nothing, and they turn it into paste. Their waste is not waste. It is mortar. It is wall. It is floor. It is the beginning of the next generation’s room.

When the air grows too dry for a grub’s outer skin, it may move toward the faint breath of a well, not because wells give water—they give warmth and mineral vapor—but because the vapor carries a softness that helps it retain pliability. The wells are lined with resin and chitin, sunken into the salt crust, opening onto fissures that exhale slow warm gas. Around the mouths of these wells, the architecture is delicate: rings of paste, struts of tube-builder calcium, curtains of shed skin weighted with resin beads to keep them from drying too quickly. The well itself may be mistaken for a chimney, a mouth, an old bone socket. It breathes. The grubs feed. The shelf grows. The city breathes.

The oil-throat moths live above the dense lower chambers.

They are not moths in the ordinary sense. They have no need of nectar, no flower to open with dew, no pollen to moisten and carry. Their wings are broad, translucent sheets of membrane, pale as old glass or thin wax, marked with dark veins like cracks in cooled resin. Their bodies are slender, dusted with fine filaments that collect salt crystals when they rest on ledges. Their mouths are strange, long, and jointed: not wet siphons but dry, articulated struts that can stand on a leaf and reach into a waxy gland. They feed by pressing these struts against the underside of a wax-leaf shrub and drawing viscous oil from the plant’s resin canals. The oil smells faintly of burnt sugar, sulfur, and citrus heat. It is heavy, amber, sweet-smelling, and it never wets the moth’s legs.

At midday, they rest folded against the uppermost leaves, their wing membranes nearly invisible against the bright salt light. When the sun shifts and shadows shorten, they tremble. Not from cold. From warmth. They have been waiting for the moment when the vent gas rises more strongly through the plant’s stems, when the leaves become oil-rich enough to yield. They lift from the canopy one by one, almost without sound. A wingbeat in the Saltloom is barely a whisper, a brush of dry membrane against dry air. Under moonlight, they move through the upper levels like small pale ships passing through a forest of coppery ferns.

They do not mourn their dead. They do not notice them. They die in the upper membranes, in the leaf cups, on the soft roofs of fungal terraces. Their husks dry quickly. They are taken apart by cleaners. Their wing fragments are folded into chamber walls. Their mouth-struts become tiny braces in the architecture, because something else found them useful. The city keeps everything, not because it is sentimental, but because in a place without rot, without wet decay, without floods to sweep waste away, a fragment that does not dissolve becomes part of the future floor.

The fur-crowned warm-tilters are larger, and they are the closest thing the Saltloom has to the idea of a creature sitting in a doorway.

They are not lizards, though their broad bodies and slow limbs may remind one of lizards. They are not mammals, though their heads are crowned with dense, mineral-tinted filaments that resemble fur. They move with a patience that would seem absurd in a wet world, as though they have never once considered running. Their legs are short, thick-jointed, and their feet are wide, furred underneath with soft, tough pads that grip pumice without slipping and without grinding. They rest on warm ledges and tilt their broad heads toward the vent columns. The fur on their crowns stands up slightly when vapor passes through it. Their nostril ridges, if they are nostrils at all, flare without drawing wet breath. They absorb warmth, scent, and oil through skin, through filament, through the fine scales along their flanks.

They groom themselves by rubbing salt against their bodies until the dead outer layer lifts off in long, dry sheets. The sheets are not waste. They become curtains, walls, roof membranes. If a warm-tilter sheds near a sheltered crack, its skin may be used by tube-wrights as a lining for a new chamber. If the crack is near a fungal shelf, the skin may be folded into paste and hardened by cleaners. If it is left on the open terrace, small crabs will scuttle over it and take pieces to their hatchings, where the dry skin becomes a cup-shaped nest. The warm-tilter is not a builder, but the city is built out of the fact that it exists.

Their eyes are not like eyes. They are broad, heat-sensitive plates along the skull, dark and matte, able to distinguish the warmth of a living body from the warmth of stone, the warmth of resin from the warmth of sun. They see with temperature. Their world is not bright and dark so much as warm, not warm, too warm, safe warm, dangerous warm, empty warm, occupied warm. They do not look for shapes so much as for places where bodies have made patterns in the heat. A wall is not merely a wall. It is the accumulated temperature of all the creatures who have lived beside it.

The husk-crabs are tiny, and they are the city’s small hands.

They are not crabs, because there is no shore, no tide, no wet sand to crawl from. But they scuttle with a similar urgency, low and sideways, with legs too fine to seem strong. Their shells are borrowed. They grow inside seed husks, split salt fruits, the dried cases of old eggs, the empty chambers left by tube-wrights. They line these shells with resin, glue grains of salt into them, decorate them with fragments of shed skin and fungal powder until each one resembles a small mineral bloom. They move in sudden bursts and then freeze. Their antennae are dry, feathery, sensitive to warmth and chemical residue. They do not carry food so much as they carry the world: a crumb of moss, a bead of oil, a shard of resin, a dead mite, a fragment of beetle shell.

They polish the lower floors with their bellies. They do not intend to. But where they travel, salt becomes smooth as bone. Their paths appear over seasons as lines of pale shine. Sometimes the paths connect two well-mouths. Sometimes they run under a warm-tilter’s resting ledge. Sometimes they spiral around a salt column, because the column was once a dead beetle, then a fungal host, then a tube-builder’s pillar, then a wall, and now it is a landmark that every husk-crab learns in its own chemical way. A crab’s memory is not a story. It is a scent, a heat, a grain pattern, a pressure, a route.

The six-legged grazers fill the open terraces.

They are pale, translucent, and not beautiful until you see them feed. Their backs are soft, rounded domes that rise slightly when they breathe, not lungs, but internal pockets exchanging mineral vapor. They have broad, blunt mouthparts, more like scrapers than jaws, and they nibble the iron-green mosses, the pewter-green crusts, the coppery fronds of salt succulents, and the waxy undersides of young fern leaves. They leave no wet stain. They leave a dry trail of powdered salt behind them, as though they had eaten something too brittle. Their bodies are adapted to the crackling of metal-like leaves, to the resistance of dry stems, to a diet of chemistry instead of juice.

They are the first to reveal the quiet motion of the place. Stand still on a salt terrace and you may think it bare. Then a grain shifts. Then another. Then an entire patch moves, not all at once but in waves, a ripple of pale backs and dry mouths. If a warm-tilter steps nearby, they do not scatter. They fold. They flatten against the salt, becoming texture, becoming crust, becoming shadow. If a fungus shelf trembles, they freeze with their mouthparts still pressed to the dry moss. If a predator approaches, they do not flee quickly; their bodies are not made for quickness. They move into narrow passages where their broad backs cannot fit, or they enter the soft layers of dead shed skin and press themselves flat until the outer pattern of the wall closes over them.

The cleaners follow the grazers.

They are smaller, almost invisible at first, pale mites with chelate forelegs and bodies hardened by dust until they look like grains that have learned to scuttle. They live in the spaces between architecture and biology: in the folds of shed skin, in the pores of salt crust, in the cracks around fungal shelves, in the seams of resin. They eat dust. They eat dead skin. They eat broken resin. They eat the husks of moth cocoons. They eat the remains of amberback larvae that never reached adulthood. They consume the thin film that forms on warm stones, the mineral residue left by vent vapor, the powder that grazers scrape from the ground. They are everywhere, and they are almost nothing.

Without them, the Saltloom would still be dense, but not urban. The shed skins would pile and block passages. The resin would harden into useless lumps. The dead would become obstacles. The cleaners turn obstacles into foundations. They pack waste into crevices. They leave excrement that becomes a thin paste. The paste dries, becomes wall. Wall becomes shelter. Shelter becomes habitat. Habitat becomes more waste. The cycle is so continuous that a visitor cannot tell where digestion ends and construction begins.

The predators are the hardest to notice.

They do not have the soft, rounded look of the common animals. Their bodies are flattened, wide-backed, lined with membranes that absorb vibration and sound. Those membranes are not wet. They are supple with a resinous oil that never beads or shines. The predator’s limbs are broad and jointed in a way that allows it to close slowly, not snap. Its head is wide, almost plate-like, with heat-pits along the jawline and a mouth that opens with a jointed hinge rather than a tearing yawn. It has no claws that would scratch loudly against salt. Its underbelly is padded with dry, tough folds that grip without scraping.

It hunts by listening with its body.

It presses its flattened head to a fungal wall and feels the tremor of grubs inside. It stands over a salt floor and notices the faint difference in warmth where a burrower has passed beneath. It moves through a passage not by sight alone but by the changing density of air, by the chemical breath of sheltered life, by the tiny vibrations of bodies that have no need to flee because they have never learned speed.

When it strikes, there is no roar. The prey is taken in a slow closing of jointed traps, a grip that does not thrash, a bite sealed with resinous enzymes that end trembling without disturbing the chamber. The predator eats in place, or drags its kill to a cooler hollow. Its waste is gathered by cleaners. Its discarded bones are taken by small builders. Its shed membrane fragments become roof panels. Its eggs are hidden in walls lined with paste. The predator is part of the architecture in a way that the other creatures may not notice, because its own death will one day be a wall, a column, a doorframe, a nursery floor.

They live in a world without speech as other worlds understand it.

Their communication is a slow language of heat, pressure, scent, and contact. Two amberbacks touching antennae may exchange a chemical record: chamber too cool, salt too loose, oil gland blocked, vent breathing strong, predator’s residue present. A warm-tilter rubbing its broad head against a wall may mark it with a scent that says nothing in words, but much in condition: this is safe, this is warm, this is mine in the way that any body belongs to a place, this is occupied, this is shared. A fungus shelf disturbed by a grub colony may release a faint mineral smell that tells nearby creatures: movement here, new paste here, wall softening here, do not press too heavily, or wait, or use the other opening.

There are no signs, no written marks, no maps, no fires, no devices. Yet the passages are clear because footfall polishes them. The bridges are strong because cordage is replaced every season by new fungal growth. The chambers are warm because the buildings lean toward the breath of the wells. The city’s organization is not imposed; it is accumulated. Each creature follows its needs, and those needs happen to reinforce the needs of others. The result looks intentional, but intention is not required. The path does not need to be planned. It only needs to be convenient. The wall does not need to be built. It only needs to resist collapse. The roof does not need to be made. It only needs to keep away the sun when the body beneath it requires shade.

What lives there, then, is not one thing, but a habit repeated by many bodies.

In the lower levels, where the light does not reach except as a dull warmth, the inhabitants are pale and sealed. Eggs rest in resin-lined chambers. Larvae move through channels packed with metallic grain and dry spore paste. Fungi digest the dead slowly, turning them into a material that can be used again. The air is close but not foul, because everything is consumed and re-formed. There are no smells of rot, only the scent of mineral heat, old oil, dry paste, and faint sulfur. The lower dwellers do not fear darkness. Darkness is ordinary. Light is occasional, and only a few know how to use it.

In the middle levels, the city is most itself. Here the passages widen slightly, the arches curve, the bridges span narrow fissures, and the paths are polished by many small bodies. Here the warm-tilters rest on ledges. Here the amberbacks carry their beads. Here the tube-wrights reinforce a crack that may become a column in a hundred years. Here the fungi and animals trade substances back and forth: fungus gives structure, animals give paste, animals give heat, fungi give shelter, dead bodies give room, living bodies give maintenance. No resident owns a room. A chamber may contain grubs one season, beetle eggs the next, a dead warm-tilter’s skin as a wall, and a living membrane roof that breathes.

In the upper levels, the inhabitants are translucent, oily, light-hungry. The wax-leaf shrubs spread broad canopies that catch the sun. The moths rest beneath them. The young ferns crackle as they expand, their fronds dry and metallic. Small grazers nibble the salt succulents on open terraces. The warm-tilters tilt toward the vents. The husk-crabs carry fragments upward, because the higher air is better for something, though no one can say exactly what. At the top, the city seems to be made of leaves and shells and membranes and pale bodies, a garden of mineral and dry life, all of it breathing slowly through the vents below.

If you look for a single animal, you will find one.

You will find an amberback, a tube-builder, a grub, a moth, a warm-tilter, a husk-crab, a grazer, a cleaner, a predator. You will find them living, breeding, dying, shedding, feeding, resting, fleeing very little, because fleeing is not their way.

But if you look for the town’s true inhabitant, you will not find it at first.

It is in the way a wall softens under many feet. It is in the way a crack seals itself with resin beads. It is in the way a shed skin becomes a roof. It is in the way a predator’s bones become a doorframe. It is in the way the fungal shelves take the weight of the chambers above them because the chambers above them were once made of the same shelf. It is in the way the air moves through hidden passages, warming what must be warmed and drying what must be dried. It is in the way the Saltloom breathes through its vents like a sleeping animal whose ribs have become buildings.

The whole bowl is one resident.

Not one soul. Not one mind. Not one species. But a living pattern of bodies, habits, and dry chemistry so tightly arranged that the difference between animal, fungus, plant, waste, and architecture becomes a useless distinction. The creatures are the walls. The walls are their descendants. The paths are their movements made solid. The chambers are their shelters turned into shells. The wells are their breath. The vents are their lungs. The salt is their bones. The resin is their blood, though not liquid enough to wet a stone. The heat is their weather. The quiet is their conversation.

They do not know they are an urban form. They know only that one place is warm, another too hot, another too cold, another too open, another too blocked. They know only that their young need narrow walls, that their dead become floors, that shed skins dry into curtains, that oil glands fill the upper leaves, that fungal paste strengthens the salt. They live as any animal lives: toward comfort, away from danger, toward reproduction, into whatever place makes the body persist. And because they have persisted for so long, their comfort has grown into geometry. Their persistence has grown into streets. Their deaths have grown into rooms. Their habits have grown into civilization, if one dares use a word that belongs elsewhere.

What lives in the Saltloom, therefore, is not a who, but a what that behaves like a who when seen from outside. It is a dry republic of soft bodies. It is a city made of living animals and their remnants. It is a town of beetles that carry resin without knowing why, of worms that build pillars without knowing they are pillars, of moths that drink oil and leave their husks as shingles, of lizards that shed their skins into walls, of grubs that eat the dead and turn them into floors, of crabs that polish streets with their bellies, of predators that become part of the architecture they hunt.

It is, above all, a community without a ruler and without an idea of community, and perhaps that is the most astonishing thing about it. No one decided that the arch should hold. No one noticed that the chamber was too cold. No one named the well. No one repaired the bridge. The bridge repaired itself because cordage grew, because warm gas rose, because footfall compacted salt, because dead things hardened, because living things preferred the shape of what was already useful. The city is not maintained by labor. It is maintained by existence.

The young land has not softened. The basalt still cuts. The salt terraces still break at sharp right angles. The glass shards are still flakes and plates, not dust. The land is new enough that some of the oldest moss mats may remember when a particular crack was not there. When the crack appeared, it did not frighten the inhabitants as a human might fear an earthquake. It changed their world by degrees. The vent gas rose through it. The air smelled warmer. The salt beneath the crack dried more deeply. Small creatures gathered near it because warmth is a kind of shelter. Tube-wrights secreted around its edges. Fungi found purchase in the dark seam. The crack became a chimney, then a wall, then a passage, then a room. The town did not heal the land. The town became the land’s new shape.

Night does not empty the Saltloom. The moon is the only lamp it has known for centuries, and it casts a clean silver light that makes the pale salt glow and the black basalt look like frozen flame. Under the moon, the city is full of movement, though almost silent. Moths rest in folded rows along the upper ferns. Warm-tilters lie on ledges, their broad heads turned toward the faintest breath of vents. Husk-crabs move in slow lines, carrying fragments that no eye would notice. Grubs shift inside fungal shelves with a dry whisper, like seeds being poured from one hollow to another. Predators press their wide bodies along passages, waiting for tremors. The moonlight does not soften the place. It makes it more visible, layer upon layer, body upon body, surface upon surface, every edge sharp, every membrane dry, every breath slow.

Dawn returns the ordinary sun. It falls clean and bright and too visible. It warms the upper leaves until oil condenses in tiny heavy beads. It makes the salt terraces shine matte and pale. It reveals the copper of ferns, the amber veins of resin, the white shelves of fungi, the dark mouths of passages, the translucent backs of grazers, the fur-crowned heads of warm-tilters, the small shells of crabs like scattered seed husks. The sun does not make the city wake. The city never slept completely. It only shifted its breathing with the heat.

There is no single creature in the Saltloom whose life can be told alone. A beetle’s life is bound to a fungal shelf; the shelf’s life is bound to warm gas; the gas is bound to a crack in the young basalt; the crack is bound to the movement of the land itself. The land is alive in the way a body is alive—not with thought, not with memory, not with desire as we would name it, but with pattern, with exchange, with persistence, with the slow accumulation of habit.

If you insist on seeing them as animals, they are small, soft, patient, quiet, mineral-tasting, oil-fed, heat-seeking, dry-breathing, slow-moving, blind in some ways and seeing in others, without names, without tools, without intention, and yet they are the builders of a city.

If you insist on seeing the city as a thing, it is not a thing at all. It is a process. It is the Saltloom becoming itself through the lives of what lives there.

The truest answer may be this: the Saltloom is inhabited by the fact of its own fullness. Every surface is colonized. Every shadow hides a passage. Every warm vent is tended by some creature or woven around by some fungus. The residents are not in the city. They are the city. And the city is not built. It is grown. It is shed. It is secreted. It is eaten. It is re-dried. It is hardened. It is breathed.

What lives there is a soft, quiet, dense, mineral commonwealth: beetles and moths, grubs and tube-wrights, grazers and cleaners, warm-tilters and husk-crabs, fungi and predators, all of them bound together so tightly that one cannot enter without touching them, cannot look without finding eyes or mouthparts or antennae, cannot stand still without becoming part of their pattern. They do not know one another. They do not call themselves a town. They do not dream of themselves as builders.

They simply live, and in living, the Saltloom lives.
