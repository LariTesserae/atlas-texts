The first inhabitant is the film.

It is not a creature in the sense that a body has edges and a place where it is not. It is a living skin, thin as a breath and broad as a basin, lying over the pale plates wherever moisture has learned to pause. In shade it looks faintly blue, not because the ground itself is blue, but because of the pigments of microscopic cells and filaments crowded into a translucent crust. In the glare of midday, that blue pales and hides, becoming a mere suggestion on the bone-colored surface, visible only where a plate has cooled enough to hold a trace of water.

If you kneel to look closely, the film is the first sign that this place is alive. It is alive in the same way that frost is alive at the edge of a pond: not with movement, but with response. It opens where a droplet settles. It swells where the dawn condenses. It seals where the heat begins to dry it. It is composed of countless microscopic makers—photosynthetic cells, filamentous organisms, dormant spores, chemical traces left behind by previous rains—and together they behave as a community so old and so diffuse that the plain itself seems to be the organism rather than anything upon it.

This is the first and largest inhabitant: the plate-building film.

It lies everywhere, though it is rarely obvious. It forms a living rind on the youngest plate margins, where mineral precipitation still occurs. It grows in the shallow hollows where water first collects, because those pits are not accidental wounds in the ground but architectural features, slowly made by chemistry and stress and the repeated wetting of cells. It covers the undersides of low ridges, fills the seams between plates, traces the edges of temporary streams with a thin green-gray skin, and leaves behind the faint mineral rings where small pools have evaporated. Those rings are not just stains. They are the edges of communities.

The film does not live on the land. It lives as land.

The broad pale plates appear to be the landscape’s foundation, but much of their visible surface is already mineralized record. The living part is usually a millimetric layer, sometimes more, sometimes less, resting atop older, hardened bodies of earlier organisms. In this way, the ground is like a city whose inhabitants constantly build with their own dead remains. A photosynthetic film secretes, binds, and precipitates mineral matter from water and from dust carried out of the thin atmosphere. It captures ions, nucleates crystals, produces sticky polymers, and slowly transforms a wet surface into a dry, bonelike sheet. The next generation grows on top of that sheet. The one after that seals its pores. Over years, decades, centuries—timescales too slow for animal life to imagine—a small moist hollow becomes a plate, a ridge becomes a wall, and the plain thickens.

The plates are not made by hands, but by cells.

The builders are not animals. They do not walk, hunt, hide, mate, or watch. They are organisms without faces, perhaps closer to microbial mats than to anything familiar. Their growth is a kind of patient mineralization. They do not need soil because there is no soil beneath them; there is no dark earth, no buried root system, no rotting layer of leaf litter. Instead, their sustenance is light, moisture, dissolved minerals, and the chemical gradients left by the evaporation and return of water. They make the ground by living upon it, then by turning themselves into ground.

There are many kinds of film. Some are blue-green and perform photosynthesis when even the thinnest layer of water remains in their pores. Some produce faint acids that etch the plate surfaces, creating the tiny hollows where moisture first gathers. Some are white or silvery where salts concentrate. Some thrive in the temporary pools, forming thin skins that capture light and oxygen. Some lie dormant in the dry cracks, their cells reduced to microscopic crusts of survival, waiting for rain. If a raindrop falls, the film can wake, swell, and resume its work within minutes. If the sun returns, it seals itself again. This is the plain’s central habit: persistence between extremes.

The most obvious inhabitants—the low plants—grow only after the films have made possible places for them.

The vegetation is sparse, but it is not accidental. A patch of gray-green life appears where the plate construction has created a basin, a seam, a shaded crack, or a rim capable of holding moisture longer than the surrounding bare ground. These plants are unlike trees or shrubs because there is no deep soil for roots to enter and no animal disturbance to open channels. They grow as surface dwellers, flattened against the made landscape. Their bodies seem less like individual organisms than like organs of the ground.

Some resemble succulents pressed into the mineral surface: thick, waxy, gray-green forms with edges that lift slightly when wet and curl inward when dry. Others look like living crusts that develop leaf-like tongues from their margins. These tongues are not leaves in the ordinary sense. They have no broad veins, no petioles, no canopy strategy. They are photosynthetic flaps, thin enough to catch the first water film, thick enough to retain moisture, sealed with oils and mineral coatings that reflect heat. When the dawn condensation arrives, they swell and flatten. When midday comes, they contract. They do not seek shade from the sun so much as negotiate with it.

Their reproduction is slow and strange because no animals move through it.

There are no insects to pollinate them, no birds to carry seeds, no mammals to graze them and distribute their remains. The plants therefore do not advertise themselves as flowering things. There is no nectar, no fruit, no bright display meant to attract a living visitor. Reproduction is conducted instead by weather, chemistry, and fragmentation.

A rain shower is not merely water; it is dispersal. Raindrops strike the plant margins and release tiny dry spore-dust or cellular fragments. Wind lifts these particles into the air, where they travel until they strike a moist seam, a basin edge, a salt ring, or a young plate film. Some fragments land bare and die, but many find the right microhabitat: a shallow hollow where water lingers long enough for cells to open and begin growth. When moisture returns, the fragment swells, takes hold with fine filaments that creep into cracks, and starts to build its own mineralized base. After many seasons, it becomes another low green mark on the pale plain.

The plants are also connected. Beneath the surface of the plates, in the seams and cracks, fine filaments creep from one basin to another. These are not roots like the roots of familiar plants, because there is no soil to penetrate. They are holdfasts, absorptive threads, chemical conduits, anchors into mineral seams. Through these filaments, moisture and dissolved salts pass between individual plant bodies. A green patch that appears alone on a broad plate may, at some depth, be linked to another patch beyond sight. The vegetation looks scattered, punctuated, isolated, but it may form slow subterranean networks of exchange—neural-like, but without nerves; circulatory, but without blood.

A single plant here is not necessarily a plant in the way a human would understand it. It may be a clone, a colony, a module in a larger process. A basin may contain dozens of separate genetic lineages, or it may be dominated by one spreading organism whose bodies are separated only by the visible gaps between leaf-tongues. The scale of identity is uncertain because the organisms themselves are uncertain in scale. A film advances by cell division. A plate grows by accretion. A plant expands by lifting its margins and sending filaments into cracks. The plain’s inhabitants are not clearly bounded; their borders are mineral, chemical, temporary, and conditional.

Without animals, the ecology is not quiet because it is empty. It is quiet because it is slow.

There is no predation. There is no fleeing. There are no eyes turning, no feet stopping, no sudden silence at the approach of something larger. But there is competition. There are strategies. There are losses, successes, failures, and adaptations. Competition is simply conducted at the speed of water films and mineral deposition. One mat excludes another by shading its edge. One plant thrives where a microbial ring has made the surface moist enough for germination. One crust survives because it secretes salts that dry and seal its pores against too much rain; another dies because it cannot withstand the heat. The organisms are not gentle because they are harmless. They are patient because the only weapons available are chemistry, growth, endurance, and timing.

Death, here, does not disappear into decay as we would expect it. Because there are no animals to consume fallen bodies, dead plant tissue does not vanish through biting and scattering. Instead, it becomes part of the building process. A dried leaf-tongue curls against a plate, mineral salts enter its tissues, and microbes coat it with a thin skin. It is sealed, flattened, and incorporated into the surface. A small dead plant patch may become the beginning of a low ridge. The landscape is its own repository. Its dead are not forgotten; they are lithified. The plain is therefore built out of both living organisms and the mineral memories of their endings.

This is why the made surface feels complete. It does not seem to depend on hidden things beneath it. It does not need burrows, hollows, nests, or animal tracks to make it intelligible. Its inhabitants do not hide. They are too slow to hide, and there is nothing from which to hide. They expose themselves in the form of their works: the pale plate, the shallow basin, the channel, the ridge, the blue film, the green tongue, the salt ring. To see the landscape is to see the community already made visible.

The plain’s sounds are not made by creatures. They are made by the processes of life.

At dusk, the plates tick. This is not the sound of animals, but of cooling minerals contracting after a day of heat. In places where a film has kept moisture beneath a surface skin, the sound can be faintly hollow, as if the ground is breathing. When rain first arrives, the hiss on hot plates is the sound of water meeting a living mineral crust. The small trickle in a seam is the movement of a channel whose banks were not carved but constructed. The whisper of a dry leaf-edge lifting is the plant’s response to a shift in humidity. The crackle of salt crystals forming is the audible end of a temporary pool. These sounds are the plain’s metabolism made audible.

If there were language here, it would not be speech. It would be gradient.

The inhabitants respond to light, temperature, wetness, pH, salt concentration, mineral availability, and chemical traces left by neighbors. They do not know one another as individuals know one another. They sense each other as boundaries and conditions. A wet cell changes the water film around it. A dry plate reflects heat differently from a film-covered plate. A salt ring concentrates minerals differently from a bare seam. A cracked edge admits more moisture and therefore invites colonization. These differences propagate slowly through the plain, creating patterns that resemble behavior but are really the results of countless local responses.

The plain has something like attention, but without a mind.

It “notices” water first. Water is the central signal. Where a droplet falls, the film swells. Where water collects, mineral rings begin to form. Where a channel runs, ridges accrete along its edges. Where a basin dries, organisms enter stasis and wait. The entire ecology is arranged around the movement and disappearance of water. In a world without animals, without deep soil, without roots or burrows or trampling, water is not merely a resource. It is the organizer of form.

At dawn, the plain is most alive in appearance. The air turns misty. The plates exhale stored heat, and tiny droplets condense on their surfaces. The blue films darken. The leaf-tongues lift and flatten. The mineral rings around old pools may glisten before evaporating. This is the hour of reopening. Dormant cells absorb moisture. Films begin photosynthesis in the thin water layer. Plant bodies expand. The ground seems almost to inhale.

Midday reverses this. The heat flattens shadows. The air shimmers above the plates. The water films vanish. The plants seal. The films dry into pale crusts. What was visible becomes hidden. The plain appears empty, bare, mineral, and still. But it is not dead. It is enduring. The inhabitants have withdrawn into the architecture of their own survival. A tiny pool in a basin may still harbor microbial films and dormant spores. A crack between two plates may still be dark with absorbed moisture. A raised edge may still hold a trace of dew. The visible world has become an inner world.

Rain changes everything.

The first drops arrive not as life-givers in a sentimental sense but as catalysts. On hot surfaces, they sizzle into steam, then multiply. The channels begin to move. The basins fill. The films revive. The plants, if they have not entered too deep into dormancy, respond by swelling and opening. Spore-dust is disturbed. Fragments are carried. Water flows along seams it has not used recently, deepening them, or it crosses new places where growth has raised a ridge. Temporary streams are born, and they are not merely streams; they are transport routes, nurseries, chemical currents, and dispersal mechanisms. A rain event may last an hour, yet its effects can last for seasons. A single shower may decide whether a basin becomes green or remains bare.

The plain’s weather is inseparable from its life.

The surface is so broad that it generates its own conditions. The contrast between hot plates and cooler moist films creates small convection. Patches of growth alter evaporation and reflection. Dust, spores, salt particles, and chemical traces rise from the living surface and enter the air. The cloud towers that form in the distance are not merely atmospheric; they are partially products of the plain’s physiology. The plain does not summon clouds with intention. It does not desire rain. But its living surface changes how heat and moisture move, and therefore it participates in making its own climate. The weather cells above it are extensions of the ground’s activity, and the ground’s activity depends on the weather cells in return. This reciprocal construction gives the landscape the feel of something slowly learning to sustain itself.

Scale changes everything. A single cell is too small to see. A microbial mat is too thin to call a plant. A basin may last only a season. A plate may last centuries. The plain may last millennia. If one stands upon it, time seems to collapse because there is no animal speed against which to measure it. There is no bird crossing the sky with a clear direction, no insect buzzing, no mammal running. Without animals, the scale of life shifts outward and downward. The smallest inhabitants work at the speed of droplets. The largest inhabit the speed of stone.

There is, at a certain scale, the possibility that the entire plain is one vast community rather than a collection of communities. But that image must be treated carefully, because it is too simple. The plain is not a single body. It does not have a central purpose. It does not have organs in the animal sense. It is not a giant sleeping creature, not a city with hidden architects, not a machine. It is better described as a distributed ecology with emergent unity. The plates, basins, films, ridges, channels, and plants form a system that maintains itself by feedback. The living surface builds the ground. The ground shapes the water. The water supports the living surface. The heat of the world drives the cycle. The wind carries the remnants. The community becomes an environment. The environment becomes a community.

At another scale, the inhabitants are unmistakably multiple. There are films that make plates, films that dissolve them, films that survive inside them, plants that grow on them, salt-loving crusts that live in their evaporating pools, dormant spores that appear inert as dust, and green leaf-tongues that respond to humidity within seconds. There are lineages that have been separated by cracks and others that have merged across seams. Some organisms are ancient and broad; some are momentary and local. To call the plain one thing is to miss its internal history. To call it many things is to miss its unity.

What lives here, then, is not a who. It is not a what, either, unless “what” can include a landscape becoming.

If one insists on names, they may be given by function. There are the plate-films, the builders of the pale surface. There are the seam-films, the keepers of moisture in cracks. There are the basin-crusts, the communities that turn shallow pools into mineral rings and mineral rings into nurseries. There are the dew-plants, the low gray-green bodies that flatten when wet and seal when dry. There are the wind-spore clouds, the dust that carries possibility from one basin to the next. There are the salt-films, which concentrate minerals in shrinking pools. There are the crack-colonies, where organisms begin again after a plate blisters or splits. None of these is a single species. None of these is stable as a word. They are ways of living, modes of persistence, forms of making.

The inhabitants do not look like inhabitants because they do not move like inhabitants. They are not creatures passing through a place. They are processes that have become place. Their bodies are not obvious because their bodies are often geological: mineral skin, crust, ridge, channel, plate. Their lives are not obvious because they are slow enough to resemble landscape. If you arrive expecting feet, wings, eyes, teeth, or trails, you will see none. The plain offers no animal signs. It offers only signs of growth.

But if you stay, the absence of animals begins to feel not like emptiness but like a different kind of fullness. There is no creature to watch the land. There is no creature to disturb it. The land is watched only by itself—by the film responding to moisture, by the plant responding to heat, by the channel responding to rain, by the plate responding to cooling. Life here does not observe the world. Life here is the world observing its own conditions and changing accordingly.

A traveler, if one could exist there without bringing feet or hunger or breath, would find no dwellings and no shelters. But the entire surface is dwelling. The basins are living rooms where water lingers. The seams are corridors where moisture and chemical traces travel. The plates are walls made of generations. The ridges are boundaries built against erosion or by erosion, depending on the scale. The temporary pools are public squares, brief and shining, where communities gather for a moment before dispersing again. The salt rings are doorways, thresholds where one pool ends and another begins. The plant patches are gardens without gardeners, each one a small concentration of color in the pale field.

Nothing here is hidden. That is the strange serenity. Because there are no animals, there is no need for concealment. Life is exposed because nothing is hunting it. It survives by tolerance rather than evasion. It does not run; it endures. It does not hide; it mineralizes. It does not flee; it enters stasis. It does not fight; it grows over. This makes the plain feel incomplete to an animal mind, but the incompleteness is only in the expectation. The plain is not missing creatures. It is a world in which the idea of creature has not taken that form.

The inhabitants have a kind of memory, but not the memory of minds. The plate remembers where water has stood. The salt ring remembers what concentration has occurred. The crack remembers where thermal stress began. The film remembers the last time it was wet. This memory is stored in mineral patterns, pore structures, chemical residues, and spore distributions. The plain’s history is not written in archives. It is written in surface. Each plate is a document. Each basin is an accumulation of conditions. Each channel is a sentence formed by moving water. The plants are punctuation. The films are grammar. The entire landscape is a text that continues rewriting itself as it dries, rains, warms, cools, splits, heals, and grows.

To ask what lives there is to ask for a creature, and the plain answers with a process. To ask who lives there is to expect a subject, and the plain answers with a collective. The “who” has no eyes, no voice, no name, no body with definite edges. The “what” includes everything from a microscopic cell in a dew-film to a broad plate extending toward a curved horizon. The life of the plain is not visible as animal movement, but it is visible as formation. The ridges are formed. The channels are formed. The hollows are formed. The plants are arranged. The films are maintained. The weather is modified. The ground is built. All of this is living work, performed by organisms that do not behave like organisms to human senses.

The deepest inhabitant is not a being but a tendency: the tendency of wet, light-struck, heat-cycled, mineral-rich surfaces to become biological, and of biology to become surface. The plain lives because it has learned—without learning, without intention, without memory in the conscious sense—to keep water close, to make moisture into structure, and to make structure into more moisture.

It is a world of films, plates, tongues, rings, cracks, pools, and spores. It is a world without walkers, without flyers, without swimmers, without watchers. And yet it is not empty. It is full of the quiet labor of organisms building themselves into landscape. It is full of blue sheen at dawn, of green lift at the seam, of salt crystallization in the edge of a dying pool, of the small crack that begins to heal because microbes have entered and minerals have begun to glue it.

If one says, “Nothing lives there,” the plain answers in slow time by growing a new ring of salt, by opening a leaf-tongue to a droplet, by advancing a blue film across a pit, by raising a ridge where water has once again found the same path. If one says, “No one lives there,” the answer is also yes, because there is no one. But the answer is also no, because everything there lives. The plain is not a place with inhabitants. It is a place that is inhabited by its own making.
