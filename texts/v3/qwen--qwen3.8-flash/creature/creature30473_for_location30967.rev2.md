No one lives there in the way a house expects to be lived in, yet the place is not empty, not dead, not abandoned. If you go looking for faces, you will find only the place’s face turned slightly toward its own making. If you go looking for people, you will find that the people have become a verb.

The resident of the pocket is, most immediately, the Making itself.

It has no body you can point to, unless you are willing to point to everything at once: the new tile still warm with a thumbprint, the half-knot waiting to be finished, the flint awl standing upright in soft clay, the reed cord tightening as water moves through it, the moss that spreads along a wall as if remembering a hand that laid it there. This being is not a ghost, not a forgotten builder, not a spirit haunting a ruin. It is the place’s own metabolism. It is a wet, hand-shaped life made of construction.

To understand what lives there, you must stop thinking of the inhabitants as separate from their habitat. The pocket does not contain a civilization. It is a civilization in the sense that a body contains organs: not by housing little people inside itself, but by doing its own living through a thousand interlocking processes. The walls build. The water carries. The moss sews. The bones sprout green. The tools become soil. The soil becomes architecture. The architecture becomes breath.

The closest thing to a single person is the pulse in the lowest terraces.

There, where the heat is strongest and the clay feels as if it has swallowed fire and become fever-damp flesh, a rhythm moves through the stone. It is not machinery. It is too uneven, too warm, too hesitant. It sounds like hand-taps. It sounds like someone outside the crack, or inside it, or somehow beneath both, working the stone by touch. If you kneel long enough with your ear pressed to a terrace, you can hear this tapping travel through the layers of tile, reed, root, and wet earth. It answers the dripping of condensation. It makes the hanging bells shiver. It makes the moss tremble with each incoming breath of steam. If you follow that sound far enough, you begin to imagine a hand.

Not a large hand. Not a human hand. Not a hand with nails or skin or the usual softness of fingers. The hand you imagine is made of pressed clay, wet and glowing faintly with heat, its ridges sharpened by recent making, its thumbprint still fresh, its fingers formed from roots, reeds, and braided sinew. It taps a wall into being. It draws a channel by pressing the side of one long digit into the ground. It curls beneath a roof of broad leaves and stitches moss to the underside with a needle of bone that is no longer quite a tool, because the moss has begun to grow through its eye-hole like a green vein. This hand is the place’s heartbeat: not a creature, not a god, not a builder, but the act of building continued without a builder.

The pocket also has creatures. They are real, though they are hard to separate from the structure. They are rare because there is little room for rare things to stand apart from their environment. Here, almost every animal is also an organ, almost every tool is also a seed, almost every living thing is also a piece of unfinished construction.

The most obvious of these are the pale drain-worms.

They live in the wider waterways, in the basins and gutters, in the hidden channels beneath tiled floors. Their bodies are segmented and slick, the color of wet bone and young fungi. Along each segment rides a small tile, fitted to the skin as if the builders had sewn armor onto the creature—or as if the creature had begun to tile itself, its skin curing in rings the way clay does near heat. They do not swim in the ordinary sense. They conduct water. They curl in slow, deliberate waves, pushing streams through narrow spaces with the patience of a hand turning a lock. Where they pass, the water moves with greater certainty. Where they sleep, condensation gathers along their backs in thick, trembling beads that look like eggs, like pearls, like the small swollen mouths of something not yet born.

They are not cute. They are not monstrous. They are necessary. They are the plumbing made animal. They are the wetness that has learned to work.

If you watch one closely, you may see that it has no eyes, or only eye-like ridges that cannot see anything except pressure and temperature. It does not seem to eat in a way you would recognize. It does not hunt. It does not flee. It moves through the drainage as if moving through the inside of a wall that has grown a throat. When it dies—and death here is quiet, immediate, and productive—its body does not rot in the normal way. The tiles lift from its segments and find new homes. The pale rings soften into clay. The reeds grow through the spaces where its spine was. A fungus mat spreads over the place where it curled last, and the moss begins to stitch itself along the outline as if mourning by construction. The drain-worm becomes a better drain, then a roof, then a floor, then a garden, then part of the pulse that taps the stone.

Then there are the blink-birds.

These are the most uncertain inhabitants. You may see one perched on a roof of dried lichen, so still that you mistake it for a carved ornament. Its body is the size of a thumb joint, its beak a narrow flint point, its eyes drops of copper that do not reflect the light so much as drink it. Its wings are not wings in the usual sense. They are membranes of leaf tissue, translucent and veined with thin fibers, laced together with something like reed thread. When it moves, it does not fly. It unfolds. It lifts from the surface as though the plant it belongs to has decided to let go of the wall for a moment and remember flight.

It blinks.

That is the strangest thing. In the dim fungus-light, among the dripping and the steam, the creature’s eyes blink. The blink is not only biological. It is structural. When it opens its hollow beak, a droplet falls into it, and the droplet becomes a seed. The seed falls onto a tile. The tile cracks. The crack becomes a channel. The channel becomes a wall. The wall becomes a place where moss grows, and the moss catches a drifting strand of reed, and the strand catches an insect, and the insect becomes bone, and the bone becomes a pin, and the pin becomes part of the bird’s next hiding place. It is not a predator in the ordinary way. It is an agent of becoming.

There are more of them than you would think, because they are not easily distinguished from leaves, from fragments of lashing, from small artifacts that have begun to twitch. They live where built and grown have failed to decide which is which: under tiles, inside drains, behind fungus mats, in the black folds of moss that glow like wet cloth. Some are almost tools. Some are almost plants. Some are almost birds. The place does not care for clean categories. It makes what it needs out of what is already near at hand.

There are also smaller things.

Beetles no larger than a seed, with shells like fired clay, move through the seams in short, careful bursts. They stop often. They do not seem to eat so much as to listen. They fit into grooves cut for their own bodies. They wedge themselves beside cords and seem, at times, to be part of the knot. If you remove one, a tile loosens. If you replace it, the joint holds again as though the creature were the missing peg. Its legs are dark and hard, like tiny reeds bent to shape. Its antennae move with the rhythm of steam. Whether it is born from the place or made by the place or simply forgotten there, the pocket does not care. It is a living maintenance mechanism, a small clay animal that belongs to the world the way a knot belongs to a rope.

There are bone-seeds too, though they are not plants and not animals. Small carved bones protrude from walls, pinned there by hand or left by some earlier working. Around each, a ring of green growth has begun. At first it seems like moss taking advantage of shelter. Later it seems like the bone is sprouting. Tiny shoots push through its pores. Fungal threads wrap it like sutures. The bone becomes a socket. The socket becomes a lamp. The lamp becomes a root-bed. The root-bed becomes a stair. A tool tries to become a plant. A plant tries to become a wall. A wall tries to become a body.

The soft inhabitants are the most pervasive and the least individuated. They are the mosses, lichens, fungi, and damp green linings. They are not a choir, exactly, but they behave like a congregation of mouths and ears. They give the pocket its light: blue-green fungal sheets across low ceilings, copper-veined moss that breathes with an inner dampness, tiny glowing spots where water beads and the air thickens. They give the pocket its sound: reed bells shaking in the humidity, the soft scraping of moss under its own weight, the low hum of wind passing through carved apertures and becoming a voice in the walls. They give the pocket its warmth: not heat alone, but the heat of living material, the warmth of a lung tiled with clay and lined with green fur.

These soft beings fill gaps so completely that they seem to be the place’s immune system and nervous system at once. When a surface cracks, fungus enters. When a joint leaks, moss stitches it. When a terrace grows too dry, condensation finds it and the moss swells until it looks like wool soaked in breath. When a sound is too sharp, the lichen absorbs it. When a sound is too soft, the bone walls amplify it. The pocket is never silent because every surface is listening. The moss listens. The fungus listens. The water listens. The half-finished tiles listen with their open pores, drinking the humid air as if they were hungry for the next breath that will set them firmly into the world.

There is no bare natural substrate for ordinary animals to hunt over. The place is too full, too busy, too recent. The air is thick with forms not yet settled into species. The ground is not ground. The slope is a wall. The tree is a bundle. The pool is a basin. The forest is an arrangement of supports, gutters, roofs, and lamps. So the creatures that live there are not the creatures you would find in a meadow, a canyon, or a rainforest. They are inhabitants of density. They are byproducts of a world that cannot stop assembling itself. They live where the pocket is not quite wall, not quite water, not quite tool, not quite organism. They live in the seam between categories, as the pocket itself lives in the seam between two summit stones.

If you press long enough, you will begin to feel that there is not one thing living there but one living thing wearing many small shapes. The drain-worm is one finger of it. The blink-bird is one eyelid of it. The clay-beetle is one nail on one hand of it. The fungus-light is one dim nerve of it. The moss is one lining of it. The half-knot is one of its thoughts still unfinished. The thumbprint is one of its memories still warm.

The builders are not gone. They may never have existed in the way you expect builders to exist. There is no village, no hearth, no bed, no grave, no child’s toy, no broken pot left behind from a forgotten household. But the place is full of the residue of making, and that residue is alive. Every surface suggests a hand that was there a moment ago. Every gap suggests a finger that has just withdrawn. Every channel suggests a palm that has cupped water only seconds before. The flint chip still has a damp edge. The cord is tied in a half-knot. A carved groove waits for a tool. A tile is set with a thumbprint glowing from the heat of its own newness. This is not abandonment. This is continuation.

The makers, if they can be called that, are distributed. They are the steam and the reed. They are the wet earth and the bone. They are the roots that have been stitched into place, trained to grow around flint pins, guided by loops of sinew until the living plant becomes a living support. They are the leaves arranged to pass water from terrace to terrace, each leaf a small gutter, each gutter a small throat. They are the reed lashing that holds a roof of broad green, the roof that holds the moss, the moss that holds the fungus, the fungus that holds the light, the light that holds the blink-bird’s eye, the blink-bird’s eye that holds the next tiny becoming.

There may be no humans here. There may never have been humans here. The pocket’s scale suggests small hands, but the place does not care whether the hands are human hands, insect hands, plant hands, fungal hands, or hands made of water and clay. It only continues to make. If there were once people in it, they did not vanish. They were absorbed into the making. Their tools became soil. Their soil became architecture. Their architecture became ecosystem. Their ecosystem became a hand tapping the stone.

What lives there, therefore, is neither a person nor an animal nor a place.

It is a wet, mountain-sized act of construction wearing the shape of a wrist-crack. It is a body made of terraces. It is a mind made of channels. It is a breath made of steam. It is a pulse made of taps. It is a family of creatures that are also organs: the pale drain-worms conducting water, the blink-birds unfolding seeds into walls, the clay-beetles listening in their seams, the moss-fungus choir filling the dark with living light. It is a society of unfinished things, where every half-knot is a promise, every thumbprint a name, every newly set tile a living act, every dead thing repressed into material, every gap sealed by green addition.

If you were to kneel inside it—and the kneeling itself would take most of an afternoon—you would not be alone. You would be inside the hand that presses the pocket open. Your breath would feed the condensation. Your weight would test a terrace. Your pulse would add a new rhythm to the drum under the lowest stones. A tile might lift itself beneath your knee. A moss fringe might close behind you like a curtain. A drain-worm would curl around your ankle and carry away the water of your shoes. A blink-bird would open its flint beak somewhere above you, catch a droplet, and begin the next wall.

And if you listened very closely, past the dripping, past the creak of wet wood, past the soft scraping of reeds and the hiss of steam through the chimneys that are not chimneys, you would hear it tapping.

Not loudly.

Not with intent as we imagine intent.

But with patience.

With the slow insistence of a thing that has decided to continue existing by adding more of itself.

That is what lives there.
