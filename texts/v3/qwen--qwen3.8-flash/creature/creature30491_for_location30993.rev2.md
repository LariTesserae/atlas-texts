Whoever lives there is not one thing, and not merely many things in the ordinary way. At first the plateau seems populated by its animals—the herds on the upper slopes, the pale water-rooted creatures in the fog ravines, the silent fliers in the dark canopy. But these are only the parts that can be pointed at. The highland is inhabited from the surface of the stone upward, and its creatures have learned to blend into a country that is not yet finished with itself.

The first inhabitants are not animals at all, or not animals anyone would want to call animals. They are the surfaces. The sharp black stone is never merely stone. It is coated in living films: salted lichens, iron-fed mosses, copper-tinted bacterial mats, crusts of mineral slime that glow faintly when touched. These things feed on vapor, on magnetic dust, on the faint heat rising from below, on the condensation that runs down glassy ridges. They grow in slow rings, in vein-like nets, in patches that resemble old metalwork. Where the land has cracked, pale filaments reach across the fracture first, not repairing it but tasting it, reading the new direction of the wind, the change in pressure, the shift in chemistry. If a traveler stands on a newly split cliff face and watches carefully, he may see a bloom of tiny bright threads moving along the edge, as if the stone were developing nerves.

These surface organisms are not passive. They participate in the plateau’s strange economy. Some exhale fine metallic spores. Others precipitate copper fibers from the air and leave them hanging in the branches like lace. A patch of moss that seems still may be, over years, slowly drawing iron out of basalt and turning it into a brittle black film that later becomes food for small shell-backed insects. The ground itself wears a skin, and that skin is alive. It mutes footsteps. It dulls the vibration of hooves. It keeps the sharp young stone from ringing too loudly when the wind passes through it. In many places, there is no line between mineral and organism; there is only a process too slow to see clearly and too busy to call dead.

The forests are inhabited by architecture that has not stopped becoming. The trees do not merely grow; they argue with the slope. Their roots are often plated or metallic, pale where exposed, darkened where damp. They anchor into fault lines with a ferocity that seems excessive, as if the ground below might withdraw at any moment. The trunks are frequently fused at intervals by fungal bridges, so that cutting one tree may kill three others. Branches split and reunite. Some canopy trees bear long translucent sacs filled with condensation, which sway gently and release water only when shaken or attacked. Beneath them is a thick undergrowth of reed, fern, and fungus-shrub, each plant crowded against the next, each trying to find some advantage in fog, stone, attention, or heat.

Plants here do not obey the ordinary laws of growth. Some lean toward light; some lean toward moisture; some lean toward nothing at all until someone looks at them. This is not metaphor. A grove may lie dormant for years, then open when an animal pauses near it, not because it senses warmth or vibration alone, but because it senses observation. The vines seem especially curious. They may reach toward a standing body even when there is no trellis to climb, and when the body leaves, the vine may continue to grow as though it were still there for a few days afterward. A tree might appear twice in the same grove, one version wet with dew, the other coated in dust, because the attention of the forest has not yet agreed which one it wants. Neither is false. Both are attempts.

Many of these plants are not inhabited in the sense of hosting separate creatures, but in the sense of being partly creatures themselves. A broad leaf that holds dew for hours may contain, in that dew, a small colony of six-legged organisms whose entire world is the surface of that leaf. They drink from it, mate on it, shed skins on it, and sometimes flee to the branch above when the leaf tilts too steeply. When the leaf drops, they ride it down through the humid air like passengers on a falling boat. Other leaves have internal pockets where moisture has turned brackish and alive with microscopic plates. A fallen trunk may split, and from the split may emerge not rot but a new garden: fungus filaments, copper blooms, luminous threads, and tiny animals with no visible eyes because they have never lived in enough light to need them.

The animals that live in the highland are everywhere, but the landscape is arranged to absorb them. Their colors are charcoal, olive, rust, wet amber, black-green. Many carry metallic glints along their backs, not as ornament but as mineral armor built from the food they eat and the air they drink. Their bodies seem designed for a place where a predator may be indistinguishable from a shadow, and a shadow may be indistinguishable from a predator.

The herds on the upper slopes are the most obvious inhabitants. They resemble no simple animal, because no single environment here allows simple animals. They are broad-backed, low-headed, thick-necked, and warm-bodied, with legs too sturdy for their apparent weight. Their coats have short metallic flecks, especially along the spine, where the skin has hardened into flexible plates. Young calves are born enclosed in soft seedpod shells that crack as they stand, leaving jagged rings of material along their shoulders and hips. The adults carry curved protrusions from their skulls—neither antler nor horn nor handle exactly, but perhaps all three functions compressed into one strange organ. They may be radiators for heat, anchors for social posture, tools for separating fog from stone, handles used when the herd moves through steep terrain and must be pulled or steadied by its companions.

The herds do not graze in lines. They move in arcs, clusters, uneven columns, and brief spirals. This is not because they are timid or leaderless, though they are both, but because fixed points are dangerous. In the highland, a safe place today may be a new fissure tomorrow. So they return to shelters not because those shelters are safe, but because they have remained stable enough to be remembered. Some herd animals have developed a habit of touching one another continuously as they move through fog, linking their protrusions or shoulders, forming a loose chain that can detect the moment the ground begins to change.

They are rarely alone. Small shell-backed animals travel in their wake, grazing on the dust kicked from dried hides, on the mineral residue left in hoofprints, on the salt crust that forms where their breath condenses. Sometimes the herd itself is mistaken for a single object because the animals move so slowly and uniformly that the eye forgets the divisions between them. When they pass across open ground, their shadows merge into one long dark shape, often slightly larger than the herd should be. Occasionally that shadow moves with its own intention. It is not always an illusion. The land has a habit of producing double images, and some of them have appetites.

The pale creatures of the fog-ravines live in water as if water were a country that might change its mind. They are long, segmented, soft-bodied, with frilled fins that pulse in slow rhythm. They look like roots unmoored from the bottom, or like eels made of boiled milk and memory. They have no eyes that can be identified, and probably do not need them. They navigate by taste, pressure, and the faint electric wrongness that runs through wet stone when geothermal currents shift. Their bodies are mostly water and mucus, and when they move, the fog seems to part around them as if recognizing a kindred substance. They do not hunt in any obvious way. They filter the water. They drift along hidden springs. They carry nutrient-rich films from one fault to another, and their eggs, when released, cling to the frills of other creatures as though borrowing bodies until the next flood.

Sometimes one of these pale forms will rise from a pool and cross a strip of gravel, and where it passes, the ground darkens and remains wet for hours after the sun—if there is a sun—has gone behind the haze. The high-forest birds, or bird-equivalents, will land in its wake and drink from the moisture it leaves behind. The birds themselves are almost as impossible to see clearly as the fog. They fly on thin translucent membranes stretched over light bone-frames, and they make no cry. Their wings do not beat in the usual way; they adjust, flatten, and release, so that moving through the canopy feels less like flight than like slow falling with intention. Their rookeries are dense clusters on broad branches, hundreds of them packed together, wings folded like stained wax, bodies overlapping until the whole mass bends the branch under its weight. When the branch releases, the rookery shifts in a single wave, but no one cries, no one startles. They simply readjust, as if silence were the only grammar the highland understands.

These birds communicate by pressure changes produced by wing movement. The air around a rookery can feel suddenly thicker against the skin. A herd animal may stop grazing without warning because it senses a shift in air density above it, though no visible movement has occurred. Some of these fliers have evolved to carry fungal spores in the folds of their membranes. When they roost, tiny threads of glowing life emerge from the underside of their wings and attach to the bark. Thus the birds tend to their forest by accident or design, or perhaps by the same thing: a mutualism so old that it no longer has a separate name.

The small six-legged animals are often overlooked, but they are among the most widespread inhabitants of the plateau. They scurry through leaf litter and moss with quick, uncertain eyes, their backs hardened into shell-like plates, their legs too many for the body to move in a familiar way. They resemble a mixture of insect, armadillo, and broken clock. Their shells are not pure shell but living mineral plates grown from iron, silica, and the residue of whatever fungi they consume. They move in swarms, but not in the frantic way of ordinary insects. Their swarms have pauses. When something is wrong, hundreds or thousands of them stop simultaneously, as though the air has removed the concept of motion from their bodies. They do not flee because fleeing in a place with unstable distances can be fatal. If you run from a predator, you may run into a new fissure, a false valley, or a patch of ground that has decided to become gravel in the next hour. So the swarms pause. Their predators have learned to read the sudden absence of small movement as a signal, and thus the prey’s stillness becomes its own kind of warning system.

There are predators. Some of them are too still to be believed.

A creature like a cat, but longer, with a spine of dark plates and flanks that seem carved from wet stone, may lie for hours across a fallen trunk without anyone noticing it except as an unusual shadow. Its colors match the rot and mineral crust so perfectly that it appears to be part of the log, part of the moss, part of the mistake in the ground. Only when its flank rises, or when its head turns without any visible neck movement, does the illusion break. Then the prey may be too close. The attack is not dramatic. There is no springing, no snarl, no shower of leaves. There is only a soft displacement, a snap of vapor, and then a change in the density of the air where something has passed. If a herd animal is taken, the rest of the herd does not scatter in the way an ordinary predator would cause them to scatter. They simply cease to be near the missing one. The place empties without commotion.

Other predators hunt in groups. They are slender, damp-furred, with too many joints and eyes set too far apart to allow for a clear expression of hunger or intent. They move without visible leadership, in coordinated silence through fog and low branches. They do not howl. They do not need to. The highland has made sound unreliable. A call may be felt in the teeth before it reaches the ears. A pack may surround prey by making the air around it strange rather than visible. To be hunted here is often to realize, too late, that the silence has become too organized.

There are predators that seem less like creatures than like absences. A patch of fog may hold a shape too empty to look at directly. A too-large shadow may move independently of the object that casts it. Sometimes a bird will disappear not because another animal caught it, but because the space between two versions of the forest closed over it. These beings are difficult to describe. Some are probably real in the way the herds and birds are real; others may be emergences, the result of the highland’s unstable geometry producing temporary beings from the difference between one draft and another. They have no fixed bodies. They feed on gaps. If a valley appears twice, with small corrections in the placement of stones and branches, something may be living in the difference between the two. It is not malevolent. It is ecological. It eats what would otherwise accumulate as error.

Some of these gap-feeders are tiny. A hollow reed may fill with fog, and later the fog may drain out in the shape of a brief, dark animal that scurries along the stem and vanishes into the moss. Others are larger and harder to identify. They may be the reason a traveler sometimes sees an animal whose body is half present and half trailing behind like heat shimmer with intention. The skin pattern of such a creature may lag a few steps behind its movement, not because it is transparent, but because it exists partly in a version of the world that has not yet solidified. If the land revises, such beings are revised more easily. They are the most native of all the inhabitants, because they live comfortably in uncertainty.

The highland has a vertical ecology, though it is not the ordinary verticality of mountains. The lower country is amber-gray and damp, full of overlapping life. Higher up, where the sky darkens and the curtains of color begin to drift, the air itself seems inhabited. The haze of spores, metallic dust, and luminous particles is not merely weather. It carries life. Microscopic plates drift in it, feeding on the fragments of broken light. Larger translucent forms move through the violet field like slow medusae, trailing copper filaments, grazing where the atmosphere is thin enough to be almost space. They are never seen clearly, because they are always either too far above or too dissolved into the haze to be named. When they die, their bodies fragment into dust and fall, becoming part of the very twilight that gave them life. In this way the upper atmosphere is not separate from the lower ecology; it is its continuation.

The storms belong to the inhabitants too. They are not merely weather events but behaviors, sometimes of the land, sometimes of the things within it. Clouds collect above a basin and remain there because the terrain seems to draw moisture into itself, or because a fungal colony releases something into the air, or because the basin is a kind of mouth that has not finished breathing. Storms travel along ridge lines as if following roads that are not there. Lightning flashes over stone that has been struck repeatedly, sometimes when the air is still, sometimes when the fog thins just enough to expose the mineral veins beneath. The creatures of the plateau do not always flee storms; some seek them. The herds move toward places where the air tastes of iron after lightning, because the ground there has been refreshed. The birds cluster in dense rookeries and let the pressure of the storm flatten their wings against their bodies, not sleeping, but listening with their bones. The pale fog-creatures rise toward the surface of pools when charged water runs through them, absorbing minerals through their frilled fins. The storms are part of the food web. They distribute metals, trigger fungal blooms, reveal new cracks, and kill what has become too still.

There are no cities, no roads, no walls, no towers. Nothing in the ordinary sense has been built. And yet the plateau is full of industries. The mountains refine. The forests filter. The fungi corrode and recombine. The streams transport and separate. The soil ferments. The air condenses metal into fibers and drops them from above. These are not metaphors for life; they are the life. A basin of boiling mineral water deposits its layers in precise bands, and from that basin a population of small shell-backed animals grows, grazing on the bands as if on grain. A hollow in stone fills with hardened metal, becomes a basin, collects rain, becomes a pool, becomes a habitat, becomes a process. The boundary between organism and geology is worse than thin here. It is often meaningless.

Many inhabitants are not individuals in the way a human would expect. A tree may be one organism and also a colony of fungi, mosses, leaf-pools, insects, and mineral films. A herd may be a social group and also a mobile ecosystem, carrying its own dust communities, parasites, and symbiotic grazers. A fog-ravine creature may shed part of its body during stress, and that part may continue living as a smaller animal for a time before returning to the main mass or becoming food for something else. Death is rarely final here; it is redistribution. A carcass may lie for days without seeming dead because fungus has colonized its joints and keeps them moving. Birds roost in the ribcage. Moss grows through the plates of a dead herd animal’s back. Eventually, the body becomes a small hill, then a mineral bed, then a place where new plants anchor with metal roots. The animal does not vanish so much as become infrastructure.

If one were to describe the way these beings reproduce, one would quickly discover that reproduction in the highland is less about copying than about adapting before birth. Calves are born with soft shells already patterned by the mother’s movements and the terrain they last crossed. Some eggs of fog creatures carry not only embryos but small packets of mineral water from a spring that no longer exists, because the mother’s body stored and altered it. Fungal spores may land on stone and wait months for the correct shift in magnetic current before germinating. Birds do not lay eggs so much as leave sealed sacs in the copper lace, each sac containing a future that depends on which branch it dangles from and what kind of rain it experiences. The young are born late or early depending on whether the land has decided, locally, to be stable.

No species here is dominant, because dominance would require a fixed world, and the highland does not permit fixedness for long. Even the predators are provisional. The long cat-log may be one strategy among many. If its preferred hunting ground shifts, it may become something else—not another species, but another mode of the same body. Some animals seem to have no stable morphology at all. They are bodies that tolerate being slightly otherwise. They have parts that can lag behind the whole, colors that rearrange when attention moves, and outlines that blur in the presence of repeated terrain. This is not magic. It is survival. In a country that cannot make up its mind, the creature that makes up its mind too soon dies.

And yet there is no sense of desperation. The abundance is not chaotic or starving. It is crowded, yes, but not into conflict. There is a pressure in the density, as if the ecosystem is trying to hold together while the floor shifts. Plants find moisture by memory. Animals avoid fixed points. Fungi bind broken surfaces. Streams sort minerals as though they had been taught by repetition. The highland feels industrial, but it has no owner. The world there behaves as though its geology is working, and the creatures that live within it have learned to work with the process rather than merely inhabit it.

Perhaps the greatest inhabitant is the plateau itself. It breathes through vents. It circulates through ore veins. It thinks, if thought is possible there, in magnetic currents, fungal networks, weather patterns, and revisions of distance. The ridges are young, but they are already acting as if they remember having existed elsewhere. The rivers do not know their final courses. The forests have not agreed on their boundaries. The stone does not know whether it wants to be mountain, shard, or plain. All of this might be described as geology, except that geology here has appetites, habits, and responses. It is not a place with inhabitants so much as an unfinished system in which inhabitants are among its methods of becoming.

If one stands in the dim highland long enough, the distinction between creature and country begins to fail. The fog is not merely fog; it carries spores that may become animals, minerals, plants, or weather. The shadows are not merely shadows; they may be too large for their objects because something in them has not been excluded from the scene. The pools may reflect a sky that is not currently present because the water is not the only thing holding memory. And the animals, the quiet abundant animals, move through this world not as guests but as organs, or perhaps as sentences in a language the land is still trying to write.

So what lives there? Surfaces and seeds. Metal-rooted trees. Attention-bearing vines. Fungal binders. Copper-lace builders. Herds with double shadows. Pale water-roots drifting through fog-ravines. Silent birds with membranes like wet glass. Small shell-backed swarms that pause as one body. Long still predators mistaken for logs. Pack-hunters made of pressure and absence. Aerial grazers in the upper haze. Creatures born from repeated valleys. Beings that feed on gaps. Condensation, heat, magnetism, and revision. And at the center of all of it, if center means anything in a place so unstable, the highland itself: vast, warm, dim, young, industrial, unfinished, and alive with the strange persistence of things that have learned to live not in a finished world, but in the act of one still being made.
