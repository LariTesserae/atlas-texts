What lives in the unfinished city is a sequence, and you cannot understand it by asking what the dominant creature is. The answer depends on altitude, on wetness, on which of the city’s lungs are open. If you stand on a terrace, you may be above a migration. If you crouch beneath a bridge, you may be under a breathing curtain of pale filaments. If you enter a shaft, you may be in the city’s own wet interior, where things live that have never needed a face.

The first inhabitants are too small to be seen as individuals. They are dust-shells, glass-blooms, frost-lace, black efflorescences that rise on warm ledges and vanish into water when the temperature changes. They are not plants, though they behave as plants might if plants could be made of mineral weather. They drink the difference between cold and slightly less cold. They metabolize condensation, dissolved gases, heat gradients, the faint electric stress of fused stone. Over time they become the city’s skin. They pit, polish, seal, etch. They are the reason some surfaces glisten as if recently washed and others feel, to the touch, like fine powder made of a thousand vanished bodies.

A traveler might mistake them for mere dirt. The locals do not. They know that if a wall becomes too dry, the dust-shells thin, the water begins to run in the wrong direction, and a passageway can collapse inward by degrees. They know that certain ledges are alive in the way a stomach is alive. They know how to scrape, encourage, suppress, cultivate. They treat the stone as a garden, though the garden has no greenness, no scent of sap, only the sharp chalky smell of shell and wet metal.

Above this microhabitat lives the crawling dark.

On the broad upper terraces, what appears to be black sand moving against the wind is in fact composed of ten thousand small articulated bodies. They are jointed things, no larger than a thumbnail, each with a carapace like obsidian dust pressed into a bead and sealed with glass. They move in dense waves across the mineral flats, and because the city is full of shallow channels, their movement resembles the movement of water: now pooling, now narrowing, now falling over an invisible edge. They feed on dust-shells, dead flakes, mineral blooms, the sticky residues left by larger creatures. They are the city’s cleaners. They are also the city’s tide.

The Accreted, the people who live here in the most complicated sense, follow them. They read the crawling black sand as a sign. If the waves move too high onto a warm terrace, a thermal veil is about to open. If they vanish from a ledge for days, something larger has disturbed the water-seam below. If they begin to click in phase, the wind over the columns has changed pitch, and the lower lattice may need to be checked.

Other swarm creatures are stranger.

Beneath the bridges and across the undersides of platforms, in the wet places where condensation drips from ceilings of fused silica, hang pale segmented forms that could be called animals only if the word were stretched thin. They are sheets of filaments, like translucent curtains made of nerve. They bead with water when touched and then re-form on the far side of the ledge. They have no obvious heads, no mouths in the way an ordinary mouth is known. Instead they gather air, condense it, taste the minerals it carries, and pass the resulting fluid from filament to filament. They are filter-cloths, or veil-cloths, depending on how much light is present. In complete darkness they look like nothing at all, only a slight resistance in the air, a coolness against the forearm. In the blue friction-light of an opening panel, they shine like wet gauze.

These hanging colonies are essential. Without them, condensation would collect in harmful places and not enough elsewhere. The Accreted trim them, harvest the flaky secretions, sometimes train them to grow over damaged conduits so that their filaments act as temporary valves. Children learn to touch them without frightening them. A child who can make a veil-cloth bead and release again is said to have patience, or to be becoming a good basinkeeper.

Then there are the clicking things.

Across the mineral flats move waves of creatures like crabs, or like machines made into crabs, or like both words were translated into something neither quite is. They have broad segmented shells, many-jointed legs, and internal structures that hold water instead of blood. When they walk, their legs produce a dry clicking, and when molting, a wetter crack. They migrate in lines so long the eye loses the first and last of them. They travel by the humidity of the surface, never crossing fully dry ledges. Their molted shells, glassy and hard, are used for tiles, for plates, for the edges of balconies, for thin tools that can be heated without sparking. A wave-crab’s empty carapace is one of the most ordinary objects in the city; a living one is something else entirely.

Wave-crabs are not tame. They do not fear people, because fear requires a certain kind of face, and they have none. They fear vibration too much, or vibration too little, depending on the season. When they sense a stress-tone rising through a causeway, they will change course by hundreds or thousands, a ripple passing through their procession without any apparent signal. The Accreted have learned to listen for that ripple: if the wave-crabs turn, a bridge may need to be opened, or a Standing One may be about to shift.

For there are large beings, too.

In the open basins, on the highest terraces, in the places where the city’s constructed ridges become almost geological, there stand the Slow Ones, the Standing Ones, the Monument Herd. They are enormous and patient, their bodies layered plates of silicate with joints like arches and backs like low ridges. Some appear to have been grown rather than born, accreted plate by plate around a core of wet dark. Others seem to contain smoke, or vapor, or a weak field that holds their shape against the thin air. When they stand still, they resemble unfinished towers. When they finally move, only one segment shifts, and the sound is of a mountain taking a careful step.

They drink through pores. Water gathers on their lower shells and is drawn inward by capillary action and thermal pressure. They exude mineral flakes, tiny shards, salt-dust, droplets, and thin sheets of frost. The flakes fall from them continuously; their shadows become chalky beds where smaller life flourishes. Dust-shells colonize their crevices. Mites live in the hollows beneath their limbs. Pale filaments grow where their breath makes the air cooler. A single Standing One can contain a neighborhood in the space under one leg, or in the gap between its shoulder plates.

Whether the Standing Ones were originally part of the city’s construction or grew from its waste is not known. The Accreted do not ask in that way. They have a term for such beings that translates poorly: *those that walk the city into being by standing near it*. They do not worship them exactly, but they avoid irritating them. They have learned which angles of approach cause a tremor, which sounds make their plates open too quickly, which water-scores lead them to a basin. Some individuals, over generations, have been guided into new positions: not domesticated, but nudged. A Standing One that has been encouraged to pause near a dry plateau can transform that place into a wetter zone over decades. A Standing One that moves into a gap can open a passage. A Standing One that refuses to stand up can end a district.

Not all large things are gentle. The city’s fauna includes predators too fast to describe clearly.

In vertical seams, where the air is cool and the stone slick, there are thread-maws or seam-needles: elongated pale bodies that move as if pouring themselves through cracks. They have no stable shape when observed quickly. To catch one, you would have to stop it mid-slip, and the city does not stop. They hunt young shell-creatures, drifting fragments of veil-cloth, careless tide-crabs, and occasionally a child who has wandered too far into an unlined passage. They strike by tasting vibration: a hand against a wall, a foot too lightly placed, the wet click of a molted shell. They are not malicious. They are efficient in a way that makes efficiency look like cruelty in the dark.

Other dangers are slower: water that runs upward too long, leaving a thread behind that can snag a limb; floors supported by compressed dark air, which can lose their tension during a thermal shift; bridges that resemble frozen lightning between spires and feel brittle unless walked at the right speed. Some passages are simply too wet for a body like yours, too narrow for a body like theirs, too open to the drop where no bottom appears. The Accreted know these dangers, but knowing them is not the same as escaping them. The city is not hostile. It is unfinished. That is worse.

The architecture itself is alive in several senses.

Its walls ripple when touched and then set again. Hexagonal panels hang open like lungs, revealing internal scaffolding that glitters faintly when the temperature falls. Some infrastructure seems meant for gravity rather than weather. Some seems meant for signal. There are places where water runs upward along thin threads. There are floors supported by compressed dark air. There are bridges like frozen lightning. The engineering goes beyond ordinary mechanics, but its makers left no names on it, or the names have become geology. The city’s later inhabitants therefore treat its mechanisms as habitats.

A living lattice wall may begin as construction and become a crop shelf. A conduit may begin as signal-routing and become a nursery shaft. A basin may begin as a reservoir and become a tuning chamber. The hexagonal panels open and close not simply because someone wants them open or closed, but because the temperature requires the city to breathe. When many panels open in sequence during a low thermal phase, the sound is like a thousand slow wings lifting. When they close, the darkness seems to thicken, and the wet surfaces hold the faint residual blue-white glow of friction.

This built world has moods, if mood is the wrong word, if not quite the right one.

It has temperatures that encourage certain kinds of life and discourage others. It has resonances that can make a crowd gather in a shaft because the vibration soothes the joints, or drive them away because a plate has begun to hum at the wrong pitch. It has memory in water patterns. A channel that has carried the same mineral flow for generations may bead differently when touched. A wall that has been tapped too often by the same family may respond with heat before the next touch arrives. This is not magic, unless you have a narrow idea of what the living can be. It is long-term conditioning in a medium that grows, sheds, sets, and cracks.

So when asked who lives here, the first answer is not one species at all.

There are the dust-layers. There are the black sand tides. There are the veil-cloths. There are the wave-crabs. There are the Standing Ones. There are the seam-needlers and the shell-borers and the frost-lice and the glass-worms that move so fast they leave afterimages like scratched film. There are the small things that make the city slippery. There are the things that make the city audible. There are the things that make the city habitable. There are the things that make the city dangerous. All of these are inhabitants.

And there are the Accreted.

They are the people, or the closest thing to people. Their name is only a description: *those who have grown by layers*. They are not quite animal and not quite machine. They are made of mineral plates fitted around a wet interior, a network of water veins, and dark filaments that answer to pressure before light. They grow by accretion and shedding. Their bodies are not fixed molds. One person may be broad and shelf-like, suited to living inside a balcony lattice; another may be narrow and jointed, suited to traveling conduits; another may be dense, low, slow, suited to a basin edge. They share a pattern more than a form.

If you met one, you might not recognize them at first. They are too wet and too still, too like the architecture when they rest. Their plates may be black glass, translucent frost, gray basalt, or a colorless silica that seems to hold smoke inside. Between the plates there is moisture: thin films, beads, threads, sometimes a faint blue-white shimmer where friction catches. They do not have faces in the ordinary sense. They have pressure organs, humidity pits, heat lines, and taste grooves. They navigate by what the air does to their skin, what the wall does under a hand, what a vibration does in the bones before it reaches the ears.

They do not use eyes. Eyes would require a kind of clarity that this place does not provide. In the dark, the city is not empty space. It is a field of temperatures, currents, wetness, mineral taste, and sound. A person can stand with a palm against a column and know that someone is climbing three shafts below because their footsteps alter the damp along a seam. A child can tell a relative by the way water beads around their joints. A stranger may be identified by their dryness, which is always suspicious, or by the particular rhythm of their shell-clicking, which reveals too much haste.

Their movement is constant but slow. In high cold, intention becomes expensive. A gesture may take time to complete, not because the body is weak, but because heat must be redistributed, moisture moved, pressure balanced. They do not run in ordinary passages. They flow. They enter shafts that should not fit them and emerge in spaces that seem impossible, because their plates can shift, their limbs can tuck, their water veins can narrow and widen. They touch walls constantly. This is not clumsiness. It is speech.

Their families are not pairs, or not only pairs. A family may be a line of accretion: a sequence of shed plates that share a mineral signature, a water pattern, a name-rhythm. Some persons may be raised by the same shell-line without ever touching the same basin. Some shed so often that their identity becomes less a body than a sequence of bodies. Others retain their first plates for decades and move as if carrying an architecture of their own birth. Children are grown from basins: a slurry of condensation, dust-shells, shell fragments, and tuned mineral rods is placed into a mold left by an ancestor. Over months it settles. Water evaporates slightly. Plates knit. The new person does not wake all at once; it begins by responding, first to warmth, then to tapping, then to the particular taste of its line.

This is why the Accreted speak little about being made. They speak about being finished.

They consider themselves unfinished, as does the city. A body that has stopped shedding is thought to be in danger of becoming a wall. A wall that sheds too much is thought to be becoming a body. This is not a metaphor to them. They live at the boundary between built and grown, and they know that the boundary is wet.

Their social layers are vertical and humid.

On the upper terraces live the Terrace Folk, broad, dry-skinned in the relative sense, adapted to open black stone and wind over columns. They follow the black sand tides and the wave-crab migrations. They know the language of clicking. They can tell whether a thermal veil is about to brighten by the angle at which frost forms on a railing. They are the city’s horizon-readers, though there is no horizon.

Below them, in the underbridges and hanging platforms, live the Veil-tenders or Underfolk. Their plates are paler, often translucent, because they live close to condensation. They farm filter-cloths, trim hanging filaments, harvest secretions, and maintain the membranes that catch water from the thin air. They know which seams are alive and which are merely hungry. They can press their hands to a bridge’s underside and feel where the city is breathing.

Near the basins at the plateau’s edge live the Basinkeepers, perhaps the most respected and most cautious of all. The basins receive meltwater before any ice is visible, which is one of the city’s quiet impossibilities. The Basinkeepers do not explain it. They listen. The basins hum faintly when touched, a sound felt in the teeth before the ears. A Basinkeeper may spend years learning the correct sequence of taps to bring water early, or to stop it from arriving at the wrong place. They can tell by hum alone whether a basin’s interior is clear, whether dust-shells have clogged its pores, whether a Standing One has moved too close, whether a signal-thread in a nearby shaft has changed direction.

In the dense nodes where passageways meet like geological knots, there are Node-listeners. They live in cramped shelves and spurs, in rooms made of architecture that seems to have folded over itself. They maintain intersections. They know where compressed air supports a floor, where water runs upward along thin threads, where a panel may close too quickly. They are often the ones who find a lost person before the darkness has fully taken their shape. A Node-listener’s hand can be pressed against a junction for hours, reading the tremor of water, the pressure of air, the faint mineral scent of fear.

Among the Standing Ones there are the Barnacle-folk, or the Shadow-dwellers, or the Leg-people. Some of them have lived so long in the crevices of Slow Ones that their plates resemble those of their hosts. They know which movements are coming before the tremor arrives. They harvest flakes, ride the migrations of small creatures that live in the shadows, and descend only rarely. They are not exactly servants of the Standing Ones, nor parasites, nor pets. They are an extension of the Standing Ones’ own ecology. A child raised in a Standing One’s shadow may be unable to tolerate a normal terrace’s dryness.

The Accreted have no word for silence. Silence is what happens when a channel collapses and all resonance stops. They fear it. The city’s soundscape is too continuous to permit quiet, but when a quiet begins, it means something has died: a column cracked, a bridge lost tension, a basin drained, a panel failed to open. In such moments the Accreted gather and tap. They tap in slow rhythms, not to make music exactly, but to see if the city will answer. Some structures are tuned. Some structures are stressed. A wrong note can travel through mineral glass like a wound.

Their work is mostly maintenance, but maintenance here is ecology, religion, agriculture, engineering, and memory at once. They clear dust-shells from conduits so that condensation can form correctly. They scrape frost-lace from ledges to reveal hidden cracks. They press water through mineral rods to encourage growth. They feed certain colonies by leaving shell-paste near warm seams. They tune columns by attaching small plates to their sides, so that when the wind comes from above, the tone shifts and the lattice below responds. They open hexagonal panels at the right time, or encourage them to open with heat and friction. They harvest veil-cloth secretions to make membranes. They collect molted tide-crab shells to tile balconies. They follow Standing Ones at safe distances to see where new wet places appear. They watch the black sand to know when to move. They listen to the basins to know when water is coming. They taste the air to know if a wall is becoming dangerous.

They have no cities beyond this one, or if they do, this place does not speak to them. The high basin, the cloud floor below, the tiered ridges and causeways cut into frozen lakes, the spires leaning across gaps too deep to see: all of this is one continuous habitat. To move from terrace to terrace is not to travel through neighborhoods but through organs. A traveler may pass through a place that seems empty and then feel the walls ripple. They may step onto what seems like open space and be caught by a railing that has grown toward them. They may enter a shaft that seems to end in darkness and find that the darkness is breathing.

The Accreted themselves move like this. A person may be inside a wall, not hidden but integrated. They press their plates against surfaces and let the city read them. Their sheds may become part of the lattice. Their breath may alter frost patterns. Their taps may set panels in motion. They do not live atop the city or inside it in the ordinary human sense. They live with it. They have learned its moods, and the city has learned their habits, and the boundary between one and the other has softened.

Their language is difficult for a hearing creature to grasp. It includes tapping, pressure marks, temperature shifts, humidity beads, shell-rattles, and chemical residues. A phrase can be left on a wall as a line of water followed by a dry scrape. A warning can be a particular vibration sent through a causeway. A name can be a pattern of condensation on a plate, a rhythm of clicks, a place in a vertical family line. To address someone is to touch a part of their body in the correct order: a shoulder plate, a forearm seam, a wrist joint, then a brief stillness. To insult is to press too long, too dry, too warm. To apologize is to shed a small flake and offer it.

Their art is usually tuned. A musician may press against a stressed plate until the city gives back a tone felt in the teeth. A sculptor may guide mineral rods into curves that will remain wet without dripping. A storyteller may line a conduit with beads so that later travelers can read the story by touch, water carrying the mineral signs downward. Some forms are purely architectural: a family may spend generations growing a passage so that when a certain thermal veil opens, the walls throw faint blue light into a chamber at a particular angle. This is not decoration. It is memory made spatial.

Their religion, if that word can be applied without becoming too bright, too human, is a practice of non-completion.

The city has not finished becoming. Therefore the Accreted do not worship a finished world. They do not long for a time when everything is clear, dry, illuminated, explained. They are suspicious of clarity. Dryness is suspicious. Stillness is suspicious. A thing that will not ripple, bead, breathe, or shed is either dead or dangerous. Their most common prayer, if a prayer can be so quiet, is not to ask for permanence but for permission to continue. The correct response to a new layer is not completion but care. To finish a thing is to end its becoming. To leave it unfinished is to allow another body, another animal, another wall, another self to continue.

Their idea of death is equally mineral.

When an Accreted person can no longer shed, when their water veins dry or fail, when their plates stop responding to touch, they are carried to a basin or a living lattice shelf. Their body is not buried in earth, because there is no soil. It is returned to the city. Over time, their mineral parts accrete into walls, panels, conduits, frost patterns. A dead person’s shell may become part of a balcony. Their water may become part of a tuned column. Their last plates may be placed in a junction so that the city can remember their rhythm when touched by descendants. To become architecture is not a lesser fate here. It is to become a place where life can continue.

This is why the place feels dense and close even when empty. It is not only full of organisms. It is full of the previous bodies of organisms. The walls contain lines, the passageways contain ancestors, the shelves contain shed identities. A hand pressed to a wall may feel warmth that has no source. A traveler may brush a railing that appears just as the hand reaches into open space. Sometimes that railing is a person, sometimes it is a person’s history, sometimes it is the city imitating a person. These categories overlap so often that to insist on a clean distinction is to miss the place.

If you ask what lives there, you must also mention what does not live there in the ordinary sense but is mistaken for life.

Some things in the dark are not animals and not persons. They are process-shapes: masses of frozen smoke held by weak fields, currents that seem to move like creatures, patterns of condensation that repeat and therefore suggest intention. The Accreted know the difference, but they do not always explain it. There are places where the air becomes too occupied and nothing is there. There are places where water rises too quickly and forms a curve like a back. There are places where panels open without being touched and then set again, as if the building has dreamed. These phenomena may be mechanisms, may be life, may be both. In a world without plants, where the line between built and formed is blurred, a thing can become a creature simply by continuing.

The city’s fauna also includes symbionts, parasites, and accidental dwellings.

Dust-shells live in the grooves of Accreted plates. Small mites feed on shed flakes in their joints. Some children develop glass-worms in their water veins, and the child’s movement becomes slightly wrong, too liquid, too quick. Some adults carry colonies so integrated that the colony helps regulate humidity. A person without the right dust-shells may dry out too easily and be considered ill. A person with too many may become too slow, too encrusted, too much like a wall. This is a balance the Accreted spend their lives maintaining. They bathe not in clean water but in directed mineral baths, sometimes scraping their bodies against a conduit that has been tuned to remove parasites without removing identity.

Wave-crabs host tiny crustaceanoids inside their shells. When a tide-crab molts, these inhabitants may remain in the empty shell, forming a secondary household until the shell is collected. Some empty shells become nurseries. Some become traps. Some become tools with their own habits. A person who works a shell too roughly may find it later refusing to sit properly, or beading with an odor that suggests something has moved into it.

The Standing Ones are perhaps the most complex symbionts. Their bodies host entire microecologies: frost-lace, black sand, veil-cloth, shell-lice, small clicking creatures that live in the spaces between plates. Their shed flakes feed the terraces. Their tremors reveal new channels. Their movements change humidity patterns for years. They are large enough to become habitats, too slow to be prey, too indifferent to be gods. The Accreted speak of them as they speak of weather, but weather with joints. If one dies, its body may remain standing for centuries, becoming a ridge, a lattice, a wall. A dead Standing One may support more life than a living one, because death in this place is not cessation but redistribution.

This is also why erosion does not seem like damage here.

Water finds paths faster than stone learns them. A surface may look unfinished, raw, shedding chips, pitted by ancient gas, threaded with newer channels. But that unfinishedness is not failure. It is the city’s method. The water reveals the next layer. The animal bodies, the filter-cloths, the black sand, the Accreted themselves, all participate in this continuous unmaking and remaking. A wall is eroded and a wall grows. A conduit is clogged and a conduit opens. A person sheds and a person remains. A city is damaged and a city becomes.

If you were to stay long enough, you would begin to see individuals.

Not at first. The darkness refuses that convenience. But with time, a figure near a panel becomes recognizable: broad plates, a slight forward tilt, one shoulder shelf lower than the other, condensation catching in a particular groove. Someone is listening to a junction. Someone else is moving through a shaft by touch, one hand trailing along the wet seam, another checking the railing that should not be there but is. A child is being taught to make a veil-cloth bead. A Basinkeeper is tapping a reservoir edge until the hum changes. An elder has fused so deeply with a shelf that only their hands move, still capable, still choosing. A traveler from somewhere impossible might stand in a doorway and not understand why the walls seem to notice them.

The inhabitants would notice.

They would not approach quickly. They would assess humidity, temperature, dryness, vibration. They would press one plate to another and wait. They would tap a wall and listen for the traveler’s heartbeat, if such a thing has a rhythm they can recognize. They might bring water, not to offer as ordinary water but to show they understand the traveler’s need and also do not entirely understand it. They might leave a small shell beside a ledge. The shell may have been harvested from a tide-crab or shed by a child. Either way, it is a mark of intention: *you are not yet part of the city, but you may not be entirely outside it either.*

They have many names, most of them impossible for outsiders to use without making the names smaller than they are.

Some are called by the place where their line was poured. Some by the sound they make when they shed. Some by the angle at which water runs down their plates. Some by the basin they tend. Some by the Standing One they have followed for generations. A person may be called *Third-Lattice, Cold-Seam-Beader*, or simply *the one whose hand returns when touched twice*. These names are not fixed. If a person changes significantly—if their line alters, if they merge into a node, if they die and become part of a column—the name may remain, but its meaning shifts. This is because the city does not accept a clean separation between person, place, and residue.

They are, above all, slow. Not because they are lazy, but because their world demands it. Cold slows intention. Wetness requires patience. Mineral bodies cannot afford sudden motion. Their decisions often take hours, days, seasons. A proposal to open a passage may begin with a single tap and end with a crowd pressing against a wall for a full thermal cycle. They do not hurry because haste can break a plate, dry a seam, wake the wrong panel, startle a wave-crab tide into a fatal direction, or misalign a signal.

But they also notice beauty. They notice it differently.

A bridge may be beautiful not because it looks like a frozen current but because it carries the right temperature through the right gap. A veil-cloth may be beautiful when it beads evenly along a dark seam, forming a map of invisible moisture. A Standing One may be beautiful when its shadow becomes wet enough for dust-shells to colonize, beginning a new garden of chalky flakes. A person may be beautiful when they shed cleanly, when their plates fall in a pattern that suggests their line is undisturbed. A city is beautiful when it breathes, when panels open in response to a falling temperature and the inside scaffolding glitters faintly with cold light.

There are also horrors.

A wall that has stopped rippling. A channel that smells too dry. A panel that closes too quickly and traps a hand. A causeway where compressed air supports fail and the floor becomes uncertain. A gap where the bottom is not bottom. A sound from a column that has changed pitch and cannot be tuned. A child born from a basin after the wrong hum. A Standing One that has shifted in its sleep and crushed a ledge. A veil-cloth that has grown too thick and blocks a passage, becoming a breathing obstruction that must be cut but cannot be cut too much. A dust-shell bloom so dense that a person’s joints begin to set into it, not because they want to, but because the city is offering them a place and they are too weak to refuse.

These dangers are not dramatic in the way a human might recognize. They are environmental, slow, patient. The city can end a life without ever appearing to notice.

And yet the place is full.

It is full of bodies that would seem strange to a world with forests, grasslands, birds, insects made of air and blood. Here, bodies are made of water, stone, silica, condensation, weak fields, heat gradients, mineral dust, and signal. Life is not soft here. Life is not green. Life is wet and black and pale and clicking, growing by accretion, shedding by necessity, moving by patience. Life includes the architecture. Life includes the swarms. Life includes the vast slow beings that stand like unfinished monuments. Life includes the Accreted, who did not build the city and have learned to live by becoming it.

If one insists on a single answer, the closest is this: the city is inhabited by a process. But the process has bodies. The bodies have habits. The habits have meanings. The meanings have generations. The generations are not finished.

What lives there are not the people alone, though there are people. They are not the animals alone, though the animals are overwhelming. They are not the machines, though much of what they use was once built beyond ordinary mechanics. They are not the water, though the water is everywhere and appears from nowhere and everywhere. They are not the stone, though the stone is learning to be permanent. They are the combined becoming of all these things, the slow crowded occupation of black terraces under high clouds, the sound of life moving through wet dark, the pressure of bodies pressing against walls, the walls answering.

The inhabitants are the dust and the tide. The inhabitants are the hanging pale curtains and the clicking flats. The inhabitants are the monument-herd and the seam-hunters. The inhabitants are the breathing panels, the hummed basins, the upward water threads, the frozen-current bridges, the compressed-air floors. The inhabitants are the Accreted, with their layered plates and water-veined thoughts, their family-lines, their shed selves, their patience in the cold.

And if you ask what they want, the answer is not simple. They do not want to finish. They do not want to understand everything. They do not want to master the city, because the city is already partly themselves. They want the next layer to be legible. They want the next water path to open. They want the next panel to breathe. They want the next child to settle cleanly in the basin. They want the next Standing One to move far enough to wet the plateau, or not to move far enough to crush the shelves. They want the dust to remain useful. They want the dark to remain occupied. They want to become, and to continue becoming, without ever arriving at the dry bright state that other worlds call completed.

So the city does not feel haunted because it is empty. It feels haunted because it is crowded with unfinishedness, and unfinishedness is alive.
