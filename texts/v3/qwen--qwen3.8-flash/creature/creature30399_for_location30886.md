What lives there lives as a chorus, not a crowd. The town is not a dense pile of dwellings but a scattering of resonant forms set along the crescent, spaced as carefully as the trees, each structure connected to the next by paths tooled into salt-crystal and lined with the living crust of the ground. The beings who inhabit it are sparse, slow, long-limbed, and tuned. They do not rush. Rushing would be a kind of violence, and violence is difficult to hide in a place where every step can be heard for miles.

They stand taller than a person on wet worlds but thinner than a reed, with three, sometimes four long legs that end in broad porous plates. Their bodies are lattices of silicate fiber, copper-thread, and translucent mineral, with joints that gleam like polished glass and chambers hollowed inside. They have no soft skin, no fur, no wet membrane that could dry and crack. Their bodies are instead sealed around membranes, folds of thin crystalline material through which gases pass in and out, absorbing and releasing, condensing and sublimating. There is no blood pooling in open vessels. Whatever travels inside them moves in capillary channels, like a fine mineral fog that becomes briefly liquid only where heat or pressure changes demand it.

When one of them walks, it makes a soft succession of chimes. When several walk, the town sounds like a dry river.

Their heads have no faces in a familiar sense. There are no eyes to blink, no moisture to spread, no wet cornea to reflect the copper-brown sky. Instead, along the front of the skull and down the flanks of the thorax, there are arrays of faceted crystal, pressure pits, and narrow slits lined with sensitive filament. They see polarized light, stress in stone, and heat gradients more clearly than they see surfaces. Their vision is not a picture so much as a field of tensions: the way a slope leans into wind, the way a tree is about to hum, the way a herd’s plates will fail if startled. They also see vibration. A disturbance on salt can be perceived as a bright ripple through their skeleton.

They hear with the whole body. Every plate, every fiber, every hollow chamber is an ear. A single stone shifting in the distance is to them as distinct as a spoken word. In the Dry Crown, silence is not absence of sound; silence is a held breath, a pause where too much is still moving through the substrate.

They breathe through vanes and flutes. Their exhale is a sour haze of sulfur and tin, a dry cloud that lingers briefly before vanishing, leaving a faint smell of struck metal and cold stone. Their voices are produced by shaping this breath through internal chambers, but speech among them is more than voice. It is chord. A conversation is a layered structure of clicks, harmonics, sub-bass pulses, and the ground-stomp of feet. They speak with the earth as well as to one another. Their names are not sounds alone but combinations of tone and resonance, a phrase that can be sung into the living substrate and remembered by the ground itself.

They do not build the way builders build in wet places. The town is grown. The arches are secretions hardened over centuries. The walls are mineral skins laid down by the substrate under careful instruction, coaxed into hollows and flutes by the singers who live within them. When a dwelling cracks, it is mended not with paste or patch but with song. The Tuned, as one might call them, hum at frequencies that encourage filaments to bridge gaps, that guide crystal veins into new patterns, that coax the town’s living skeleton into a safer shape.

Children are not born wet and screaming. They emerge from nursery arches at the town’s edge, soft lattices grown from seeds taken from the substrate and tended by elder singers. A juvenile begins almost colorless, mute, fragile, and hollow in places. Years pass while its chambers deepen, its plates harden, and its flanges learn to receive the wind. When it first finds its own tone, that tone is its name. Later it may learn other names, other registers, but its original chord is kept by the ground. When the being dies, the ground remembers it.

Death in the Dry Crown is not rot. There is no decay as one might imagine from damp worlds, no wet softening, no collapse into blackness. Instead, bodies shatter, weather, dissolve into dust, or become mineralized into the substrate. The Tuned do not bury their dead so much as return them. A body may be placed among the hollow trees, where its mineral lattice becomes a seed-structure for new growth. It may be broken into plates and set along a ridge so the wind can take it apart gently, carrying its atoms into the living skin of the world. Some graves become small forests of crystal stems. Others become bells.

Around the town move the animals.

The salt-plate herds are the most obvious. They are broad, shield-backed creatures, segmented like armor, low enough to slide through dust ribbons, wide enough to cover their young beneath their plates. They travel across the flats in families of dozens, sometimes hundreds, their legs striking the salt crust in slow syncopation. Their backs are ridged with crystalline fins that catch the wind, and when the herd moves, the sound rolls across the valley like distant hail. They are not beautiful in any wet sense; they are too clean, too bright, too dry. They move like plates of armor animated by patience.

The plate-herds feed on the living skin of the salt flats, which hardens under the pale filtered sun and lifts into microscopic fringes at dusk. Their mouths are not chewing organs but broad sealed membranes that press against the ground and draw volatile compounds, mineral threads, and microscopic life into internal filters. They do not drink. They absorb the moisture-equivalent of their world through membranes and gas exchange, drawing sustenance from the dry chemistry around them. At night they graze the lifted fringes in long, slow waves. In the morning, the flats are bare again, and the herds lie in the shadow of crystal shrubs, motionless, humming with the residual tone of their feeding.

The dust-skimmers are different. They are thin, flat creatures that glide over the ground like sheets of living mica, supported by dozens of tiny filament legs. They do not walk so much as hover just above the ankle-high dust, riding the low electrostatic currents that move through the matured powder. Their bodies are nearly transparent at the edges, rimmed with gold and cobalt veins, and when they pass over a basin, they leave behind a faint shimmering wake, as though the dust has momentarily remembered an older form. Skimmers feed by filtering microscopic crystals, spore-like mineral cells, and the drifting fringes that rise from the ground skin. They are silent except for a thin singing at the edges of their fins, a sound like wire held in a cold wind.

Among the hollow trees live smaller creatures: the thread-grazers, the shard-moths, the copper-laced walkers that resemble no familiar insect because they lack wetness, eyes, or soft tissue. Thread-grazers are delicate, glass-jointed beings that move along the crystalline shrubs and filament mats, feeding on the gas exchange of the plants. They appear as pale sparks at night, moving through the blue-black veins of the stems, leaving trails of faint phosphorescence. Shard-moths are not moths, but they behave like them: dry-bodied, flanged, and attracted to resonance. They gather at the mouths of hollow trees at dusk, when the wind changes pitch, and their wing-plates click softly against one another until the tree’s song becomes a chorus with them.

Predators exist, but they do not hunt by speed alone. In a world where sound carries with terrible clarity, speed is dangerous. A fast animal is an animal announced by every stone it disturbs. Therefore the predators are acoustic. The vane-hunters, slender and sharp-angled, lie motionless in wind-carved basins, listening for the wrongness in the ground’s skin: a plate-gazer startled from grazing, a skimmer’s fin out of tune, a thread-grazer’s rhythm broken by thirst or fear. They move then not with sudden leaps but with careful silence, placing each broad foot pad so that it touches only dead zones in the substrate. When they strike, they do not bite like a fanged thing. They produce a focused tone. The sound finds the brittle weakness in prey plates, stresses the crystal lattice of the legs, or vibrates a membrane until it loses tension. A herd can be separated by a single hunter’s note.

But most life in the Dry Crown is defensive, woven into resonance rather than flesh. If a predator comes too close to the town, the living ground can harden beneath its feet, forming a brittle ridge that sends a warning pulse through the mineral network. The pulse reaches the nearest hollow trees, and the trees answer with low bass throbs. The bells in the town’s arches begin to move even without wind, as though the buildings are startled awake. The Tuned emerge without urgency, their bodies dim with slow chemical light, and by the time they gather at the crescent’s edge, the predator is no longer attacking the town. It is being listened to until the listening becomes too loud for it to bear.

The hollow trees are not only habitats; they are civic architecture, organs, ancestors, and musicians. Their fluted trunks contain chambers large enough for the Tuned to enter, and many of them do. Some families live inside the lowest chambers, where the stone is warmest from the day’s filtered sun. Others climb the higher branches, where the mineral threads fan out into nets and the wind plays continuously. The trees are tended. The Tuned do not cut them. To cut a hollow tree would be to sever a note from the forest. Instead, they repair them. They graft filament threads into fractures. They scrape salt-like veins to alter pitch. They seal wounds with mineral paste grown from the substrate and press their own bodies against the bark until the tree accepts the patch as part of itself.

Old trees become communal instruments. A single hollow tree may contain hundreds of chambers, each producing a different tone, some open to the sky, some sealed except for narrow slits. In winter or its equivalent—when the wind thins and the air grows more metallic—the forest plays a deep, shifting music that can last for days. In the town, the inhabitants sleep according to that music. They rise when the lower tones strengthen. They gather in the crescent’s center when the high stems begin to shriek. They do not consider the sound pleasant, not always. They consider it necessary.

The ground itself is the deepest life of the place.

It is called the living substrate because it behaves as one organism stretched thin beneath the land. It covers every exposed surface: salt flats, stone flutes, wind basins, cracks in mountains, the undersides of crystal shrubs. It is not green, and it is not moss in the way of water.
