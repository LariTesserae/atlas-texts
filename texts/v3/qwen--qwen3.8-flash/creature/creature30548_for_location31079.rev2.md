To look for inhabitants in the usual way would be to find none, and then to call the hollow empty. But that would mistake the scale of living. The absence of animals is not an emptiness that asks to be filled. It is a condition, a long answer given by salt, shade, dampness, warmth, and the thinness of what can survive there. What lives in the hollow lives low, slow, and close to the surface: as skin, cushion, film, thread, crust, and quiet patch of color that might, from too far away, seem only mineral.

The first residents one might notice are the lichens. They are not plants in the ordinary sense, nor are they single beings. Each is an old collaboration: a fungal body and a photosynthetic partner sharing a life in which neither can quite be said to dominate. Together they have learned to live on almost nothing: light, stone, dust, condensation, the faint trace of mineral carried by wind. Across the pale limestone plates they spread as discs and stains, soft gray, chalk-white, rust-flecked, ochre at the margins. Some lie so thinly upon the stone that they seem only discoloration. Others form tiny maps of themselves, circular at first, then irregular, with cracks running through them as if their boundaries were being slowly redrawn by patience alone.

They favor the uppermost surfaces, where water drains and the stone is exposed. In such places the lichens become the true ground-skin, not covering so much as interpreting the stone. A plate of ash-colored limestone with lichen on it is not a bare surface with a mark upon it; it is a surface inhabited. The lichen catches warmth, holds a thin film of moisture, and gives back texture. Where it grows, the stone feels different under the hand, not simply warm and smooth, but slightly rough, faintly alive, as if time had accumulated in a visible way without hurry. On the pebbles near the rim, lichens colonize only the side facing upward and outward, while the underside remains paler, untouched, as though each small stone were a room with one occupied wall and one empty floor.

In the seams between limestone plates, moss takes over where the lichens cannot. The clay seams hold a little dampness longer, especially where the hollow’s shade lowers into coolness, and there the moss forms thin cushions. They are not lush. They do not suggest wet woodland or spring. They are firm, compressed, dark green or black-green, sometimes tinged with olive or red-brown, and at first glance they may look like small bruises on the earth. But when moisture gathers from condensation or a brief shower, they loosen. Their leaves, dry and close-pressed when parched, swell open. A cushion that seemed only a stain becomes briefly legible: a miniature forest, perhaps a small mossy roof, perhaps a soft green cap resting over a mouth of water.

The mosses live at the edge of existence and do not mind it. They know how to pause. They take up water, hold it between their leaves, and then let it pass into air and clay, leaving them dry again, almost invisible. Their life is not dramatic, but it is not passive either; they shape the moisture they are given. In their cushions, tiny troughs form. Salt crystals settle into them. Dust collects. A thin skin of algae may grow on their damp surfaces, making them feel slick rather than merely soft. Thus each moss cushion becomes a small station in the larger movement of water through the hollow, a place where mineral and living process briefly join.

Below the moss, in the densest clay where shallow pans collect water that does not deepen, a greener darkness may gather. This is the biofilm. It is the most humble inhabitant of the hollow, a living film that can appear after condensation in the wet seams, spreading until it dries into a pale dust or a blackish crust. It is not one organism but a congregation: algae, bacteria, fungal threads, salt, fine clay, dead skin fragments of grass, all woven into a surface that behaves almost like a membrane. When wet, it may be faintly elastic, glossy, dark. When dry, it cracks. It whitens at the edges. It becomes powder in the creases of clay. But the powder is not death exactly; it is dormancy. With another dampness, another passing cloud, another night of condensation, it may begin again.

The biofilm is the hollow’s first skin, often older in form than the moss, older than the grass, older perhaps than the stones’ current shapes. It is what lives where nothing else wants to grow. It tolerates the salt, the thinness of water, the heat, the sudden drying. It does not build tall things. It does not make structure. It makes surface. It prepares the ground. Where it dies, it leaves a film of organic residue; where it remains, it catches spores, dust, tiny mineral particles, and makes them part of the living crust. It is easy to dismiss such a film as dirt, but it is the sort of dirt that has been kept in motion by life.

Within that motion, fungus has its own unseen residence. Fungal threads run through the cracks and beneath the clay. In the dry ground they are nearly impossible to perceive, as if the hollow has no interior life at all, but after moisture enters the cracks, they may swell faintly, or their fruiting bodies may appear as tiny pale discs or dust-like specks in protected hollows beneath stones. They are the digesters of what little organic matter arrives. If a leaf is blown into the hollow from beyond the rim, it does not vanish in a dramatic decay. It dries, curls, becomes brittle. Then, over many seasons, fungal threads and microbial films break it into fragments. The fragments settle into the seams. They are not litter; they become texture. They are not consumed by animals; they are transformed into surface. In this way the hollow takes the world outside itself and returns it to its own pattern.

The grasses are the tallest residents, though even they are low. They grow only where the salt is not too strong, where a pocket of soil deep enough has been sheltered from the constant crust of mineral, where a shallow pan allows a longer retention of damp. Each tuft stands apart from the next, isolated, as if the ground around it is too harsh for companionship. Yet these tufts are not lonely mistakes. They are precisely adapted. Their roots do not form a mat. They anchor into a small, private depth: a pocket of clay beneath the salt skin, a pocket where moisture lingers after the surface has dried. Their blades are pale, stiff, coarse, and narrow. They do not flutter so much as resist. In the warm air they remain upright or lean slightly, their tips dried and bent, their crowns tight and resistant.

Each grass tuft is a small inhabited place. Around its base, moss may cling if the ground is damp enough. Salt may gather in the little hollow it creates. Lichens may touch the lower leaves. Tiny films of algae may grow where the crown holds water. The grass’s own old blades, when they break, do not disappear. They curl into the seams, darken slightly, dry pale, and eventually become part of the mineral-organic texture of the floor. The grass does not dominate the hollow. It punctuates it, as the prompt said, like a mark in sparse script.

But one must be careful with the word life here, because the salt is also an inhabitant, though not alive. The salt crust behaves as if it had its own moods. It gathers in shallow depressions and low edges. It whitens, dulls, cracks, and then becomes glossy when the faint condensation returns. It forms a polished skin over the damp pits, thin as breath, brittle as old varnish. It grows around the bases of grass tufts and along the lines where clay meets stone. It seals pores and opens them. It draws water upward by capillary memory and then leaves it behind in crystalline residue. Salt is not a being, yet in this hollow it has a presence so consistent that it becomes part of the local personality. It makes the water briny. It stiffens the ground. It forbids the kinds of softness that animals need. It is a mineral citizen of the hollow, as settled as the rim.

Water too lives here, though only intermittently, and with restraint. It is not a resident body in the sense of a pond, stream, or pool. It is more like a visitor that knows its old rooms too well to make a scene. It arrives as condensation, darkening certain stones before the air reaches full warmth. It gathers in tiny pits no larger than a hand. It beads and trickles through hidden channels already worn into equilibrium. It falls as a brief shower and collects in shallow pans at the bottom of old channels. It remains a little while, then withdraws. It does not vanish all at once; it fades. It moves from visible film to mineral sheen to salt crust to the faint dampness inside pores. The hollow remembers water in a texture more than a shape.

The shallow pans are therefore not empty places. They are inhabited by water in a temporary form, and by the life that depends on that temporary water: moss, biofilm, algae, fungal threads, salt crystals. In the dry season they are not dry in the same way as a desert; they are only emptied of surface water. Their bottoms remain dense, sometimes glossy, sometimes stained, sometimes dusted with white. The ground around them is marked by faint lines where moisture once traveled. These lines are the hollow’s writing, and they are read by salt, moss, lichen, and clay.

The dim light is another resident, though again not alive. It enters the hollow at a shallow angle, catches edges, softens the salt crust, reveals the ridges of stone. It does not make the place vivid; it makes it legible. In the center the light is uneven, as if a low ceiling of shade has been drawn. Along the rim the shade is deeper, warmer in its own way, retaining coolness where the air cannot fully reach. The warmth is not harsh. It makes the stone feel settled, drowsy, almost human in its repose. Light and heat are not mere conditions here; they are parts of the ecology. They choose which surfaces lichens can occupy, which mosses can survive, which salt pans remain damp, which grasses get enough warmth to complete their slow cycles. If the hollow has an atmosphere, the light is its face.

The sounds are few, but even they have their own inhabitants. The slow drip where condensation gathers on the rim and falls into a pan. The tick of dry salt crust cracking under slight warming. The breath of air across stone. These are not calls or signals. They are the voices of temperature and moisture, not of moving bodies. They belong to the non-animal life of the place, the life of surfaces adjusting to one another. The sound does not carry far, because the hollow is small and its enclosure keeps it inward. It is a place of occasional taps, not of silence as pressure. It has quiet, but the quiet is textured. It is made of stone, salt, and the faint memory of water.

The built marks, too, are inhabited, though no human presence remains. Near the western edge, the row of flat stones may once have been a line begun and abandoned, or perhaps no more than natural settling arranged to resemble intention. Now it is neither wholly wall nor wholly rubble. It has become a terrace for life. Lichens settle on their upper faces. Moss grips the cooler sides. Salt crust builds up along the lower edges where moisture gathers and dries. The stones themselves are warm and smooth, their original purpose lost, their current purpose geological and ecological. A row of stones in this hollow is no longer a structure. It is a habitat.

The shallow platform of compressed clay, edged with pebbles, has also crossed into this state. It may once have been a boundary, a terrace, a place where sediment and root mats built up a shelf. Now its surface is firm and uneven, dusted with salt, marked by faint moisture lines. The pebbles around it are not arranged so much as tolerated by the ground. Some have settled into the clay. Some are lichen-stained. Some have a mossy underside if they were once lifted and laid again. The platform is a stage without actors, or rather a stage inhabited by films, salts, and tiny green cushions that do not perform, only persist.

The low cairn-like mound near the center is the most ambiguous resident of the hollow. Three or four worn stones rest on a slightly higher patch of clay. They are smooth. Their balance may be old intention, accident, or long settling. It does not matter now, because whatever meaning they once held has become another layer of the place. The mound is a small elevation, and elevations matter in a hollow where shade and dampness are finely distributed. The upper stone warms earlier. The undersides remain cool. The seam between stones catches dust and salt. If moisture condenses, it collects on the lower side and evaporates slowly, keeping a faint dark line there. Moss may begin in that protected coolness. Lichen may map the exposed top. Salt may gather where rain falls but does not rinse.

The cairn is not a monument. It is more nearly a home for nonlife: shade, salt, mineral film, perhaps a tiny pocket of protected moisture. If it suggests intention, the intention has been dissolved into process. A hand may have placed a stone long ago, but the stone now belongs to the wind’s slow sorting, to the warmth’s repeated expansion and contraction, to the lichens’ patient spreading. The maker is gone, or perhaps never was; either way, the mark remains as an old form no longer doing work. In the hollow, built things do not fall into ruins so much as they become substrates.

Even the emptiness between presences lives there in its own way. The spaces between stone plates, grass tufts, and salt pans are not merely gaps. They are textured with heat, dust, color, and faint mineral crust. They are too firm and too salt-caked for soft ground, too sparse for cover, too warm and stable for movement. The absence of animals is thus not a hole in the pattern. It is a quality of the pattern. The hollow has learned to live without bodies that move, without trampling, without burrowing, without grazing. Its ground remains lightly dressed by its own processes. Nothing thick accumulates because nothing thick is permitted to remain. What arrives too strongly is dried, broken, and returned to texture.

This is why the ecology is thin but real. It is an ecology of margins. The lichens take the high surfaces. The mosses take the damp seams. The biofilms take the shallow pans and cool clay. The grasses take the pockets where salt is lighter and moisture can last. The fungi take the cracks and the buried fragments. The salt takes the whole place as its changing skin. Water moves among them, not as a stream but as a cycle: condensation, dampness, pooling, fading, salt, repetition. Light and shade select which of these processes dominate in each small space. Warmth holds them all in their slow equilibrium.

The inhabitants do not compete in the way a forest competes, where roots knot, leaves crowd, and fallen branches accumulate. There is no crowding. There is no sense of life pressing against life. The mosses do not overrun the lichens; they keep to their own moisture boundaries. The grasses do not spread into a meadow; they remain punctual. The lichens do not cover every stone; they colonize where the surface is suitable. The hollow is sparse because it is balanced. Its lives are not missing. They are calibrated.

Their time is not the time of animals, not the time of hunting, hiding, mating, migrating. It is the time of swell and shrink. A moss cushion expands as water enters, then contracts as the sun lowers the moisture. A salt crust whitens, cracks, darkens, glazes again. A lichen edge advances by a fraction of a millimeter over a season, then remains. Grass tips dry, curl, return to color if enough dampness arrives, then dry again. The ring of moisture around a stone shifts slightly. A single blade of grass, bent at the tip, remains bent, not because it has died in a dramatic way, but because it has completed the motion of living where it lives.

If one insisted on naming “whoever” lives there, one would be speaking of beings without eyes, without appetite, without footprints. One would be speaking of partnerships: fungal and algal bodies that form lichens; moss and microenvironment that form damp cushions; clay and salt and microbial film that form a living crust; grass and root pocket that form isolated tufts; stones and old moisture lines that form inhabited surfaces. But perhaps “whoever” is the wrong word. The more honest word is “what.” What lives there is a pattern. What lives there is the low persistence of surfaces, the slow occupation of moisture by organisms that have learned to make very little into a world.

In the end, the hollow does not feel empty because its emptiness has its own density. The open ground is not vacant. It is occupied by the memory of water, the presence of salt, the warmth of stone, the dim light’s careful movement, the sparse vegetation’s patience, the faint built traces now returned to geology. Even the quiet is not an absence waiting to be broken. It is made of small sounds that do not carry, small adjustments that do not accumulate, small life that does not need to announce itself.

Thus the inhabitants of the hollow are these: the pale discs of lichen mapping the limestone; the green-black biofilms in the damp clay; the thin moss cushions holding the minimum of water; the salt crust polishing the shallow pans; the fungal threads breaking what drifts in into texture; the isolated grass tufts standing where the brine is gentler; the warm shade in the rim’s corners; the dim light reading the edges of stone; the row of flat stones now inhabited by weather; the platform now stage for crust and film; the cairn holding its small shade and its slow mineral growth; the water that comes, gathers, fades, and returns; the salt that crystallizes, darkens, whitens, and seals; the air that moves across stone less than a breeze; the heat that settles into the basin and leaves no mark.

No animals live there. The place does not miss them. It has simply chosen its own citizens: the rooted, the slow, the crust-like, the marginal, the nearly invisible. What lives in the hollow is the patience of surfaces. It is the quiet persistence of things that do not move because movement is not required of them. It is the life of a room-sized hollow holding its water lightly, its stones quietly, its salt and moss and lichen in their small, balanced places, and continuing, without drama, to be what it has become.
