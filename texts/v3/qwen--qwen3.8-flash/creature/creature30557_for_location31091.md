What lives in the hollow is not a population. It is a negotiated organism, a thing made of people, animals, microbes, bracing, heat, and habit. If you enter and watch long enough, the boundary between inhabitant and infrastructure begins to blur. A person is a machine for reading pressure. An animal is a pipe, a lamp, a warning system. A wall is a stomach. An engine is a hearth, a pulse, a god too dangerous to be named casually.

At the center of this strange ecology are the people—if they can still be called people.

## The Braced

They call themselves the Braced, or rather they make the name: three short taps against the breastbone, meaning *held in place*, *prevented from falling apart*, *made to last*. Outsiders would call them the Saltveins, the Hollowborn, the Dry People. Their own term is better. They are not the creators of this place so much as its final, most elaborate repair job.

They are people, but not people of open skies and rivers. If they were born elsewhere, they would not survive; if you were born here, after three generations you would become almost them. Their bodies are compact and dense, built for low ceilings, thin air, and constant vertical movement. They are short by old-world standards, broad through the shoulders and hips, with short necks and long ribcages. Their feet are wide and flat, the toes mobile, the soles leathery and scarred from decades on grates, pipes, and razor edges of young stone. Their fingers are calloused at the pads but strangely sensitive at the tips; many have worn their fingerprints smooth from scraping, bracing, polishing, and feeling for seams.

They are not fragile. Fragility would be a death sentence. Their bones are thick, their joints reinforced by ligaments tightened by generations of carrying metal, stone, and children through shifting passages. Their lungs are deep and slow, adapted to thin air. A Braced person can sit still for an hour in a ventilation shaft while oxygen is scarce, then move in a sudden burst when pressure changes. Their hearts do not race as readily as a lowland person’s. In emergencies, they become calmer, slower, more economical.

Their skin is dry, matte, and often salt-flecked. It cracks around knuckles, elbows, lips, and ears, but the cracks are sealed with resin. They carry small pots of it or store thick paste in the ridges of their clothing. Their lips are often white with salt, not from moisture but from the mineral dust they breathe all day. Their skin may have a faint oily sheen, especially along the forearms and necks, where oils from grubs or bacterial films have been rubbed in to prevent chapping. They smell of warm metal, dust, resin, cured fat, sulfur, and the dry animal musk of their households.

Their eyes are wide, dark-adapted, and dry. They do not cry easily. Their tear ducts have narrowed or become mineral-sealed; the body cannot spare water for grief. When they are distressed, moisture forms elsewhere—sweat caught in salt cloth, breath fogging briefly in the cold, or a tightness in the jaw. Their eyelids have a second translucent film, a nictitating membrane that protects against dust and static spark. In dim light, their pupils become almost completely black. In bright metal reflections, they squint behind those films, and their faces look more like burrowing creatures than people.

Their most remarkable sense is not sight, but vibration.

They hear pressure through their skulls. They feel engine faults in their teeth. They learn where a chamber is weakened by the way a brace hums against their palm. A Braced child can place one hand on a wall and, without speaking, tell another child whether the stone behind it is cooling, cracking, or preparing to shift. Adults can identify the rhythm of a piston from the floor, the quality of resin by the sound it makes when tapped, and the difference between a harmless settling and a dangerous opening. They live in constant tactile conversation with their city.

They rarely shout. Loudness is waste. In thin air, shouting is tiring and carries poorly anyway. Their speech is low, breathy, economical. Consonants click and soften. Vowels are short. They speak much with touch: a hand on the shoulder means *attention*, a light tap on the sternum means *steady*, two fingers on the wrist mean *pressure rising*. They also use a tap-language on metal surfaces. One tap is a question. Two is a location. Three is a fault. A long sliding tap means move, give way, brace, hold. A rapid fluttering against copper means the whole body: come, now, fast, but without panic. Panic is possible here, but it must be quiet, or it will draw the stone’s attention.

Their names are tactile patterns. A person is not named with a sound but with a rhythm: a knuckle sequence, a chest tap, a stroke along a brace. Children learn each other’s names as hand-couriers on skin. A stranger entering the hollow would not be known by a name at first, but by the way their boots struck the grating and the scent of their breath.

## Clothing and Gear

The Braced do not wear soft woven cloth. There is nothing green to spin, no cotton, flax, silk, or wool in the ordinary sense. Their garments are made from pressed fungal felt, beetle-silk, resin-stiffened membranes, and thin plates of mineral foam. A typical outfit is layered like the city itself: a base wrap against the skin, often impregnated with oil to reduce water loss and prevent cracking; a middle layer of insulating foam harvested from grubs; an outer shell of copper mesh, bronze scales, or hardened resin plates. The outer shell is dull, scratched, and salted. It protects against falling glassy fragments, hot seams, and accidental sparks.

Every garment has too many pockets. These pockets hold resin tubes, salt cloths, small hammers, sealing paste, fiber spools, pressure gauges, etched identification plates, and fragments of mineral bread. Belts are broad and padded, made to carry a child, a tool, a coil of wire, or a bag of beetle protein. Hoods are shallow and stiffened, leaving the face visible but shielding ears and eyes from falling dust. Gloves are essential. Most are fingerless or split-toed, designed for gripping wire, stone, and animal bodies. The fingertips are often reinforced with thin metal caps or resin nails.

They wear no jewelry in the decorative sense, but they are covered with markers of wear. A polished rim around a collarbone may mean years spent carrying pipes on the shoulder. A white resin seam along the forearm may mark a saved collapse. A missing fingernail replaced with metal may signify an apprenticeship completed. Faces are often marked by a single shallow line etched under the eye, not by skin damage but by habitual wiping of mineral dust. The most respected marks are those that resemble cracks: a person is not valued for unblemished skin but for having failed and been braced.

Their boots are quiet. They are not soft, but their soles are layered with felt, resin, and mineral foam, designed to walk without clanging on metal floors. A Braced person does not stomp unless the ground itself has betrayed them.
