No single body lives there. The basin is inhabited by processes that have acquired skins, edges, appetites, and habits, and it is not always possible to tell where one creature ends and the wet stone, the mineral film, the sound, or the unfinished architecture begins. If you ask what lives in such a place, the answer is not “animals,” exactly, because the life here is not only made of bodies. It is also made of vibration, moisture, salt, breath, pressure, and the patient tendency of stone to become something other than what it was.

The oldest residents are the films.

They are not plants, though they may be mistaken for moss at first glance. They are living skins spread across the wettest ground, clinging to the edges of puddles, staining the raw cliffs, sealing tiny bubbles of warm gas beneath translucent layers. Some are black and slick. Some are chalk-white. Some shine with a faint oil-sheen that breaks into dull rainbow patches when approached. They eat minerals, condensation, and the sweetness carried in warm vapor. They breathe by exhaling tiny bursts of gas, and when pressure is applied to the spongy pale substrate, the bubbles that rise are not merely geology’s breath but the life of microbial communities passing through it. These films are the first thing that lives, because they turn raw water and raw stone into a medium in which something else can appear.

They are also the place’s slow nervous system.

A film across a shallow pool may sense the passage of a warm breath or the vibration of a distant crack in the stone. If the wind changes, the films ripple without becoming water again. If a pulse moves through the basin, the mats may brighten faintly, as though remembering. Some of the dark clumps that look like moss are not moss at all, but colonial bodies with many small openings. One might call these openings eyespots; another might call them mouths. In this place the distinction hardly matters. They are apertures for sensing vibration, humidity, pressure, and mineral content. They breathe with the wet air. They hold water against the ground. They make the silence less complete.

The plants, if they can be called plants, live in this film.

The pale stalks that dot the wettest places are not rooted in soil because there is no soil. They stand in salt, fine grit, and bacterial paste, and they do not seem to want sunlight. They want wetness. Their bodies are fleshy, fibrous, rope-like, and sometimes they give when touched, releasing a tiny sound like a damp cloth being separated. They hold water in their tissues, then pass some of it back into the ground, forming rings of moisture around themselves. In their folds, the smallest scrapers live. In their secretions, mineral films grow. They are less vegetation than plumbing made of life, channels by which the place learns to keep water longer than the stone would naturally allow.

The most numerous residents are the scrapers.

They move in loose bands across the wet stone, dark and slick, many-jointed, soft-shelled, clicking like a crowd of small keys. Some are insect-like, but not insects. Some resemble juvenile crabs without the confidence to call themselves crustaceans. Some have too many legs, too few eyes, too many vibrations. They feed on the mineral skins forming at the puddle edges, using mandibles that resemble tiny tuning forks. When one approaches a wet surface, it taps the stone lightly with those forks, listening to the reply: too much salt, too much water, too much heat, too much chalk, too little iron. A scraper chooses its place to eat by sound.

They are editors of the basin.

They scrape away old films so that new films may grow. They leave behind shallow circular grooves, small rectangles, and dotted arcs that look almost like markings, though no one can say whether they are writing or waste. Their shells click in patterns, and bands of them may shift direction together when the tower pulses or when the cliffs begin to hum. Their droppings are not merely waste; they are seed. A line of scraper excrement laid across a wet stone may become a living mat within hours. Thus the scrapers do not merely eat the place. They teach the place how to become edible by other things.

Above the ground, but never truly leaving it, drift the ribbon-born.

They are translucent animals with long, folded bodies and slow beating lobes that are not wings, not fins, not exactly breath. They float just above the wet stone because the ground itself is warmer than the thin air above, and because the tower’s exhalations make pressure shelves they can ride. They are not birds, though some might mistake them for birds if they had never seen a bird’s bone, heat, or anger. They are not fish, though they swim as if the air were thicker than it should be. They are not balloons, though some are filled with warm gas from their own metabolic bubbles. Their bodies are mostly moisture and delicate mineral membranes, and they feed on spores, dust, fine salt, the aerosols of microbial mats, and the faint sweet particles that rise from the foam-breathing ground.

When they touch water, they do not merely disturb it.

The small rings left behind on the surface of a puddle do not fade quickly because they are not only ripples. They are packets of chemistry, gametes, dissolved salt, and tiny suspended creatures. A drifting ribbon-body brushing the surface may be eating, laying seed, or speaking. Schools of them pass over the basin in loose, shimmering shoals, and when the wind shifts, they change formation without visible effort, as if they are all answering a pressure the human ear cannot hear. Some of them are parasitized by mites that glow faintly amber. Some are pregnant with mineral shells that will become the homes of smaller things. Some are young enough to be nearly invisible, only heat-blobs with a hint of ribbon, but older ones gather dust and become ghostly, then stone-like, then eventually fall into a puddle and feed the films.

The largest ordinary residents are the grazers.

They stand along the rim where the air moves most, low and broad, six-legged, their backs flat as unfinished slabs. They do not look like animals made of meat, but like animals made of stone that has remembered being meat. Their legs are wide, blunt, partly webbed with a pale tissue that grips the wet ground without crushing it. Their backs carry crusts of mineral, mats of foam-life, and sometimes clusters of smaller creatures who have never known the world outside a grazer’s body. They have no visible eyes. Their faces are blunt and smooth. Along their flanks are moist pits, dark and trembling, which open and close slightly with each breath. Those pits read pressure, humidity, vibration, and chemical content. They are how the grazer sees the world.

The grazers do not eat the sparse plant-stalks.

They eat less directly. They extend slow, pale tongues into the wet air or across mineral films, drawing out threads of moisture, dissolved salts, suspended spores, and warm gas. They may drink pressure, or at least they consume the difference between pressure states as if it were nutrition. Their bodies work by maintaining internal gradients, by keeping one side of themselves wetter than the other, by filtering vapor through porous tissues, by converting mineral solution into solid crust. They are breathing stones, walking filters, living foundations.

The land accepts them.

When a grazer steps into a shallow puddle, the water gathers around its legs, then withdraws slowly, leaving a print that holds moisture longer than ordinary ground. The print is not merely a mark; it is a temporary ecology. Within hours, a film appears inside it. Within days, scrapers come. Within weeks, a small pale stalk may root there. The grazer is not just an inhabitant. It is an architect. Its body heat slows evaporation. Its feet compact the foam-ground. Its back sheds mineral crumbs. Its hum, produced by fluid moving through wide chambers inside it, vibrates the stone beneath it and encourages certain mats to grow faster. If a herd of such animals stands together, the air around them feels denser, warmer, sweeter, and the tower’s vapor bends toward them.

The parasites and mutualists are small enough to be almost ideas.

Some of the flying things do not live in the air. They live on the backs of the grazers. They drop from above and land without piercing flesh, without visibly biting, without taking anything solid. They press thin, tuning-fork limbs against the grazer’s flank pits or mineral back, and for a moment the grazer stiffens. Then it relaxes. Then it continues. The parasite feeds on the tremor of that response. It absorbs the mineral-rich exudate that the grazer’s body releases when stimulated. It may taste only of pressure: a sip of the grazer’s internal gradient, a mouthful of conditioned humidity. Some parasites are harmful. They provoke the grazer too often, until the grazer’s back becomes too soft or too salt-stained. Some are helpful. They remove excess mats, prevent the crust on the grazer’s back from overgrowing, and carry genetic material between grazers. They are not clearly predators. They are not clearly symbionts. They are a kind of intimate weather.
