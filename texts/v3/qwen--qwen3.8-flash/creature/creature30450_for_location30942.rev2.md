What lives there is not a single nation of creatures, nor a forest that merely happens to contain animals. It is a layered occupation: slow bodies, quick bodies, almost-invisible bodies, and bodies that have learned to make the emptiness into a medium rather than a barrier. The inhabitants of this plateau are not all obvious at once. They reveal themselves only in intervals: a pulse moving through a hollow stem, a click on a root-cable, a breath-ghost rising from a stone ring, a pair of eyes opening and closing in the black between towers. To live here is to exist in a condition of patience and timing. Nothing in this place has the luxury of being careless, and nothing is as absent as it first appears.

The first and most enduring residents are the plants, though calling them plants may be too simple. They are more like architecture that has decided to breathe. Their vast hollow stalks are not just shelters; they are instruments, pumps, lanterns, and nerve channels all at once. Each tall body is an individual, yet it behaves as if it remembers being part of something larger. Its roots grip the scree. Its broken limbs are braided back into the slope. Its sap carries light in dull blue-green waves when something passes through it. Its skins stiffen, sing, and groan in response to frost. Its fruits wait for months in a state of almost absolute stillness, then burst with a noise like a shot fired into an empty room.

These plants do not merely tolerate the wind; they make meaning from it. They do not merely suffer the cold; they use it to tune their bodies. They do not merely drink water when it comes; they speak through it. A basin dampens with mist, and an hour later a grove is whispering with tiny taps. The air grows thin and sharp, and a stem fills and empties itself by the drop. Frost advances across a ridge, and the whole plateau gives a long wooden sigh. If one stands still long enough, the feeling grows that the vegetation is not a collection of specimens but a single nervous system distributed across a landscape—so slow, so dispersed, and so strange that it seems almost geological. And yet it flinches. It leans toward moisture. It remembers, in its own plant-way, the locations of water. It learns, over generations, which cracks hold brine and which ledges freeze into death.

Beneath the larger bodies live the smallest, the ones no one would think to count unless they crouch at the edge of a pool. The shallow briny basins are alive with films. Pale mats, barely thicker than breath, spread across stone or float at the margins. Transparent larvae, if they deserve a name, turn slowly in the liquid like motes of thought. There are bacterial-like crusts that taste of metal and old salt, organisms that have no eyes, no bones, no wings, no face, but without which nothing above them could survive. These are the oldest citizens of the water. They make the pools more than mirrors. They give them smell and temperature and memory. The dark skin of a pool may look like glass, but it is also a city: layered, cold, chemically attentive, producing faint phosphorescence in some places and nothing at all in others.

Among the open spaces move the small creatures of the web. They are often the first living things the eye learns to detect once the mind stops insisting that life must be large to count. They are climbers, skidders, and leapers, with bodies not built for ground as it exists in warmer places. They cross voids on root-cables stretched between stems. Their limbs are long and spread when they freeze, and their fur lies flat against them until they resemble lichen drifting on the wind. They are pale-eyed in the way of animals that have learned to trade color for sensitivity. Their tails are stiff, not decorative but balancing rods, and their hands and feet have broad pads that let them stop on a frozen tendril without crushing it. They make themselves thin, still, and wrong-looking when threatened, not because they are hiding behind something, but because they become the background for an instant. In the black air, that is a kind of invisibility.

Some of these small ones travel by sound more than sight. They hop from node to node with dry footfalls, clicking through a lattice that is also a resonant chamber. Their hearing is so finely distributed that they can tell where a stem is damp, where a fruit has split, where a boulder has shifted. They do not fear every noise; they learn to sort the plateau’s grammar. The tap of condensation is food. The whistle of melt is passage. The low groan of frost is warning. The sudden crack of a seed-pod is either danger or opportunity, depending on whether one is eating its dust or seeking its shade. They make nests in the joints of branches, or in the curled undersides of pale mats, or inside the hollow columns where the young sap still carries a warm flicker. They are not gentle because they are small; they are gentle because if they are not careful, the web will fail them.

The broad-backed grazers are more difficult to miss once you know where to wait. They move through the lower basins in slow, shaggy herds, their bodies built for the retention of heat, their backs wide enough to carry small ecosystems of dust, salt, and spore on their fur. They eat cup-leaves and folded rosettes, but they do not merely graze; they negotiate with the plant-country. Their hooves strike the root-plates with a clatter that becomes part of the plateau’s music. Their passing warms the air for a moment and leaves behind a smell of hot iron and crushed ice, as if something molten had fallen into a stream and instantly frozen. They are animals of memory, in the sense that a herd can remember the locations of reliable moisture for seasons after the ground has tried to erase it. They can tell, by taste or by some deeper bodily knowledge, which shallow pools are brine and which are melt, which mats are safe to walk and which are thin over dark water.

Their calves are not born in softness. They are born in a world of hard edges and delayed arrivals. A newborn must stand among stems that may snap if touched too strongly, and the air itself is so thin that breath must be learned almost at once. The calves keep close to the adults’ legs, not because of any sentimental crowding, but because that is where warmth persists. The adults form rough rings when frost thickens, and their bodies turn until they can feel whether the wind is rising from the basins or dropping from the ridges. If a herd moves through a field of stalks, the noise it makes is not one sound but dozens: hoof on wood, hoof on stone, hoof on the dry skin of a braid. The plants do not hate them; the plants depend on them. Where a grazer bends a stem, new growth later threads into the bend. Where a herd presses a slope too hard, a root sheet tightens beneath the soil and keeps the whole terrace from sliding.

Overhead, if one can trust the word overhead in a place where the sky is not a blue ceiling but a black lens, there are the pale gliding things. They are not so much birds as elongations of silence. Their wings are membranes, almost transparent, and they ride the faint thermals that rise from the liquid pools. In the dark, they are hard to see except as brief disturbances of space. A cut in the air. A sudden thinness. A flash of pale skin catching blue sap-light for less than a second. They dive when certain low hums are made from below, whether the hums come from animals, weather, or the plants themselves. They hunt moths, scavenge split fruit, and sometimes take small climbers from the upper stems. Their nests are not nests in the familiar sense. They are sealed caps of chewed bark and plant wax, fitted into the mouths of hollow stalks where the wind cannot fully enter. When the young cry, the sound emerges distorted and lengthened, and for a moment the whole tower seems to become a throat.

And among all these lives, there are those who have learned to make the plateau into a place of meaning. People, or something like people, live scattered across it in small family bands, often so thin through the land that their presence seems accidental unless one looks for it. They do not build cities, because cities would be arrogance here. They do not clear broad lanes, because the web cannot be cleared without injury to its architecture. Their homes are temporary or seasonal: a hut bent from branch-mat at the base of a great stalk, a roof of overlapping cup-leaves tarred with wax, a circle of stones around a melt basin, a bundle of cut stems propped against a cliff to shelter sleep from the cutting night. Their tools are few but exact. Obsidian flakes knapped into needles. Bone hooks carved from the dead. Stone weights to hold down skins against the wind. Fire-stones sealed in dry seed-husks so that warmth may be carried without being killed by cold. Nothing in their works is monumental, but much in them is thoughtful.

Physically, they are adapted to this world in the way that a people becomes after many generations have learned to fear the wrong things. Their eyes are large and dark-adapted, with wide pupils that do not need much light to find shape. Their hearing is unusually fine; they can tell the difference between a stem carrying sap and a stem carrying gas, between a creature moving on wood and one moving on stone. Their hands are toughened in the places that grip frozen tendrils and calloused on the palms from pressing to stalks to listen. Their faces may be nearly hairless or lightly furred depending on lineage, but their bodies retain heat by central mass and by waxy oils rubbed into skin and garment. Their breath ghosts outward quickly and vanishes, so that even their speech seems half-dissolving before it is complete.

They use sound more than sight to know one another. A person far away is not first identified by a silhouette, but by a pattern: three taps on a dry stalk, a whistle drawn down through a hollow, a long note held against the inside of a ribbed leaf. Their names, if those can be called names, are not words but intervals—a short sequence of pitch and rhythm, often impossible to say correctly without the help of a bone pipe or a notched stick. Children learn to answer to their names not by recognizing syllables, but by recognizing how a vibration should travel through the body if it is meant for them. To be known is to be resonant. To belong is to have a sound that returns.

Their movement is deliberate. They do not stride carelessly across the scree because a misplaced weight can bring down brittle stems. They do not build permanent fires on the ground because the mineral-rich sap and trapped gases can turn a small flame into a strange, brief conflagration, blue at its foot and green higher up, dying as if embarrassed. They carry heat when they can and leave it when they must. Their paths are worn not through the most pleasant land but through the safest: along root-plates that can bear pressure, across stone shelves where the wind is broken, around basins where frost is thick enough to trust. They leave markers behind them: a knot tied in a tendril, a small cairn above a hidden pool, a dried bundle at a junction, a stone fitted into a notch so that the wind will whistle in a certain direction instead of another. These signs are not mere decorations. They are grammar. They are memory. They are the way one person can speak to another through the empty dark.

What they believe, if belief is the right word, is bound tightly to the rhythms of the place. The plants are not gods, but they are elders in the sense that they are older, stranger, and less easily corrected. The pools are not merely water; they are mirrors, and mirrors in a dark country become sacred almost by necessity. To look into one is to see not only one’s own face but the faces of everything around it: pulses, glows, distant eyes, the faint blue-green of sap moving through a stem behind you. The people understand this. They speak into basins less to pray than to measure distance. They leave small offerings not out of piety alone but because leaving something can mark a place as known, and a known place is a place one may return to when the cold turns cruel.

They are not many. A family may be three, five, seven at most, because any larger unit would have to consume too much moisture, too much fuel, too much patience from the web around them. Sometimes bands meet during bloom-thaws or at the great splitting of fruit, when seed is abundant enough to justify gathering and the air is thick with sound. At such moments the plateau becomes less empty, not because crowds form, but because scattered lives recognize one another through echo. Voices answer from different basins. Stalks are struck in sequences too regular to be weather. A herd moves aside. The gliders wheel overhead, disturbed or curious. For a short while, the plant-country feels populated in a way that makes the darkness almost social.

The old among them are valued not because they are wise in abstraction, but because they can predict the land’s moods. They can tell when a frost quake is near by listening to the tension in distant stems. They can smell the thinness of a pool before it becomes dangerous. They can place a hand on a column and know whether the next gust will produce a groan, a whistle, or silence. They are living maps of sound and timing. The young learn from them slowly. At first, children are taught not to speak loudly, not because of etiquette, but because noise can mean death: it may attract a glider, startle a grazer into breaking a slope, or cause a brittle stem to snap above someone’s head. Later, they are taught the difference between sounds that must be answered and sounds that must be endured.

There are dangers, and the people know them without drama. A frozen pool that looks solid but is only one step deep. A fruit burst that sends sharp flakes through the air like glass rain. A gas pocket in a column that ignites if struck at the wrong angle. A storm that makes the whole plateau roar from every direction at once. A fall that is not so much from height as from the betrayal of a stem that seemed firm and is not. They have learned to live with such risks, not by becoming careless, but by becoming precise. They do not conquer the environment; they negotiate with it. A fallen stalk may be left in place because it supports soil. A crack may be seeded with fiber-vines because the people know that young stone will continue to fracture, but can be made to fracture more slowly. A basin may be avoided for a season because the mats have thinned and the water is too low.

Death, too, is not hidden here. The dead are buried shallow beneath cairns, or laid in sealed plant-caps where the wind cannot scatter them fully, or left where frost will preserve them until the body becomes part of the web. Their bones may later be carved into pins, their tools left for others, their fire-stones passed hand to hand until no one remembers who first kept them dry. There is no vanity in their works. A hut may stand for one season and return to scree and lichen within two. But the marks they leave—stones placed in notches, knots tied in tendrils, paths worn in the safest places—accumulate into a kind of memory. The landscape does not forget their presence, even if it is slow enough not to acknowledge them directly.

If one watches closely, it becomes clear that the people are not separate from the rest of life here. They use the same architecture. Their small families rest inside the same plant-towers that harbor glider young. Their herding, when they herd, follows the same timing as the broad-backed grazers’ movements. Their children play with the same tapping rhythms by which the small climbers speak across the void. They gather seed after the burst-events and use the same frost-nicked mats that other animals touch into contraction. Their language echoes, in its own way, the whistles and groans of the stems. They have become a human-shaped extension of a non-human ecology, not because they have surrendered their personhood, but because this place demands that personhood be shaped around restraint.

What lives there, then, is a network of beings who understand emptiness as a condition, not an absence. The plants are slow citizens. The pools are chemically alive. The small creatures are travelers along living wires. The grazers are warm, shuffling islands of movement and smell. The gliders are pale cuts of weather seeking prey. The people are scattered listeners, makers of tiny signs, carriers of fire in husks, children of timing. All of them are occupied with the same difficult art: how to live without breaking the thing that holds life together.

If you stand at the edge of one of the melt basins in that first minute when frost begins to appear, you will see all of it at once in the only way this dark place allows—not clearly, but in relation. The water becomes a mirror. The mirror fills with pulses. The pulses come from plants. The animals in the spaces between are only eyes and motion. Somewhere a stalk sings as damp arrives. Somewhere a hand strikes wood and waits for answer. Somewhere a grazer snorts and shifts its weight on a root-plate, and the land sounds like a forest being shaken by unseen fingers. None of these events is accidental. None is isolated. They are the lives of this place, arranged not in a crowd but in a chord. The plateau is not empty. It is occupied by a vast, cold, patient chorus that has learned to live by resonance, by timing, and by the strange miracle of using the emptiness itself as a medium for life.
