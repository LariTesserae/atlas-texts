They are not a swarm. If a stranger approached from the rim road in the pale blue afternoon and stood long enough to let the haze of steam and soot separate into objects, the first thing they would understand is that this is not a city pressing outward. It is a population distributed across the basin like the water itself: present in many places at once, nowhere so thick as to choke the ground, yet never absent from the land.

The people who live here are still human, in the broad sense, but they have been shaped by the basin as thoroughly as the stone has been shaped by the drainage. They are not delicate, not soft-bodied, not hurried. They are not brutal either, except in the way that a person becomes brutal when they have to move wet clay and hot iron for forty years. Their bodies are thick through the chest and forearms, their feet broad and slightly splayed from years on slippery slopes, their necks long from looking up at machinery and down at water channels. Many of them have a strange stillness, even when working. They do not rush, because in a place where enormous things move slowly, rushing is only another way of making a mistake.

Their skin has taken on the character of the place. The sun is strong at that height, but the air is never simple warmth. It is laden with vapor, mineral, and heat radiated from stone and metal. People who live in the open terraces are weathered brown or grey-brown, their faces cracked in the particular way of skin that has been dried by wind and wetted again by condensation day after day. Those who live lower, near the yards and troughs and furnace sheds, often seem almost waxy in the evening light, as if a thin film of oil and mineral has taken up residence there. They wash themselves with lye, clay, and the rendered fats of reed-root and fish, but cleanliness in this basin is not the same thing as dryness. No one expects surfaces to be dry. No one expects themselves to be dry.

Their hands are among their most characteristic features. They are rarely smooth. The knuckles are swollen, the fingertips flattened, the palms marked with the little ridges that come from long work with rope, cable, ceramic edges, and hot iron. Many workers have burn scars that are not fresh but old, silvery, shaped like the outlines of the pipes and plates they touched. Others have chemical discoloration: yellow-brown stains around the nails, greenish marks at the wrist, the permanent dull shine of lime in the creases of skin. A child born here begins handling water before handling toys. By the age of ten most can feel the difference between a wet stone that is merely cold and one that is cold because seepage has been running behind it for years.

Their hearing is perhaps the strangest adaptation, though it would not be described that way by the inhabitants themselves. They would say only that the basin has “voices,” and that those who cannot listen are trouble. These people are not deaf, not exactly. Their ears are filled and hardened in the way of people who have grown up inside enormous noise. They have lost some sensitivity to sudden high sounds—the shriek of a rail car, the spit of a cracked valve—and gained an uncanny awareness of low, rolling pressure. They feel the stampers in the sternum before they hear them. They can often tell, by a faint change in the hum through a railing or a wall, that a pump has caught air, that a bearing is warming, that a cable is beginning to slip. The constant roar of the works is not, to them, a roar. It is a body of weather. A visitor hears noise. The inhabitants hear maintenance.

They also know silence, but they distrust it. In this place, silence does not mean peace. It means that something has stopped. It may mean a machine has seized, a flue has clogged, a channel has failed, a valve has been left shut against a warming line. When silence falls into a yard where it does not belong, people turn their heads as if a bell has broken. A child might cry before an adult notices the wrongness of the air. The dead zones are the only real quiet the basin permits, and even those are not fully silent, because the stone itself carries something: a faint, deep hum, a residue of vibration, the memory of pressure.

They live in homes that are not merely built but tuned. A family dwelling here is not necessarily large, but it is always placed with care. Before a house is raised on a terrace, a listener—sometimes called a dead-zone finder, sometimes simply a house-tender—walks the ground, presses palms to the retaining walls, strikes a small iron against the stone in several places, and listens to the reply. If the ground gives back a long, hollow roll, the site is refused. If it gives back a short, clean tone, or a soft absorption, it is approved. A dwelling should not be a resonator unless it is made to be one. The best houses are set behind natural baffles, beneath stone shelves, or beside empty yards where the noise arrives broken and spent.

Their houses often have no straight interior walls in the older parts. The curves and arches are not merely structural; they are acoustic. Doorways bend slightly. Chimney flues are angled to break the wind’s tendency to howl. Courtyards are sunk below the street, or walled with ridged masonry, so that sound can lose itself in them. The roofs are not pitched for snow—they do not get that kind of cold—but for the shedding of condensation and the directing of steam-light rain. They are broad, slightly domed, or shallowly sloped, faced in ceramic tile, slate, or baked clay. At night, when lamps glow inside, they often look like low stone turtles crouched in the wet, breathing out thin curls of steam from their vents.

Inside, they live lightly by the standards of crowded cities. There are fewer possessions, because the air takes things back. Iron rusts quickly unless sealed. Wood warps and rots at the base unless kept off the floor. Cloth holds damp unless treated with wax, tallow, or mineral oils. So furniture is made of stone, fired clay, bone, ceramic, and well-tended metal. Beds are raised on short legs and can be drawn together or apart. Floors are not merely covered; they are drained. A living room may look empty to an outsider: a few low seats, a stone basin in one corner, shelves of sealed crocks, walls hung with tools wrapped in oiled skin. But every surface is arranged to shed or gather water. There is a groove around the threshold. There is a catchment in the wall. There is a vent behind the stove that will not let smoke settle in the room even when the wind turns against the flue.

The people move through their settlements slowly, as if carrying a weight, though often there is none. Much of this is habit. In a landscape of open distances and heavy work, hurrying is exhausting and dangerous. A person will stand beside a wet stone curb and watch a pool tremble before continuing. This is not idleness. The tremble tells them that a pump is running somewhere, that a cable is moving, that a valve is open, that a distant machine is doing something that changes the water’s path. Reading motion through still water is one of the first skills of childhood here.

Their work is not merely labor. It is an extension of their senses. In the stamping houses, the operators do not sit at panels of blinking lights, at least not in the older yards. They stand on platforms near the floor, hands on iron rails, feeling the slow lift and descent. A machine that takes minutes to complete its cycle is not something one watches from a safe distance. One learns it. The worker’s whole body enters the rhythm: the slight pause before the counterweights begin to move, the long metallic sigh before the head lowers, the final report that travels through the ground. Those who tend these machines are not fast people. They are people who can wait with perfect attention. A young person who cannot remain still through three cycles is reassigned.

In the pumping yards, work is quieter but more anxious. These places smell of wet clay, hot bearings, and the particular metallic steam of iron that has been kept damp for years. Pump-houses are often low, thick-walled buildings set into the benches, with great round windows or open louvers for venting heat. Inside, the noise is less of a crash than of a pulse: a deep, slow throb that moves through the floor and into the legs of whoever stands near. The workers here are gauges. They can hear whether a piston has begun to catch, whether water is arriving too slowly, whether the channel behind the wall is starting to fill with silt. They carry small brass taps and waxed cords. At the end of a shift, the most trusted of them are often sent to press their cheeks or palms against the outer casing of a pump, to listen through bone, because the machine’s health may be written in a place no instrument can easily see.

The kiln workers have the most weathered faces. Their skin is often deeply cracked around the eyes and mouth, stained with ash and clay dust, made shiny by heat and then by evening condensation. They move like people who have learned to avoid wasting breath. The kiln yards are open to the sky but filled with columns of firebrick stacked in patterns that are not merely storage; they are flow. Heat rises between them in channels. Smoke leaves by routes that have been tested for generations. The workers know the difference between a brick that is properly baked and one that is only hot, and they know it partly by sound: tap the stack, listen to the ring. A dull answer means moisture inside, and moisture in a kiln is either the enemy or the method, depending on the stage. They speak little while working. Their hands say the rest.

The cable yards and winding rooms belong to a different temperament of person. These are often tall, thin workers with long fingers and careful gestures, because cable is unforgiving of sudden force. They live in the hum. The cable spools turn in enormous slow revolutions, and the wire itself can sing under strain. The workers can feel the tension in the soles of their feet through the floor plates. A bad turn in the spool is not heard at first; it is felt as a wrongness in the air. A skilled spinner can stand beside a winding line for hours, one hand lightly on a guide, watching the movement of a thousand strands. They do not produce objects for use in a small way. They produce the connections by which the basin can hold itself together.

The people have a language, but it is not a single thing. In the lower yards, where the noise is too dense for continuous speech, they use a mixture of short words, tonal calls, hand signs, and taps against metal. Many of their “words” are not really words at all but pressure patterns: two quick knocks, one long, a drag of the knuckles along a pipe. A mother may call a child in from a wet terrace with a special rhythm struck on the door lintel. A crew may warn of a shifting load with a single hum in the throat. This kind of communication makes visitors feel excluded, but it is not secret. It is simply practical. A language born in noise cannot be all vowel and long sentence.

In the quieter places, especially on the rim slopes and in the dead zones between old foundations, speech resumes more fully. There, people talk slower than outsiders expect, often with long pauses. Their voices have a low resonance. Some say it is because they speak as if their chest must carry the sound through damp air. Others say it is because high pitch is wasted in this basin; it scatters against stone. When a person from here does speak, they tend to speak economically, but they are rarely in a hurry to end the sentence. They have an ear for rhythm even in conversation. They may repeat the last phrase of a statement under their breath, adjusting its tone, as if checking how it sits in the air.

Children are raised in the works, not by accident, but because the works are the body of the settlement. A child here learns geography first through sound and water. By the time they are old enough to walk without constant watching, they know which terraces are slippery in the morning and which dry enough by midday. They know where the noise becomes pressure and where it becomes mere echo. They know which pools are clear enough to see the bottom and which are only beautiful because of mineral film. They know, often better than adults from outside, how to test a wet surface with the sole of a foot before putting weight on it.

These children are quiet not because they are solemn but because they are listening. They climb less recklessly than children in softer places, but they are fearless of water. They can lie on a flat stone shelf and watch condensation gather in the hollow of a carved groove. They play games in which the point is not speed but timing: waiting for the stamp, matching the pump, counting the cycles until a reservoir fills. Some of these games have rules that seem absurd to outsiders: a child who laughs at the wrong moment loses, because laughter can startle a worker carrying a hot or wet load. Others are harmless: tapping different heights on a wall and trying to produce a sequence.

The population is not uniform. Along the rim, where the land rises away from the main basin, there are lighter, more mobile people, less deafened by the works and more connected to the open sky. They live in smaller terraced towns, raise sparse fields of grey-green shrubs, and keep watch on the drainage channels. They are often paler, sharper-featured, with voices that carry more naturally. They look down into the basin as both familiar country and another world.

In the furnace districts, the people are darker, with heat-scarred arms and eyes perpetually narrowed against the glare of white-hot clay. They sleep in shifts and often look like they have aged twice as fast as other people, though many live long lives. Their houses are set far enough from the direct heat to avoid blistering, but close enough to catch the warmth in winterless nights. They have a reputation for calm, because calm is survival around molten material and sudden steam bursts. A furnace worker who panics is dangerous. A furnace worker who rushes is more so.

Along the canals and seep pools live the wettest of the inhabitants, people who seem to have absorbed the habits of the damp itself. They move through the shallows on small pontoons of fired clay and sealed reed. Their skin is often pale in places where the sun should touch it, because they spend their days in the low humidity of water rather than in the crisp glare above. They know the taste of the water: whether it is rising from the lower benches, whether it has passed through iron-rich strata, whether it will soon leave a bloom of pale green film. They cultivate reeds, bind wet clay with plant fiber, and harvest the slow-growing fish that inhabit the reservoirs. They are not fishermen in the romantic sense; they are water-tenders.

This is a people that does not eat well by the standards of green, abundant lands. Their diet is mineral, damp, and deliberate. The canals give them pale, fleshy fish with little bones and a taste that people from elsewhere describe as iron or steam.
