Ask the Bellows Hollow who lives in it, and the answer arrives in stages, because the first stage is not an image at all. At first you hear only the wet percussion, the hiss of the earth opening and closing. You see black stone, steam, thin shafts of light, a place so sparse that the air itself seems to be the only thing occupying it. But the hollow is not empty. It is only waiting for the right eye.

If you stand very still, if you let your breath settle and your vision grow patient, the space fills itself in a thousand tiny increments. It becomes a country of surfaces, each one populated. There are no grand dwellings, no lairs of large beasts, no nests of recognizable warmth. What lives here lives in wetness, on wetness, through wetness, and in the intervals between one wetness and the next. Its inhabitants are not a single community gathered around a shared landmark; they are scattered into thousands of minuscule worlds, each one no larger than a droplet, a moss leaf, a rim of mineral crust, a shadowed crack in glassy basalt.

The first residents are not plants and not animals. They are the films.

Every surface in the Bellows Hollow is either wet now, was wet recently, or is about to become wet, and wherever water has paused long enough, life has learned to cling. There are skins of bacteria and algae over the stone, thin as breath and just as changeable. Near the cooler pools they are green or olive; where the steam vents are warmer, they take on a yellow, orange, rust-colored cast. These are not painted marks left by minerals alone. They are living mats, layered like oil on water, built from microbes that feed on dissolved iron, sulfur, silica, calcium, and the faint carbon carried through steam and runoff. Some of them exhale oxygen. Some consume hydrogen sulfide as if it were bread. Some leave behind delicate threads and crusts of iron, slowly staining the basalt like old blood in water. They are the hollow’s first architects. Where they settle, droplets begin to hold longer. Where droplets hold longer, the mosses can begin.

In the shallow basins and seep-rims, microbial films form the true soil of this place. There is no loam here, no humus, no accumulated forest floor. The ground is only stone and water. Life must make its own edge where none exists. So the films grow: slick, granular, sometimes nearly transparent, sometimes bright with color, sometimes dull as ash. They coat the basalt so thinly that you might mistake them for dirt until they lift and tremble with a current of steam. They are the base of every chain, the first grazing field, the first nursery, the first wall, the first medicine. Tiny animals crawl over them. They filter from them. They are born on them and die beneath them.

Above the microbial films come the mosses, liverworts, and algae of the green islands.

They appear only where the light is thin and the water is persistent. On a flat ledge where condensation gathers steadily, a dark green cushion forms. At first it seems like velvet laid by invisible hands. Closer, it becomes a forest. Each stem is a tower; each leaf is a roof; each fold is a pool, a cave, a street, a hunting ground. The moss does not drink through roots, for there are no roots to drink. It lives from the dampness of air, from the trickle that runs over its surface, from the brief mercy of every dripping drop. When the chamber breathes and vapor moves across it, the moss swells, lifting from black to green, from dull to vivid, as if remembering something. Its curled leaves open. Its tiny gemmae cups glisten. It becomes a wet city of branches.

The liverworts spread beside it, flat and pale, like small lichen-tongues pressed against stone. Algae line the pool margins in emerald fringes, bright enough to seem almost liquid in the dark. In the deeper corners, where the light cannot reach, some mosses grow so thin that they are almost black, stretched toward the faintest glow, living on the edge of starvation.

All of them are concentrated. They are not a forest in the ordinary sense. A forest is a mass of individuals, competing and reaching upward. The moss of the Bellows Hollow is more like an island kingdom, each cushion a separate ecology, each patch a tiny planet with its own weather. The moss holds the water that would otherwise be lost to the cracks. It slows the drops. It shelters eggs. It catches dead insects. It turns steam into standing water, and that standing water into life.

And inside each moss island, there is a world so crowded that a single square centimeter may contain more living bodies than a square kilometer of open ground in a human city.

Rotifers drift in the droplets held among the leaves. They are small beyond ordinary thought, jewel-like, their cilia beating in tiny wheels to pull bacteria and mineral particles into their mouths. They feed, they divide, they pass. Springtails bounce across the wet surface, so small they seem to be grains of animated salt. When the steam lifts and the air shudders with pressure, they leap, each with a forked tail beneath its body snapping it away from danger. They graze algae, fungal threads, and dead particles. To them the moss is a jungle; to them a droplet is a lake; to them a falling grain of mineral dust is a boulder that may bury them.

Mites move through the filaments with long, pale legs. Some are pearl-white, some rust-red, some translucent enough that their internal bodies are visible as a flicker of shadow. They hunt smaller animals. They feed on eggs. They ride on larger insects from one moss-pool to another. In the dry intervals, when the breath of the vents falls and the surface loses its water, they become almost invisible, tucked into leaf joints, waiting for the next mist.

Tardigrades are there too, though you would never see them without magnification. They crawl among the moss like slow eight-legged bears made of glass. When the moisture fails, they curl into little sealed seeds and sleep, perhaps for hours, perhaps for longer than a human would believe. When the steam returns, they unfold, shake themselves, and resume their grazing as if they had never ceased to live. To be alive in the Bellows Hollow often means to be able to pause, to wait inside a dry husk until the world breathes again.

In the pools themselves, life takes another form.

The shallow basins are no more than a hand’s width, but in them there are currents too small to see, temperatures that vary by a finger’s breadth, and enough oxygen to support animals that move with patient, cold-blooded hunger. Midge larvae are among the most numerous. They lie in the silt and mineral dust at the bottom, sometimes red from the hemoglobin in their blood, sometimes pale, sometimes wrapped in cases made from the smallest grains of shattered basalt. They breathe through fine filaments or through their skin. They filter what the steam and rock have carried in. They are the living sediment, the pool’s memory, its digestive layer.

Caddisfly larvae, if they live here, do not build houses from twigs, for there are no twigs. They build from grit, from chips of mineral crust, from tiny shards of silica and calcite, from grains of black glass. They carry these cases over their soft bodies like portable ruins. They scrape biofilm from stone with mandibles shaped for a task no human hand could notice. Their existence is a quiet industry: they collect, attach, graze, grow, discard, repeat.

Amphipods scuttle beneath them, pale crustaceans curved like fishhooks. Many of them are blind, though in the pools where a shaft of light falls they may possess eyes reduced to dark pinpoints, useless for seeing but perhaps enough to sense the tremor of a shadow above. Their antennae are longer than their bodies, waving constantly through the water, tasting movement, temperature, chemical change. When a drop falls, they scatter into the gaps under stone. When the pool is calm, they return to their grazing. They are the hollow’s small, cold engines of consumption.

In the deeper pockets, where the water cannot be seen and the basalt closes in, there may be ostracods—seed-shrimp no larger than a grain of sand—clacking softly at the bottom. There may be copepods with translucent bodies and forked tails, swimming in jerks like tiny sparks. There may be isopods, blind and white, moving with an almost geological slowness across the wet rims, their armored backs looking like small stones that have learned to forget they are stones.

These creatures do not live in water as a lake lives, nor in mud as a riverbed lives. They live in moments of water, in temporary surfaces, in the pause before evaporation or drip or collapse. Their world is constantly appearing and disappearing. A larva may be born in a pool that will vanish by night. A springtail may wake on a stone that was dry the day before and will be dry again before it can feed. A moss mat may be a jungle for hours, then a desert for days. Survival here depends not on permanence, but on the ability to read the hollow’s pulses.

The air, too, has inhabitants.

When the steam vents breathe, the hollow lifts small clouds of life into itself. Midges rise in swarms so fine that they seem to be made of dust. They hover in the shafts of light, spinning like motes, drawn upward by warmth and vapor. They land on wet basalt, on moss, on the rims of pools, on each other’s wings. They mate in the humid dark. They lay eggs where a film of moisture may survive long enough for larvae to hatch. They die on ledges, and their bodies become food for beetles, mites, and microbes. In this way, the vent’s breath becomes a nursery, and the steam becomes a medium of birth.

There are flies and gnats too, perhaps with darker wings than their ancestors near open country, perhaps paler, perhaps shorter-lived. There may be fungus gnats whose larvae chew through damp moss, whose adults drift like flakes of ash in the vapor. There may be wingless insects, no longer needing to fly in a world where flying is almost enough to kill one with exhaustion or expose one to the sudden fall of hot steam. In the Bellows Hollow, wings are dangerous things. The air can rise, can sink, can change temperature without warning. Flight is a gamble. Walking is the safer art.

The largest visible predators, if any, are probably not large at all.

Spiders live in the narrow spaces between basalt columns. They string sagging threads from stone to mineral shelf, from ledge to rim, from one dry place to the next. They do not build grand orb webs here; there is too much mist, too little stable anchor, too much air movement. Their webs are sagging traps, irregular nets strung low above wet ground, catching midges and gnats when the swarm lifts. Their bodies are pale and rounded, like small seeds of fat. Their legs are long and thin, trembling with every vibration of water. Many are nearly blind. They see the world through tremor, through pressure, through the tiny language of a thread bending.

Pseudoscorpions ride the air on the backs of larger insects. They appear in the hollow as tiny pale crabs, perhaps a millimeter or less, with pincers that look almost ornamental. They hunt mites and springtails in the moss and mineral crust. They do not sting. They seize, inject, dissolve, suck. In the Bellows Hollow, even the smallest predator is a chemist.

Beetles patrol the wet rims. Their bodies are low and smooth, built for slipping between stone and water. Their legs click faintly as they move across mineral crusts. Some have eyes too small for their heads, or none visible at all. They hunt by smell, by touch, by the memory of where a pool was, where a larva hid, where a drop fell. A beetle that has lived in one crevice for all its adult life may know its wetness as intimately as a mammal knows its home. It drinks from condensation. It feeds on eggs, larvae, and the dead bodies that collect in the moss folds. It burrows under a thin layer of grit when the air grows too dry, too cold, or too loud.

If there is a vertebrate in the Bellows Hollow—and the description of the place insists there may be small fish or salamander-like animals in the colder, oxygen-rich water—then it is the creature that changes the scale of everything.

It might be a fish the color of weak tea, almost translucent, lying in the bottom of a pool with its fins motionless. Its eyes are not eyes in the ordinary sense; they are dark slits under skin, or sometimes barely more than faint stains where eyes once were. Its scales are so fine that it appears naked, as if the water had forgotten to make a boundary around it. Its gill covers open and close with a rhythm that matches the steam’s breathing, though it does not know that it does. It eats amphipods. It darts at midge larvae. It is the first thing large enough to be seen as a whole animal, but even it is not large by human reckoning. It is only a small, cold life moving under the black glass surface.

More likely, in the crevices where the water is thin and the stone is damp, there is a salamander-like animal. Imagine a creature slender as a vein, with a tail flattened like a blade and limbs so small that they seem almost decorative. Its skin is moist and translucent in places, through which you might catch the faint pulse of a heart. If it is aquatic, feathery gills flare like ferns from the sides of its head, pale red in the dim light. If it is lungless, it breathes through its skin, living on the very atmosphere of the hollow, depending on the water films that coat the stone. It may move only when the moss swells, when the air is heavy enough to let it breathe. When the vents quiet, it withdraws into the deepest seepage, into a crack no finger could reach, and waits.

This creature, if it exists, is the Bellows Hollow’s vertebrate monk. It does not need the larger world. It does not need birdsong, or open sun, or soil, or leaves that fall each year. It needs only cold water, humid stone, and a body small enough to survive on the edges of a few insects. It is the only inhabitant large enough to suggest a face, and even that face would not seem human. It would have the patience of stone and the silence of vapor.

But if the hollow has a monarch, it is not the fish, not the salamander, not even the moss. It is the rhythm.
