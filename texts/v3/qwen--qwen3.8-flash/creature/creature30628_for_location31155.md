Everything that lives here first agrees to be wet.

If you step onto a cushion of peat and lift your foot, you may imagine you have disturbed nothing more than moisture. But the place is not simply wet; it is inhabited by wetness made active. The first citizens of this basin are not animals with faces or feet. They are skins of chemistry, thin as breath, older in their habits than any individual thing. They live in the iron film that darkens a stone’s face, in the yellow scum along a warm rim, in the white lace left where steam condenses and dries, in the black bubbles rising through mud that has not seen oxygen for a year. They do not announce themselves as creatures. They announce themselves as color, smell, stain, temperature, and the slow maintenance of the world.

The rustworkers are among the most ancient and the most industrious. They are bacteria and microorganisms that do not merely tolerate the iron in the groundwater; they eat its chemistry. Where water seeps through mineral-rich basalt and climbs upward through fissures, they live on the boundary between dissolved and deposited, between reduced and oxidized. Over days and nights they draw iron from the water and leave it behind as a coating so fine it seems like shadow. If you scrape one with a fingernail, the powder comes away orange-brown and gritty, like old paint. But beneath it another film is already re-forming, patient and invisible, taking the iron again. They do not build for beauty, though what they make is often beautiful: concentric rings at the mouths of warm springs, orange feathers of sludge clinging to sedge roots, ribbons of stained mineral in channels where the current slows. They are the basin’s painters and metalworkers, turning water into rust.
