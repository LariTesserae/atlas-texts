There is no ordinary population in that city. If you ask for a crowd, a market, a quarter of homes with smoke or children or doorways left open, you will hear the answer as a roar and find nothing. But the place is not empty in the way a dead place is empty. It is empty in the way a bell is empty: made for sound, tuned by absence, and lived in only at the precise points where the sound forgets itself.

If there are “people” there, they are not people in the way the surrounding world would understand the term. They are what may remain of the original builders, or what those builders became after centuries of living inside a city whose atmosphere is pressure, whose food is mineral, whose water has been forbidden, and whose every wall is an ear. The city lost its common humanity long ago, and in its place kept a few quiet survivors who have learned to live as rest notes in a chord too vast to name.

### The Hushfolk

They live in the silent nodes.

A visitor could pass through one or two of them without knowing what was seen. The Hushfolk do not stand in open plazas or walk the wide avenues when the deep tones are moving. They gather in low courtyards, behind thick curved walls, inside recesses shaped like closed mouths, and in gardens so dense with glossy stems that their bodies are mistaken for leaves. Where the resonance cancels itself, they emerge. Where it thickens, they vanish into the architecture.

They are short, broad, and dry.

Their bodies seem made of the same patient materials as the city: pale blue-white bone, copper-flecked skin, stiff joints, and surfaces polished by long handling. Their skin is not soft or wet. It is thin and close-fitted, like dried parchment or polished leather, with a faint metallic sheen at the temples, wrists, and throats. Where the air is warmest, it takes on a dusty blue cast. Where it is cooler, it looks almost stone-colored. They have no sweat, no tears, no damp breath. If cut, they do not bleed wetly; they release a fine coppery powder and a stiff pale thread of fiber, as though the body were constructed from plant-like matter bound with mineral salts.

Their skulls are broad and low, with thick ridges over the temples. The external ears are gone, replaced by smooth plates of bone fused to the sides of the head. These plates do not seal them off from sound; they make the skull itself an instrument. A Hushfolk hears first through the floor, then through the walls, then through the chest. They feel footsteps in stone the way other beings feel touch. They can tell the weight of a stranger’s body by the faint harmonic disturbance it makes when it crosses a bridge or stands in a plaza. Their voices, when they have voices, are not spoken so much as struck.

Their eyes are pale, wide, and unreflective, adapted to the soft brightness of the quartz ceiling and to the absence of shadow. They do not squint in the glare, but they blink little, because blinking is unnecessary when moisture is absent from the air. Their pupils are faintly crystalline, catching and diffusing the even light. At a distance, their faces look carved rather than alive, as if they were young statues left to dry in the sun. Up close, you see movement in them: the subtle shift of muscle beneath dry skin, the tiny tremor of a finger against stone, the way they lean their bodies toward walls as if listening to the heartbeat of the city.

Their hands are long and narrow, with thickened fingertips and joints that click softly when they move. Those hands know stone by its hum. A Hushfolk can press one palm to a wall and tell whether an arch is cracked, loose, balanced, or ready to fall. If it is cracked, they feel the fault as a sour note. If it is balanced, it rings clean. They do not merely build; they tune.

They are rarely more than a few dozen families in the whole basin. Some courtyards have no inhabitants. Some hold one solitary figure. Others have five, seven, or twelve, arranged in loose households around a central silent chamber. They do not need many people because the city itself is the larger organism. To add too many bodies into the acoustic sanctuaries would crowd the silence, alter the air pressure, choke the plants, and break the balance. The Hushfolk are not the rulers of the city; they are its caretakers, and perhaps its symptoms.

### How they live

They do not live on streets. They live in the pockets between streets.

Their homes are not constructed from rooms so much as selected from voids. A home is a curved chamber, a sheltered courtyard, a space behind a perforated bridge, or a vaulted niche where the sound flattens. Inside these spaces, the walls are worn smooth by generations of hands and shoulders. The floors are often planted with the same hollow-stemmed vegetation that grows throughout the basin, so that a household lives partly within a garden and partly within a building. The boundary between wall, plant, dust, and body is less absolute than it would be elsewhere.

They sleep with their backs against thick stone. This is not comfort but survival: the stone absorbs and redistributes the city’s resonance, and a sleeping body placed correctly against it can remain safe through a deep-tone surge. They do not sleep like exhausted animals collapsing into a bed. They settle into a position, check the vibration with an open palm, and then become still. When the roar rises, their bodies align with the null. When the tide retreats, they wake before the light softens, because the air changes first.

Their days are not measured by sunrise and sunset, because the crystal ceiling only brightens and dims. They measure them in pressure. The city has movements. A “low wind” is when the deepest tones fade and the higher registers thin to a glassy hiss. A “thickening” is when the lower channels fill with the long, rolling pulse that shakes dust into standing spirals. A “flowering” occurs when particular intervals pass through the stone and the dry blossoms click open in the quiet courtyards. A Hushfolk wakes by feeling the floor and knows whether the day is for tending, moving, hiding, or listening.

They move through the city only during safe intervals. When they must cross an open avenue or plaza, they move quickly and low, sometimes pressing one hand to their own skull plate to stabilize the incoming vibration. They do not shout, because there is no need: any voice large enough to be heard over the city would damage the throat and disturb the stone. Instead, they communicate by tapping, humming, and placing hands on walls. Their personal names are not sounds but short signatures of touch and tone. A mother may greet a child by tapping two quick beats and one long slide on the child’s breastbone. The child answers with a higher flick of the fingers against the wall. To hear one of their names is to feel it in the bones.

Their speech, in the deepest silence, is strange and spare. They make little use of words as other peoples do. Their language is built from friction, resonance, and pauses. A sentence may be a sequence of clicks, a hum held in the throat, a scrape of copper against stone, and then a silence so specific that it carries meaning. They do not laugh. Laughter, with its wet throat and rising breath, would be impossible. Their closest equivalent to joy is a shiver of recognition: the sudden feeling that a wall, a plant, or another body has entered into the same frequency.

### What they eat and how they survive without water

They do not drink.

They do not need pools, rivers, cisterns, or dew. Their bodies are built around a different kind of intake: the breathing of warm mineral air, the absorption of dry vapor from the channels, and the eating of plants, insects, dust-laden lichens, and crystalline seed-meals. Their mouths taste salt and heated metal, and they enjoy flavors that other beings would find intolerable: scorched stone, copper dust, powdered roots, and the faint alkaline bite of the white deposits that grow in the gutters.

Their staple foods are dry. They gather the papery flowers when they open during favorable chords, cracking them against stone until the thin crystalline seeds come loose. These seeds are hard, mineral-heavy, and faintly sweet in the way of certain dry lichens. They grind them between flat stones until they become a pale powder, then press the powder into little cakes or mix it with warm dust to make a gritty paste. They also eat broad hollow leaves, not whole, but split along their tuned ribs and chewed until the fibrous pulp yields its stored vapor. The leaves taste of metal and resin, and when chewed, they release tiny sparks of dry pollen that glitter inside the mouth.

Some Hushfolk keep small gardens of their own: tight, shaped masses of glossy growth in the corners of silent courtyards. They do not water these gardens. They tune them. They prune certain stems to increase resonance, open certain channels to let warm air enter, and close off others when a plant grows too thick for its own body and begins to make a sour, choking note. A healthy plant in their understanding is not one that glistens with moisture but one that hums clearly when struck.

Their animals, or near-animals, are also food in some cases, though they do not hunt greedily. The copper-furred quadrupeds are too rare, too strange, and too useful for indiscriminate killing. Some Hushfolk may take an old or wounded one, but they do so with ceremony. The insects are more common, though still few. The small dark beetles with ringing shells can be eaten after being ground and toasted; the translucent quartz-winged insects are less nourishing but prized for their crystalline edges, which are sometimes chewed and swallowed as part of a rite.

### Their tools

Their tools are simple because sophistication would be a mistake.

There are no machines, no glass lenses, no wire motors, no pumps, no valves requiring moisture or grease. They use hand-shaped stones for scraping. They use bone awls for piercing dry fiber. They use wooden shafts hardened by fire and bound with sinew, though even the sinew seems stiff as dried reed. They use copper clamps and pins, hammered with patience around cracked joints. They carry stone hammers and sounding rods, tools that are not quite building implements and not quite musical instruments, but both at once.

A Hushfolk’s most valuable tool is a copper band worn smooth by handling. It is not used only as a fastener. It is used to listen. A good band, when struck gently against stone, reveals whether the wall is hollow, cracked, too thick, too thin, or balanced. If a band rings too sharply, the arch beneath it is under strain. If it mutters, something has shifted below. They know their city by the voice of metal on stone.

They also use dampening chalk, a porous white material scraped from old channel deposits. This can be powdered and pressed over the skull plates during brief crossings of loud areas. It does not block sound so much as dull it, absorbing certain frequencies and giving the body time to adjust. Children are not given dampening chalk until they are old enough to understand its limits, because too much damping can be as dangerous as too much exposure. A Hushfolk must know how to hear the city, even while hiding from it.

### Their society

They have no great government. They have no army, no treasury, no empire of stone and ambition. They have households, courtyards, and memory.

Their leaders are not kings but hearers. The most respected Hushfolk are those who can identify the subtlest shifts in the resonance. A good hearer can tell whether the lower tones are deepening before the first dust spiral rises in the plaza. A better one can predict which plants will open, which insects will climb, which quadrupeds will vanish into wall recesses, and whether a certain bridge will become too loud to cross within a few hundred beats. These people are not worshipped. They are needed. If their judgment fails, they are quietly ignored.

Kinship is acoustic as well as biological. A family may include those born in the same courtyard, those whose bodies resonate in similar frequencies, and those who maintain a shared set of silent paths. They recognize one another by the way a person presses to stone, by the rhythm of steps, and by the particular tone a body gives off when held against a tuned surface. Strangers are identified almost immediately as disturbances. A traveler who does not know how to move without blocking a null will be noticed before being seen.

They are not friendly to intruders, but not hostile either. They are cautious because the city is dangerous and because the arrival of another body can alter the balance. If a stranger stumbles into a courtyard, the Hushfolk do not flee unless the stranger is loud, erratic, or blocking a critical sound path. They may simply become very still, aligning themselves to the nearest recess. To the outsider, it can seem as if the courtyard is empty again, until the realization comes that a person has been standing there the whole time, pressed so perfectly into the geometry of the wall that the body has been read as architecture.

If a Hushfolk decides to speak to an outsider, they do not ask for food, shelter, or trade. They ask for balance. The question is usually expressed by tapping the floor: Do you know which rooms are silent? Can you stand in a place without making it wrong? Will you leave?

The last question is the most important.

### Their relationship to the creatures of the pocket

The copper-furred quadrupeds are not pets, though they share the silent courtyards and sometimes sleep in the same hollows as Hushfolk. They are more like living indicators of safety. A Hushfolk watches a quadruped not because it is charming, but because it knows where the nulls are. If the creature presses itself into a certain recess, the Hushfolk may understand that the surrounding wall is safe. If it refuses to enter a courtyard and instead stands with its broad skull tilted toward the floor, that is a warning: the sound below is rising faster than it appears.

The Hushfolk call these animals by names that mean, roughly, “bone-ear,” “wall-shadow,” or “quiet that walks.” They do not chase them, trap them, or herd them. If one is found dead in a garden, its body is placed in a shallow niche where its fur will dry and its bones will become part of the resonance. A copper-furred skull, sealed with fine copper filings and set into a wall, can make a useful low-tone resonator.

The insects are treated as workers. The translucent quartz-winged things are left alone or encouraged, depending on season. They pollinate the dry blossoms and carry pollen dust through the channels, which may help certain flowers open when particular chords pass. The darker beetles, whose shells ring faintly, are more complicated. When too many collect in one courtyard, the sound grows thin and brittle, like a room filled with broken glass. The Hushfolk sometimes remove them with dampening chalk and a soft brush, placing them near the shafts where the air is harsh enough to disperse them.

The pale many-limbed thing that emerges from the shafts is neither pet nor pest. It is a guardian, though not in any sentimental sense. It moves slowly at the edge of a dry opening when the thermal breath changes, and its presence causes the smaller insects to vanish and the quadrupeds to flatten themselves against the stone. The Hushfolk do not approach it closely. They leave polished dust, copper filings, and small carved bones near the shaft mouth. If the object is taken, they understand that the deep channels need maintenance. If it is not taken, they understand that something below is wrong.

They do not fear the shaft-thing. They respect it as a body made of the same forgotten logic that built the city. It may be older than the Hushfolk. It may be one of the first inhabitants. It may be the last. Its many pale limbs are not frightening because they are threatening, but because they are appropriate: a creature evolved for climbing shaft walls, pressing against resonance, and carrying the city’s breath back and forth in darkness.

### Their beliefs

They do not believe in the city as a god. They believe in it as a condition.

Their sacred thing is not the sound, nor the silence, but the relationship between them. To them, the city is a living instrument that has continued after the disappearance of its original players. Some say the first people did not die but were absorbed. Some say they were refined into Hushfolk. Some say the city itself became the mind, and the people became its organs. They do not argue loudly, because loudness is impossible in the plazas, and even in the gardens it feels careless.

They believe that every living thing has a frequency. A plant, an animal, a wall, a child, an elder, a stranger—all make a tone in relation to the whole. To harm the wrong thing is not to sin in the abstract sense, but to introduce dissonance. Too much dissonance can crack a bridge. Too much crowding can choke a null. Too many voices in one garden can prevent flowers from opening.

Death is not mourned with wailing, because wailing produces wet sounds and uncontrolled pressure. The closest they come to mourning is a sustained tremor in the hands against stone. The dead are placed in shallow chambers or niches, often in the same courtyards where they lived. They desiccate. The warm mineral air draws the last dryness from them. Eventually, their bones are removed, cleaned, and set into new walls. Some become chisels. Some become pins. Some are ground to dust and mixed into dampening chalk. Some are sealed inside hollow columns, where they will resonate with future chords.

A Hushfolk is not remembered by a name so much as by a place. One says: “This is the recess where old Thresh-Hum lay.” “This is the bridge that remembers Kite-Bone.” “This is the garden that once answered to Soot-Leaf.” The city itself becomes the archive. If a person’s bones are placed in the right wall, and if the air changes at the right interval, the wall hums softly, and everyone who has ever lived in that courtyard feels the same faint recognition.

### The bone-plated animals

The city’s quadrupeds are among its most elegant inhabitants.

They are low-slung and broad, with fur the color of old copper, fine and stiff as filament, not soft. Their skulls are unusually wide, with thick bony plates sealing the ears into narrow channels. Their legs are short but powerful, ending in blunt feet that do not clatter. They can stand motionless for long periods, pressed into the corner of a courtyard, and their bodies become visually continuous with the stone. Their eyes are pale and huge, adapted to brightness without shadow. They do not drink water because there is none; they take in mineral vapor through specialized membranes in the roof of the mouth and through the pores of their dry, leathery skin.

They eat mostly the tough inner piths of hollow stems, the papery flowers, and the crystalline seed casings. They are not selective about texture. They will chew stone-dust, bark-like fiber, and dry lichen with an unhurried patience. Their teeth are broad and flat, worn into ridges that match the patterns of their food. Their stomachs are sealed and dry, acting less like organs for digestion than like bellows for processing vibration.

When the roar intensifies, they align themselves with nulls. This is not panic; it is technique. A frightened animal flees, but a bone-earling finds the geometry of safety. It presses its broad skull to a curved wall, flattens its ribs to a particular angle, and becomes acoustically invisible. Its fur may absorb the smallest high tones, and its plates redirect the larger ones around its body. To a human observer, it seems to disappear into the architecture. The wall does not open; the creature simply stops being a disturbance in the sound field.

They reproduce slowly. There are never many young at once. A newborn quadruped is small, stiff-legged, and already wide-eyed, with the bone plates at its temples still soft enough to feel the first vibrations of the city. It must learn, within days, where the quiet places are. If it fails, it may be crushed by its own confusion, too far from the wall, too open to the roar. The survivors grow into animals of extraordinary stillness.

They are not intelligent in a human way, but they are acoustically wise. They know the city’s moods. They know when the shafts are breathing too hard. They know when a bridge is becoming dangerous. They know when a courtyard has gathered enough dust to become soft, and when it has gathered enough resonance to become deadly. If a Hushfolk hears a bone-earling tap its forehead against stone in a particular rhythm, it understands immediately: leave that place.

### The insects and the shaft-warden

The insects are rare, and that rarity is functional.

There are no swarms because the city cannot tolerate swarms. A million bodies, however small, would dull too many surfaces, clog too many fluted channels, and mute too many stems. So the insect life exists in careful numbers. Each species has a role in the tuning.

The translucent quartz-winged ones are perhaps the most beautiful. They look like fragments of glass given legs. Their bodies are dry and hollow, and when they move across stone they emit a faint chime, not from rubbing parts together but because the city vibrates them. They are drawn to the high places: the upper lattices of towers, the bridges with bored undersides, the thin rims of crystal veins in the dome. They climb when the sound lowers and bury themselves under dusty mulch when higher tones pierce the stone. Their wings are used in Hushfolk craft. A single wing, bound into copper, can serve as a tiny resonator in a child’s game, a tool for testing whether a hollow stem is clear or choked.

The darker beetles with ringing shells are the cleaners. They move through the fine gutters at the bases of buildings, eating dead organic fragments and mineral powder. Where they go, old channels open. Where they cluster, the air becomes thin and bright. They orient themselves by vibration, not by sight. If a plaza begins to develop a dissonant flutter, the beetles may gather there in strange numbers, as though trying to tune the space by occupying it. The Hushfolk dislike this, because beetles are good for cleaning but bad for resonance when they are too concentrated. They use soft dust and copper plates to lure them away.

The rarest inhabitant of all is the pale many-limbed creature that lives beneath the floors. It has no common name because few can point to it and expect others to remember. The Hushfolk call it something like Underwarden, or the one who breathes the shafts.

It is not frightening in the way a predator is frightening. It is frightening in the way an empty room is frightening when the sound in it is too perfect. Its body is pale, segmented, and many-limbed, built for slow movement along the walls of deep shafts. It does not seem to have eyes, or perhaps its senses are distributed along its sides. When it emerges near the edge of a shaft, the air pressure changes in a way that makes teeth ache. It moves with terrible deliberation, its limbs testing the stone as though the shaft were an instrument whose notes it is checking.

It may feed on mineral deposits, dead dust, and the organic fragments that fall into the shafts. It may help maintain the city by removing blockages, smoothing walls, and depositing chalky secretions in cracked joints. Its secretions are not wet, but dry crystalline paste, like powdered quartz pressed by pressure and warmth into stone. It may also be a signal creature: when it appears, the lower thermal currents have shifted, and the city’s next deep chord will be different from the last.

The Hushfolk do not chase it. They do not kill it. They leave it small offerings of copper filings and bone dust. If it takes them, they know that the Underwarden is still attending to the shafts. If it does not, some go deep enough to check the lower channels themselves, though that is dangerous. If they find one of its molted limbs in the dark, they keep it sealed in a niche because it is believed to remember the pressure of the city’s deepest breath.

### The dry gardens

The gardens are not passive decoration. They are not “places where plants happen to grow.” They are living bodies that help shape the city’s music and are shaped by it in return.

Each plant is a resonator. The hollow stems are pipes. The broad leaves are membranes. The curled flower buds are sealed chambers waiting for a specific chord. The roots do not drink; they breathe, anchor, and feed by extracting mineral vapor and dry salts from the powder beneath them. The ground itself is not soil but a complex of silt, ash-like residue, dried organic fragments, and stone dust, all moving in patterns determined by vibration.

When the sound is too loud, the gardens withdraw into silence. Their stems tighten. Their leaves curl into fists. The tiny dry flowers close. The plants become dense, matte, unresponsive, as if they too are hiding in recesses. When the resonance lowers, the gardens open with startling speed. Stems relax. Ribs shift. The papery flowers burst open with a sound like beads shaking in silk. This sound is small, almost invisible in the great roar outside, but inside the courtyards it is clear and private. To a Hushfolk, the opening of a garden is a sentence.

Some plants are more than individual organisms. In larger gardens, the root networks interweave so completely through the dry mineral powder that it becomes hard to know where one plant ends and another begins. The city’s builders may have planted them that way. A whole courtyard garden can respond to a single touch as if it were one body. If a Hushfolk strikes a hollow stem in one part of a garden, another stem may tremble across the space, not because of wind, but because the two are tuned together.

The plants can house creatures. The quadrupeds sleep among their broad bases. The insects climb the highest stems to escape low tones. Hushfolk sit in the center of the foliage, their dry bodies matching the matte texture of leaves. Some gardens become sacred, not because they are old, but because they have retained the correct silence for generations. These are places of birth, death, repair, and council. To damage them is a crime, but the punishment is not imprisonment. It is exile into sound.

### The city itself as inhabitant

If one asks whether the city is alive, the answer depends on what kind of life one is willing to recognize.

The city is not alive in the way an animal is alive. It has no heart, no skin, no breath in the ordinary sense. But it is built of stone and air and crystal and dust, and those substances move through it in a coherent pattern. It has cycles. It has memory. It has moods. It repairs itself through mineral deposits, through hand-tuned copper bands, through the slow pressure of dry roots, and through the constant shaping of air. It is not a building. It is an organ.

Its streets are passages of breath. Its plazas are resonating chambers. Its shafts are lungs. Its dome is a lens and a reflector. Its fluted channels are veins for a substance that is not blood but warm vapor. The Hushfolk are not its rulers; they are its nervous system, its maintenance crew, its living tuning fork. The plants are its foliage and membrane. The animals are its mobile cells. The insects are its cleaners. The Underwarden is perhaps its oldest immune function, moving through deep places, testing walls, sealing cracks, and returning to darkness.

The city has learned its own shape.

It knows that too much growth chokes silence. It knows that too much emptiness makes sound unbearable. It knows that certain arches need to be repaired when the copper bands green at the edges and hum sour. It knows that certain steps wear down at the center because of generations of feet that stopped pressing them long ago, and that this worn hollow is not damage but memory. It knows that dust is not filth but movement, not decay but music, and that even the dead are not gone if they have been placed correctly.

The city does not sleep. It shifts.

The roar is never identical from one cycle to the next. The thermal currents rise from below and move through the channels; the crystal dome refracts and softens the light; the gardens respond; the animals align; the insects climb or vanish; the Hushfolk listen and move. The whole basin operates like a vast living chord, with the people inside it reduced to a few delicate overtones. It is not a ruin. A ruin has lost its function. The city still functions. It simply no longer serves the original purpose. Or perhaps it does, in a way so refined that it has become its own ecology.

### What it would be like to meet the inhabitants

If you entered through the fault in the pale cliffs and walked for many minutes, you would believe you were alone. The brightness would be even and sourceless. The dry heat would feel pleasant until the taste of salt and metal filled your mouth. The roar would arrive slowly, then all at once, pressing into your chest and teeth until you wanted to escape into silence.

Then, if you found a courtyard whose geometry canceled the sound, you might see movement at the edge of the garden.

A Hushfolk would be pressed against a wall, barely distinguishable from stone. Its skull plates would catch the light faintly. Its dry skin would be the blue-white of the city, flecked with copper. Its eyes would be pale and wide, not startled, only assessing. It would not speak. It would place one palm flat on the wall and feel your disturbance. Then it might tap once, twice, slowly on the stone beside its head.

That tap would not mean greeting. It would mean: you are too loud for this space.

If you stood still and lowered your body, breathing through your mouth rather than speaking, the Hushfolk might tilt its head. A dry flower near its foot might click open in response to the change in air pressure. Somewhere deeper in the courtyard, a bone-ear quadruped would lift its broad skull and look at you with eyes like pale stones. A few quartz-winged insects would shift along the stems, drawn not to your face but to the resonance of your bones.

The Hushfolk would watch to see whether you were a threat to the garden.

If you were not, it might do something more unsettling: it would guide you to a recess and leave you there. Not as a host, but as a maintenance action. Because the city cannot allow too many bodies in one silent place. You would feel the floor begin to hum again, and the sound would swell, and the Hushfolk would vanish into the geometry of the wall, and you would realize that you were only safe while you did not stay.

If you remained too long, the garden would begin to close around your presence. The stems would tense. The leaves would curl. The dry flowers would click shut. The quadrupeds would press themselves into farther corners. The insects would climb to the highest stems. The Hushfolk would not drive you out with weapons or shouted warnings. They would simply withdraw from you, and the withdrawal would be enough. In a city made of resonance, rejection is felt before it is heard.

### What lives there, finally

What lives there are the quiet nodes and the things that gather inside them.

There are the Hushfolk, few and dry-bodied, descended perhaps from the original builders, changed into something better suited to a city that no longer tolerates ordinary humanity. There are the bone-plated quadrupeds, not tame, not wild in any simple sense, but living instruments of safety. There are the quartz-winged insects and ringing beetles, cleaning and pollinating and carrying the garden’s dry pollen in glittering dust. There is the pale many-limbed Underwarden, tending the shafts below and answering to currents no surface creature can fully understand. There are the hollow-stemmed plants, the papery flowers, the mineral roots, the garden masses that open and close like slow lungs. And there is the city itself, the basin-wide organism of stone, air, light, vibration, and balance.

It is not a place for crowds. It is not a place for empires. It is not a place for love songs or market noise or the ordinary wetness of living. But it is alive. Its life is just too quiet in the wrong places, too loud in the right places, and too ancient to be explained except through the body’s response.

To ask who lives there is already almost to answer.

No one lives there except the spaces where no one can stand for too long.

Everyone lives there, if everyone means the entire tuning: plant, animal, dust, copper, stone, breath, and the last stubborn remnants of people who have learned to exist as silences inside a roaring city.
