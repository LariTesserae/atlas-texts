A single drop falling from the beard of dead willow does not arrive in emptiness. Before it reaches the pool it may touch a spider’s thread, a caddisfly’s pebble case, the shell of a water beetle, a child’s palm, the green seam of a moss wall, the tip of a reed, the rim of a clay pot, a hanging hide, the beak of a waiting martin, the flank of a newt, the worn stone lip of a channel. By the time it settles, the drop has been announced by half the living town.

That is the first thing one learns about the inhabitants of the Weeping Sill: no one lives there alone. The hollow is not a stage on which creatures act. It is itself a crowded body, a breathing floor, a cup of roots and water and slow decay. The people who call it the Weeping Sill, and the outsiders who call it Moss-bowl or High Hollow or simply the Sill, are all citizens of something older and more diffuse than a settlement. Life in the Sill is not set upon the land; it is stitched into it, woven by moss, bound by roots, held in by sedge, cooled by peat, made audible by frogs, carried upward by tiny bright droplets in the capillary hair of the ground.

The oldest residents are not people at all.

Moss lives everywhere in the Sill, and moss is not decoration. It is architecture. It pads the stones that form the meeting-square. It thickens the roofs of houses until a dwelling looks, from above, like a green mound breathing smoke. It fills the cracks of boulders and climbs the faces of walls with the patience of frost that has forgotten to melt. In the shade of overhanging rock it grows in cushions so deep that a hand sinking into them meets cool black water beneath. Along the channel edges it braids itself into dark velvet lips, drinking the runoff and holding it long after the rain has passed. Where a stone is too hard for soil, moss makes a beginning. Where peat is too wet to stand upon, moss gives something softer to the foot. It is the first layer of the hollow’s floor and the last layer of its houses.

Under the moss lies peat, and under the peat lie the ghosts of plants that died in previous seasons and were not allowed to dry. The black earth is made of decay suspended between water and time. It is dense with fungal threads and the slow work of roots, full of air pockets where larvae and tiny creatures move unseen. If a person lifts a chunk of damp turf, the underside is a city: white filaments of mycelium, red worm-bodies, beetle grubs, the pale threads of bog-plants, grains of sand, fragments of bark, seeds waiting through another year. The Sill’s ground is not simply soil. It is compost and sponge and memory, and everything that lives there feeds into it and feeds from it.

Lichen paints the boulders in blue-gray, rust, and pale yellow. Some lichens grow so flat on the stone that they seem to be stains; others raise their little cup-shapes and catch rain in their own small mouths. They live slowly, slowly, the way the Sill itself seems to live. They do not need to rise far from the stone. They do not need to compete with reed or willow. They claim the wet faces of rocks and the north sides of boulders and are content. A traveler passing quickly might think them part of the mountain’s coloring, but a child with a bone needle could trace their edges and find them lifted like thin leaves from the stone.

Sedge and bog-grass braid the living floor. Their roots knit the peat together, and their blades throw tiny shadows across the mat when the wind moves through them. They make the paths harder, the pools softer, the edges of the hollow more forgiving. Where water runs shallowly, they catch fine sediment; where frost comes, they hold moisture in their dark rhizome knots. They are grazed by insects, nibbled by voles, stepped by feet, cut for thatch, split for mats, twisted into cordage. Nothing in the Sill is left as merely one thing. A plant becomes food, wall, fuel, medicine, shelter, rope, bed, boundary, and return to soil.

Dwarf birches stand along the upper edges where the mat is thinning, their bark pale and papery, their branches bending into the wind. Stunted willows thicken the wetter margins, sending roots into the bog and sending branches toward the sky with a kind of wet determination. Their roots are anchors. Their branches are fences. Their stems are woven into bridges, into door arches, into house walls, into fish traps, into frames for drying racks. They are trees, and they are tools, and they are living buildings. They are trained rather than merely felled. A willow stake may be planted beside a channel in one year and tied into a bridge span in the next, bending without snapping, learning the town’s need.

In the shade of rocks and under the broad-leaved plants, ferns unfurl. Their curled beginnings appear like questions raised in the moss. Cotton grass spreads white tufts over the pool edges when the wind falls and the surface becomes still enough for reflection. Bog-plants, sedges, mosses, lichens, reeds, rushes, water-lilies in the warmer pools, and whatever small green things grow in the wet corners of walls and roof gardens make up a total vegetable world. If there is bare ground in the Sill, it is rare and brief. Stone is claimed. Wood is claimed. Clay is claimed. Even a hearthstone left too long in the rain becomes green with moss and silver with dried mineral.

Among these green architects live the smaller citizens, too many to count and too small to notice unless one bends close.

Springtails leap from wet litter. They are no bigger than grains of salt and can spring suddenly, as if disturbed by an invisible clock. Mites move across moss cushions like tiny eight-legged seeds. Snails glide along reed stems and stone edges, leaving trails that shine faintly in the cloud-lit air. Slugs, more cautious and more vulnerable to birds, hide under bark and in leaf folds until night or rain brings them out to graze the green films that grow in damp places. The underside of a loose stone is a gallery for their slow work.

In the water itself, abundance thickens. Caddisfly larvae carry little houses made of sand, gravel, leaf fragments, or small twigs. They crawl along the bottoms of channels like moving pebbles, each one a builder living inside its own architecture. Midges rise in columns from still pools when the air warms, thin and dark and persistent, sometimes so numerous that a hand thrust into them is lost. Mosquitoes find skin in the evening. Gnats cluster where peat is soft. Water beetles dart beneath surface films, carrying small bubbles of air as they move. Dragonflies hover over pools and lay eggs into the surface; their larvae live beneath, patient and hungry, hunting other smaller things. At the margins, tiny crustaceans and worms turn mud into soil. Algae and diatoms coat any surface that remains wet long enough, turning a stone, a pot shard, a wooden stake, the edge of a hide drying above a channel, into a faint green-brown field.

The amphibians are among the loudest and most obvious of the hollow’s residents. Frogs are not merely creatures that live in the pools; they are part of the Sill’s daily speech. Their spawn darkens the shallow margins in clusters and films, and tadpoles blacken the water where the sun warms it slightly more than elsewhere. Frogs call at dusk, after rain, before rain, through the whole night, then quiet again when the light rises. Their voices are not decoration; they are information. The people watch them. Frogs know whether water has grown too still, too rich, too cold, too empty. If frogs are silent, something has shifted in the channels. If they call too wildly, a pool may be drying too fast or filling with silt. Children learn to read frogs before they learn to read trade marks.

Newts move across the wet paths in their clumsy, bright way, especially after warm rains. Their skin is slick, their eyes large and startled-looking. They live in the same pools as frogs and tadpoles, eating smaller creatures, being hunted by birds and mammals, slipping under stones when a shadow passes. Toads may be rarer than frogs in the high wet hollow, but their warts and slow hops belong to the same world of damp stone and night air.

Fish live where water moves and where springs keep the channels cooler and clearer. In the deep runnels, small silver-bodied fish shelter in the shadows beneath roots and stones. Minnows gather in shoals where the light passes through surface water and the bottom is smooth. Bullheads and loaches hide under pebbles, stirring up silt when startled. In the swiftest spring-fed channels there may be trout, lean and quick, resting behind boulders and taking small insects that drift past. Fish do not make the Sill’s loudest music, but they make much of its hidden abundance. They are pulled from traps, caught on lines weighted with pebbles, speared by patient hands, smoked on racks, boiled with herbs and bark, fed to dogs, buried under new growth when too many remain for the day.

Birds take the whole built world as nesting material. Swallows and martins live in the eaves of houses under the broad, mossy roofs. They come in season, darting through the upper air above the pools, skimming wet surfaces for insects, building mud pellets and straw nests against wooden beams. They are welcome. A house without swallow nests is often a house too wet, too cold, or too new for the birds’ choosing; a house thick with them means insects and shelter. In the willow thickets, warblers and finches hide among the thin leaves, their bright notes breaking the gray air. Buntings feed along the sedge margins. Dippers bob and dive from channel stones. Wagtails flick along the shorelines, taking up insects from the wet pebble edges. Snipes and sandpipers probe where the mat thins and worms or larvae lie beneath. In the upper rocks, ravens patrol with ragged, loud calls, their black wings making the sky seem suddenly wider than the bowl.

Owls sleep in shaded crevices during the day and hunt over the hollow after dusk. They live in the architecture of the ground itself, in gaps beneath boulders and among the roots of old birches. At night their calls travel strangely far through the wet air, carrying over roofs and pools and stone channels as though the hollow were a room. Bats come later, or earlier, depending on the warmth of the evening. They move over the pools with quick turns, drinking from wet leaves or catching insects just above the surface. Their wingbeats are faint, but their presence is known: if bats rise, there are midges below, and if midges rise, water is near.

On the terraces above the wet floor, goats, sheep, and hardy cattle are kept. They belong to the Sill but not to its soft heart. They are moved to and from the upper slopes, their hooves worn into trails, their bodies smelling of wool and dung and milk. They trim the grasses before roots choke, carry fleeces that will become coats and blankets and padding, give milk that may be made into cheese or boiled into stews, and provide hides, horn, bone, sinew, and dung for fuel. Their presence is felt by the sound of bells, the smell of sheep-wood, the sudden bleat of a lamb caught on a wet ledge. They are part of the town’s outer breath.

Smaller mammals live at the edges and under them. Field voles tunnel beneath the sedge mat. Wood mice climb into store-houses if the granary floors are not sufficiently sealed, and for this reason the Sill’s people set traps of woven willow and sharpened stakes, and keep dogs where they can hear the scratch of claws in the dark.
