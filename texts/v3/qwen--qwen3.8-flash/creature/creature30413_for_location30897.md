What lives there can be named, but the names feel provisional, because the valley itself is provisional. Nothing arrived in a rush. Nothing invaded, conquered, or introduced itself with drama. The place began to be inhabited the moment a pipe joint made a cave, the moment a vent gave off enough warmth for moss to choose it, the moment a channel held water long enough for a fly to mistake it for a lake. The creatures that live here are not exotic because they are monstrous. They are strange because they fit too well.

## The tile-deer and the pale herds

The most obvious inhabitants are the herds.

They move across broad ceramic decks and open basins, animals with narrow legs and pale coats that catch the light in a way that makes them seem less like flesh than like pieces of the landscape given the idea of walking. Their bodies are compact and angular, their necks long in a way that suits the verticality of the valley, their faces quiet and alert. They do not look soft. They look prepared.

Their hooves are hard enough to click on polished metal and pale tile, but that click is not loud. It is small and rhythmic, a dry percussion that stops when the animals sense a change in the air. At the tips of the hooves there is a thin, resinous edge, dark as oil, that lets them stand on slick surfaces without splaying. They can climb ridges, cross gantries, and balance on the sharp margins of recently extruded platforms with a sureness that suggests the built world was made for them, or that they were made, very quickly, for it.

They do not eat grass alone. The pale tufts growing from drilled holes are only part of their diet. They lick mineral films from cold metal. They bite at stiff silver shrubs. They graze lichen from the undersides of walkways. They chew condensation from moss so thick that it has become almost a sponge. Some herds prefer warm sections near vents and furnaces, where the air smells of oil and faint salt; others live higher, on the open ridges, where the light is so bright and cold that their hides turn almost white along the flanks and a deep ochre along the spine.

They are most often seen in early morning, clustered near vents and cooling pipes, standing shoulder to shoulder against the chill. When they move, they move without hurry. A herd crossing an open basin can look like a line of small stones deciding to become animals. When the sub-bass turns beneath the valley, one of the deep mechanisms adjusting pressure or rain, the herds shift direction without visible cause. They seem to know before the mist knows. They seem to have learned the valley’s rhythm better than it has learned its own.

Newly poured edges trouble them. They will stand at the rim of a platform, heads low, listening with their ears laid flat, refusing to step onto a surface that is still too sharp or too recent. If left alone, the herd waits. If forced to move, the lead animals cross with a strange carefulness, as if each hoof must first confirm that the ground still agrees to be ground.

## The shard-goats and wall-climbers

Higher, where the architecture becomes too steep for the herds, live the climbers.

They resemble chamois, but that resemblance is only surface truth. Their bodies are shorter, more compact, and their feet are broad pads of gray rubbery flesh, ridged and sensitive, able to grip composite margins stepped like teeth. They climb the engineered walls, the outer sides of stacks, the narrow ledges between buildings, the vertical pipe forests, the overhangs beneath bridges. They seem to find the valley’s impossible angles ordinary.

Their horns curve backward like handles or hooks, not for violence but for resting on pipes, for pulling themselves up, for bracing against warm flues in cold wind. Their faces are narrow, their eyes set wide. When they stand against a wall, they can become nearly invisible: their coats are mottled in pale grays, oxidized blues, and a chalky white that looks like weathered tile until they blink.

They live in family bands of two or three, or occasionally in larger loose aggregations along favored thermal columns. They drink from the threads of condensation that run down vertical pipes, waiting for drops to gather at joints, then licking the metal with patient tongues. They feed on moss, lichen, the inner bark of shrubs that cling to vents, and mineral residues left where hot machinery cools. They are not loud. Their movement is almost silent, except for a soft scrape against composite or ceramic, and occasionally the faint click of a hoof against cable.
