What lives in the Hollowloom is not one kind, not one species, not one civilization in the narrow sense. If you stand long enough on a terrace, the answer becomes plural. It becomes a chorus of small movements, a soft negotiation of water, metal, flesh, root, and wing.

The people there call themselves the **Combed**.

They say this not as a grand title, but as a plain description. The Hollowloom combs water; the Combed live in the combing. They are the folk of the low places, the inhabitants of a swallowed town that has been taught by time to become a living room of stone. Outsiders, if there were outsiders, might call them mossfolk, dripfolk, cistern-dwellers, or root-tenders, but none of those names hold them. The Combed are too wet for folk names. They are too close to the city to be called wild. They are too alive for a city.

They live in houses, yes, but also in pipes, in the spaces behind engine grilles, in leaf-laid lofts, in basins of polished stone, in wall-niches furred with living moss, and in the slow, creaking seams where the built world has been persuaded, over centuries, to join the plant world. They do not merely occupy the Hollowloom. They help it continue.

---

The first thing you notice about a Combed person, if you happen to see one emerge from a low passage or descend a stair worn by mineral breath, is that they seem too suited to their surroundings to be foreign to them.

They are compact and low-set, built for ceilings that arch rather than tower, for stairways that curve around basins, for movement across damp terraces. They are not dwarfish by any simple measure, but they have learned the shape of a room-sized world in their bones. Their spines are supple, their shoulders broad, their hands long-fingered and wet at the tips. Their feet are wide and flat, with toes that spread like rootlets to grip slick stone. They move with a strange, careful silence, as if every floor were a sleeping animal they do not wish to wake.

Their skin is warm and faintly slick, not unpleasantly so, but like a leaf kept too long in a jar of soft water. It takes on shades of grey-green, brown, and pale silver, with darker patches where mineral deposits form. In some people, fine freckles of lichen grow along the jaw and shoulders, cultivated without intention or with devotion—no one is certain where the distinction begins. Over the palms and soles, a tough fungal rind forms, which they can peel away when it becomes too thick, as one might trim a fingernail, though they do so rarely and with reverence.

Their hair is usually heavy, damp, and dark, but it is rarely left alone. It is woven with moss threads, tiny seed-capsules, copper rings, and living fibers. A Combed person’s hair often hosts a small society of its own: minute beetles that eat stray spores, pale mites that clean oils from the scalp, tiny springtails that leap from strand to strand, and occasionally a moth that has decided to sleep there. Children are teased for having “bird-hair,” because sometimes a stray thread-crow settles in them, and elders are respected if their hair supports healthy clumps of luminous lichen. They do not consider this vanity. It is housekeeping.

Their eyes are large, dark, and adapted to scarcity. They see well in the dim amber of oil lanterns and the faint blue-green of mold-light, but bright lamps hurt them. They blink often. A Combed person can look sleepy to a surface visitor, but the half-lidded gaze is a defense. They are not tired. They are listening with their faces.

Their ears are the most revealing part of them.

They are unusually mobile and sensitive, cupped slightly backward, and tuned to the dripping and humming of the Hollowloom. A Combed person can tell the difference between water falling onto copper, onto moss, onto a leaf-shingle, or into a basin that has grown too full. They can hear when a pump is beginning to strain, when a cable has loosened in its sheath, when a swarm is shifting above the ceiling. Their hearing is so precise that it looks like a form of prayer.

Their lungs, too, are changed. They breathe humid air easily; dry air makes them ill. Their ribcages are deep, their throats wide, and along the sides of their chests are faint ridges and small flaps of tissue that help them retain moisture. These are not gills, not properly, but they make the act of breathing feel continuous with the dampness around them. When a Combed person exhales in a cooler pocket of the chamber, a small mist appears, and they may smile as if the room has answered them.

Their voices are soft, slightly wet, and often lowered by reflex. In the Hollowloom, even a whisper can travel through stone, and a shout can startle a flock and send dozens of creatures tumbling from a ceiling-root. So they speak carefully. Their language is full of taps, breath-sounds, and half-clicks. They do not like to break the living noise. They speak with water in their mouths often, not because they are always thirsty, but because a damp voice carries better in a chamber that already tastes of rain.

---

They do not dress as the surface folk would dress. Their garments are not protection from the world so much as extensions of it.

The most common clothing among the Combed is a fitted shell of leaf-felt, grown from overlapping cultivated leaves stitched with fine fungal thread and held at the edges by copper bands. This shell is warm when wet and surprisingly breathable when dry. It takes the color of whatever mineral water washes over it, so a worker who tends a cistern may return with a blue-grey stain across their shoulders, while a lamp-gardener may wear a faint golden shimmer from lichen dust.

Over this they may wear loose sleeves of insect-wing cloth, translucent and veined, gathered from the molted wings of large film-moths. These sleeves do not keep them dry—they are never dry—but they catch light beautifully when a lamp moves beneath them, turning the wearer into a flickering, half-seen creature. Many Combed also wear aprons made from the shed skins of basin-walkers, cured with salt and rendered tough as old leather. These aprons are often mapped with scorch marks, moss-growth, and small stains, and a worker’s apron is more personal than a face.

They rarely wear shoes, but they carry them: soft-soled wraps of woven root and mineral-treated hide, bound over the feet when they must walk through newly spilled acid-water, hot pipe zones, or freshly cleaned engine courts.

Their clothing is alive with maintenance. A tear is repaired by introducing a graft, a damp moss patch that is allowed to knit itself into the fabric. Copper buttons are often replaced by seed-beads that swell when wet. If a garment dries too completely, it is said to “forget itself,” and must be returned to a humid niche for a day before it can be worn again. To leave clothes hanging too long in a dry pocket is considered careless, almost disrespectful.

They do not wear jewelry in the ordinary sense. Instead, they carry water-signs: tiny clay beads hollowed to hold a single drop, copper pins curved to catch condensation, leaf-cups clipped to a shoulder or hip, and thin tubes of reed or metal slung against the skin to feed moisture to the mouth without stopping. A traveler, a rootwright, or a pump-tender may wear several at once, so they always taste of faint metal and green water.

---

The Combed do not all do the same work, and yet no work is considered entirely separate.

The **water-combers** are the most honored among the humble tasks. They move through the terraces at irregular hours, not because they are idle, but because the water is irregular. They carry catch-leaves, long trough-spoons, and narrow glass vials for sampling. They know which walls bead first, which tiles channel best, which basins hold too much salt, which roots must be trimmed so that a drip can fall properly into a reservoir. They can look at a damp stone and know whether the next drop will come in a minute or an hour. They are not seers. They are readers of persistence.

A child is taught to comb water before they are taught much else. The earliest lesson is not how to catch a drop, but how not to waste one.

The **rootwrights** maintain the living architecture. They move with knives of fishbone, copper, and sometimes hardened beetle-jaw. They cut, guide, graft, bind, and persuade. A wall may be failing not because the stone is weak, but because the roots behind it are pulling in the wrong direction. A roof may leak because a vine has learned, without instruction, to grow into a seam. A stair may become treacherous because pale rhizomes have pushed up through the tread and made it slick. Rootwrights do not fight this growth. They negotiate it. They sing low humming songs against the wood, which is partly superstition and partly vibration science, because certain plants respond to the sound of a moving body and grow differently when encouraged by resonance.

They have thick hands, often scarred with green-white lines where vines have squeezed them. The eldest rootwrights move slowly, but they understand the Hollowloom’s bones better than anyone. They can press an ear to a wall and tell you if the chamber is resting or remembering.

The **enginewrights** tend the machines that keep the settlement turning. They are part mechanic, part herbalist, part nurse. They feed nutrient slurry to pumps, lubricate bearings with rendered fats and mineral mosses, and clear the pale fungal rind from wires before it grows too far into copper. They do not regard the machines as dead. They regard them as stubborn organs in an enormous body that has not yet learned to rest.

A turbine is not cleaned with harshness; it is coaxed. A dynamo is not restarted with violence; it is warmed, touched, allowed to wake. Some pipes are said to have tempers. Some boilers are said to dream. The enginewrights do not necessarily believe this literally
