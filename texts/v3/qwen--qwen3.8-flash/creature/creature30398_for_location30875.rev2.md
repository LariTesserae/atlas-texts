There is no one living in the hollow.

Not a person. Not a herd. Not a family with a fire-place, not a sleeping body, not a nest tucked against the rim. No animal has claimed it. No bird has looked down and found a place worth perching in. No burrowing thing has dug. No crawler, skimmer, gnawer, singer, hunter, or scavenger has made the hollow its territory. The absence is not a wound. It is a completion. The hollow is too finished for such things.

But that does not mean nothing lives there.

What lives there is barely “whoever.” It is mostly “whatever,” and that whatever is so slow, so dry, so close to stone, that it can be mistaken for a stain, a crack, a shadow, a habit of the earth rather than a life. The hollow is inhabited by a low arithmetic of presences: pale crusts, smoke-like lichens, curled tufts of moss, hairlike root threads, salt-bounded films, and invisible mineral-dwelling organisms that live by remaining.

They do not crowd. They do not compete loudly. They do not build. They persist.

---

### The skin on the floor

At first glance, the floor of the hollow is only cracked mineral dust. But if one looks more closely, the pale polygons are not merely plates of dried sediment. Many of them are bound together by a thin living skin.

It is a biocrust.

Not one creature, not one plant, not one fungus alone. It is a community pressed into the surface like a fingerprint left by centuries. It is made of filamentous cyanobacteria, dead cells, mineral grains, dust, salt, and the faint ghost of organic matter too slowly turning over to be called soil. The crust is grey, tan, sometimes faintly olive, sometimes almost black where the light is thinnest. It is not vivid. It does not need to be seen. It only needs to not lose itself.

These organisms live at the margin of life’s ordinary appetite. They photosynthesize when the faintest light reaches the rim and they can absorb enough moisture from air, from rare dew, from deep stone exhalation. When dry, they collapse into a dormant state so complete that one could almost swear they are part of the crust itself. Their cells shrink, their filaments fold inward, their metabolism slows to almost nothing. They become a mineral pause.

But if a thin film of moisture touches them, they can wake.

Not loudly. Not visibly. There is no trembling, no unfurling, no sudden green triumph. The hollow would not celebrate it. But the crust may begin again its silent work: binding dust, capturing carbon, cycling trace nitrogen, giving the floor a slight cohesion it would not have if it were only salt and stone.

This is not a creature with a body. It is a body made of many tiny bodies, living as one surface.

If a foot breaks the crust, it is not a wound that will bleed. It is a slow ruin. The hollow has no hurry in which to heal. It may take years. It may take centuries. The broken edge will curl again. The dust will settle. The mineral skin will return, if conditions allow.

---

### The lichen at the rim

Where there is enough light to be called light, but not enough to make a bright thing bright, there are lichens.

They grow on the rim, on the low shelves, on the smooth lips where wind has worn stone into softness. They are not leaves. They are not flowers. They are not grass. They are composite lives: fungus making a structure, photosynthetic partner making food. They are partners so intertwined that one could not say where one begins and the other ends.

They appear as grey-black smears, thin as smoke, spreading over mineral flakes. Some look like old paint that has been carefully applied by no hand. Others break into tiny flakes at the edges, the way dried skin can, though they are alive.

These lichens are the hollow’s most visible residents.

They live in slow motion. A millimeter may be an achievement. A decade may pass without any observable change. They do not seek water. They wait for it. They do not seek shelter, because they are shelter, a miniature architecture of fungal fiber holding algae or cyanobacteria near enough to dim light.

They reproduce by fragmentation, by shedding powdery soredia, by tiny pieces of themselves loosening and finding purchase on a nearby rock shelf. There is no blossom, no fruit, no dramatic dispersal. Their offspring are fragments. Their future is drift.

They are colorless in a way that makes them seem already fossilized. But they are not dead. They are simply living at the low threshold of patience.

To the human eye, the lichens near the rim might look like stains, like mineral bloom, like the faint memory of something that was once alive. That is exactly their survival strategy. They do not advertise. They persist.

---

### The curled moss: lace without softness

In one or two places, a small tuft of moss stands apart.

It is no larger than a hand’s breadth. Pale, tan, faintly olive, curled inward like old lace pressed against a cold stone. It looks dead. It looks like something that fell from a wetter world and dried out long ago. It looks like a fragment of a plant, not a plant.

But it is not dead.

Mosses are among the most patient of visible lives. They have no true roots, no vascular ambition, no need to become tall. They live near the surface, one cell thick in places, relying on the slow exchange of light and moisture. When water is scarce, they do not necessarily die. They desiccate. Their cells lose water, their leaves curl inward, their color fades, their metabolism slows to an almost imperceptible hum.

The hollow’s moss tufts live in that curled state most of the time.

They are not growing toward anything. They are not reaching. They are not competing for space. They are holding themselves together. They are waiting.

If rare dew forms in the cooler part of the day, or if a deep moisture pulse rises just enough to wet the mineral dust without pooling, the moss can recover. Not with speed. Not with drama. The tiny leaves relax. The color darkens. Photosynthesis resumes at a whisper. The moss begins again, not as if it had never been lost, but as if remembering the shape of water.

Then the hollow dries again.

The moss curls.

The color fades.

The patience returns.

A human might step over it without noticing. A hand might touch it and feel only brittle dust. But it is alive in the way that stone is not alive, except that stone is its partner, its shelter, its memory of moisture.

---

### The pale root-threads: dormant waiting things

In deeper pockets, where almost no light enters, there are root-like fibers.

They are pale, hairlike, bent sometimes, sometimes standing slightly from the surface as if undecided whether they belong above the crust or beneath it. They are white or tan, dry, delicate, but not soft. They do not smell of decay. They do not have the blackened look of rot. They look like threads pulled from the edge of death but not yet released by it.

Some may be the roots of a larger plant growing outside the hollow’s rim.

Some may be moss rhizoids.

Some may be fungal filaments mistaken for roots.

Some may be old root tissue that has not fully died, locked in a state so low that it resembles a relic rather than a living part.

The description of the hollow does not allow us to know, with certainty, who owns each thread. That uncertainty is itself part of the place. Life here is not clear. It is not abundant enough to present obvious bodies. It hides inside ambiguity.

But these threads do not live with the ordinary purpose of roots in a garden: to drink eagerly, to anchor, to carry nutrients upward in green abundance. In this hollow, a root may be a memory of drinking. It may be a waiting thing. It may be a filament of tissue holding on, because the conditions are not quite hostile enough to kill it and not quite wet enough to let it grow.

If there is water deep below, it may touch these threads in the faintest way. Not as flow. Not as pool. Not as trickling seam. Only as vapor, as molecular presence, as a long hesitation in the stone.

The roots do not drink visibly.

They wait.

That is enough.

---

### The salt-biofilm and endolithic life

The hollow has thin ridges of salt-like efflorescence where moisture from deep stone tried long ago to surface and then vanished. But even here there may be life.

Not animals. Not plants in the ordinary sense. Not insects or worms or crawling things. But microscopic organisms: cyanobacteria, bacteria, archaea, fungal hyphae, and other life-forms that are not “whoever” but are certainly not “nothing.”

Some of these may live inside pores of the mineral crust. They may be endolithic life: organisms that inhabit stone itself, not by boring holes like visible creatures, but by occupying tiny voids, fractures, salt films, and mineral boundaries.

They live by trace chemistry.

They may feed on the faintest oxidation of minerals. They may survive on salts, iron compounds, sulfur traces, or the slow breakdown of ancient organic dust. They may not be decomposers in the usual sense, because there is no abundance to decompose. Instead, they are processors of the almost nothing: the microscopic recycling of the faintest organic residue into mineral form.

Their bodies are too small to see without magnification. Their lives are too slow to notice. They do not bloom. They do not move. They do not call. But they are part of the reason the hollow is not sterile.

Sterile would mean no living activity at all.

This place is not sterile.

It is sparse.

There is a difference.

---

### No animals, and not even the memory of animals

The absence of animals is not accidental.

There is no food web strong enough to support predators. There is no grazing abundance. There is no carcass, no dung pile, no rotting mass, no insect colony, no burrow system. If an animal did come, it would find no reason to stay. There would be nothing to eat, nowhere to hide, no moisture to drink, no warmth worth a nest, no shadow deep enough to make a lair.

Even the smallest insects would not find a colony here.

Insects require moisture, organic accumulation, decay, temperature variation, and social or solitary life cycles that depend on abundance. The hollow has only a minimal biology. It has surfaces, not habitats. It has persistence, not plenty.

So no animal lives there.

That means no eyes. No jaws. No wings. No legs. No antennae. No tracks. No calls. No dung. No nests. No predator. No prey.

The living things that occupy it do not hunt. They do not flee. They do not mate in visible ceremonies. They do not fight for territory. They do not sing. They do not die in bodies large enough to be remembered.

They live by becoming part of the stone.

---

### Death without decomposition

There are deaths in the hollow, but they are quiet deaths.

A patch of lichen may fail after centuries of survival. A moss fragment may dry too long and lose viability. A root thread may finally give way. A cyanobacterial film may be destroyed by a shift in wind-borne dust or by a mineral change too slow for human patience to notice.

But death does not become a spectacle.

There are no rotting carcasses. No fly-covered remains. No bloated decay. No fungal blooms with fruiting bodies. No black humus accumulating beneath dead things. The dryness prevents it. The scarcity prevents it. The mineral nature of the floor prevents it.

When something dies here, it does not sink into rich soil. It remains as fragment, as flake, as pale thread, as salt-rimmed residue. Its body becomes dust. Its chemistry becomes crust. Its organic memory becomes mineral.

The dead feed the living only at the most attenuated level.

A dead moss leaf may become a surface for new crust.

A dead lichen fragment may be broken down by microbes so slowly that it remains recognizable for years.

A dead filament may become part of the grey film that looks like a shadow.

Death is not dramatic. It is another form of persistence.

---

### The hollow is not a body, but it has a physiology

If one asks what lives there in the broadest sense, one might say: the hollow itself has a kind of physiology.

Not because it is conscious. Not because it is magical. Not because it has a will. But because its components interact in a way that sustains itself.

The stone holds heat.

The dry crust resists movement.

The salt ridges mark where moisture attempted and failed.

The lichens gather faint light.

The mosses retain the possibility of water.

The root threads maintain a connection to depths no one can see.

The microscopic life recycles what almost nothing is.

Together, they form an ecology with the population of a thought.

It is not a forest. It is not a field. It is not a meadow. It is not a garden. It is closer to a sealed mineral page covered with faint, living notation.

The hollow does not have an organism.

It has a condition.

That condition is alive in the weakest, most ancient sense: it responds, it persists, it waits, it fails, it recovers, it slowly returns to its own shape.

---

### Humans do not live there

If someone from the primitive age outside this hollow came near, they might glance in and see nothing.

No cave worthy of shelter. No spring. No edible plant. No game trail. No animal remains. No safe place to build. No reason to settle.

They might stop. They might look. They might touch the warm rim with hands shaped by tool-making, scraping, carrying, and striking. They might drop a flake of struck stone into the hollow and then walk on. But that would not mean they lived here.

No fire has burned in this hollow.

No grave was marked here.

No boundary was drawn here.

No child cried here.

No elder rested here.

No tool was crafted here.

No animal was trapped here.

No water was drunk here.

No one has made a home in it.

If a human body came here, it would not become part of the hollow’s life in any meaningful way. It would not be buried. It would not feed animals. It would not sink into rich earth. It would dry in the mineral stillness, if the circumstances allowed it to be here at all. But no evidence suggests that anyone was ever brought here for that purpose. The hollow is not a cemetery. It is not a shrine. It is not a hideout. It is not a landmark with stories.

It is untouched.

And that untouchedness is the reason it remains what it is.

---

### The resident is not one being but a partnership

If one insists on calling the hollow’s inhabitant “it,” then the best name is not a species name.

It is not “the moss” or “the lichen” or “the crust” alone.

It is the coalition of those things.

The resident is the grey film on the floor.

The resident is the smoke-like lichen at the rim.

The resident is the curled lace of pale moss.

The resident is the root-thread waiting below the crust.

The resident is the microscopic life inside the salt ridges.

The resident is the slow partnership between stone, fungus, cyanobacteria, algae, and desiccation-tolerant plant tissue.

If one were to personify the hollow, it would not have a face, limbs, voice, or hunger. It would have edges, textures, and patience. It would not say “I am here.” It would demonstrate its presence only by its refusal to be entirely sterile. It would not look like a creature. It would look like a place that has learned to hold life without letting life become noisy.

It would be a being of dryness.

A being of warmth.

A being of faint light.

A being of stillness.

Not magical.

Not conscious.

Not supernatural.

Only alive in the smallest possible way.

---

### Their way of living is not growth but continuation

Ordinary life shows itself by growth.

A sprout rises.

A leaf expands.

A flower opens.

A wing moves.

A paw prints.

A song fills air.

Here, life does not show itself that way.

Growth is not impossible, but it is almost invisible. A lichen may advance a fraction of a millimeter. A moss may gain a new thread of tissue. A crust may bind dust over decades. But none of these changes can be witnessed within a human lifetime with any certainty.

So the mode of life here is continuation.

It is the ability to remain alive without appearing to live.

The moss remains alive by being almost dead.

The crust remains alive by being almost stone.

The lichen remains alive by being almost stain.

The root thread remains alive by being almost relic.

The microbes remain alive by being almost invisible.

They do not conquer the hollow.

They inhabit its margins.

They occupy niches too small for stories.

They survive because the environment is too stable, too slow, too dry, and too sparse for anything faster or hungrier to exist here.

---

### They are the hollow’s furniture

The description says the sparse life is the furniture of the place.

That is true.

The hollow has no tables, no hearths, no beds, no shelves, no walls built by hands. Its furniture is biological and mineral.

The salt ridges are carved edges.

The cracked plates are floorboards.

The lichen patches are rugs laid by no one.

The moss tufts are old fabric left in a corner.

The root threads are loose strings beneath the surface.

The biocrust is the dust that has learned to hold.

The rim is the threshold.

The dimness is the ceiling.

The warmth is the air.

Nothing is arranged for comfort.

Everything is arranged for persistence.

If a person were to enter, there would be nothing to sit on, nothing to eat, nothing to drink, nothing to gather, nothing to burn, nothing to hide behind, nothing to mark, nothing to remember. The hollow would offer no hospitality. It would offer only texture, temperature, silence, and the faint evidence of life pressed near the edge of death.

---

### The hollow’s inhabitants do not experience time as urgency

They do not fear drought.

They do not expect rain.

They do not compete for sunlight.

They do not mourn their slow growth.

They do not notice the absence of birds.

They do not know they are rare.

To them, the hollow is not a desert. Not a trap. Not a barren thing. It is simply the medium in which they can continue.

The moss does not think of itself as stranded.

The lichen does not think of itself as colorless.

The crust does not think of itself as thin.

The root thread does not think of itself as pale.

They have no nervous system with which to notice the world, no brain with which to suffer scarcity, no eyes with which to be lonely. Their “experience,” if it can be called that, is chemistry and physics: water, light, temperature, salt, mineral surface, cell wall, filament, spore, fragment, waiting.

Their time is not the time of hunger or fear.

Their time is the time of stone.

A century is a mood.

A human lifetime is a passing breath of air.

A footstep is a disaster for the crust, but a disaster without witness.

The hollow does not hurry.

Its inhabitants do not hurry.

They are not in a race with anything.

They are only in a state of almost continuing.

And almost continuing is enough.

---

### If one could shrink down, what would be seen?

Not a city.

Not a wilderness.

Not a battlefield.

Not a nursery.

Not a feeding ground.

Instead, perhaps:

A grey floor like parchment.

A lattice of cyanobacterial filaments.

Dead cells fused with mineral grains.

Tiny salt crystals catching dim light.

A lichen flake lifted at one edge, dark beneath, faintly olive at the margins.

A microscopic spore dusted across a crack.

A moss leaf curled inward like a closed hand.

A hypha passing through pore space, invisible, patient.

A root thread pale as bone, but not bone.

A fragment of old life not yet fully returned to mineral.

The hollow would reveal itself as a slow biological surface, a living film pressed onto warm stone, breathing almost not at all.

There would still be no animal eyes.

Still no movement.

Still no sound.

Still no society.

Still no drama.

Only the faintest proof that life can inhabit a place by becoming almost indistinguishable from patience.

---

### So what lives there?

No one.

And:

- the biocrust on the floor,
- cyanobacterial filaments in the mineral skin,
- salt-bound microbial films,
- endolithic bacteria and fungi,
- lichen at the rim,
- fungus-and-algae partnerships growing by fragments,
- desiccation-tolerant moss tufts,
- pale root-like threads,
- rhizoids,
- hyphae,
- dead-cell dust,
- spores,
- fragments,
- dormant waiting organisms,
- a thin mineral ecology of almost nothing.

If the answer must be singular, say:

The hollow is inhabited by a slow, sparse, composite life that lives by remaining close to stone.

If the answer must be personal, say:

The resident is patience, with a body of crust.

If the answer must be poetic, say:

Life in this place has become a grey fingerprint on the floor of the world.

If the answer must be plain, say:

No animals live there. No humans live there. No constructed being lives there. Only lichen, moss, root-like fibers, salt films, and microscopic mineral-dwelling organisms persist.

If the answer must be true to the description, say:

The hollow has inhabitants, but they are not a crowd. They are the faintest possible answer to “alive.” They are the thin, warm, dark, dry proof that life can occupy a place without filling it, without moving in, without making noise, without needing to be seen.

They live there by being almost stone.

And perhaps that is enough.
