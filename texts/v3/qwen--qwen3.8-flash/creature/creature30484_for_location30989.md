No single being lives in the bowl.

If you look for one creature and try to make it the owner of the place, the place will not offer you one. It has no king among the moss, no hermit in the hollows, no watcher on the rim. What lives there is less like a collection of animals and more like a single slow community that has forgotten where its members end and one another begin. It is a nation of damp things, a quiet parliament of skins and films and soft bodies, packed so completely that even their individuality seems, after a while, like a rumor.

The oldest and truest residents are not the creatures with eyes or limbs. They are the films.

Before any moss can become a cushion, there is a gloss of life on the stone: microscopic algae, pale bacterial skins, fungal threads no thicker than a hair, spores suspended in the water’s surface tension, tiny protozoan things that live and die inside droplets no larger than a bead of sweat. These are the basin’s first citizens. They do not have bodies you can point to. They are the wetness becoming animate. They make the stone taste alive. They are grazed by snails, consumed by midges, carried on the feet of salamanders, and spread by the slow movement of water from one terrace to the next. They are the reason the basin seems not merely wet, but inhabited at its surface, as if the stone itself has learned to breathe in the dark.

Then come the plant-creatures, if such a word is allowed.

The mosses are not passive ground-cover. They are the basin’s primary architecture, its memory, its skin, and its keeper. Every moss cushion is a standing colony, a miniature society with layers upon layers, a cathedral built one cell at a time. The moss does not merely grow; it arranges. It holds water against its own body. It shelters insects. It gives purchase to lichens. It forms hollows for droplets and channels for their slow overflow. It receives the dead and returns them as pulp. It does not remember individuals, but it remembers pattern. If a pebble is dropped onto it, the moss will fold around the pebble as though making a cradle. If a salamander passes through, the moss will straighten afterward with no record of the crossing except a faint dampness in the shape of a trail.

Around the moss move liverworts like tiny open hands. Sedges rise from saturated peat with translucent sheaths. Ferns unfurl not toward sun but toward moisture, their fronds pale and narrow, soft as inner ears. Lichens creep across stone in skins of gray and rust, neither plant nor fungus alone, but a partnership so intimate it appears as a single living surface. The fungi are quieter still: threads between roots, caps small enough to shelter under a fingernail, no dramatic light, only the faint wet sheen of being alive. In total darkness they do not need to announce themselves. The moisture does that for them.

The animals, meanwhile, are mostly too small to be believed from a distance.

There are eyeless insects that crawl across moss like living dust. They are pale, leggy, or rounded and bead-like, depending on what they eat. Some move by sensing the film of water on surfaces; some walk upon it. They do not hunt because they do not need to. The basin provides a constant harvest of spores, decay, algae, and shed skins. They touch one another constantly, not with fear, but with the casual friction of things that have never needed to learn boundaries.

There are tiny pale snails no larger than lentils. They graze the mineral gloss off stone and moss, leaving behind trails that shine faintly in the dark. Their shells are not bright or ornate; they are smooth, thin, almost damp-looking even when dry, which is to say even when not fully dry. They move without urgency. They seem to know that no weather follows them and no season threatens them. Their lives are slow and curved, like the motion of water over a rounded pebble.

There are moths, too. Not large moths, not the kind that fly against a lamp. These moths have translucent wings so thin the veins stand out like pale lace. Their bodies are not fluffy with warmth, but sleek with moisture. They rest on the walls of the basin, often touching other moths, sometimes one upon another, as if the only true safety in such a place is to be surrounded by similar damp softness. They do not flutter. They do not scatter. They move very little, and when they do, it is because the film of water on their wings has shifted, not because they were startled. In the darkness, motion is rare enough to be considered a language, but these moths speak it softly, in half-intentions.

There are salamanders no longer than a finger. These are perhaps the most complete vertebrate residents of the basin, though they barely qualify as animals in the ordinary sense. They are slender, glossy, with tiny limbs and ribs visible through skin that seems to exist only to stay moist. Their gills are reduced but not absent. They rest in the same fold of moss for long intervals, not sleeping exactly, but listening with their bodies. They taste the air with their mouths. They sense pressure changes in the water film. They eat larvae, springtails, small midges, and other tiny things too quick to see. Their violence is small and immediate: a flick, a mouth closing, a tiny body disappearing into another body without ceremony. There is no drama in it, no chase across open ground, only the patient closing of a wet mouth.

There are soft, blunt-pawed animals like shrews or water-mice, though they are neither exactly. Their fur is so dense it sheds moisture without losing warmth. Their eyes are almost gone, covered by small folds of skin. They do not need to see in a world that speaks through dampness, temperature, scent, and slope. They feel the thickness of moss, the pull of water into a hollow, the softness of another animal’s flank, the faint mineral taste of stone. They live in hollows not because they are hiding, but because the hollows have been made for them by erosion and moss. Their young are born into the same wet world their mothers know, and no one teaches them the shape of the basin because the basin does not change.

There are midges hanging in the air without swarming. They are barely more than wetness with wings. They seem to rest in flight, suspended by the saturation of the air itself. Their movements do not disturb the stillness; they are part of it. Sometimes they land on the backs of moths, on the rims of pools, on the faces of stone, and then, after a moment, move on again without leaving a trace. Their lives are short, but their deaths are absorbed so completely by the moss and water that no corpse ever appears to interrupt the order. They may not be midges at all in the ordinary sense. They may be only a stage in the basin’s breathing.

There may also be creatures too small to notice: blind crustacean-like things in the pools, pale springtails, mites that look like moving grains of dust, tiny predators no larger than a needlepoint, larvae that construct temporary cases out of mineral grains and then abandon them in the same pools where the grains were first gathered. These larvae are the closest thing to builders the basin possesses. They carry tiny fragments of stone, bind them with silk, make a portable shelter. But the shelter is not meant to last. The larva grows, sheds the case, and the case settles into the pool, where it becomes only another grain among grains. The act of making is therefore real, but it leaves no archive. Nothing is remembered except by the wet surface that holds it for a moment.

If there is a true resident, then, it is the surface itself.

What lives there is a being made of many surfaces: the shining skin of a stone, the damp coat of a shrew, the glossy back of a snail, the translucent wing of a moth, the membrane of a moss leaf, the film of water between them all. These surfaces touch constantly, and in touching, they form one continuous skin. The basin does not have creatures living on it so much as creatures living as it. They are not inside a room; they are the room’s moisture becoming alive. They are not in the moss; the moss is them as much as they are the moss.

Their world is not lit. It is not weathered. It is not seasonal. It is tactile and chemical. They do not know one another by sight, except perhaps faintly, as shapes of dampness. They know one another by pressure, temperature, smell, mineral taste, and the faint electrical change in water when a body enters it. A salamander passing under a stone makes a ripple of recognition that travels through the surrounding wetness. A snail crossing a moss cushion leaves a trail that says something, though no one can say what. A midge alighting on a leaf causes a surface to dimple and then settle, and that settling is enough.

There is no communication that rises above this. No one calls to another. No one sings in the moss. No one warns of danger, because the basin has no outside from which danger could come. No predator of size enters it. No bird nests there, for the pools are too shallow and the air too still. No fox, no owl, no cat. No human has stayed long enough to leave a fire scar, a carved mark, a broken twig bent into a trap. The largest danger is drying, and drying does not come. The largest mystery is the thin boundary between one creature and the next, and that boundary is soft.

These creatures do not seem to fear one another, because they do not seem to have separate enough selves to fear.

They rest together not out of love, but because space is already filled. They do not compete so much as compress. If a pebble must be moved, several bodies press against it without conflict. If a leaf holds dew, a dozen small legs touch it at once. If a pool receives a falling droplet, the droplet is not a visitor but a completion. The basin’s life has no ceremony, but it has exactness. Nothing falls where it should not. Nothing rests where it will not fit.

The tools they use are as temporary as breath.

A shrew-like animal may nudge a pebble under a root to make a ridge, letting water gather at the end of a moss cushion. It will drink from that ridge for perhaps one season, though there is no season. Then moss will creep over the pebble, or water will dissolve the edge of the root, and the ridge will become only a memory of movement in the surface of the stone. A leaf may be bent to hold dew, but it will curl and collapse. A twig may rest against a hollow and slow the flow of a tiny channel, but it will rot into pulp and the channel will widen around it. Even the larvae’s cases are made of borrowed grains and returned as grains. Nothing is stored. Nothing is inherited. Nothing becomes culture except the basin itself.

The creatures live not by building the place, but by being built into it.

They do not age in any obvious way. This does not mean they are immortal. It means their deaths are so quiet, so immediately recycled, that the community appears unchanged. One snail shell may fill with spore-dust and become a hollow for a springtail. One salamander body may sink into moss and be taken apart so evenly that no one sees the taking apart. One moth wing may fall into water and spread a film of paleness across the surface before dissolving into use. Loss does not appear as loss because it appears only as redistribution. The basin does not mourn. It reorganizes.

So when asked who lives there, the answer is not a name.

It is a density.

It is a crowd of tiny wet bodies moving without urgency through a dark bowl of stone. It is moss and mineral, spore and saliva, wing and shell, fur and film. It is eyeless insects and blind salamanders, moths resting on one another, midges suspended as if the air were thick enough to hold them, snails drawing shining trails that are longer-lived than their shells. It is fungi too humble to shine, lichens too slow to be noticed, ferns folded like sleeping hands. It is all the life that can exist without sun, without fire, without witnesses, without future.

And if you press hard enough to ask whether there is one presiding intelligence, one creature that has learned the shape of the bowl so completely that the bowl belongs to it, then the answer is yes, but only as a way of speaking.

The presiding thing is the basin itself.

It is the stone, wet and rounded. It is the water, present in every hollow. It is the moss, the film, the trail, the glow. It is the creatures not as individuals, but as their habit of being there together. They do not live in the place; they are its living. The place does not contain them; it performs them. Its life is not dramatic, not heroic, not evolutionary in any way that could be pointed to. It is established. It is complete. It has too many mouths, too many skins, too many soft bodies to be reduced to a single creature, yet not so many that anything is missing.

There is, in the end, no person at the center of the bowl.

There is only the center.

The center is a salamander under a leaf and the leaf over the salamander and the moss holding the leaf and the stone holding the moss and the water holding all of it, and none of these can be removed without the rest adjusting around the absence. If one were to name the resident, it would have to be called something like the wet commons, the small damp parliament, the congregation of surfaces, the folk of the held breath.

But they would not answer to it.

They would only shift, very slightly, as the darkness shifts, as water shifts, as one soft body makes room for another soft body, as the whole living bowl continues without need of name, witness, or change.
