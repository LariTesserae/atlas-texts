There is no single tenant to point at and say, *they live there*. The Drip-Hall is inhabited the way a hand is inhabited by heat: not by one body, but by a crowded agreement. It contains small human households, herds, lofts, crawls, drips, roosts, and niches. It contains laborers and cleaners, signal-keepers and water-readers, warm-blooded engines and cold-blooded sentinels. It contains green life that is permitted only where it does not become too greedy. It contains the room’s own strange body, its sweating walls and remembering floors, until the line between “who lives here” and “what lives here” grows soft as damp mortar.

The people are the most obvious, and yet they are not the masters of the place so much as its careful servants. They are short where the arches are low, broad in the shoulders from lifting wet pots and pulling ropes, and soft-eyed from moving between shadow and slanted light. Their hands are more familiar to them than their faces. A stranger would not know them by name so much as by how they touch things: how they test a tile with the ball of a thumb, whether a channel runs clear, how they lift a lid with two fingers and a bent wrist to avoid spilling the moisture inside. Their skin is never quite dry, not because they are unwashed, but because the air has texture, and that texture settles on them. Their hair smells faintly of oiled cloth, smoke, wet clay, and goat fur. The wool they wear is damp when it is taken off and warm when it is put back on. They sleep in layers and rise from them like water rising through stone.

They are not a single family in the simple sense. They are several households arranged around duties. Some live nearest the Grinding Pot, where the floor is worn into a shallow court of use. Some keep the high lofts, where the air is drier and the rope ladders swing. Some are always near the braziers, feeding dung cakes and adjusting the iron rings until the heat spreads in a usable circle instead of making the room bake too close. Others are the children who learn to run with pots, to climb without knocking over grain trays, to hear whether the rats are working or hiding. The oldest among them know things no one wrote down: which basins will overflow first, which crack always gives the most condensation, which goat will not move if its mat has been shifted, which shelf will hold a certain weight because the wood was cut from a particular tree decades ago.

The children are the strangest inhabitants, because they belong to the hall completely. A surface child might grow up thinking that rain falls from sky to earth. A Lower-Room child grows up knowing that rain can be given back. They learn to stand under the made ceiling without fear when the first cold drop comes through a pipe or a waxed hood. They learn not to wipe water from a tile too soon. They learn that a wet crescent by the grindstone means the south basin is drawing too much. They learn to place a foot in the Grinding Pot and feel the thin river of water move across their toes, then pull back without complaint when the hooves startle. They learn the rules of the room before they learn the names of the creatures in it: no goat near the crabs, no water-creature in the grain loft, no rat left dead in a tube, no bat disturbed at noon. They are not raised to own the place. They are raised to answer it.

The people call themselves by tasks more than bloodlines. There is the Drip-Reader, who sits sometimes for a whole morning near the north tiles and notices when a channel is trying to move somewhere new. There is the Hood-Setter, who climbs and adjusts the leather mouths over the ceiling pipes when the weather above changes. There is the Plate-Striker, who decides when the bronze must be sounded and who always seems to know, from the way the echoes come back, whether the room is too quiet, too hot, or too crowded. There is the Mat-Layer, who keeps the goat platforms dry and knows the difference between a mat that has been wrung well and one that looks dry but still breathes damp. There is the Pot-Husband or Pot-Wife, who hears when a clay vessel cracks because someone has dragged it against the wall at the wrong angle. These names are not always formal. Sometimes a person simply becomes the one whose name is remembered beside the thing they do.

They move through the hall with a practiced economy. No one crosses an open path when a worn groove will do. No one sets a pot where water will pool unless water is wanted. If a person has to pass a shelf of drying sprouts, they bend their shoulder to the wall and their hip to the edge, leaving a polished arc in the brick that has been worn there by everyone who came before. Their feet know temperature by tile. A woman can tell, while reaching for a lamp, whether the Grinding Pot is clear, cloudy, or beginning to run over the lip. A child can tell, without looking, whether the crabs are clicking. A man can tell from the tapping of quail on shelves whether the warm wall is too warm or whether one of the lizards has disturbed the lower roost.

They do not speak loudly unless the room demands it, and yet they do not fear noise. The Drip-Hall is loud with its own life, so human voices must cut through it or wait for it. They speak in short sentences and use hand signals when the braziers are going and the goats are restless. Their language has many small percussive sounds: tongue clicks like a beetle’s shell, quick taps like a rat in a tube, soft whistles that can be heard over dripping. They have names for the ways rain behaves inside the made ceiling: one word for the thin mist that gathers on tiles and another for the single large drop that falls before a whole wall darkens. They say the hall “remembers,” and they mean it literally, not as poetry, as a warning or a fact.

Their bodies have begun to match the rooms. They walk with a slight inward lean because low arches and crowded platforms have narrowed the natural path. They sit easily on stools with one knee raised because the floor is often wet and they have learned not to let clothing touch stagnant water. Their ears grow good at picking out small signs: the hiss of a bad bronze tap, the wrong silence of a basin, the particular clatter that means a goat has shifted onto a dry corner that should not have been dry. Their hands are rough in places and tender in others, depending on whether they mostly lift pots, scrape moss, tie mats, or grind grain. They are always slightly damp with something. They are also always slightly careful.

They eat mostly what the room can spare. Porridge made from grain that has been soaked, measured, and ground on saddle querns. Cakes cooked on warm stone and softened with goat milk. Eggs taken from quail nests at the hour when the birds are most settled. Sometimes snails, when the air has been kind and the shells are full. Sometimes a few crabs, in a broth flavored with dried herbs and the faint salt memory of the basins. Their food is not rich by surface standards. It is efficient. It is eaten standing near the Grinding Pot, sitting on worn stone, or on a shelf if the floor is too busy. The meal becomes another sound: spoons, chewing, a goat sneezing, the drip continuing beneath it all.

But the humans are only one part of the population. They are outnumbered, out-furred, out-winged, out-legged. The true inhabitants are a federation of species.

The goats are among the most important and the least elegant. They live on raised platforms of woven mats over reed baskets, so that their heat rises into the lower air and their waste falls where it is supposed to fall. They are too many for the visible space, but the space is deceptive; every goat has its own shelf, its own corner, its own preferred plank. They clatter when the hall’s rhythm changes, and they settle into dry spots when the damp creeps too far. Their bodies are soft engines. They warm the room from below. They smell of hay and milk and animal sleep. Their eyes are flat and knowing. They do not fear the lizards much, but they distrust any sudden shift in the water sound. An old doe may stand for long minutes staring at the Grinding Pot, as if she has seen the room’s next weather before the humans have.

The quail and pigeons are not birds of open sky. They are room-birds. They know the distance from perch to perch, the warmth of the wall behind them, the best shelf for sleep and the best shelf for waiting. The quail make the earliest dry noise, the pigeons a softer, more patient cooing. They roost high because they are assigned height, and they know their level. If a pigeon is put too low near the basins, it becomes uncertain, its feet too wet, its feathers taking on the smell of crabs and stale water. If a water-creature is put too high, the rule breaks and the room feels wrong. The birds are not decoration; they are part of the sound schedule. The quail shift and clatter when someone is moving overhead. The pigeons murmur when the braziers are lit. Their calls are not beautiful, but they are informative. They are the morning chorus and the warning bell.

The bats are the most mysterious of the high dwellers, though they ask for very little attention. They hang in niches carved into the ceiling vaults, folded into themselves like dark leaves. They are not pets. They are not feared. They are kept because of the insects, and also because of something less easily stated: their flight. When they drop down and pass near the shafts of light, the room seems to widen for a moment. The quail, oddly, are calmed when the bats move at their allowed hours. Perhaps the birds take it as a sign that the hall’s interior weather is stable. Perhaps the people learned this first and invented the explanation afterward. Either way, no one shooes them at noon. If the bats are restless, the people adjust the fire, open a hood, or wait to see where the condensation will form. The bats are weather-keepers as much as insect-eaters.

The rats and mice are the hall’s smallest mechanics. They live in stacked pottery lofts above the door, reached by rope-and-wood ladders, and they are not pests because the people have decided they are not pests. They are part of the labor system. They knock dry seed pods loose, drag strings of husks back to nests, test where the grain has become too wet, and make a faint drumming sound in the clay tubes when they are working. Their claws are quick and irregular, like someone tapping a table with a broken stick. They drink at the Grinding Pot during their own hour. They know the people’s moods and avoid certain lofts when a brazier is too hot. Their bodies in the tubes are a kind of taboo, not because death is sacred in some obvious way, but because the blockage brings water, and water is useful, and one should not waste useful blockages by carelessness. Or perhaps that is how the rule was made. The rats are neither loved nor despised. They are employed.

The lizards are the most self-contained residents. They sit on warm stone near the braziers and match the color of the room so closely that they seem to have been painted from the walls themselves. They do not make much noise except when something disturbs them: a quick tick of toes, a small dry hiss, a sudden movement so precise it feels mechanical. They eat beetles and young rats if the lofts are disturbed, and they know where the heat gathers. If the lizards are unusually restless, the air has changed. If they all shift to the far side of the brazier, someone will soon scrape moss from a tile or pour a new basin. They are not sentimental creatures. They are living weather-glass.

The snails are the mapmakers. They climb the damp mortar where condensation pools and leave silver lines across tiles, along basin lips, up the sides of pots. At night they gather near the Grinding Pot for starch and damp. They are fed skimmed goat milk as a rare treat, whether that makes them taste sweeter or whether the old people simply invented a kind story to encourage careful milking. Either way, the snails move slowly and deliberately, and the room’s routes are visible because of them. When they stop climbing the rim of the Grinding Pot, the air has changed and the people know it. When they begin to cross a tile too freely, a leak has found a new path. Their trails are not waste; they are messages. The people can look at a silver thread on the floor and tell whether water has been coming too often from the south wall or whether a basin has been left half-full for too many days.

The crabs are the water’s cleaners and its alarm system. They live in the low basins, in shallow ceramic ponds, in the dark corners where standing water reflects light. They click their claws against fired clay. They scuttle sideways when a shadow passes, not with panic exactly, but with the practiced suspicion of small creatures who know that water can be both shelter and trap. They eat what floats into the basins. They keep the water from becoming too dead. And if they stop moving, something is wrong with the water. A still basin is more dangerous than a loud one. The people know this without needing to be told. If a crab is quiet for too long, they check the channels, sniff the pots, look at the Grinding Pot for cloudiness. The crabs are not celebrated, but they are watched. They are the hall’s water-pulse.

The beetles are everywhere and nowhere, moving under the lips of jars, turning pot bottoms into crawling maps. They are fat in the damp places, iridescent in the light shafts, dry and black in the warmer corners. They whisper when they cross the tiles. They are eaten by lizards, avoided by goats, tolerated by birds, and sometimes, if too many appear, their presence is considered a sign that a mat or a grain tray is beginning to rot. They live in the interstices. They make the undersides of things busy. They are part of the small machinery that keeps the made surfaces from becoming too still.

The insects around the braziers are another kind of presence: gnats, midges, flies, and other tiny creatures attracted to warmth and the smell of oil. They flicker in the light shafts. The bats eat them. The people curse them and do not curse them enough to remove them, because removing them would mean disturbing the bats, and disturbing the bats would disturb the quail, and the whole chain is too useful to break for a few bites.

The plants are few but persistent. They live only where they have made themselves necessary or beautiful in a controlled way. Moss creeps along lower walls and gathers in dark cracks. It is scraped away when it makes the threshold slippery, left alone when it hides a leak that no one has bothered to fix, and allowed to spread when it keeps one tile too damp for something else to take. Lichens pale the stone near the shafts. Ferns curl in troughs where water seeps too often. Sprouted grain sits in shallow trays, bright green and wetted each day, a small indoor field that must be watched before mold turns it white and useless. Vines climb from high pots so they can decorate without taking over. None of this is gardening in the way of open sunlight and rich soil. It is a negotiation. The plants are residents because they earn their place. If they begin to crowd, they are reduced. If they hide a useful crack, they are left alone. If they make the room more likely to hold water, they become part of the system.

The most numerous inhabitant may be water itself, or the room’s relationship to water. The Drip-Hall does not merely contain water. It behaves as though water were one of its people. Water has habits. It follows old stains. It darkens the same tiles before others. It makes small rivers across the floor without needing a source that can be seen. It gathers in basins and vanishes into hidden holes. It becomes mist, film, condensation, trickling, indoor rain.
