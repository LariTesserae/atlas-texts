What lives in the Ninefold Hollow is not a population in the way a town has a population. There are no citizens, no families, no shepherds, no masons, no children in the streets. The Hollow is occupied instead by a slow mineral ecology so intimate with its architecture that the boundary between building and body dissolves. The city is alive; the animals are its symptoms, its grazing, its predation, its remains; and the empty spaces between the streets behave as though they, too, are residents.

The deepest inhabitants are the sessile colonies that make the town. They are not creatures with faces, nor organisms that seem to “think” in any recognizable way. They are sheets of pale, lichen-like filaments embedded in calcium-silicate, spreading through the crust of the Hollow. Some are the size of a hand. Others are broader. Many are barely visible, a faint chalkiness in the black glass or the white dust. They live as a law of growth: where one colony meets another, they secrete a dry cement that stiffens into vertical forms; where they fail to meet, open lanes remain; where their secretions spread over older structures, walls thicken, arches close, rooms narrow, streets widen. In this sense, the builders are not absent from the city. The builders are the city. The columns are their bodies. The streets are the gaps between their bodies. The plazas are the places where several colonies have withdrawn from one another and left a common emptiness.

To call them “colonies” is almost too animal a word. They do not walk, hunt, flee, or gather. They grow, crust, accrete, and incorporate. A hand placed on a wall might feel only stone, but the wall is a record of their slow meeting, a fossilized social pattern of contact and distance. Their life is not a life that can be startled by sound or light. It responds to temperature, to mineral flow, to the faint pulse of the phosphorescent veins beneath the ground. In the cold deepening hours, the walls contract; channels reopen; dry fogs of microscopic salt rise from the streets and move like a tide through the lattice of the town. In warmer periods, those passages close again. The colonies do not decide to do this. They simply continue being the condition by which the Hollow behaves like a city.

Above and within this living mineral framework, the rosettes form the Hollow’s quietest gardens. They are not plants in any ordinary sense, and not fully mineral either. They cling to the bases of columns, spread over black glass, dust the pale courts, and turn whole stones into chalky crusts that flake at the touch. Their bodies are flat, waxy, scalelike, built not to rise into branches or leaves but to lie along architecture as though the city were ground and the stone were sun. They catch the blue-green glow running through the crystal walls and convert it into faint heat, and that heat drives their growth. When the dry sublimation fog recedes, they contract into tight balls, almost indistinguishable from pale grains of salt. When it returns, they open like slow hands. Up close, one can see that the apparent dust of a plaza floor is moving, expanding, drawing itself back again. They are beautiful in the way frost is beautiful: precise, temporary, accidental, and utterly indifferent to being admired.

The needlelings are the Hollow’s most elusive animal residents. They are thin, six-legged grazers with translucent shells and no visible eyes. They move along the undersides of arches, into the hollow channels within the walls, and through the negative spaces the mineral colonies have left behind. Where they feed, they strip the pale mineral crusts and leave behind a faint trail of dust that glows only for a moment before vanishing. They do not chirp, hiss, stamp, or speak. Their silence is so complete that one might stand in a plaza and never know that they are there, moving through the upper parts of the colonnade or traveling through the veins inside the walls. They do not use the city as a shelter. They use it as a landscape. The roads of the needlelings are not the streets below; they are the hidden passages of the stone, the channels of the city’s own body.

The glass-cloaks are the rarest and most unsettling of the fauna. They are predators no larger than a fox, with bodies like folded shards. For long periods they are motionless, draped across low platforms or wedged into doorways, their edges catching the dim light so subtly that they seem like architectural accident: broken crystal, cooled glass, a wall fragment fallen into shadow. They do not hunt by sight. The Hollow has no need for that. They hunt by vibration. A needleling touching the wrong arch sends a pulse through the mineral lattice. That pulse travels through veins and walls, through the city’s living network, and a glass-cloak feels it. Then it unfolds—not with the urgency of a beast pouncing, but with the terrible patience of something that has waited for the geometry of the city to deliver its prey into place. Its movement is silent. Its stillness is camouflage. When it stops again, it becomes building.

There is no chorus, no herd, no swarm, no bright bird, no insect noise, no predator’s call. The life of the Ninefold Hollow is scattered, severe, and almost invisible. But it is not dead. The ecology is complete in miniature: mineral colonies build the city; the city provides structure; rosettes alter the surface; needlelings graze those surfaces; glass-cloaks harvest the needlelings; the dead become substrate for new crystal growth. A predator’s rib may become a bridge. A grazer’s shell may become a column’s core. Dust from a shattered cloak may seed a fresh vein of glowing mineral. The Hollow does not clean up after its inhabitants. It incorporates them. Death here is not disappearance. Death is urbanization.

Even absence has a kind of residence. The large, pale courts between the colonnades are not empty failures of construction. They are shaped by the flow of dry fog, by the spacing of mineral growth, by the rhythm of contraction and expansion. The gaps between buildings are as significant as the buildings themselves. The Hollow behaves as though emptiness were a material: something that can be carved, filled, veiled, brightened, widened, narrowed, closed, or reopened. A doorway may remain a doorway for a time and then fill with crystal; a wall may thin into a window; a plaza may become half-buried in fresh salt; an arch may form where none existed by morning. The city is not static enough to be a ruin. It is not active enough to be alive in the way an animal is alive. It is a pattern of becoming, and that pattern is populated by the things that participate in it.

If a person walked there, they would not meet an inhabitant so much as realize that the place is inhabited around them, through them, and beneath them. They might hear a dry click from inside a column and know only that the structure is adjusting itself. They might see dust rearrange into branching lines and mistake it for wind, though the wind is too sluggish for such work. They might mark a wall and return to find the mark gone, not healed, not painted over, but simply absorbed into the continuing growth of the crust. They might step over what appears to be a fallen pane of glass and realize, too late or not at all, that it has felt the vibration of their boot.

What lives in the Ninefold Hollow, then, is a town made of biology and mineral law fused so thoroughly that one cannot say where organism ends and architecture begins. The residents are the pale colonies, the rosettes, the needlelings, the glass-cloaks, the glowing dust of their remains, and the negative forms of their streets. None of them are people. None of them are gods, spirits, ghosts, or abandoned makers. None of them are building the city for any future use. The city does not belong to them. They belong to it. They live as its surfaces, its hidden passages, its predation, its weather, its slow breath. The Hollow’s only true inhabitants are process itself and its products: the living crystal, the dry fog, the blind grazers, the waiting shards, and the emptiness shaped into streets.
