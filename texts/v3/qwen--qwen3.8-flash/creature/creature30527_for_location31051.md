This emptiness is not barren in a harsh sense; it is a settled emptiness, and its inhabitants are the reason for the settling.

At first, if you came down the slope of pale grit and stood in the center of the hollow, you might think it had no life at all. There are no burrows open at eye level, no nests, no tracks crossing the floor, no leaf or twig to mark a season. Nothing announces itself with motion or voice. But life in this place is not the life of sudden things. It is the life of thresholds: the damp becoming less damp, the dust becoming a skin, the light becoming warmth, the warmth becoming breath. The hollow is occupied by processes that have become so patient they resemble patience itself.

The most obvious resident, once you know how to look, is a living film.

It covers the lower basins and the backs of the salt shelves, thin as the breath on the inside of a glass. In the dry part of the cycle it is nearly invisible, only a faint mineral roughness, a pale fuzz of salt crystals with no clear boundary. When the morning condensation appears, it darkens just enough to be noticed. Then it takes on a color that is not quite green, not quite silver, something like the shadow of a wet shell: blue-gray, pearl-white, sometimes threaded with a warmth like diluted gold. It is a colony of microscopic beings, cells and filaments and crystal-bound organisms that live at the surface of the water without needing a pool. They drink light through the translucent ceiling and feed on the slow exhalations of the stone. They turn the mineral damp into a kind of edible silence.

If you kneel and put your face close to a patch of this film, you will see it moving without moving. It swells in places where moisture gathers. It contracts where the floor breathes too quickly into the dry air. It forms tiny terraces and rills that look, at first, like geology, but then show a consistency that is more organic: repeated patterns, branching lines, little basins that collect condensation as if they were made to collect it. The film is not a crust laid on the floor. It is a surface that has learned to live as a surface.

When the clouds pass overhead and the hollow dims, the film often brightens. This is not because it contains light, but because it exposes moisture, and moisture refracts what little remains. At those moments, the whole lower part of the hollow can appear as if it has been polished. A small creature crossing it may become visible, not by movement, but by the faint displacement of the film: a tiny bright ripple, like the trace of a needle dragged through milk.

Within this film live the smallest animals of the hollow.

They are the things that would be invisible to a human eye unless magnified: bottle-shaped animals no bigger than grains of dust, spinning cilia around their mouths; flat, segmented creatures that move with the careful slowness of things that have no need to hurry; nematodes like threads of pale water; tardigrades that enter and leave life as if it were a dry season that could return at any moment. These are not permanent inhabitants in the way a human might think of permanence. Many of them lie dormant for months or years, folded into salt dust, reduced to almost nothing, waiting. Then a wetness returns, or a cup of condensation appears in a depression that was dry the day before, and within hours the tiny bodies unfold and begin to move as though they had been sleeping rather than absent.

When the pools are large enough to be called pools, even if they are only a finger’s depth, they become worlds. A puddle in a natural cup can contain a whole economy: microscopic plants and animals, bacteria, mineral crystals, eggs, larvae, waste, and light. If you look into such a pool with good magnification, you may see a faint agitation in the water that is not current, because there is no current. It is a population feeding. Small translucent crustaceans turn in circles around the edge. Rotifers open and close at the boundary between water and salt. Something like a white flake drifts past, and you realize it has legs, then realize it does not, then realize the water itself has made you misjudge its density.

These waters do not flow, but they are not dead because they do not flow. They wait. They gather, evaporate, leave behind a ring of salt, become dry, then gather again. The creatures living in them have adapted not to the presence of water so much as to the memory of water. Their eggs hatch when the damp reaches a particular threshold. Their young mature only if the pool lasts long enough. Their adults may lay eggs in the salt itself, embedding them in microscopic crystals so that the next rainfall, next condensation cycle, next breath of the hollow, can wake them. If the water vanishes too soon, most die. If it returns too late, many remain sealed in dust until the next chance. Life here is not a line. It is a pulse.

As the film extends upward onto the cooler walls, another group arrives: the small crawling animals.

They are mostly too small to see clearly at first. Their bodies are pale because there is nothing in this place that requires dark color. There is no deep shadow in which to hide from predators by night, no dense vegetation in which to blend by day. There is only light, stone, salt, and moisture. So the animals that live here are often nearly transparent, or white, or the color of old lenses. Some have the shine of wet crystal on their backs. Others look like fragments of the ceiling that have fallen and learned to walk.

The springtails are the most common. They do not jump the way their relatives above ground do, or if they jump, it is only in a short, startled way, as though motion itself is costly. They graze across the living film, using tiny mouthparts to scrape up the microscopic growth. They travel over the ledges where moisture is trapped in shallow hollows. Their bodies are so light that they seem to stand on top of the damp rather than sink into it. When the floor is dry, they find cracks and turn themselves over like seeds, letting the salt dust cover them. When it is wet, they emerge and feed.

Mites live among them. Some are round and pale, like beads of dew that move. Others are longer, legged, quick. They ride on the backs of larger creatures not out of friendship, but because movement is a resource. A mite that clings to a passing crustacean may cross an entire basin in a few seconds. A mite that cannot move as far waits for the damp to come to it.

There are also the small beetles, or beetle-like things, whose shells have become almost as clear as selenite plates. They do not shine. They seem to swallow light slightly, so that their bodies are not dark but faintly cloudy. They are the color of breath on glass. They move slowly across the lower slopes and along the salt shelves, feeding on decay, on microscopic growth, on other small animals that have died. They are not beautiful in the way of animals people name. They are more like living debris that has learned to persist. When they die, their bodies often become centers for crystal growth. Salt and calcite form around them in delicate fans, and within a few years there may be a small white sculpture where an insect once lay: a beetle encased in mineral lace, its legs still posed as if it were about to turn.

Not all of the hollow’s creatures are so passive.

In the cracks behind the salt shelves, where the air moves slowly and moisture lingers longest, there are predatory things. Small white pseudoscorpions wait in the dark crevices of pale stone, their pincers held open, their bodies translucent enough that the beating of their hearts may be visible if one could look close enough. They hunt mites, springtails, larvae. They are patient in the way the hollow itself is patient. They do not chase far. They let the world pass near them and then close.

There may also be slender, threadlike creatures that resemble centipedes, though they are not as large as any human would notice at first. They move through the micro-terrains of the floor with a ripple along their many pale legs. They are blind. They navigate by heat, moisture, and vibration. They can find a patch of living film before any human observer could see it. They may hunt the tiny crustaceans that have strayed from the edge of a puddle into the dry salt, or they may merely taste the damp trails left by other animals, reading the hollow’s movements like a text.

These predators are not fierce in any dramatic sense. There is no growl, no leap, no struggle. Predation here is more like a slow subtraction. A tiny animal touches the wrong thread of a predator’s body. The next moment it is gone. The predator withdraws its head and returns to stillness. The floor appears unchanged.

The larger creatures of the hollow are rarer, and perhaps most visitors would never see them even if they stayed for hours. But if the damp returns often enough, and if the film remains unbroken, there is something else: a slow, broad-bodied animal that moves through the grit as if it were part of the floor.
