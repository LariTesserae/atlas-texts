If one asks who lives there, the answer begins with the wrong word.

There are no people in the basin, no crew walking the benches, no engineer at a switch, no pilgrim kneeling by a valve. There are no animals of the old kind, no bodies with eyes and appetites, no mothers, no young, no herds, no predators. Yet the place is not empty. It is inhabited by forms that have accepted the terms of its existence. The basin does not host life so much as it has produced a new kind of life: one without blood, without breath as breathing is commonly understood, without faces, but not without appetite, persistence, rhythm, or a kind of attention.

What lives there is what the water permits, what the sound allows, and what the worn machinery has, over centuries, made possible.

---

At first the inhabitants seem no more than staining.

The thin silver mosses are the basin’s skin, but not a passive one. Each thread is capillary, drawn from mineral-rich condensation, anchored by roots that drink not soil but the paste of black grit, flaked alloy, and slow-seeping salts. They grow where metal breathes, where the difference between cold and warm is steady enough to hold water in suspension. Under magnification—or under long looking—the moss is not simple at all. It is layered. One stratum holds moisture, another conducts it sideways into the nearest depression, a third sheds excess onto the plates below. The moss does not so much colonize as manage. It makes the ground livelier than it would be alone.

Where the moss is brightest, the pale lichens map the reservoir walls in irregular continents. They are slower, harder, more patient than the moss. They do not need warmth so much as time. Their bodies are films of mineral crust and biological discipline, spreading millimeter by millimeter across damp alloy. They feed on suspended grit, absorb the faint metallic taste of the air, and convert it into surfaces that record the flow of water. Some lichens have become so integrated with the made floors that they appear to be part of the architecture itself: a blue-grey patch along a reservoir rim, a whitish lace beneath a steam seam, a dark stain shaped too consistently to be accidental. They are not stains. They are residents.

The rust-fungus is stranger still.

Where moisture, heat, and exposed iron meet in a stable relationship, a white or ochre bloom forms, soft as powder and hungry as acid. It does not merely corrode; it processes. It eats alloy slowly and excretes a pale ceramic dust, and that dust becomes substrate for other growths. In its densest forms, the rust-fungus resembles a cushion of old skin, cracked and luminous in the low light. In its sparse forms, it appears as branching veins, a faint network beneath paint and grime. It grows into cracks, along pipe seams, beneath insulator bases, in the joints of pump housings. It does not move, but it advances. It is not a plant, not quite a fungus, not quite a mineral deposit, but something that has found the basin’s chemistry and learned to use it.

The reedlike things are the basin’s closest thing to grass, though they are not grass.

Their stems are blue-grey, hard as drawn metal, hollowed by the movement of water and vapor. They grow in shallow channels where suspended grit provides silica, and where vents disturb the air in regular intervals. Their leaves, if such flat structures can be called leaves, are thin as wire and sharp enough to cut a fingertip if one approached without knowing. Their roots do not seek soil; they seek joints, drains, sumps, and the porous edges of broken concrete. Some clusters sway in no visible breeze, but because a nearby pipe releases a pulse of steam. Others stand almost perfectly still, tuned to a frequency of sound too low to hear but felt in the teeth. When their hollow stems fill with condensation, they ring faintly, a small dark tone moving through the plain like a whispered name.

The mosses, lichens, fungal blooms, and reedlike colonies are the basin’s first visible tenants. They are its skin, its fur, its lichenous memory. They do not run or swim or hunt. They persist. They adjust. They make the surfaces more receptive to life.

---

But the water is the most restless mother.

In the shallow sheets that lie between rusted plates, there are creatures of surface tension. They have no bodies in the familiar sense. They are skins without interiors, membranes held together by wetness, grit, and the exact balance between stillness and disturbance. They appear first as rings: a faint ellipse in a puddle, a trembling line where mineral paste has gathered into an unstable boundary. Then they thicken. They acquire a rim. They begin to move.

These are the water-skins.

They are born when condensation collects over a pocket of air, when a drop falls through a grate, when a vibration passes beneath a pool and leaves a suspended film intact. They drift over the made ground, carrying black flecks, pale flakes, oil residues, and sometimes fragments of lichen spores. They have no eyes, no mouths, no limbs, but they respond to pressure. A footstep in the distance, a valve seating, the cough of steam, a drip from a pipe—each sends a ripple that can gather, divide, or drive them. Some are as wide as a hand, thin as breath, trembling on the surface of a shallow channel. Others collect into clusters that resemble wet flowers, their petals formed from mineral rings and black dust.

They feed by trapping suspended particles, pulling grit through their membranes and depositing it at their edges, where it becomes paste. They reproduce by splitting into smaller skins when a current crosses their surfaces. They die when a too-heavy raindrop punctures them, when a pump surge tears the water into motion, when the surface dries, or when a resonance breaks the film’s coherence. Their death is not violent. It is a withdrawal. The boundary fails, and what was briefly held becomes again scattered water and dust.

They are the basin’s shortest-lived inhabitants, and perhaps the most faithful reflection of its mood: transient, fragile, born from repetition, undone by a single change in pressure.

---

Deeper in the machinery, another kind of life tends the worn places.

In bearings that no longer hold true, in shafts that turn against corroded sockets, in the black gaps between pump housings and foundation plates, there is a living film that has learned to move by shear and heat. It is dark, sometimes bronze-black, sometimes a deep oil-brown with pale metallic glints. It clings to moving surfaces but does not obstruct them. It is thinner than paint, stronger than expected, and capable of migrating slowly along shafts, threads, and the rims of worn holes.

This is the oil-lace.

It is not lubricant alone. It is not residue alone. It is a colonial substance formed from old oils, ceramic dust, metal flakes, microbial film, and the heat of motion. Where a bearing has worn, oil-lace gathers. It forms lenses over the worst spots. It draws moisture into itself. It creates tiny barriers that redistribute load, not because it understands load, but because the chemistry and geometry of its growth favors it. Over long intervals, it has tamed surfaces that should have failed entirely. It has not repaired them so much as incorporated them.

Oil-lace moves. Not quickly. Not with direction in the way a creature would choose direction. It moves when a shaft turns, when heat expands it, when vibration breaks its surface into threads, when gravity pulls it toward a warmer joint. If split, each fragment can begin to reorganize, drawing dust and oil back into a skin. If a bearing heats too suddenly, it thins and recoils; if moisture enters, it swells and migrates. Its responses are so steady that one might mistake them for instinct, except that no nervous system exists to support instinct. It is habit made substance.

Around the edges of pump houses and valve stems, where metal has been worn to curved hollows, oil-lace can form what look like small organs. Dark nodules. Glossy pads. Thin discs. They pulse faintly when the machine breathes. Sometimes, if one watches a worn joint long enough, one sees them contract—not because anything is contracting, but because the surface tension and heat are shifting. They are not hearts. They are places where motion has learned to accumulate.

They make the machine more alive than the machine itself.

---

The pipes are not empty.

Even where no steam moves visibly, there are growths that have learned the interior of conduits as their habitat. In channels carrying water at low temperatures, pale filaments spread along the curved walls, soft as fungal threads but mineral-hard at their centers. They feed on suspended salts, trap fine particles, and grow in patterns that mirror the flow. They are called, if they are given a name at all, the drain-threads.

At first they appear as nothing more than streaks. Later they branch. Later they braid themselves around cracks and ridges left by corrosion, and then they are no longer merely adhering to the pipe; they are shaping it. They strengthen some parts by depositing ceramic dust; they erode others by acid from the water they hold. Over time, a section of pipe may become a living vessel, its interior surface no longer simple alloy but a composite of metal, mineral, and slow biological architecture.

Where drain-threads meet pressure changes, they move without seeming to move. They swell where water is pulled through. They flatten where flow reverses. Their tips, if tip is the right word, can be drawn toward cooler water by the chemistry of dissolved gases. They have no hunger that a person could recognize, but they seek the richest current. They have no nervous system, yet when one section dies, the colony redirects growth away from the failure as if it had learned.

In some places they have grown so thick that the pipe becomes a tube within a tube. The inner drain-structure has its own rhythm. It coughs in the surges. It hums when water moves fast. It falls silent when the cycle slows. It is not a parasite. It is more like an organ that grew by accident and stayed because it improved something no one remembers needing to improve.

---

The transformer pits and broken electrical seams have their own tenants.

There is no sun here to favor green growth, but there are weak arcs, corona glows, and the pale blue light of discharge.
