## The Citizens of the Green Street

There is no single answer to the question of who lives in the Drowned Terrace, because the place does not recognize the boundary we are accustomed to drawing between a resident and a house, between an animal and a garden, between a town and the water that has entered it. If you look only for faces, you will conclude that no one remains. If you look for bodies moving through lanes, for beings that feed, shelter, hunt, nest, repair, graze, climb, swim, hide, and wait, you will find that the settlement is crowded with inhabitants—almost too crowded for the eye to accept at once. The human town is gone, but it has been replaced by a dense, patient citizenship, and its members occupy the streets not as visitors, but as the true successors of those who once cut stone and wove reed and packed clay.

The first of these citizens is not a creature at all, but the water itself. It is too often treated as a medium, something in which other lives happen, as if it were merely empty. Here, it is an actor. It enters from the melt channel at one edge and leaves by another, perhaps through a fracture so narrow that the water passes through it without appearing to pass through anything at all. It breathes against the moss curtains. It presses the surface into a trembling ceiling. It lifts fish scales into silver and lowers leaves into shadow. It carries silt into the courtyards, sorts it by current, and then takes it away again. It cools the stones and warms the underside of the winter air into a thin film of mist. It is not alive, but it behaves as if it were a resident with an ancient claim to the place, and the other inhabitants obey it as a civic law.

On the surface, there is a population adapted to living at the border between air and water. Diving spiders cross the meniscus as if the surface were a field, their legs holding tension with tiny careful pressure. When they plunge, they carry with them a bubble of air caught in the fine hairs of their bodies, a bright little helmet, a borrowed piece of sky. They swim downward through the reed stems and into the submerged town, still breathing their stored air, hunting among the green shadows of the streets. Some of them have made further homes than mere visits: beneath the surface, at the edge of a mossy wall or inside the shelter of a collapsed lintel, they construct silken bells—small inverted rooms in which they trap air, a chapel of breath suspended in water. They lay their eggs inside these air-gathered nurseries. They repair the bells when the current loosens them. They come and go between the surface and the street, carrying life back and forth between two worlds as if the terrace were a divided country with open borders.

Near them, on the underside of the surface itself, there are beetles, backswimmers, and the smaller, more angular forms of water boatmen. These are citizens of the ceiling. They hang from the skin of the water and swim by rowing with flattened legs. Where light passes through them, they become brief dark commas in the bright moving ceiling. At night, after the wind has stilled, they leave the shallower courtyards and drift toward the middle of the terrace, where the water is deeper and the old foundations become long corridors of shadow. Some lay their eggs inside plant stems, piercing the living tissue to insert them, so that even a piece of pondweed may contain the hidden life of an insect for days or weeks before it breaks into a new body.

Overhead, birds use the terrace as if it were a mirror. Dippers work the shallow margins, where water passes over gravel and roots, and they can do what most birds cannot: sink into the submerged street and walk along the tops of old stone walls. They do not simply dive; they enter the architecture. A dipper may land on a moss-covered roof fragment, look downward through the water, and then let go, dropping through green light to stand on a wall that once supported a doorway. It moves along the submerged masonry as if reading it, hunting insects and small fish in the crevices of human work. When it surfaces, it shakes off the basin in a brief white spray and returns to the reeds as if it has only stepped out of one room into another. Warblers, lighter and less armored, work the upper margins: the tops of reeds, the tangled grasses at the shore, the places where the water’s edge softens into vegetation. Their songs fall into the basin and are received by the water as if by a cupped hand. The air boundary does not silence the bird; it doubles it. A call overhead becomes a pale ripple in the liquid below.

The most obvious citizens, however, are not those that pass quickly across the surface, but the plants, who have taken up the entire town and made it theirs. In any human city, we imagine walls as belonging to the builders. In the Drowned Terrace, the walls belong to moss, algae, pondweed, and root.

There is a green class of inhabitants that does not behave like individual creatures but like a distributed body. Filamentous algae coat every surface with a soft film that moves like slow thought in the current. Where they cover stone, the stone is no longer bare. Where they cover timber, the timber becomes a shelf for life. The finest algae are barely more than a shimmer: gray-green hair, a breath of color, a dusting that turns a dark foundation line into a meadow when light strikes it. Beneath that shimmer there is a whole microscopic population—diatoms and other organisms that live on the algal film and on each other, grazing and being grazed, building tiny glass shapes, dying, becoming food again. A single hearthstone, once set by hands to hold fire, is now a landscape under the fingernail. It is a pasture, a city, a reef, and a nursery.

Mosses are the more dignified citizens. They grow in curtains along the edges of roof stones and door lintels, where the current is gentle and the surface remains stable. Their leaves are so small that they seem to be made of a single thought: soft, pointed, green with a blue undertone, each one a tiny body waiting for water. When submerged, moss can photosynthesize slowly, holding light within its thin cells while the water moves through it. It does not merely decorate the town; it repairs the relationship between stone and life. It binds dust and silt. It holds invertebrate eggs. It shelters shrimp-like scuds and larval insects. When a wall has become a curtain of moss, it is no longer dead. It is hospitable. It has become a citizen with a trade: filtration, shelter, softening, retention.

Pondweed rises from the floor in long, swaying strands, forming submerged forests in the courtyards. These plants occupy open spaces the way people once did. They need light, water, and a place to anchor. The old courtyards provide all three: a flat floor, a shallow depth, and protection from the worst of the wind. In the shallower margins, broad-leaved plants spread themselves across the surface near the shore, making little green floors of their own. The undersides of these leaves are dense with life: snail eggs, leeches, caddis cases, hydra, tiny crustaceans. To flip one over with the current is to see an entire underside world clinging there like a second forest.

The rushes and reeds are the architects. Where walls once stood open, rhizomes have entered and finished what the builders began. A reed does not need to know that a wall is a wall; it only knows a channel of weakness, a pocket of silt, a crevice where light and water meet. It sends a stem upward and a root sideways. The human structure becomes a scaffold, and the plant makes a street of itself. In autumn and spring, the reed tops are full of small insects, while below, their roots hold water-living communities against the current. A reed bed is a wall, a forest, and a nursery all at once.

But the true richness of the terrace lies beneath the plants, in the animals that have inherited the geometry of the town.

The fish are the most obvious inhabitants, and perhaps the most fitting. A drowned town is a city of channels, and fish are the beings for whom channels are the whole world. Small minnows are the citizens of motion. They move in clouds through the courtyards and along the lane, their sides catching the shifting light and scattering it into brief silver flickers. They are not merely swimming; they are using the old layout. A street offers them a cleaner current, a straighter path, a corridor with fewer obstacles than the tangled courtyards. A school of minnows is traffic: the place where a former lane once carried footsteps now carries hundreds of small bodies, all turning together according to pressure and movement.

Paler fish dart through doorways. Their bodies have adapted to the light and shadow of the submerged streets, taking on the colors of gravel, silt, and moss. A doorway is no longer a place where a person crossed from yard to house; it is now a passage between two currents, a place of ambush and shelter. A small fish will pass through without hesitation, because for fish the doorway has always meant opening, and an opening means water. It does not know that once it had a human threshold. To it, the opening is simply the way the street narrows.

Other fish hover over the silt. Darker, heavier, with fins working quickly against the current, they are citizens of the floor. They read the bottom as a text: the texture of gravel, the position of a fallen stone, the smell of a sheltered crevice. Where a foundation has collapsed, the scattered stones become a maze, and a fish can hold its body still beside one of them as if it were standing in the doorway of its house. These are the beings that know the town by touch and pressure. Their lateral lines are organs of architecture; they feel the shape of walls, the speed of channels, the displacement made by another body moving near them. The town has become legible to them not visually but hydrodynamically.

Along the bottoms of old stone-lined channels, sculpins lie on the gravel like living stones. They do not need to rush. Their flattened heads, broad pectoral fins, and patient bodies are made for a life close to the bottom. They rest in the hollows where the water moves around a fallen beam, waiting for small invertebrates to come within reach. They are the guardians of the gutters, the former drains of human streets now transformed into hunting lanes. In the deeper corners, larger fish patrol with muscular slowness. Trout or char—mountain-fish forms shaped by cold water and clear light—wait where the channel bends and the current quickens. They do not waste motion. They stand in moving water with the patience of old stone, then strike with precision so sudden that it seems as though a piece of shadow has broken and leapt. A strike rings across the whole square of water. For a moment, the terrace becomes a drum.

The crustaceans and mollusks are another kind of urban life. Freshwater snails graze the moss-covered walls, leaving behind slow, clean paths in the green. They are gardeners of a kind: not cultivating, but trimming the film, opening surfaces for other life, carrying minerals and microorganisms with them wherever they go. Their shells are small, smooth, spiraled houses, the oldest architecture among them. Some snails lay translucent egg masses on leaves or stones, little packets of life suspended in the current.

Crayfish are the masons, the night scavengers, the cleaners. They live in the crevices of the human foundations, where the stones have left gaps wide enough for their bodies but narrow enough for their claws to grip. They pull detritus into their burrows. They drag snails into hiding. They hold leaves in their front legs and tear at them, breaking down the plant matter into smaller pieces that become habitat and food. They are not peaceful. Their claws click against stone and timber, a sound faint but audible when the town is quiet, like a small gate opening somewhere below the street. A hollow log that once formed part of a wall becomes a resonant chamber for their movements. Their presence fills the old structures with a dry, granular life beneath the water.

Leeches stretch between stalks of pondweed. They are not feared by the terrace; they are part of its economy. They attach to passing fish, to snails, to other creatures, drinking small portions of blood or body fluids, then releasing and drifting again. Their bodies are dark, ribbed, patient. They live on the edge of the plant forests, using the current as a highway. They are both predators and scavengers, and their role in the ecology is less dramatic than it appears in human myth, but real nonetheless.

The insects are perhaps the most numerous citizens, and their work is continuous. Caddis larvae carry their cases along the stones—little mobile houses made of sand grains, leaf bits, shell fragments, and spun silk. They move through the streets like slow, armored hermits, grazing on detritus and microalgae. Their cases are a kind of portable architecture, and when they mature, they build pupal chambers inside stones or plant stems. A single wall can contain dozens of their lives, all of them quiet, all of them dependent on the old cracks.

Dragonfly nymphs crouch in the shadows of reed roots. They are ambush hunters, their bodies adapted to stillness. Their lower jaws fold beneath their heads, hidden like a trapdoor, ready to spring forward with brutal speed when prey drifts too close. They hunt small fish, tadpoles, crustaceans, larvae. They live submerged for years, growing slowly through many molts, and then, at last, they climb out of the water into the air. A dragonfly resting on a submerged wall’s upper edge is not a newcomer; it is a creature that spent most of its life inside the town. Its wings dry in the mountain wind, and then it flies above the same streets it once crawled through as a larva.

Water beetles scuttle along the ceiling of old windows now filled with root. They carry air beneath their wing cases and emerge at the surface to replenish it. They swim with a directness and speed that makes them seem like tiny mechanical travelers moving through the city’s forgotten openings. Their larvae hunt among the moss curtains, their bodies elongated and predatory, living in the submerged rooms of the town. At night, when larger fish are less active, the beetles may take the more open streets.

Even the smaller life forms have neighborhoods. Freshwater shrimps—scuds, amphipods, tiny bent forms—move in great numbers over the surfaces of moss and silt. They eat decaying leaves, algae, and each other. They are the food that keeps the larger citizens alive. Isopods, related to woodlice but adapted to water, roll and scurry among the lower stones. In the soft rot of peat and timber, there are mites, springtails, larvae, fungi, bacteria, and a whole network of organisms whose bodies are too small to be seen by the naked eye, but whose labor is visible in every darkened beam and broken lintel. Nematodes thread through the sediment. Rotifers open and close like tiny machines, filtering the water. Tardigrades crawl among moss and algal films, surviving desiccation when the terrace’s edges are exposed, becoming active again when water returns. Hydra, minute but elegant, extend tentacles from reed stems and capture passing creatures smaller than themselves. Flatworms glide over stones, their eyes little more than dim spots in a translucent body. These beings live in the slime of the world, and they are essential citizens. Without them, the town would rot in an instant.

The timber of the old houses has not disappeared into nothing. It has become another kind of city. Beams that once supported roofs are now soft, brown, furred with biofilm and moss. Their interiors are full of channels carved by larvae and bacteria. Fungal networks and bacterial mats digest the lignin and cellulose slowly, in the cold dark. Wood that was cut by stone and bone is now being unmade by living mouths far older and more patient than any tool. The timber is a nursery for caddis, a shelter for sculpins, a surface for snail eggs, a sponge for algae, a bridge for beetles. It is not merely rotting. It is hosting. It is becoming a living architecture.

The clay walls have a different life. Where clay remains, it forms hard, pitted surfaces that collect mineral crusts and tiny rootlets. Some invertebrates anchor themselves there. Others come to eat the microscopic growth that forms on the clay when light reaches it. The old wattle—woven reed frames once packed with clay—has become a trellis. Where reed has rotted away, clay remains as a kind of fossil pattern. Where reed persists, it grows into a submerged lattice, and small fish dart through the spaces of the weave. The builders made a wall. The water made a frame. The insects and roots made it into a reef.

Hearths have become the most tenderly inhabited of all places. Fire was once the center of the home; now moss and small life gather where heat was. A hearthstone, still sitting where a family placed it, is a flat green garden. Snails graze its edges. Shrimp hide beneath its shadow. A tiny fish may come to rest there, as if remembering some warmth no longer present. The cold water does not punish these stones. It softens them. It rounds them. It makes them safe.

Doorways are the most uncanny places because they remain so human and so useless. No door hangs. No hand opens them. They are holes in walls, and now they are swimming places for fish and hiding places for crayfish. A pale fish passes through one as casually as if it had been built for its body. A minnow school might pour through a house entrance and spread out through the rooms, turning an interior into a public hall. At night, the open doors are more active. Fish rest along the thresholds. Crayfish come out from the foundations to hunt. Insect larvae hide in the corners of lintels. The doorway no longer separates privacy from street; it separates two kinds of shelter.

The streets themselves are still legible, and the inhabitants have not misunderstood them. They have simply reinterpreted them. The old lane, with its gravel and root, is a highway. The courtyards are feeding grounds. The foundations are walls of protection against current. The fallen roofs are bridges, perches, and spawning surfaces. The builders imagined rooms, but they made niches. They imagined homes, but they made habitats. They imagined permanence, but they created patterns that another order could learn to use.

There is no human king among these citizens, no single being that holds the terrace. The authority is distributed: light governs the plants, current governs the fish, stone and root govern the invertebrates, wind governs the surface, cold governs time. Yet this distribution does not feel chaotic. It feels settled. Each organism knows its place in the geometry because it has spent generations learning it. The fish know which channels carry food. The snails know which surfaces are edible. The spiders know which reed stems are safe for their air bells. The crayfish know which cracks are defensible. The moss knows which stones remain stable through winter. No one is wandering by accident. The town has become a set of instructions for life.
