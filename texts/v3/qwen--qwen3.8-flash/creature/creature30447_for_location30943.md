There are no creatures to notice at first, and that is the beginning of the answer. Nothing walks the pale floor, nothing digs a burrow against the heat, nothing flies from one dry blossom to another. The basin does not offer shelter in the way animals understand it. It offers pores, seam-edges, thin moisture, and the slow patience of mineral skins. Whoever lives there therefore does not live there as a visitor. Most of them live there as the place itself.

The smallest and most abundant inhabitants are the seam-folk: films of cyanobacteria, microscopic algae, calcium-precipitating microbes, and salt-tolerant bacteria that occupy the narrow junctions between plates. They are too thin to call plants and too widespread to call organisms in any individual sense. They are more like living varnish. In damp seams they appear as a faint greenish-black sheen, almost like old ink drawn between pages. On exposed plate edges they become chalky rime, where their dead bodies and secretions become indistinguishable from the mineral skin of the land. They do not eat in any obvious way. They catch light, catch dust, catch moisture, and excrete cement. Wherever water beads for a few hours, they spread across it. Wherever the bead vanishes, they leave behind a thin crust that will be picked up by wind, salt, or root.

These microbial skins are not merely present on the ground. They are one reason the ground exists as ground. The basin’s vast surface is not bare rock; it is an accretion of living residues, sorted and repeated until it resembles a tessellation. Every pale plate has been helped into being by such films. A shallow pool begins as moisture, then algal mat, then carbonate precipitate, then a new patch of surface. The pool closes, but the life that made it persists as mineral structure. In that sense, the basin is full of ancestors: not bones, not bodies, but skins turned into stone.

Beneath the plates, the fungal inhabitants work at a different tempo. They are not mushrooms in any forest sense; their fruiting bodies, when they rise above the surface, are solitary mineral shelves, pale as bone, with undersides faintly dusted in spores. They stand alone for kilometers, like broken table-stones left by no hand. They do not need an animal to scatter them. Wind lifts their spores into the long, lazy currents that rise off the heated plates. Sometimes rain splash carries them into fresh crevices. A spore lands, swells in a capillary seam, threads into dust, and begins to bind.

Fungal threads are the true architecture of the hidden layer beneath the surface. They cross from root mold to pore, from plate seam to plate seam, carrying tiny quantities of moisture and secreted acids that loosen and reconsolidate mineral grains. Where a root once grew, fungi may now occupy the channel, drying into a dark lace, rehydrating into a pale sponge. They create the basin’s secret dampness. They are the reason the land can appear dry and yet be full of concealed wetness. Where the floor has cracked or lifted into a trapdoor, exposing fibrous webs to the air, it is usually fungi that begin to seal the wound. They bind wind-borne sand, dead phytoliths, calcium flour, and microbial slime into a rough new patch, no longer matching the old plate perfectly, but continuing the pattern.

The most visible residents are the plants, though “visible” is generous. They do not crowd the basin. They mark it. The boot-collared reeds are the most characteristic: pale, ribbed, dry-stiff stems with thick sheathing bases that look as though the plant has grown into its own mineral collar. They are not elegant in the way of river reeds. They are hard-edged, sparse, and strangely monumental when standing alone. A single reed may rise from a shallow pan, its base sealed into the plate by calcium, its leaves casting thin parallel shadows across the ground. Others grow in loose rings around temporary water. These rings are not accidental gardens. They are the plant’s response to moisture trapped by the made surface. The reeds have roots that spread sideways under the plates, reaching hidden damp without appearing to crowd one another. From above, they look almost deliberate, as though placed by an unseen hand. From beneath, they are tangled, interlocked, and inseparable from the substrate they help construct.

Their reproduction is equally quiet. There are no bright petals calling to insects, no fruit built for consumption. The flowers are small, pale, easily missed, more like joints than blossoms. Pollen drifts on wind currents generated by the thermal differences between bright plates and dark seams. Sometimes rain moves gametes along shallow runnels. The plants are tolerant of self-fertilization, but they also benefit from the basin’s weather: a gust can carry pollen across kilometers, and a storm can push it into the damp pores where germination begins. Seeds are tiny, dust-like, coated in silica or calcium-rich dust, designed not to float gracefully but to land and wait. Many remain dormant for long periods, surviving in hollows until a single rain episode softens the dust around them and triggers the first root’s mineral-tasting search.

The succulents are another kind of resident, more secretive than the reeds. They occupy shallow dips and sheltered seams where moisture lingers a little longer. Their leaves are waxy, pale blue-green, yellowish-grey, or almost white, often dusted with a powdery mineral bloom that reflects sunlight. They are not lush. They are not full of easy water. They are careful reservoirs. Their roots spread wide just beneath the plate surface, touching hidden damp chambers without breaking the floor into obvious wetness. Some grow slowly for years, adding only a few leaves, then flower once, rarely, producing pale capsules that split in dry wind. Others do not seem to flower much at all, reproducing by detached fragments or by tiny bulbils that fall into cracks and become part of the next seam-crust.

Their relationship to the ground is not separate. A dead succulent leaf does not rot in the usual way; it dries, calcifies, and joins the surface. Its waxy residue helps bind microbial films. Its minerals become part of the next plate veneer. Its body may seal a small crack, or leave behind a negative mold where water will collect later. In this place, decay is not disappearance. It is translation.

Lichens are the cartographers of the plates. They do not cover every surface; they create thin maps, rings, streaks, speckled colonies, and faint radiating patches, often beginning near the edges of plates where mineral buildup is thinner and pores can be accessed. Some lichens are pale grey-green. Others are rust-stained from iron in the substrate. On newly exposed surfaces they grow slowly, loosening the surface just enough to allow dust and spore to settle, then trapping both in a crust that eventually becomes a new microplate. They are among the first inhabitants of damaged spots. When a plate lifts unevenly and exposes the root-lattice beneath, lichens spread as thin green borders around the wound, not healing it as a body heals, but colonizing it as a place colonizes its own broken edge. Wind will then carry sand into the opening. Rain will wash it. Fungi will bind it. Lichens will mark it. The breach becomes a new pattern.

There are also the algal pool-sealers, temporary beings that appear when shallow depressions hold water. In a fresh bowl in the center of a broad plate, rain may last for hours or days. At first the water looks like a mirror over stone, but beneath it a faint greenish-black film begins to spread. Cyanobacteria and algae form a living skin that catches dust, slows evaporation, and precipitates carbonate. As the water thins, the film dries into a pale crust. Salt blooms around its edges. The pool becomes a plate again, but a plate marked with the ghost of water, with faint concentric lines and mineral scars. These organisms are short-lived in form, but long-lived in consequence. They build the floor by dying.

The basin also hosts things that are neither clearly alive nor clearly geological but behave like both. Salt rosettes pry open seams. Mineral flakes loosen under heat and settle into hollows, where dust and spore mix into a thin loam too ephemeral to call soil. Tiny fragments of dead reed skin drift across pans, sometimes lodging in pores and becoming nuclei for new microbial growth. Silica casts left by dissolved stems become ridges in the next surface. Root channels, once the plant is gone, become the hidden veins that later water follows. The plain is full of such residues. They are not dead matter merely lying about. They are the materials from which future life is assembled.

Because there are no animals, nothing here is hunted, grazed, carried, or consumed in the familiar way. The population dynamics are not based on appetite but on availability of surface, water, and time. Plant growth is not checked by insects or herbivores; it is checked by the physics of the made floor. Too much expansion and roots may lift a plate. Too little moisture and a colony dies. Too many fungi in one seam and the channel narrows. Too much salt and growth retreats. The ecology is architectural. Every organism is partly a resident and partly a builder. A reed does not simply stand in the basin; it holds a seam, traps dust, shades a pore, directs water, and leaves behind a mold that may become the next capillary passage. A fungal shelf does not simply stand alone; it scatters spores, modifies airflow around itself, and slowly incorporates the ground beneath it into its own structure.

There is a kind of sociability here, but it is slow, distributed, and unacknowledged. Organisms do not cooperate with intent. They do not recognize one another. Yet the surface is full of mutual dependencies. Microbes feed on root secretions. Fungi transport water between plates. Plants stabilize fungal threads. Lichens expose surfaces to microbial films. Algae turn pools into plates. Salt opens cracks that roots then widen. Dust settles in pores and becomes a seedbed. The whole environment is a network of reciprocal transformations. Nothing simply exists beside something else. Each presence modifies the conditions for the next.

This makes the basin’s “inhabitants” difficult to separate from the basin itself. The larger life is the made plain. It has pores for mouths, though it does not eat. It has seams for joints, though it does not move as a creature moves. It has capillary passages that resemble veins, drawing water away from the sun and returning it to the edges of pools. It has thermal cycles that resemble breath: rising heat at midday, falling coolness at dawn, the soft exhalation of steam after rain. It has a nervous system only in the sense that stress and pressure are distributed through plates, roots, and fungal threads. It has memory only in the sense that past growth is encoded in mineral layers, root molds, and channel orientations. It does not have a mind. It does not desire repair. Yet it repairs. It does not have a body. It is becoming one.

The whole floor behaves as a distributed organism without centralized control. A crack does not simply break; it invites growth. A pool does not simply dry; it deposits. A storm does not merely disturb; it redistributes water, spore, dust, and heat across vast distances. The basin may respond to a distant rain event by changing its local winds as cooled plates alter pressure. It may shift a few millimeters across a seam because a root swells with moisture. It may close over a shallow bowl with microbial carbonate before it can become a lasting pond. These are not conscious acts, but they are acts nonetheless. They are the movements of something made alive by countless smaller lives.

At night, this distributed presence becomes more perceptible. The air cools. Dew forms first in the seams, then on plate faces. The ground begins to drink from the atmosphere. Under this moisture, microbial films darken and soften. Fungal threads rehydrate. Hidden chambers swell very slightly. The plates relax into their cooling contraction. If one listens carefully, the land makes its own small sounds: the dry tick of mineral flakes, the faint creak of a hinge-ridge adjusting, the whisper of wind through hollow stems. It is not the sound of a sleeping creature, exactly, but the sound of a surface continuing its slow maintenance.

At dawn, the basin is briefly glittering, as though the ground is sweating light. The reed stands appear black and thin against the white shine. The pools, if any remain, look like fragments of broken mirror sealed with salt lace. This is when the invisible life is most visible: not as individual organisms, but as pattern. Dew outlines the pores. The seams become darker than the plates. The whole surface reveals the logic of its construction. One can see, for a short time, that the land is not merely lying there. It is arranged.

By midday, that arrangement nearly disappears into brightness. The heat rises. The air shimmers. The plates reflect light until the ground seems to participate in the sky. The reeds stand like dry calligraphy. The succulents pull their leaves inward. The microbial films dry to chalk. Fungal shelves cast small precise shadows. The basin seems emptied of life, as though all its inhabitants have withdrawn into the seams. But this is only the surface impression. Beneath, water moves. Beneath, hyphae shift moisture. Beneath, salts migrate. Beneath, the next plate is already beginning from the residue of the last.

If there is a being here that deserves to be called a resident in the singular, it is not an animal and not a plant. It is the whole assemblage: the plates, the pores, the fungal threads, the algal skins, the boot-collared reeds, the succulents, the lichens, the salt, the mineral dust, the dead root molds, the living precipitates, and the weather all acting upon one another. It is a creature made of process. It does not have a face, a voice, or a boundary. Its boundary is the horizon. Its breath is convection. Its blood is capillary water. Its bones are fossilized stems. Its skin is the pale plate tessellation. Its death is not death but conversion: a body becomes dust, dust becomes seedbed, seedbed becomes cement, cement becomes surface, surface becomes home.

So whoever or whatever lives there does not live upon the basin in the ordinary sense. Most of them are the basin, slowly becoming itself. They are the small builders that make the floor. The plants that mark the seams. The films that seal the pools. The threads that hide water. The lichens that colonize wounds. The fungal shelves that scatter spores across empty kilometers. And, if one insists on calling it a resident, the whole made plain, quiet, immense, and active without motion, living at the pace of mineral growth, with no mind to know it is alive, and no mouth, and yet full of mouths, and no hand, and yet full of work.
