**The Inhabitants of the Kettle-Colonnade**

What lives in the Kettle-Colonnade is not, at first, a society. It is a set of slow answers. The standing stones answer wind. The basalt answers water. The moss answers light. The pale mineral threads answer stillness by becoming floor. And among these patient replies, there are the people: not many, never enough to fill the plaza, but present enough to call the place inhabited.

They do not give themselves a single name, or they do not expect strangers to ask for one. Outsiders may hear them called the Tenders, the Few Who Walk the Streets, the Basin-Wrights, the People Who Return. The names mean nearly the same thing: they are the ones who live where the town has not yet finished being lived.

If you enter on an overcast morning, when fog is low and the columns are dark with wetness, you may see two or three figures moving at opposite ends of a causeway, so far apart that their shadows do not meet. They wear cloaks of woven reed, sedge, and grass, frayed at the hem from brushing damp stone. Beneath the cloaks are shorter garments of scraped hide, fastened with rawhide cords and toggles of antler. Their hands are broad, red, cracked in the way of hands that belong to both work and weather. Their nails are edged with pale mineral. Their knees are often green. A copper bead may hang at a throat or wrist, old, green-rimmed, traded up from lower valleys and worn more like a memory than an ornament.

Their bodies have learned the basin’s rules. They walk carefully over wet causeways because moisture makes the stone louder. A careless footfall can send a bright pop through a plaza, startling midges, disturbing fog, and waking a channel’s hollow voice. So they step with their weight placed by intuition, heel then toe, listening to the stone beneath them. They tilt their heads at narrow channels. They pause where reed bundles block a mouth. They kneel to listen at cracks. To an outsider, this looks like superstition. To them, it is housekeeping.

The Kettle-Colonnade is maintained less than tended. Its inhabitants do not behave as conquerors of the site. They behave as midwives. Where water has begun to lay down a shelf of white mineral, they examine it. If the shelf blocks a house-door, they chip it back with quartz and bone. If it forms a good lip around a rain trap, they let it grow. If it narrows a procession lane, they widen it; if it promises to become a floor strong enough for a hearth, they mark its edge with stones and leave it alone. They are always correcting, always agreeing, always leaving room for the next season’s decision.

There are roles among them, though no one wears a crown for it. Some are channel-wrights: they know where reeds must be replaced, where clay stoppers have cracked, where the water’s course has begun to wander. Some are moss-scrubbers: they scrape excess growth from key stones, gather cushions from dry ledges, and bind moss into pads for feet, bedding, and tinder. Some are sound-tenders: they place stones to deaden echoes near sleeping houses, move drying racks away from dripping ledges, and tune rain traps so the patter falls into dry pits instead of singing against storage baskets. Some are fog-readers: they know by the sucking sounds in the pools whether moisture will rise or lift, and they call with soft taps if an evening is going to be dangerous for bare foot or grain.

Their speech is quiet. The wet basin makes too many echoes; a raised voice can bounce from causeway to column and return as a false crowd. Therefore they speak with clipped, low phrases, or not at all. They have a grammar of knocks: one short knock against a dry stone means here is stable ground; two knocks mean here is hollow; three quick taps ask is the channel clear? A sweep of a reed across a slab says water is coming. A palm pressed flat against a column says listen. Children learn to hear the difference between a wet stone that answers and a wet stone that merely repeats. They learn to move through fog without calling out, because the place will not remain silent just because they need help.

The households are small and separated by enormous streets. This is not loneliness so much as spacing. A single occupied house may stand beside a plaza wide enough for a festival, its reed roof sagging, its doorway facing another structure half a wet field away. Smoke from one hearth does not meet the smoke from the other unless the wind is strong enough to carry it across the open grammar of the town. Yet the streets are not empty of relation. They are lanes of obligation. One house may be responsible for a side channel; the next for a grain platform; the next for the rain trap that feeds the lichen beds. The town’s families, if one calls them that, are bound by shared maintenance more than by proximity.

Only a few houses are lived in through the year. Others open when seasons call: when rain needs testing, when fog must be read, when a ritual channel is cleared, when a family from below comes to visit, or when a person decides that a particular wall-base has become enough of a house to sleep within. A winter house may become a storehouse by spring. A platform once used for drying may be moved aside for a marriage or an alignment night. A doorway may lead to a blank floor and be swept as though someone expected to cross it within days. The Tenders treat empty houses not as ruins but as unfinished guests.

Their technology is humble and exact. They use rounded beach-stones bound with rawhide as hammers, but choose each head for its ring when struck. They sharpen quartz and obsidian for scraping mineral crust, antler for prying reed stoppers, bone for testing channels. They weave baskets tight enough to carry wet clay and moss, and they seal them with a paste of reed fiber and ash. Their clay pipes are shallow-fired and fitted to drain condensation from certain walls. They make no great machines and desire none. The basin is already a machine: stone, water, wind, and moss doing slow work together. A human hand’s job is to guide one part away from another.

Food is careful, not abundant. A handful of grain carried up from the moor below may be kept for weeks. It is thickened with ground horsetail shoots, boiled with reed roots, or mixed with lichen flour after several waters have taken away its bitterness. In spring they harvest tender sedges and the pale hearts of rosettes by the pools. In autumn, when the marsh pools shrink a little under colder nights, they collect snails from lichen walls and, rarely, small frogs from under moss mats. These are not eaten often. They are considered gifts from a wet body not yet friendly enough to offer much. The main meals are stored and traded: dried grain, dried roots, perhaps fat from below in a sealed bladder. Their cooking fires are kept small and set on dry slabs, because too much heat near wet basalt can crack the ground and make the streets shout.

There are few children among them. This is not because the basin is hostile to children, but because the basin is unfinished, and the Tenders know that raising a child here means teaching a child to listen to stone before trusting it. When a child is present, the whole town seems to make room for the child without crowding it. The child may sit on a dry causeway and tap a hollow slab with a bone pick, not for games but to hear whether the stone has changed overnight. Another child may be set to carry a reed rope from a drying rack to a channel stopper. They are given small tasks that are not small: testing whether a lip of new mineral has become too sharp for bare feet, watching which moss pads are worn through, learning how to breathe slowly near a pool so the fog will not know they are there.

The animal inhabitants are shy and few. Frogs live under moss mounds in small dark hollows. They call thinly when fog lifts, each voice like a needle tapping glass. Snails climb the lichen walls, pale shells dusted with mineral, leaving slow threads that become lines in the basin’s unfinished script. Midges rise above the pools in spirals when a figure passes, then settle again, their bodies too small to matter but their presence making the pools seem alive. A pipit may land on a column once or twice a day, head turned, listening to the hum. It does not nest there. It visits. Beetles move under wet bark? no tall trees. Maybe water beetles in pools. There are no dogs, no livestock, no barns. The basin has no need of them. It supports life as a newly plastered room supports a candle: enough to prove that occupancy is possible.

The green inhabitants are more numerous than animals. Moss is a resident. It grows in cushions that hold water and dry slowly. Sedge mats weave the shallow edges. Horsetail rises in jointed stems, giving people tinder and rope. Lichen spreads in gray-copper continents, marking which stones have been wet long enough to accept it. Broad rosette leaves gather near water, their surfaces beading with film. These are not scenery; they are citizens. They decide where water can hide, where stone can breathe, where a person may kneel without soaking through.

Beyond these, there are things that are alive by the basin’s own standards. The mineral seepage is a slow animal made of whiteness. It moves across the stone in visible patience. It thickens over cracks. It makes lips over channels. It forms steps that can be climbed before the year is out. The water itself is not merely liquid; it is a writer. It enters the streets as a sentence, edits a wall, leaves a blank space, and returns next season to add another clause. The wind is another inhabitant, with mouths in the columns and hands in the reeds. Fog is a guest who walks the perimeter and sometimes remains for days, making the town feel crowded by absence.

The people live with this. They do not worship the basin so much as keep appointments with it. On certain cold nights, when fog settles like poured milk, a few will stand on the platform near the central channel. They do not shout. They carry hollow stones filled with pebbles and shake them once or twice, not to summon the town but to ask whether it is awake. If the sound returns clean, they mark the floor with a line of reed. If the sound returns muddy, they know the mineral has closed too much and needs scraping. There are rain-trap songs, low hums with closed mouths, sung where dry moss can deaden echo. There are first-echo rites when a new causeway is laid: one person walks it barefoot while another listens from a standing column, and together they decide its name. There are names for the town’s moods: the morning when the basin breathes through a thousand tiny mouths; the afternoon when the basalt becomes a drum; the night when the wind makes the columns groan like a forgotten choir.

Their calendar is wet. The year is not measured only by sun or frost but by how water behaves. First freezing film is one season. Fog that lifts to reveal new mineral lips is another. The day a channel begins to click like a bone rattle means the stone is tightening. The week when the plaza floor becomes a mirror and the standing stones darken is called the time when the town remembers its own face. When the marsh pools go quiet, when the sucking sound ceases and only thin wind remains, the Tenders know they must check the reed stoppers and the clay pipes, because the water has begun to hide.

Their homes are not places of privacy so much as places of trial. A doorway faces inward to a room, but also outward to a plaza that may flood by morning. A wall-base may become a real wall if mineral continues to climb it. A hearth-circle may be used by five people one night and left empty for a month, its stones still warm with memory or still cold with disuse. The houses do not seem to protect the inhabitants from the basin so much as teach the inhabitants how to live inside the basin. The roof does not defeat rain; it negotiates with it. The walls do not reject water; they learn to let it bead and pass. The people live within these negotiations, and they begin to resemble them.

Some of them are old in the way of the high country: thin, watchful, hands scarred by flint and mineral. They sit on dry ledges during the day and scrape lichen from storage stones. They know which channels will sing in wind and which pools will swallow a voice. Their eyes have the squint of glare from wet basalt, but their hearing is strange and good. They can tell whether a crack is opening or closing by the way water trembles in it. When they walk, they often stop first at one standing stone, then another, touching the wet surface with two fingers, as if checking the city’s pulse.

Some are young, perhaps only a handful, because the basin supports no large population and perhaps does not want one yet. They move quicker than the older Tenders, less carefully near dry stone, more fearlessly near pools. They practice sound: they knock patterns into channels, they hide behind columns and wait for the wind to answer, they test how far a pebble-click can carry. They are not playing. They are learning the town’s grammar. If one of them becomes a fog-reader, it will not be because someone chose it in a ceremony, but because it was noticed that this child stopped at each wet stone as though hearing a whisper others had missed.

The outsiders who sometimes climb to the rim do not know what to think of the place. To them, it looks like a town emptied. To the few who live inside, it looks like a town filling too slowly. They have no word for crowding. Their word for many people is close to their word for many stones in a channel: blockage. Their word for neighbors is close to their word for the wetness between stones: the thing that must be allowed to move. They are not lonely in the way an abandoned market is lonely. They are anticipatory. They leave a dry platform swept. They leave a reed curtain tied back. They leave a bowl of grain on a sill, not always for a god, sometimes for the first stranger who arrives and finds the town waiting.

Even the people imagine those who are not there. A woman may lay a reed basket in a doorway that has never had a child in it. A man may tap a channel with a bone pick while speaking aloud to the absent: “the stopper holds, the mouth is clear, if you come at first rain, you will find the path.” Some houses are built for relatives who may live far below and never climb high enough to visit. Some plazas are wide for processions that have not yet formed. The Tenders do not see this as foolishness. They think the town is asking for more than its current inhabitants can provide. If the basin can write itself into city, then future people may also write themselves into the town. They maintain the grammar before the speakers arrive.

At dusk, when the basin cools and the fog begins to crawl from the lower terraces, the Tenders move toward the few occupied hearths. They carry peat and dry grass from protected pits, reed torches capped with clay, and small baskets of lichen. They do not enter their houses at once. First they listen. They stand at the edge of the plaza and let the sound settle into its night forms: the moan between columns, the drip in hollow basalt, the hiss of grass seed heads, the sucking breath of the marsh. Then they step inside the sound and become part of it. Smoke rises from one hearth, thin and gray, and is caught by fog so that it seems to lie flat over the streets like a forgotten road. From outside, if a traveler is far enough away, the place may look inhabited by fires and voices. But the fires are few, and the voices are mostly water, and the traveler is already inside the illusion before knowing it.

At the center of the basin, where the channel sings, one figure may remain after the others have gone to rest. This is not always a guard or a priest. It may be only a person who has become quiet enough to hear the town changing. She kneels. She touches the wet stone. She watches the pale film move across it, not fast, not slow, but certain. She is not afraid of the mineral. She is not afraid of the water. She is thinking about tomorrow’s scrape, the place where the channel needs clearing, the way the eastern lip has grown thin and sharp, the empty house across the plaza whose doorway is beginning to become a floor. She breathes into the fog. The fog beads on her cloak and trembles with her. She returns one hand to the stone, then stands.

What lives in the Kettle-Colonnade is a few people, many mosses, a handful of creatures, a thousand tiny sounds, and one slow geological intention. It is a settlement whose population is mostly potential. Its inhabitants are not only those who eat and sleep there, but those who enable eating and sleeping: the water, the mineral, the columns, the fog, the lichen, the reed. If you stand there long enough, you may notice that the people are not the only ones tending the town. The town tends its own becoming. And the few figures moving through its wet streets are its caretakers, its witnesses, its rehearsal company, its first guests, and perhaps its most reluctant parents.

They live there because the place asks to be lived. They return again and again because every season the town has changed its mind a little, and someone must read the new draft.
