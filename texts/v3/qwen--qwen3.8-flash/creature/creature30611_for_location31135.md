In the Kiln-Grove of Hand-Made Leaves, whoever or whatever lives there is best answered at several scales at once. The place does not have a population so much as a slow, layered parliament of dry bodies, made gestures, and living repetitions. It is inhabited not by creatures alone, but by the habits of creatures; not by builders alone, but by the traces builders left behind until those traces became behavior.

If one insists on the smallest named residents, they would be the **Grainwrights**, the rice-sized beetles that move through tile joints like punctuation marks. Their backs are hard, low-fired shells painted in muted green and oxblood, and their antennae are not antennae in any wet, animal sense, but fine braids of hair or mineral thread. They do not scurry with the nervous quickness of ordinary insects. They crawl with the patience of something that has learned the exact width of every crack. They scrape pollen from the undersides of ceramic leaves. They carry dust into seams. They eat the frayed edges of old stalks and leave behind small rounded pellets that the grove accepts as grout, as new tile-surface, as the beginning of a shelf.

If one presses an eye close to a tile, one sees them working as a community without a city. A Grainwright may pause beside a hairline fracture for the length of an afternoon, turning its tiny head as if reading the break. Another comes and climbs over its back without collision. A third pulls a strand of fiber from a moss cushion and begins to stitch a leaf-edge that has cracked in the dry warmth. They do not seem to notice a visitor unless one’s shadow falls wrong. When a hand enters their field of motion, they do not flee as animals flee from predators; they rearrange, the way sand rearranges when a stone is set down.

But the Grainwrights are not the deepest answer.

There are also the **Mender-Clouds**, which are not separate species so much as temporary bodies. A Mender-Cloud may begin as a cluster of Grainwrights moving through a dry thicket, then gather moths, seed-husks, fragments of molted skin, bits of painted tile, and threads of braided fiber until, for a little while, the cluster resembles a hand. Not a human hand, necessarily. It may resemble a claw, a loop of loom-shuttle, a thumb-pressed seam, the negative space between fingers. It moves with the same eerie exactness described by witnesses of “clusters moving together so exactly that they appear to be one larger creature.” It will approach a leaf whose edge is fraying. It will weave a strand of fiber through the crack. It will press a shard of bone-like clay into place and leave behind a small thumbprint in still-warm dust. Then it scatters, no one seeing the moment of division.

This is perhaps why there are no obvious builders walking the paths.

The builders are not hiding. They have become a verb.

When a wall grows by being laid in the right sequence, that is not construction as an outside hand imposes itself on inert matter. That is the grove accepting its own way of continuing. The Mender-Clouds are the visible, brief form of that acceptance. They do not speak. They do not remember as individuals remember. They remember as patterns remember: by returning, by repeating, by wearing the same grooves into the same fibers until the grooves look inevitable.

The larger residents, however, are unmistakable once one has stood still long enough.

There are the **Potbacks**, long-necked quadrupeds that step from pot to pot as though pots were stepping-stones across a dry lake. They stand taller than a deer, but their movement is slower, more deliberate, and more architectural. Their legs are dry and jointed like carefully fitted rods, their feet broad and rimmed with curved ceramic plates that click faintly when they descend onto a tile ledge. Their backs are not merely backs. They are shelves in motion. Ridges of fired clay run along their spines, and from these ridges grow thick mats of pale gold and green hair-moss. Small leaves sprout between the shoulder blades, brittle and stitched at the edges, as if their bodies were still in conversation with the grove’s vegetation.

A Potback does not eat with urgency. Its mouth opens as a hard, resinous cleft, and its tongue—if one may call it that—is a brush of braided fiber and fine mineral bristles. It scrapes the undersides of leaves for pollen, and it gnaws the dried nodes of stalks, chewing slowly until the material becomes a paste it presses into the cracks along its own flank. Its droppings are pale, rounded, and useful; they settle into gaps in the floor and harden. In this way, Potbacks do not merely live on the landscape. They repair it. They spread it. They carry seed-beads in the furrows of their backs and deposit them near hollow cylinders and buried pots, where the grove tends to build new growth around them.
