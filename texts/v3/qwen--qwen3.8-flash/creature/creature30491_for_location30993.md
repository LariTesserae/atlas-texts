No one lives there, if “someone” means a person, a nation, a maker, a city, a flag, a grave with a name. Nothing has been built. No road has been cut. No fire has been kept burning long enough to make a hearth. There is no evidence of intention in the human sense, no ruins, no tools, no gods, no villages, no attempt to make the land stop being what it is.

And yet it is not empty.

It is populated—densely, patiently, invisibly—by beings that have learned to live in a country that has not finished deciding its shape. If you ask what lives there, the answer changes depending on where you stand, how long you wait, how still you become. The inhabitants are not only animals, nor only plants, nor only fungi, nor only molds and films and spores. Some of them are processes. Some of them are patterns. Some of them are barely continuous. Some of them exist as corrections, drafts, repetitions, and consequences. The highland has no population so much as a living syntax: many beings, many behaviors, all arranged around the same impossible fact—that the ground, the air, the water, and even the sense of solid stone are not entirely settled.

The first and most numerous of its inhabitants are not animals at all.

They are the skins.

They are the mosses, the lichen films, the copper-tinged bacterial mats, the black fungal threads, the pale crusts that spread over broken stone within hours of exposure. They are the living dusts and breathing sheens that turn a fresh fracture into something almost soft. They creep across glass ridges, fill the pores of basalt, climb the sides of cliffs, and settle in the hollows where condensation collects. They do not merely cover the place; they negotiate with it. They catch mineral dust, bind fractured stone, hold water where the ground would otherwise shed it, and create thin membranes of habitat over surfaces that would remain sterile. Without them, the highland would be too harsh, too unstable, too newly broken to support much else.

Among these films is the great network that might be called the Binders.

The Binders are fungi, mostly, but not entirely. They are root-thin, webbed, and patient. They grow through leaf litter, through cracked stone, through fallen trunks, through the undersides of moss mats, and through the soft places where water has begun to seep under pressure. They have no faces, no obvious bodies, no single location. One might stand on a slope and know they are there without seeing a cap or thread. They bind broken surfaces together. They stabilize ground that would otherwise shift. They hold together edges where the land has cracked too fast for soil to form. In the fallen trees, their black blooms and luminous threads turn death into slow industry: decomposition, recombination, mineral transport, water retention. They are the quiet engineers of this ecosystem, doing at the scale of stone and root what no machine or mind has designed.

If a traveler stayed long enough, they might begin to suspect that the Binders are not merely stabilizing the land, but learning it.

Not learning in the way an animal learns, not thinking in the way a mind thinks, but holding patterns. They grow differently in wet places and dry places. They respond to heat that rises from below. They spread toward mineral currents. They form arcs and channels where water moves, as if remembering paths before the paths exist. They can make a slope safer by knitting its fractures, or they can make a hollow more permanent by trapping silt and seed. They are one reason the highland is able to sustain such crowded, contradictory life in a place that should not have settled enough soil, enough warmth, or enough consistency to support anything.

Plants are the second great inhabitants, though “plant” is only the nearest human word.

The green life of the highland is not a background for animals. It is aggressive, layered, and active. It grows with too many ambitions. It does not merely colonize; it negotiates with instability, reaching in directions that cannot be trusted. The trees have roots that branch too much, splitting around fractures, gripping stone with metal-tinged anchors, holding on as if the ground might change its mind under them. Their root systems are themselves ecosystems, wrapped around mineral veins and fungal networks, forming little knots of moisture and shadow. They grow on slopes, in cracks, and on tops of ridges where moisture should not be enough, as if they remember where water once was, or as if they can feel water before it arrives.

The thickets are full of life that is not fully animal and not fully plant. Reed-like plants stand in pale rows where hidden springs push upward through fault lines. Ferns uncurl into spaces too small to seem generous. Fungus-like shrubs with broad, glossy leaves hold dew for hours, not because they are beautiful, but because the air is always giving something and the ground cannot be trusted to. The leaves are dark and waxy, sometimes copper-veined, sometimes edged with fine bright hairs that catch mist and turn it into droplets. When they hold that water, they become small reservoirs, tiny habitats, surfaces where other organisms live: spore mites, filamentous slimes, translucent polyps no larger than a thumbnail.

There are also plants that appear to respond to attention.

This is not metaphor so much as behavior. A grove may seem ordinary until you enter it, and then leaves turn. Not toward the sun—there is rarely a clean sun here—but toward movement, toward presence, toward the pressure of being looked at. Some flowers open only when a shadow passes over them. Some vines shift their grip when you step near, as though they have felt your heat, your breath, your intention. Some trees appear to have two possible versions in the same place: one wet, one dry, one real, one remembered. It may be optical distortion, it may be spore haze, it may be the mind failing to hold still in a place that does not hold still—but some botanist, if one ever came here, would eventually write it down as fact rather than illusion. In this country, being observed may be part of the ecology.

The herds are among the most visible creatures, though “visible” in the highland is a relative condition.

They move across upper slopes in loose columns that never quite form straight lines. They are warm-bodied, broad-backed, low-headed, with thick necks and strange curved protrusions rising from skull or shoulder. Their hides are charcoal, olive, or dark amber, often marked with black bands or patches that make them look like shadows detached from bushes. Along their backs there is sometimes a metallic glint, as if they have been plated with thin sheets of copper or iron, not as armor so much as regulation. The protrusions may be antlers, but they behave strangely. Some are hollow. Some are vascular. Some warm when the animals drink from hot pools, and cool when the fog thickens. They may be radiators, or heat sinks, or sense organs, or handles, or all of these at once. In a place where temperature and moisture are never simple, the herd’s body becomes a kind of living instrument.

They graze on tough grasses and low shrubs, but they also lick mineral crusts, chew salted bark, and drink from places that are not obviously water. They are not cattle, not deer, not quite anything that would fit a single ecological niche. Their hooves are broad and padded, silent on gravel, sinking into moss without sound. When they stand still, they look like low hills that have forgotten they were once animals. When they move, they do so with an odd consistency, as though the landscape has decided to rearrange itself around them. They avoid fixed points. They do not follow straight paths. They graze in arcs, turn at angles that seem unrelated to wind or predator, and shift their columns according to pressures a human would not notice. They are herd animals, but the herd does not depend on a leader. It depends on pattern.

Sometimes a herd casts a single shadow.

Because the light is scattered by haze, because shadows are never fully black, because the spores and dust catch and bend what little radiance there is, a moving column of animals can leave behind a darkening that is more coherent than the animals themselves.
