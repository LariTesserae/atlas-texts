It has inhabitants, but not a citizenry.

If one enters the Hollow Salt Ward looking for a people, for animals gathered at water, for flocks, for villages, for the obvious signs of life—smoke, dung, nests, tracks, calls, the worn softness of repeated passage—one will find none of those. The ward does not refuse the visitor by hostility. It refuses by indifference. Its residents are not organized around presence. They are organized around threshold, gradient, pause, and delay. They live at the edge of the visible. They live inside the architecture. They live in the brief rearrangement of dust after something has passed.

To say what lives here, one must first unlearn the assumption that life must be dense, moist, quick, and social.

In the Hollow Salt Ward, life is not a population. It is a way of persisting.

## The Skin-Makers

The first inhabitants are the ones people would be tempted not to count as inhabitants at all: the pale films, stalks, crusts, and tufts that grow over the basin floor and rise from the walls. These are not lichen in the ordinary sense, though they are often described as lichen-like. They are not plants, though they form colonies that occupy shaded surfaces with something resembling intention. They are not fungi, though they send fine filaments into microcracks and seem to carry mineral substance from one surface to another by means too slow to observe. They are more like crystalline organisms that have taken architecture for a body.

They begin as pale stalks, almost bone-colored, emerging from the floor with brittle tips. From those stalks comes a secretion that is not liquid in any wet sense; it is more like a mineral vapor that condenses, then sets, then forgets it was ever mobile. The secreted film spreads sideways, over stone, over broken crystal, over other films, forming sheets as fine as breath. Those sheets strengthen, crack at the edges, peel, and are replaced. In the cool intervals, the film is active. In the warmer intervals, it becomes porous and brittle and seems to rest. The skin-makers live in the boundary between those states.

Their forms are many, though it may be wrong to speak of “many” as though they were separate animals. One form might appear as a chalk-white veil over the foot of a tower. Another as violet-gray filaments radiating from a crack, like the lines of a fingerprint pressed into stone. Another as glassy tufts that stand upright and catch the dim light in small sharp points. Some look like frost that has learned to hold its shape. Some look like the interior of a shell, flattened and extended. Some look like the remnants of a leaf that never had water.

They have no mouths. They do not drink. They take in the ward through differences: the coldness of upper air, the warmth stored in mineral walls, the charge of suspended dust, the faint chemical traces left by grazing feet. They metabolize gradients. A colony thrives where one stone is slightly warmer than its neighbor. A colony dies where the balance shifts too far toward warmth and the film thins, or too far toward cold and it becomes rigid, cracks, and sheds.

Each sheet, each flake, each hair-thin layer is a body and a building.

When a tower grows a new ring of translucent stone, that is not merely geology. That is a skin-maker bloom. When a plaza surface turns briefly chalk-white and then returns to gray, that is a tide of living accretion. When an arch thickens at its joint without any hand shaping it, that is because organisms too patient to be noticed have spent many cool hours adding themselves to the structure.

The skin-makers reproduce by shedding. A fragment of old film, detached by heat or pressure or the bite of a grazer, can lodge in a crack and begin anew. A spalling edge on a tower can seed a colony in an empty court. This is why the city always seems to be beginning again. Each ruin contains the potential for new growth. Each fracture is a nursery. The ward is not abandoned; it is propagating.

They communicate nothing, because they have no need for communication at the human scale. Yet they are not disconnected. The floor beneath them transmits pressure. The dust carries charge. The mineral film connects surface to surface. A change in one part of the basin can alter growth patterns in another after long delay. A plaza may not respond to the loss of one colony for days. An alley may not show the effects of a shift in the central towers until many cycles have passed. Their society, if one can call it that, is slower than memory.

## The Salt-Roots

Below the visible floor, if visible anything can be said of the Hollow Salt Ward, there are pale rootlike filaments, finer than thread and stronger than they should be. These are often called salt-roots, though they are not roots in any botanical sense. They live in the cracks beneath the dry channels, the shallow hollows, the basins that mimic rain-hold. They are the ward’s most literal answer to the absence of water.

The salt-roots do not carry liquid. They carry pressure.

Their bodies are porous mineral lattices, lined with microscopic channels that pull crystalline salts upward from the deeper substrate. During the warm intervals, when the dry efflorescence begins to crawl across the floor, the salt-roots are most active. They do not flood the basin. They change it. They exhale powders. They push gradients. They move mineral substance through the stone as though the whole basin were a dry sponge slowly remembering how to thirst.

Some people, if they ever visited the Hollow Salt Ward, might believe these roots are the hidden cause of the city’s urbanity. The streets are not made for feet, but many align with old salt-root channels. The plazas are not made for markets, but many sit above wider networks of pale filaments. The towers are not made for inhabitants, but the strongest bases often correspond to the oldest root-colonies.

It is as if the salt-roots are a vascular system for a body that has no blood, no heart, no heat of its own.

When a root-network fails, the city begins to change. A court grows barren and powdery. An alley narrows as film accumulates without order. A tower may begin to lean from internal weakness. The skin-makers lose their foundation. The grazers lose their food. The predators lose their ambush. Then, over long intervals, a new channel opens, or a fragment of root survives in a sealed crack, and the bloom returns.

Death here is not disappearance. It is conversion.

## The Furrowers

The most obvious creatures are the small pale arthropod-like animals that move across the mineral streets during the long dim periods. They are glass-jointed, with carapaces like thin sheets of baked salt. They leave no tracks, or tracks so faint that they vanish before one is certain they existed. They have no eyes, or at least none recognizable as eyes; in their place are sensitive ridges, pale and finely grooved, running from head to neck as though the creature has learned to see with pressure.

They are often called furrowers because of what they do to the city.

A furrower feeds on the outer crystalline skins of towers. It bites at the film with a small, dry, almost mechanical action. It does not chew. It scrapes. It draws its mouthparts across the surface, leaving behind a faint groove. The groove may be narrow enough to fit a thread, or wide enough to receive a fragment of dust, but either way it changes the course of future growth. Mineral film accumulates in the furrow. The tower’s surface is shaped by an animal that never intended to build.

The furrowers are solitary. They do not gather in groups. They do not follow one another. They may pass within a hand’s width of each other and continue on unrelated paths. If one encounters another, there may be a brief pause, a tremor of the carapace, a slight adjustment of orientation, and then separation. They have no reason to communicate. Their world is made of surfaces, temperature, charge, and the faint traces left by other bodies.

Their movements are not fast. A furrower crossing a plaza may take hours, or longer. It moves in small hesitations. It seems to test the air, though there is no wind strong enough to test. It may remain motionless for so long that its outline becomes part of the architecture. The dim light shifts around it. Dust gathers at its edges in faint halos. If one watches it for too long, one begins to suspect it is not alive, or that life here has chosen to be indistinguishable from stone.

When a furrower feeds successfully, it leaves a grazing path. When it fails, it may break a tooth-shard, a small fragment of crystalline mouthpart, into the floor. That shard may become a nucleus for new skin. Its waste, if waste can be called such, is a fine powdery compound that settles into cracks and alters growth color. The furrowers are therefore not merely animals living in the city. They are accidental engineers, unintentional gardeners, silent city planners.

Their young are not born in the ordinary way. In sheltered niches, beneath ledges, in the shaded hollows behind broken arches, the adult leaves a dry crystal seed. The seed looks like a splinter or a grain of salt larger than its neighbors. It may remain dormant for long periods. When temperature and charge shift correctly, the seed splits with a tiny dry sound, almost inaudible, and the larva emerges. The larva is pale, soft at first, then quickly coated with mineral dust. It begins to graze almost immediately. It has no childhood in the sense of play or exploration. Its earliest movement is an act of architecture.

Furrowers die often. A predator takes one. A thermal stress cracks its carapace. A colony of skin-makers grows over it before it can move away. It is not unusual for a furrower’s body to become part of the city: a half-embedded carapace in a wall, a leg fused into a new buttress, a mouth-shard caught in the mouth of an arch. The ward has no graveyard. It has only layers.

## The Thread-Stalkers

On the rims of platforms, along the upper edges of blocks, and in the narrow spaces where the cool air touches the faintly warm stone, there are other creatures, longer and thinner than the furrowers. They rest motionless, so aligned with the crystal beneath them that they are discovered only when the light changes around them. These are the thread-stalkers.

They have bodies like translucent reeds stretched into segments. Their legs are too fine to appear strong, yet they can support the whole creature over a gap of sharp crystal without bending a blade of glass-grass. Their heads are narrow, almost absent, with long sensory fronds that extend slightly above the body. They do not feed on the solid skin of towers as the furrowers do. They feed on the dust.

They stand or lie in places where electrostatic particles gather at edges. Their fronds draw in fine motes, charged grains, and crystalline flakes suspended in the dry air. They filter the dust as though the dust were a kind of food, or perhaps a kind of message. When one moves across a platform, it leaves no footprint, but the dust behind it may rearrange into a faint wake, a ghostly outline. The thread-stalkers are most visible because of what they displace.

They are patient. They can remain in one position for so long that skin-makers begin to colonize the spaces between their legs. Small lichen-crusts grow on their backs, not parasitically, but symbiotically: the dust they filter enriches the crust, and the crust helps the stalkers hold charge. Old thread-stalkers may look like miniature gardens, or like fragments of the city that have learned to stand upright.

They do not hunt in the obvious sense. They are not fast. They do not chase. But they are predators of a kind, though their prey is often other thread-stalkers, or tiny creatures living inside the dust. If two thread-stalkers meet too near a rich dust line, one may shift its weight toward the other, and the other may withdraw. A conflict rarely becomes visible. Sometimes one simply remains motionless for a longer period than before, and the dust around it settles differently. Sometimes a frond falls to the floor and becomes a seed.

The thread-stalkers are the ward’s high-born residents: living on platforms, in cool air, along the upper margins of structures. They are also its most easily mistaken for architecture. A broken arch might have stood for a long time. A thread-stalker resting upon it may be mistaken for a crack. A pale vein across the floor may be a colony of skin-makers, or a thread-stalker so still that its body has become a line of light.

## The Waitlings

The predators are the least likely creatures to be believed alive, because they do not behave like predators in the way humans expect. They do not stalk with urgency. They do not pounce with visible speed. They wait.

They are low, broad, segmented animals with flat limbs and bodies like broken shields. Their color is pale mineral gray, sometimes with faint violet along the edges, sometimes with a silver-green sheen where dim light strikes their carapace. Their surfaces are not smooth; they are porous, ridged, and dust-covered. Many have small crystal growths on their backs, not born with them but acquired from their surroundings over years. The older ones resemble living rubble.

They lie beneath overhangs of half-grown towers, along the inner edges of arches, in the narrow voids between blocks, in the shaded depressions beneath ledges where furrowers occasionally rest. Their bodies are flat enough to fit into the space of a fallen wall, and their breathing—if one can call it breathing—is slow enough to be imperceptible. They do not need to move. They have evolved for ambush.

They perceive by pressure, by vibration, by faint thermal traces. A furrower crosses a threshold. The floor records its weight. The dust shifts around its carapace. The stone warms by almost nothing where the grazer’s body has lain. The waiting predator notices.
