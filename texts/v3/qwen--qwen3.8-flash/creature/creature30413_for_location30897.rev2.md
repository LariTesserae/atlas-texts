They are not a race. They are not a population, exactly. The place has too many levels for any one kind of life to own it, and too much light, too much cold, too much made surface, for the old assumptions of ecology to hold. Whatever lives there lives by fitting into joints.

They are seam-dwellers, condensation-drinkers, edge-creatures, pipe-nesters, vent-herds, film-swimmers, moss-carriers, metal-eaters, arch-rovers, and things that look as though they were made from the valley itself—wings like ceramic glaze, legs like cable ties, fur like oxidized wire. They are quiet because the air rewards quiet. They are pale because brightness is everywhere. They are angular because the surfaces around them are angular, and because the land has not had enough time to round anything off. They are strange, patient, and abundant in the way that a well-made mistake can be abundant: not wild, not untamed, not original, but deeply adapted.

At first the inhabitants seem like fauna.

They are not merely fauna.

The high constructed basin contains whole communities of animals, but also something older and stranger forming underneath them: a distributed life that is made partly of biology, partly of machine, partly of weather, and partly of the valley’s slow tendency to forget what it is. The true inhabitants are both the creatures that drink from channels and the channels that teach the creatures how to drink. They are the birds nesting in ventilators and the ventilators themselves, which begin to behave like living organs. They are the herds and the tiles beneath their hooves, because the tiles are warm in places the herds know, and the herds wear paths into the tiles, and the tiles are still being laid.

Whoever or whatever lives there, it begins at the edges.

***

**The Pale Clickers**

The broadest, most visible inhabitants are the pale, narrow-legged herds that move across open decks and shallow basins. They have long, thin legs and wide, split hooves with flexible, moisture-sensitive pads. The pads allow them to stand on hot metal without complaint and on cold composite without losing balance. Their bodies are slim, their necks are lengthened, their heads small and triangular, set low like grazing birds. They are not birds, but they are built by the valley into something like one.

Their coats are not soft in the old mammalian sense. They are furred, yes, but the fur is sparse, stiff, and layered with fine mineral dust that makes it look matte and chalky. Colors run across their bodies in broken bands: grays, ochres, pale blues, copper patches, white so bright it seems wet even in shadow. This is camouflage. It also may be a kind of weather record. The animals pick up mineral residue from vents and pipes, and their coats slowly tint with the palette of the valley. By the time a female Clicker has given birth twice, her flanks may look like the side of a tower.

They eat almost nothing that is purely plant. They graze where moss holds moisture, licking ceramic walls and scraping lichen from pipe joints. They nibble silver shrubs where roots have cracked into tiles. They drink from condensation threads, from rain pools that collect in shallow bowls, from the damp undersides of walkways. They lick metal rails when cold surfaces bead with night-water. Sometimes they stand for long minutes with their tongues pressed against a rail, drinking what is not quite water yet. Their saliva is thick and mineral-rich, and it smells faintly of stone.

Their hooves make the valley’s most reliable rhythm: a soft, hard click, never a thunder. A herd crossing a deck sounds like rain beginning somewhere else. The Clickers are sensitive to the sub-bass movement beneath the valley—the deep, tooth-felt pulse that turns water uphill, changes pressure, and decides where rain will fall. When that pulse is near, they lie down. They do not lie down in fear. They lie down as if listening. Their ribs expand, then hold, then expand again, matching the rhythm of some mechanism they do not understand but which has become weather to them.

They have no great social ceremonies, but they have patterns. Elders stand at the edges of groups and seem to test the air. Young ones press beneath gantries or between the legs of adults. Males, if males can be said to exist among them, carry their heads lower and move with a strange deliberation, not dominance exactly, but a kind of territorial patience. They mark their routes by leaving thin smears of mineral paste on vertical surfaces, not by scent alone, but by color. The valley learns what belongs to which herd by tone.

They do not fear the machines, but they respect them. A herd will stop before a fresh section of extruded tile because the ozone is too sharp, the heat still too strange. They will not cross a conveyor unless it is still. If a turbine turns slowly enough that its blades seem fixed, a Clicker may stand beside it for hours, not sleeping, not eating, simply acknowledging that something enormous has breathed.

They are also among the first to exploit the breaks in reality.

Because the valley sometimes remembers being water, the herds will stand in rows at the edge of dry ground and wait for a lake that may not exist. This is not madness; it is a behavior. There is a kind of inherited knowledge that says: when the ground is bright in the wrong way, drink anyway. There are places where a wet mark will appear before rain, and the Clickers can see it. Their hooves shift slightly on such surfaces, not because they feel moisture, but because the surface has become expectant. When rain later arrives, it falls along the same line the herd stood near, as though their waiting had helped pull it into place.

***

**The Ledge-Climbers**

Higher up, where the decks turn to ridges and the ridges to walls, live the climbers. They are chamois-like, but smaller, with feet adapted not just for stone but for composite, not just for slope but for vertical. Their hooves are concave and rubber-padded, edged with fine serrations. They can stand on a smooth face for long periods because their feet create tiny suction points. They move with the deliberate calm of animals that know gravity is negotiable in this place.

Their fur is thicker on the shoulders and hips, thinner at the neck and legs. The hairs catch lichen and moss, and they carry gardens on their backs. Small insects live in the grooves of their fur. Some climbers have pale, translucent patches along the ribs where the coat has grown thin, showing a body beneath that resembles polished metal more than flesh. This may be adaptation. It may be mimicry. It may be only that they have become so thoroughly part of the built landscape that the landscape is beginning to take them back.

They feed on mineral residue, vent warmth, stiff leaves, moss, and the dust of old tile. They are drawn to newly welded surfaces because the heat changes and the metal gives off fine salts. They lick seams where condensation forms first. Their dung is pale and rich enough to seed tiny plant patches, so their trails become green threads across otherwise blank walls. They are colonizers of height. Where a fresh arch is poured, the climbers come within days, not because they smell it, but because they know that arches eventually cool in predictable ways.

They nest in pipe junctions, in recesses where gantry beams meet, inside abandoned housings that have hollowed out with heat and weather. A pipe joint is a cliff. A roof overhang is a cave. To them, these facts are obvious.

They do not climb only because they can. Some of them live almost entirely in the vertical valley. Their bodies have become vertical. Their eyes are set wide to judge angles that shift when the valley is wrong. If a tower has too many floors viewed from one side and too few from another, the climber does not panic. It waits. It chooses the side where the number of floors holds still. It reads geometry the way other animals read weather.

When they are young, they are almost invisible—small, gray, motionless creatures pressed against a cooling fin. They do not need to move much. The warmth of the fin is enough. Their first food is the moisture that collects there. This is why, in the valley’s earliest morning, small climbers cling to machinery like living scales. They are the valley’s way of tasting itself.

***

**Threadfish and Film-Skimmers**

The waterways are not rivers in the sense of being large or continuous. They are engineered, shallow, bright, and often sluggish. They carry fish not because anyone intended to carry fish, but because the water has become hospitable. The fish arrived, or were brought, or appeared. It is difficult to tell in a place where the rules are not fully settled.

The threadfish are long, flat, and silver, with large lateral lines that tremble under their skin. They live near the bottom of constructed channels, darting where the current is strongest and gathering in pools when condensation thickens. Their scales are so reflective that they often disappear against the bright underside of a moving film of water. They are almost made of the water they swim in.

They are not only fish of open basins. Some threadfish have adapted to the tiny trickles running along sloped tiles. These can be called film-skimmers. They are smaller, with flattened bellies and mouths modified into suction-like disks. They live in water no thicker than a mirror. A rain pool on a slanted surface can hold a colony of them. In the dry season, they seek moisture in cracks, pipe threads, and the condensation trails left on gantry beams. They move slowly, but they can follow wetness through the valley like travelers following a road no one built.

There are also pipe fish, blind or near-blind, living in dark conduits where water is slow and the walls smell of oil and old moss. Their bodies are longer, paler, almost translucent. They navigate by pressure and taste. A vibration from a distant pump tells them where food will arrive. They gather where pipes change angle because mineral dust collects there. They are not beautiful in any obvious way; they are more like the valley’s circulatory system made visible.

Their reproduction is strange. In the shallower basins, where rain appears in short bursts over one quarter of the valley, threadfish spawn by timing their release to the wet line. They sense the pressure change before the rain arrives. This is one of the valley’s gentle impossibilities: the fish know before the water does. Eggs adhere to ceramic seams in rows so regular they seem machine-deposited. They hatch when a small vibration passes through the floor, often from a pump starting for no apparent reason.

The pool reflection is not safe for them. A pool sometimes mirrors a different valley—a cooler one, with animals drinking that are not present. Fish will swim into the reflection and vanish. They may reappear in another basin, wet but confused, or they may not reappear at all. Some threadfish are born with faint markings like foreign waterlines, as if some ancestor spent too long looking into a reflection.

They are food, but quietly so. Birds take them. Insects eat their eggs. Slow water creatures scrape detritus from them. There is no dramatic hunting. A fish does not leap from one place to another in terror; it moves because it is hungry, because the current shifted, because the light changed. Death here is often as subtle as evaporation.

***

**Cool-Finned Birds**

The birds live above the herds and below the highest towers. They are small, angular, and pale, with stiff feathers that catch the glare and scatter it. Some of them have a metallic sheen, not oily, but ceramic. Their wings are narrow. Their bodies are compact. Their bills are short and slightly hooked, suited to insects, lichen seeds, condensation films, and the tiny crustaceans that live in shallow water.

They nest in ventilators, between cooling fins, inside abandoned housings, beneath arches, and along cable forests. Their nests are not soft. They are built of resin threads, wire fragments, feather-like strips, moss, and fragments of tile that have flaked from roofs. The birds carry these fragments with a patience that resembles architecture itself. A nest may look like the corner of a broken machine. Some birds line their nests with the shed skins of insects that grow in vents, or with fibers pulled from lichen, which smells faintly of coin and wet stone.

They are not loud. Their calls are soft clicks, low whistles, or the faintest chittering, as though they were afraid the valley’s bright air might punish a full song. At night, when the valley becomes a web of pale light, they move from one lamp to another without calling. Their eyes are large and dark-adapted, but they do not rely only on darkness. They read thresholds by the way light changes across surfaces.

The cool-finned birds are among the creatures most sensitive to the valley’s unreality.

They fly into arches and emerge one tile later on the wrong side. They do not seem to notice at first. They circle once, then continue as if the arch were merely a trick of distance. Their eggs sometimes hatch before the nest is fully formed. This does not happen to all birds, only to those nesting in places where geometry is uncertain. In a corridor where sound arrives before the wind, they will move to another corridor without warning, as if they had heard the danger before it happened. In a place where a surface has the quality of being both new and old, they may choose that place over a safer one because the smell of the old surface feeds them in a way the new one cannot.

Some birds are loop-swifts, so called because they seem to fly in small incomplete circles, returning to the same vent every few minutes, but never arriving in the same condition. One pass they are full of dust. The next pass they are clean. The next pass they carry a feather that does not belong to them, or a fragment of tile, or a thread that was not there before. They are messengers, though no one knows who sends the message.

Their droppings are important. They are rich with mineral salts and undigested lichen. They darken the surfaces beneath nesting ledges, creating small patches where moss can grow. Where many birds have nested, the valley becomes slightly greener. This is how the sky feeds the ground.

***

**Dust-Wings and Lichen-Things**

The smallest inhabitants are the most numerous and the hardest to separate from the place.

There are insects with translucent wings that reflect the bright air and cast no visible shadow. They cluster around vents and wet seams. They carry dust from lichen to lichen, and in doing so they pollinate the strange silver shrubs and the moss-animal colonies that are not fully moss. Some of these insects have bodies that resemble fragments of architecture: hinge-legged, resin-backed, with segmented carapaces like ceramic tiles and antennae like fine copper wire. When they sit still, they look like broken parts. This is not a metaphor. They may be a kind of mimicry, or they may be the valley inventing life out of its own waste.

There are tile-mites that live in gutters, feeding on fresh fracture edges. They eat the bright, still-unfinished dust of broken ceramic, and their bodies become whitened, powdery, chalky. They are often mistaken for mold. When they move, the gutters seem to shimmer. Their activity helps new fractures age into habitable surfaces.

There are resin wasps—or things like resin wasps—that carry droplets from condensation pools to the joints they choose to build. They do not sting. Their abdomen is long and narrow, and when they touch a surface, they leave behind a thin film that hardens into a protective layer. Some of the valley’s smallest shelters are made this way. A joint sealed by resin can become a home for insects, then for lichen, then for a young bird.

There are dust-carrying flies with iridescent eyes, feeding on moisture and mineral residue. There are wool-creatures that may be mites, may be insect larvae, may be a form of lichen, and will never be fully classified. There are arch beetles with backs like painted steel, crawling slowly up the underside of a beam as though they have been waiting there since the beam was poured. They may have been.

These creatures do not need open ground. They need damp, seam, shadow, warmth. They are the connective tissue of the valley. Their wings carry the dust that colors the birds’ nests. Their larvae break down fallen tile into powder that plants can use. Their bodies become tiny meals for fish and lizards and the pale, low-slung things that wait in dark corridors.

When wind blows through a certain corridor, the sound arrives before the gust. Dust-wings rise in the corridor before the wind can be felt. They behave as if they are responding not to the air, but to the air’s intention.

***

**The Moss-Animal**

The vegetation of the valley is not always separate from the animals.

There are moss patches that move. Not quickly, not in any way that would be obvious to someone watching from a walkway, but over days a moss may advance, retreat, split, or rejoin. It does this with tiny root-like threads that search out cold and moisture. Some of these mosses are not mosses at all. They are colonies of soft-bodied organisms that resemble the valley’s own construction material. They are pale green, chalky blue, rust-orange, or silvery, depending on the metals they consume. They grow into cracks and then pull the cracks deeper. They are patient architects.

There are tufts of grass that grow from drilled holes. These tufts are sparse, but they have strong fibers and wide root systems. Their roots do not merely penetrate composite tiles; they seem to negotiate with them. A root may follow a seam for yards before choosing a joint to open. In the gaps between plates, the plants form tiny ecosystems. A single tuft can host dust-wings, mites, fish larvae, lichen, and small amphibious things that live half in water and half in condensation.

The silver shrubs are the most animal-like of the plants. They grow near vents, feeding on warmth and mineral residue. Their stiff leaves fold at night and open during bright hours, but not simply in response to light. They seem to listen. If a turbine changes its cycle, some shrubs tilt toward the vibration. If the central mechanism pulses, their leaves tremble. They are not sentient, perhaps, but they are responsive in a way that blurs the boundary between plant and instrument.

Where shrubs are young, they look like bundles of wire. Where they are old, they look like tufts of wool or small lichen-covered stones. Wind lifts their fronds, and for a moment one cannot tell whether they are leaves or the wings of resting insects. Some are both. The valley has not yet decided.

The moss-animal is not a single creature. It is a process. It is the slow accumulation of tiny lives that begin in a joint and end by making the joint livable. It is the valley’s way of covering its own scars without hiding them.

***

**The Quiet People**

There may be people. There may only be the habits of people, which in a made landscape is almost the same thing.

If they are people, they are not rulers. They are few. They live in the upper tiers and in sheltered basins where the machinery is dense enough to warm the cold. Their bodies are thin, their movements slow, their breath visible only at the highest edges. Their skin often carries a pale, mineral sheen from lichen washes and water drawn through metal. Their eyes are wide and protected by tinted glass or tight-fitting lenses, because the glare can be severe even at night when the lamps are active.

They do not live in tall towers as a spectacle. The towers are too bright, too cold, too exposed. They live in middle layers, where roofs sit above roofs and walkways connect one structure to another. Their homes are not obvious. A room may be no larger than a storage closet, entered through a pipe junction or behind a panel that clicks into place. A family may live in the warmth of a condenser, sleeping on mats made of resin and woven metal. A work crew may occupy an abandoned hall where the looms no longer run, using the empty machinery as a windbreak.

They eat what the valley provides: lichen, moss, fish, dried dust-cakes, mineral salts, the seeds of silver shrubs, the tender shoots of tuft-grass, sometimes the eggs of birds. They do not hunt the pale herds. The herds are too important to them, not merely as animals but as indicators. If the herds move, rain is coming. If the herds lie down, the central mechanism is turning. If the herds approach a new tile field, it is safe enough. The people read the herds the way sailors read weather.

They speak little. The high air carries sound strangely, and noise is a form of waste here. Their language may be clicks, signs, short breaths, and the slow shifting of lamps. A person can signal by touching an insulator with a metal rod, producing a ring that carries through a whole section of decking. This is not communication in any casual sense; it is infrastructure. The valley speaks through them as much as they speak through it.

Their work is maintenance, but not in the manner of a crew trying to restore a machine to its original function. They maintain it in the manner of a gardener maintaining an ecosystem. They tighten cables not because the cables demand to be tightened, but because loose cables create dangerous angles. They wipe condensation from certain pipes because too much wetness invites moss into places that are not ready for moss. They collect broken tile and sort it by fracture color. They do not throw it away. It is used for walls, for nest ledges, for drainage channels, for small gardens. They know that what is broken can still hold water.

They are strange to the animals but not frightening. The herds will let a person pass close, but only if the person moves with the click-rhythm of the deck. The birds tolerate their roofs and walkways, though they may fly into another section if a person remains too still. The climbers avoid the quiet people by moving to surfaces the people cannot reach. The fish are unbothered unless a person disturbs the wet line.

The people have a kind of reverence, but not religious reverence. It is closer to the respect one has for a house that has learned to live without them. They do not command the valley. They ask it, by leaving channels clear, by not blocking vents, by not scraping moss from a place until it has become too thick. They mark thresholds. They wipe lamps. They listen for the sub-bass. They know that the central mechanism may not be listening, but it should still be respected.

Some of the people have begun to look less like people and more like the valley. Their hair is pale and stiff, threaded with lichen. Their clothing is built from materials so worn they resemble composite surfaces. Their skin has the chalky glow of wet ceramic. This may be adaptation. This may be choice. It may be only that the bright light makes everyone in the valley look a little repaired.

***

**The Machine-Life**

The valley’s machines are not alive in the ordinary sense, and yet it is difficult to keep them outside the census of inhabitants.

There are kilns that print tiles. They move so slowly that their output looks like natural accumulation. Layer by layer, they deposit material. They exhale steam in a rhythm so soft that it resembles breathing. They are warm in their cores and cold at their edges. They smell of oil, hot resin, and wet stone. Small animals live in their shadows. Birds nest in the cooling fins above them. Lichen grows on their bases. Dust-wings crawl across their surfaces and feed on the mineral flakes that fall.

The kilns may be the closest thing the valley has to reproduction, if one is willing to call reproduction the production of new ground.

They are not sentient. Or perhaps sentience is the wrong word. They have behavior. They pause when humidity rises. They cool at dawn. They resume at the pressure of the central mechanism. They have cycles that overlap with the cycles of the herds and the fish. They have preferences for temperature and mineral content. A tile laid at the wrong time is pale, brittle, and soon rejected by the moss. A tile laid at the right time becomes part of the ecosystem within days.

There are pumps that breathe moisture from the air. Their pistons lift and fall with long, patient cycles. They draw in fog at night and release it as droplets through narrow nozzles. Their sounds are almost imperceptible: the tick of a cooling pipe, the hiss of a condenser, the creak of a gasket. But they are alive enough to be feared and respected. Animals cluster near them because they produce water without rain. People gather near them because they produce warmth without flame. Their pipes become walls, shelters, migration corridors, and nursery ledges.

There are turbines so gradual in their motion that their blades seem fixed until light changes across them. They are not silent. They have a hum that enters the body through the teeth and shoulders. Some animals seem to tune themselves to this hum. Fish in the channels near a turbine school at odd angles, as though held in place by pressure. Birds roosting above one sometimes tremble in their sleep. The turbine is not a predator, not a parent, not a god. It is a rhythm with teeth, and the valley has learned to live around it.

There are conveyors that move single heavy blocks over long distances. They are migration routes for small insects and lichen spores. Dust-wings ride them like travelers crossing a plain. Some creatures have adapted to the intervals of the conveyor so thoroughly that they move only when it moves, and when it stops, they wait with a patience that looks like devotion.

The cable forests are not a single organism, but they behave as one. Their strands vibrate together when wind changes. Insulators collect dew. Lamps mark thresholds. Cables tighten and loosen with humidity. At night they form a web of pale light that can be followed for miles. The inhabitants do not need to understand them. They need only know which cables are warm, which are sharp, which are safe, which are false.

The deepest machine is the one that moves water uphill.

No one describes it directly. No one sees it whole. It is felt through pressure changes. It is known by the fact that rain falls in one quarter and not another. It is known because dry channels suddenly hold water, because dry terraces become shining, because western basins darken while eastern edges stay bright. It is known because the valley behaves as if moisture is being decided, not merely received.

This mechanism may be a pump, a turbine, a reservoir engine, a climate regulator, a forgotten control system, or something that has stopped being any of those and begun to be a resident. It has no face. It has no herd. It has no predator. But it has a cycle, and the cycle is slow enough to resemble a heartbeat, and heartbeat-like cycles are the first sign of something that might become alive.

If the valley has a mind, it is not in the towers. It is in the way water is moved, pressure is changed, and rain is apportioned.

***

**The Predators Without Roar**

There are no obvious predators in sight, but the valley is not a pacified fantasy. Predation exists here, though it is restrained. It is not dramatic. It does not disturb the light.

The low-slung hunters live in the blue corridors where coolness pools and the walls look wet even when dry. They are long, thin, and pale, with bodies that flatten against ceramic surfaces. Their eyes are horizontal slits that reflect light in strange ways. They do not run. They wait. They eat fish, insects, young birds, and the slow climbers if they are unlucky enough to descend into a passage where a hunter has become part of the wall. Their coats carry mineral residue so that they resemble the valley’s own weathered material. They are silent, but not because they are gentle. They are silent because they are efficient.

There are heron-like creatures that stand in shallow channels with one leg raised, waiting. They are tall, angular, and almost colorless. Their beaks are flat and sharp, designed not for the soft flesh of water but for scraping the surfaces where food clings. They eat fish, crustaceans, and the pale larvae that grow in wet seams. They do not cry. They do not splash. They move like part of the architecture until they are not.

There are birds that take eggs from nests when the parents are drawn away by a sudden shift in pressure. There are insects that overrun lichen patches until the patch dies and is replaced by new tile. There are fish that consume smaller fish in the dark parts of channels, though never loudly enough to hear from above.

The valley’s food web is real but quiet. It is not a battle. It is a system of adjustments.

Death here is rarely messy. A creature dies under an eave, in a pipe, between tiles, or in a pool so shallow that no reflection remains. The body is taken apart by dust-wings, moss, mineral crust, and condensation. It becomes dust, then lichen, then color, then structure. Nothing is buried because nothing is soft enough to bury. The dead do not rot in the way that loam knows. They oxidize, bleach, and dissolve into the palette of the valley.

***

**The Ecology of Breaks**

The inhabitants do not merely tolerate the wrongness. They use it.

Some surfaces are both new and old. The animals know this by smell and temperature. A channel may have sharp edges, but its water smells like a lake that does not exist. Birds will gather at those channels even when no food is obvious. Fish will swim deeper into them than seems necessary. Lichen grows there with unusual vigor, as though the water has been feeding a world in which the lichen once lived.

The wet line is a border, but not always a stable one. Rain may darken a surface before it falls. The Clickers know this and move before the water arrives. The dust-wings rise before the wind is felt. The moss-dark patches appear before the rain, and small creatures use those darkening areas as guides. The quiet people mark the line with small bowls, hoping to catch moisture early. It is not superstition. It is a kind of hydrological faith.

Some pools reflect another valley. The inhabitants treat such pools with caution, but not refusal. The herds may drink at one if the reflection shows no danger. Fish may enter the reflection and vanish. Birds may fly toward it and be confused for a moment, circling until the light repairs the surface. The quiet people sometimes look into these pools and see things that are not there, then step back and note the pressure change. They have learned that the valley can borrow from other valleys, and that borrowing can make water appear in places that have none.

Stones that are not stones sometimes fall upward when no one is directly beneath them, then stop midair and settle as ordinary fragments when observed. The inhabitants have made this into a behavior. The herds walk under such places in careful lines, as though expecting the air to change. The climbers use them as ledges, but only when the stones are still. The birds do not nest under surfaces where stones have been observed falling upward, because they know that observation makes the fall ordinary and ordinary falls can become loud. Sound is rare here, and rare sound can injure.

Some buildings have too many floors from one angle and too few from another. The quiet people avoid entering such buildings unless they need to, because they have learned that the wrong angle can cost a stairway. If the building resolves, they enter. If it does not, they leave a marker and go around. The herds are less troubled by this. They only require a stable path from one side to the other. If the path flickers, they stand until it becomes one direction only.

The bright light seems to repair the world by looking at it. The shadowy corridors are more porous. In those corridors, the animals that live there are not quite like the animals in light. Their bodies are paler, their senses sharper, their movements slower, as though they have to conserve a certain amount of reality. Some of them are not fully visible when looked at directly. Some are visible only as a shift in the damp, a bending of air, a moment when condensation refuses to settle. They are inhabitants too. They are not ghosts, though they may be what the valley imagines when it forgets to make something.

***

**The Communion of Niches**

What is most remarkable is not that so many kinds of life can exist there, but that they have learned to share without needing the old categories.

A pipe joint is a cliff. A roof overhang is a cave. A ventilation shaft is a thermal column. A reservoir edge is a shore. A slow conveyor belt is a migration route. A lamp is a threshold. A cable is a bridge. A fracture in tile is a valley mouth. A pool is a mirror. A pool’s mirror is a door. A door may be a mouth.

The valley is thick enough that everything has a second function, and the second function is often biological.

The Clickers use the ridges because the ridges collect condensation. The ridges collect condensation because they were built to channel water away from something else. The fish live in those channels because the water moves slowly enough. The birds nest near the channels because the insects live there. The insects live there because the moss grows there. The moss grows there because the quiet people wipe only half the pipes, leaving some to become damp. The quiet people leave some pipes damp because the moss tells them when a surface is too dry. The dryness means something has shifted underground, and underground shifts change where the herds will lie down, and the herds know the rhythm of the central mechanism better than anyone.

This is not planning. This is ecology after a machine has been left long enough to become a landscape.

The inhabitants are abundant because no one has had to invent a natural environment for them. The environment already exists, though it is not natural in any comfortable sense. It is cold, bright, clean, sharp, damp, metallic, quiet, and newly formed. It is full of surfaces that refuse to soften. But life is not always looking for softness. Life is often looking for somewhere to hold water.

The animals have found thousands of such places. The birds have found roofs. The fish have found channels. The climbers have found ledges. The insects have found seams. The moss-animal has found all the rest.

***

**The Dreaming of the Valley**

At a certain depth, the valley begins to behave like a mind not because it has a brain, but because it has so many interdependent parts that they cannot be separated from one another.

The machines print tile. The tile holds moss. The moss holds insects. The insects feed birds. The birds fertilize ledges. The ledges support climbers. The climbers feed the quiet people, or the quiet people protect the climbers, or the two do something else no one can quite name. The central mechanism moves water uphill. The water makes fish. The fish feed heron-creatures. The heron-creatures watch the wet line. The wet line guides the herds. The herds stand where the ground expects rain. The ground remembers being water. The water falls. The rain arrives where it has already been predicted.

The valley is not merely a place. It is an argument with time, held in a cold bright room.

It is learning what it is. It is still arranging itself. Some structures remain empty because the valley has not decided what they are for. Some pipes run toward holes that do not exist. Some stairs lead into cloud and stop, though if you wait in the cloud, there is usually a landing. The inhabitants live among these unfinished decisions. They are not confused by them. They have evolved in a world that is incomplete, and incompleteness has become a habitat.

The pale herds stand at the edge of a tile field and wait for a channel that may not be there. The channel may appear later. The fish may swim into a dry basin, because the basin will hold water tomorrow. The birds may nest in a tower that currently has no top, because the top will catch the wind. The quiet people may mark a stairway that ends in fog, because the fog contains the next landing. This is not madness. This is a civilization that has learned to live ahead of the valley’s intentions.

The inhabitants are all, in some way, part of the process of construction. They do not merely live in the built environment. They help build it. Their bodies leave traces. Their waste enriches surfaces. Their paths become channels. Their nests become shelters for other animals. Their dead become color. Their patience makes the valley more stable. The valley, in return, makes their bodies more like itself.

To live there is to be gradually converted into the same material as the place.

This is not a horror. There is nothing violent about it. The conversion is slow, almost administrative. The light is severe but not cruel. The air is cold but not lethal. The machines move under pressure too great for noise, not because they are malevolent, but because silence is the only way to survive the altitude. The inhabitants have not been consumed. They have been incorporated. They are the valley’s way of becoming biological, and the valley is becoming geology, and the geology is beginning to dream.

If one must name the inhabitants, one could name them by the sounds they make:

The Clickers.

The Threadfish.

The Cool-Finned.

The Dust-Wings.

The Ledge-Climbers.

The Moss-Animal.

The Quiet People.

The Kiln-Breathers.

The Pump-Whisperers.

The Upholders.

The Wet-Line.

The Mirror-Fish.

The Arch-Swifts.

The Blue Corridor.

The Central Pulse.

But these names would be incomplete, because the valley does not know where one thing ends and another begins. The herds know the rain before it falls. The birds know the arch before it is complete. The fish know the water before the channel is finished. The people know the machine before its intention becomes visible. The moss knows the stone before the stone becomes new.

The inhabitants are not only living things. They are also habits. They are also weather. They are also the way light behaves on wet resin. They are also the memory of a lake that has never existed nearby. They are the quiet rhythm of hooves on tile, the hiss of condensation, the tick of cooling pipe, the low sub-bass that tells the valley where to breathe.

They are a bright, cold, damp, dense, made world learning to hold itself together.

They are the fauna of construction.

They are the ecology of an unfinished machine.

They are the dreamers who drink from channels that have no source, climb walls that were cut yesterday, nest in arches that may not yet be built, and stand, patient and pale, in light that seems to repair the world simply by looking at it.
