Whatever lives here does not simply occupy the pocket. It is what the pocket is made of.

There are no herds, no flocks, no nesting sites in the way a surface creature would understand them. There is only a continuous, breathing density: surfaces that are also bodies, bodies that are also homes, homes that are also geology. If one were to separate the inhabitants from the place, the place would stop being itself and become only an empty hollow, which is to say nothing at all. Here, life is not content inside a container. Life is the wall, the floor, the light, the dripping, the clicking, the pressure, the smell of wet chalk and salt and old leaf. The pocket is a small bright world, and everything in it is either a body or the residue of a body that has not yet decided whether to be alive or architecture.

If a name is needed for the first and most numerous residents, the name would be something like the clickbacks.

They are pale insects, no longer than a fingernail’s edge, flat against the world as if they had learned, from the moment of birth, that standing upright was a foolishness. Their backs are not truly shells, though they look like broken shell: thin, white, faintly blue-veined, made of mineral dust and chitin and some fungal matter that has grown into them until the animal cannot be told apart from its own carapace. They have no eyes, or no visible eyes. Where eyes should be there is instead a broad, smooth headplate, and from the front of it extend antennae like split roots, curled and tasting. They cannot see light; they sense it the way a stone senses water, by becoming more sticky, more responsive, more likely to gather life upon itself.

Their legs are many, short, and adapted for gripping wet surfaces. Each foot ends in a hook of hardened mineral, and when they move across crystal ledges or shell floors they make the sound that gives them their name. It is a small sound, but not a trivial one. A thousand clickbacks moving along a damp seam of clay produces a continuous clicking, like a room full of tiny machines that are not machines, like the ticking of a clock made of wet bones and glass. The click is not communication in the way a bird’s call is communication. It is more like weather. It tells other animals where the surface is dry, where it is damp, where a pool is forming, where the light has become thick with salt. A sharp tone means something has shifted. A low click means something else has attached. A rhythm means the pocket is moving, even though the pocket does not change.

Clickbacks feed by scraping luminous films from the walls. They do not eat moss so much as they eat the mineral skin the moss leaves behind. They lick the damp white crust that forms where light meets clay. Their mouths are broad and rasping, lined with tiny teeth like sand grains. Inside their bodies, a symbiotic yeast ferments salt and moisture, and this fermentation gives off a faint blue-green glow. A healthy clickback is almost a living lantern. An injured one darkens. A dead one bursts, and its spilled glow feeds the fungal skins of others.

They are never alone, even when alone. A clickback’s back is a garden, a nursery, a ruin. Fungal pelt grows across it like a white fur. Eggs of smaller creatures cling to it. A patch of pale moss has rooted in the groove between its wings. Its own molted exoskeleton may still be attached to it like an old coat. Its abdomen may carry another larva inside. Another clickback may ride upon it. To point to one clickback and call it an individual is already to lie, because every small body in this place is a settlement.

They form slow files along roots and broken stones, carrying things. Grains of sand. Drops of water held in curled mouthparts. Shell chips. Fragments of eggshell. Fragments of their own kind, not always dead. Their carrying is not transport. It is maintenance. By moving grains, they repair the pebble-lined hollows. By pressing fragments into clay, they create the strange structures that look half-built. A row of pebbles along a wall is not a path because someone chose it as a path. It is a path because thousands of clickbacks passed along it, and pressure and dampness and repetition made the line firm. A cairn of shells is not a monument. It is an archive. It is the accumulated intention of many bodies that knew how to stack, how to wedge, how to lay eggs in the highest and driest places. It is also, perhaps, a kind of ruin, if ruin can exist before a civilization has had time to become complete.

There are adult clickbacks, but adulthood in the pocket is not a final shape. It is merely a state of being crowded by other forms. A fully grown clickback is larger only by a millimeter than a young one, and its larger size is quickly used up by the things growing on it. Many remain larval forever. Their bodies stay soft. Their wings remain folded dust. Their carapaces never fully harden because something else has colonized the place where hardness should have formed. The pocket has no room for completion, so life becomes a ladder of stages that never leave their first rung. Larvae are not immature adults. They are the mature form of a place that has chosen to remain unfinished.

Among them move the dust-moths.

The dust-moths are not moths in the way a lamp-lit room would recognize them, but they share a distant kinship: wings of soft scale, curled fern-like antennae, bodies pale as buried cloth. Their wings are damp. They do not fly. Flying would mean exposing themselves to too much movement, too much verticality, too much empty air, and the pocket does not trust empty air. Instead they move in short glides from one wet stone to the next, or crawl with the wings folded close, trailing a dust of mineral scales behind them. This dust catches the chamber’s white-green light and makes their passage look like a slow smear of radiance across the floor.

A dust-moth’s antennae are its most sensitive organs. They are curled at the tips and covered in tiny pores that taste the air’s moisture and vibration. When the chamber is noisy, the antennae twitch. When a droplet is about to form, the moth leans toward the place where sound thickens. Dust-moths are drawn to resonance because resonance warms the water in their bodies. They lay their eggs in the loudest corners, where the constant clicking and creaking create a slight heat, or at least the illusion of heat, or at least a condition in which moisture will condense in the right way. In the quiet places, their eggs will not hatch. In the loudest places, they will.

They feed by pressing mouthparts to the small pools held in shells, to the beads of moisture in crystal cups, to the damp backs of larger animals. They are not parasitic, exactly. They are more like gardeners. As they drink, they spread spores from the vein-fungus onto the surfaces they touch. In return, their scales make those surfaces more receptive to fungal growth. A dust-moth’s leg can plant a future wall. Its wing can scatter a small ecology. When it dies, its body collapses into a soft mound of scale-dust and salt, and from this mound a thread of pale moss may grow, or a crystal may begin its first false tooth.

The dust-moths also groom. Clickbacks clean the scale dust from their antennae and legs in exchange for a powder that the insects use to line their egg-chambers. A line of pebbles may have been arranged first by water, but a pebble can be moved by a moth’s wing, then re-set by a clickback’s claw, then cemented by fungal skin, then claimed by a threadling’s tail, then forgotten. In this way, intention appears without a maker. The maker is the whole chamber, acting through its residents.

Beneath the clickbacks, beside them, inside the mouths of their burrows, live the bead-carapaces.

They are small, round, crustacean-like animals, but the name is not precise. Some resemble isopods. Some resemble barnacles turned inside out. Some resemble mollusk hatchlings fused to the shells of others. Their most striking feature is their eyes: too many eyes, black beads clustered on stalks, packed into ridges of the carapace, embedded in the backs of older animals, arranged in rosettes near the mouth. They do not see images. In this chamber, light is too even for seeing in the ordinary sense; everything is already visible, no shadows to reveal shape. Their eyes have therefore become something stranger. They measure surfaces. They detect curvature, wetness, tension, the shape of a droplet before it forms, the exact moment when a shell holds enough moisture to become a habitat. They also sense sound, not through ears, but because each bead eye is connected by fine nerves to a membrane that vibrates when the pocket clicks. To look into a bead-carapace is to look into a being that sees the world as a pattern of damp geometries.

They move slowly, flattening themselves against stone as though they know that verticality is dangerous. Their legs are broad and fringed, and they use them to scrape fungal skins from the undersides of rocks. Their mandibles are flat and grinding. They are often found in shell cairns, not because they built the cairns deliberately, but because they secrete a chalky adhesive from glands on their backs, and this adhesive helps bind loose shells together. Over many generations, their secretions become part of the mineral crust that holds the pocket’s structures intact. A curved shard of bone propped against a crystal nodule may have been placed by a root, by water, by a clickback carrying it, or by the slow growth of a bead-carapace colony that used the shard as a wall for their secretions. The distinction does not matter. The chamber is made of habits.

Some bead-carapaces are hosts. They settle on the backs of dying insects, not necessarily killing them, and add a new layer of armor. Others attach themselves to shells, then to each other, forming living tiles across a wall. In time, the boundary between the original animal and the added colony becomes unclear. A single “bead-carapace” may be a mosaic of several individuals, their carapaces fused, their nerves interlaced, their molted shells forming a small ruin. When one of them dies, the rest continue moving through it. The dead body becomes floor. The floor becomes shell. The shell becomes nursery.

Deeper in the damp places, where a fingernail-sized pool may sit in the bowl of a broken eggshell, live the threadlings.

They are the softest things in the pocket. They are salamander-like, no longer than a finger, with bodies translucent enough that their folded gills appear as pale white leaves beneath the skin. Their heads are broad and flat, their mouths small, their eyes tiny blind spots covered by thin skin. They cannot stand for long, and they do not try. They lie like threads of water-colored flesh across stones, or curl inside shell hollows, or slide between the legs of insects as though they were roots moving through soil. Their gills are not hidden; they rest along the neck and shoulders like frilly combs, and they taste the air as much as the water.

Threadlings require shallow moisture but cannot be submerged, because there is no river, no pool deep enough for swimming. Their world is a set of almost-puddles: the hollow of a bone, the curve of a pebble, the center of a shell, the cup of a moss leaf. They live in these temporary vessels, and when a vessel dries, they do not migrate far. They press themselves flat into the dampest crevice and wait. They can tolerate near-drying for long periods because their skins have become part of the chamber’s fungal network. A pale thread of mycelium runs through their bodies, not as disease, but as a second skin. It helps them absorb mineral light. It also gives them a faint internal glow.

Threadlings are hunters and gardeners at once. They eat tiny crustaceans, fungal buds, unhatched eggs, and the soft inner linings of molted shells. But they also carry moss. The pale moss that clings to their backs holds water, and by moving through dry patches, they bring dampness to surfaces that would otherwise be sterile. Their feces are rich in salt and spores. Where a threadling has rested, a white film may bloom the next damp pulse. In the loudest corners, they lay eggs in a gel that warms from the chamber’s vibrations. The eggs are translucent, and inside them one can sometimes see the folded gills of the young, already curled before they are born, already adapting to a world without room.

They are the only inhabitants that seem to understand silence. Not silence, exactly; nothing is silent here. But they listen to the chamber the way a surface creature listens to a stream. When a sound collapses, they hide. When a resonance spreads, they gather. They can feel the near-drip before it appears. They lean toward the almost-fall of water, not to drink, but because the tension of the droplet is the closest thing the pocket has to weather.

Above them, through the ceiling of clay and rootlets, enter the root-tendril creatures.

They are not plants, though they look like roots that have learned to be animals. They are not animals, though they have bodies that pulse with mineral fluid. They are pale cords grown through the broken rootlets that reach down from the sparse grass above. Each one is a filament thicker than hair, threaded with tiny white muscles, and covered in nodes that taste the light. They move very slowly, by bending and extending, and they create the chamber’s most ruin-like structures: root fragments woven through mineral strands, curved arches, half-buried beams, and the dark lines crossing the ceiling like veins.

A root-tendril creature feeds by conducting mineral light. The roots from above bring traces of surface water and leaf litter, but down here those substances have already been transformed. The tendril absorbs salt from the damp air, exudes a white powder, and builds small crystalline lattices along its length. These lattices make the tendril glow faintly, and the glow makes it sticky. Moisture clings to its body. Spores settle on it. A thread of water may travel along one of its fibers for hours, then disappear into a pore. If a tendril is severed, the wound does not bleed so much as bloom: a pale fuzz of fungus rises over it within the next pulse, and the broken end begins to grow sideways because verticality is forbidden.

They are responsible for many of the pocket’s unfinished arches. A curved stone leaning into a wall may have been held there by a root-tendril that grew beneath it. A pile of shells may look like a collapsed tower because a tendril once passed through the pile and then died, leaving its body to become mineral support. These are not buildings. They are the fossil habits of slow bodies. They are ruins of a civilization that did not need to call itself a civilization.

The root-tendrils are also hosts. Smaller insects crawl along them. Dust-moths rest in their folds. Bead-carapaces attach their young to the nodes. A single tendril may carry a whole horizontal city: eggs on its underside, moss on its upper side, crystal teeth at the joints, and a faint click when the chamber’s sound presses against it.

If there is a being that comes closest to ruling the pocket, it is not a ruler. It is the vein-fungus.

The vein-fungus is not a single organism in the way a mushroom is a single organism. It is a skin, a net, a nervous system, a light producer, and a memory. It covers the backs of smaller animals, fills every groove, coats the shells, glazes the clay, and runs through crystals as if the mineral world were made of pale threads. In places where it is dense, the walls take on a bluish radiance. It metabolizes the moisture and salt films and in doing so produces the chamber’s strange cold brightness. The light does not reveal the fungus. The fungus makes the light.

But its role goes beyond illumination. The vein-fungus makes surfaces sticky. Where its spores settle, the mineral film becomes tacky, and life attaches there because the dampness holds, because the spores feed the young, because the fungi respond to vibration. The fungus grows toward resonance. It thickens around loud corners. It spreads along the paths of clicking legs. It warms the egg chambers not with heat, but with chemical activity, with a slow metabolism that turns salt into light and sound into growth. It tells insects where not to stand and where to lay eggs. It does not command. It simply becomes easier to obey.

The vein-fungus colonizes death so quickly that death is never merely death. A clickback that stops moving is not left behind. It is wrapped in white filaments, its carapace softened, its shell turned into a fungal shelf, its body into a nest for eggs. A threadling found stiff in a shell pool will have its gills replaced by pale moss-like growth, and from this growth a new crystal may begin. A bead-carapace that dies becomes a living floor for others. The fungus turns corpses into terrain. It turns molts into foundations. It turns eggs into architecture. It is the reason nothing seems wasted and yet nothing can be added.

The plants are minority residents, but they are essential.

The pale mosses are not abundant by surface standards, but inside the pocket they are everywhere because they are so small. They grow in shallow grooves, on wet clay, in the curve of eggshells, on the backs of threadlings, in the furrows of a clickback’s abandoned shell. They resemble forests compressed to a few grains of dust. Their leaves are not true leaves but translucent films that catch condensation. Each moss cushion is a basin, a sponge, a nursery. It holds water no deeper than a fingernail, and this tiny reservoir becomes the world for threadlings, unhatched larvae, and crystal buds.

The fern-like fronds are more reluctant. They curl up before they can open. They look unfinished because they are unfinished. Some have animal bodies inside their stalks, small pale worms that have taken root there. Others are host to fungus that makes them glow and prevents their growth. They are not so much plants as negotiations between root, salt, and spore. Where one stands, a whole micro-landscape forms beneath its curled tip: a pool, a line of grains, a nest of eggs, a crystal just beginning to point. The pocket’s green life is sparse because the green life does not trust completion. It waits. It remains in the condition of almost growing.

Even the crystals belong.

They are not alive, but they are not merely geology either. They are the chamber’s soft bones, its teeth, its lenses. They begin to bloom where moisture and vein-fungus and mineral salt meet, but they never reach the size of mature crystals. They remain in the state of sharp first points, always just about to become stones, always already part of an ecology. An insect clicks against one and feels the chamber’s temperature. A droplet rests on one and magnifies a grub. A dust-moth lands on one and leaves scale. A root-tendril touches it and becomes fused to it. The crystals make the pocket’s sound possible. Without their edges, the legs would not ring. Without their ridges, the eggs would not gather vibration. Without their lenses, the light would not become sticky enough to bind life.

There are also the unhatched.

They are not dead, nor alive in the way one expects. They are eggs, larvae, and larval things that may never become adults because the chamber has no room for adults. Some exist as curled grubs inside shells. Others as white threads of muscle waiting for a moisture that will not come, and yet another moisture that has already come. They fill crevices. They line tunnels. They press against the underside of pebbles. They are not an unused reserve. They are part of the structure. An unhatched grub can become a floor tile. A cluster of unhatched eggs can become a wall. The pocket does not require every life to express its final form. It only requires that each life occupy a place, and most of these lives occupy their places with great devotion.

The inhabitants have no vertical bodies. Nothing tall survives. A stem that begins to straighten becomes damp, soft, colonized. An insect that lifts its thorax too high is pulled back down by the weight of the things growing on its back. A threadling that tries to stand becomes a nest. The pocket is a world of horizontal compression, and its beings are built for pressure. Their backs are flat. Their legs are hooked. Their bodies curve. Their wings fold close. Their tails lie along the surface. They do not climb toward light; light comes to them, and it comes as a binding. They do not seek open air; they seek the next surface on which they can rest. They do not avoid darkness; darkness is irrelevant. They avoid completion.

They respond to the chamber’s pulses as a single body with thousands of limbs. When one wall beads with condensation, a thousand antennae lift. When a crystal creaks under pressure, a line of clickbacks stops. When a shell holds a new pool, a dust-moth drifts toward it, a bead-carapace flattens over it, and a threadling slides into its shadow. This is not cooperation as moral action. It is ecology made immediate. The place is too small for privacy, too full for isolation, too loud for silence, and too bright for concealment. Every movement ripples through the floor, through the fungal skin, through the crystal lattice, through the sound, until other bodies adjust without ever having seen the first.

Their society, if one dares call it that, is a society of surfaces. A body is a surface. A shell is a surface. A pool is a surface. A molt is a surface. A fungal skin is a surface. The inhabitants live on one another, not through cruelty, but through necessity. To touch is to attach. To attach is to build. To build is to become a place. A “nest” may be a depression lined by dead exoskeletons, held together by chalky secretions, warmed by vibration, and colonized by a fungus that makes the walls glow. A “wall” may be a living carapace of many animals pressed against one another until they become a single barrier. A “tower” may be a mound of molted shells, egg fragments, and root fibers, built by creatures that do not know they are building, or perhaps know it in the way a crystal knows how to grow.

There is no ruler, no chief, no single maker of the pebble rings. There is only the accumulation of tiny actions that are too small to see from outside and too numerous to separate from inside. A line of pebbles appears because every clickback that passes has a habit of pressing its forelegs at a certain angle. The angle is learned from the chamber, not from memory. It is learned because the chamber’s surfaces are sticky, because moisture collects at the edge of the line, because the spores grow there, because the young hatch where the spores feed the adults, and because the adults return to the same damp geometry. Intention emerges not from mind alone but from repetition made inevitable by pressure.

When the chamber goes through one of its near-drips, the inhabitants show something that looks, to a surface mind, like attention. A droplet gathers on a shell. The shell is white, veined with blue, and fuzzy with fungal spores. Beneath the droplet, a pale grub twists in the damp mud without needing to. Around it, a ring of beetle legs clicks against a crystal in a rhythm that is not a rhythm. Behind them, a moss cushion holds a nest of grains no larger than salt. Above them, a root from the surface presses through the ceiling and splits into hair-fine threads that glow faintly from mineral salt. The droplet swells. It catches the light. It makes a tiny lens inside the chamber’s greater lens. Through it, the grub below is magnified. Through it, the crystal becomes a mountain. Through it, the whole pocket appears as one wet eye.

The chamber leans toward the almost-fall.

Clickbacks stop their file. A dust-moth opens its wings, not to fly, but to fan the air around the droplet. A bead-carapace withdraws its stalked eyes and flattens its shell. A threadling tilts into the shallow pool beneath the shell, its folded gills brightening. A root-tendril extends one filament and lets it hover in the air, waiting to catch whatever falls. A fungal skin tightens. A crystal hums. The droplet stretches, brightens, trembles. The sound in the chamber thickens, becomes a low pressurized roar, the roar of a living instrument held breathless.

Then it does not fall.

It returns to the edge. The grub twists. The beetle legs click again. The moss releases a bead of water. The dust-moth folds its wings. The threadling slides back into its almost-pool. The root-tendril bends and goes on. The event has happened and has not happened. It will happen again. The pocket has made its permanence out of suspense.

This is what lives there: not one kind of creature, but a civilization of attachment, a chorus of small bodies that have learned to live as place. The clickbacks are the floor’s rhythm. The dust-moths are its breath. The bead-carapaces are its lenses. The threadlings are its damp heart. The root-tendrils are its beams. The vein-fungus is its skin and nerve. The crystals are its unfinished bones. The unhatched are its future, which is also its present, which is also its wall.

They are all pale, all pressed flat, all covered, all glowing faintly, all listening to sound as if it were wind, all afraid of standing upright, all drawn to the places where light becomes wet. They do not need to grow. They do not need to fly. They do not need to cross a distance, because there is no distance without another body already living inside it. Their lives are not stories of becoming something else. They are stories of becoming more of what they already are, over and over, in the same bright condition, until the boundary between animal, fungus, mineral, water, and chamber disappears.

To call them a habitat is too weak. To call them a population is too separate. They are a pocket-world’s own living flesh, a dense and luminous body of tiny beings that have agreed, without agreement, to hold the place together. They are the reason the structures look unfinished: because every structure is a body in the process of being forgotten and remembered. They are the reason the walls are only beginning: because they have never been allowed to finish. They are the reason the light feels older than daylight: because it is made of so many small lives that no single life can be called its source.

Whoever lives there?

All of them. And yet “all of them” is still too generous, because none of them is quite one thing, and every one of them is already many. The inhabitants are the clickback carrying the egg, the egg feeding the fungus, the fungus shining on the shell, the shell holding the pool, the pool magnifying the droplet, the droplet almost falling, the almost-falling becoming the chamber’s permanent breath.
