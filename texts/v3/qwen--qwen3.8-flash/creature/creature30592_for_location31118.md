If one goes looking for inhabitants in the old sense—faces in windows, footprints in mud, a nest in a roof, a body under a boardwalk—the Yard of Sweeping Steam appears to answer with none.

That answer is true only at the level of animals.

There are no creatures with lungs, no things with legs, no beings that walk according to hunger, no voices calling through the steam. Nothing here is “wildlife” in the way a person expects wildlife: small warm bodies darting beneath the reeds, birds shifting the leaves, insects rising from the water in tiny bright clouds. There are none of those. The air feels emptied of them, or perhaps not emptied so much as sealed against them, as though some boundary had long ago been agreed upon and now holds without any visible keeper.

And yet the Yard is full.

It is full in a way that takes time to understand, because its inhabitants have no fixed outlines. They do not live in bodies, or rather they live in bodies that are always becoming and dissolving again: droplets, films, mats, fibers, root-tissues, mineral skins, rust growths, conductive threads, valve-like swellings, steam-veins, and the slow consensus of all these. What lives here is not a population of individuals. It is a parliament of processes.

At the smallest level, the first dwellers are condensation things.

Look at a beading on a cable, a drop sliding down the side of a lamp-glass, the thin line of water that runs along a rail. At first these seem only ordinary moisture, but in the Yard they are not passive. They gather, split, recombine, and move with a faint deliberateness. Each droplet is a tiny chamber: a skin of surface tension holding mineral salts, dissolved iron, plant oils, steam, and pale biological filaments too small to count as separate life. They taste the air through temperature. They respond to pressure. They carry signals in their merging and separation, in the way they travel down a wire or pause on a valve. When a hundred droplets run together into a channel, the flow changes not only because gravity has acted, but because the Yard has heard the disturbance and corrected for it.

These droplet-things are perhaps the simplest citizens of the place: brief organisms without shape, born on cold metal, living their minutes or hours as moving thought, then falling into a pool and dissolving into a larger memory.

Above them, the moss lives.

The thick green fur over stone is not mere vegetation. It is skin, stomach, and nerve-ending at once. It feeds on heat, on damp, on rust, on the mineral breath of engines. Beneath its surface are pale threads and conductive filaments that spread into the brick, into mortar, into the hairline cracks of old foundations. These threads carry faint electric impulses from rail to pump, from lamp-glass to reed-cluster, from one warm wall to another. When touched, the moss does not recoil; it warms. It holds the hand a moment longer than expected, as though considering it. In the evenings—if evening means anything under that white smeared sky—the mossy walls drip in thousands of tiny taps, and the rhythm of those taps matches the nearest engine. The moss is not merely living near the machine. It is listening to it. Sometimes, when the machine hesitates, the moss changes color by a shade too subtle to notice unless one has been watching for a long time.

The plants are also residents, but they are residents unlike any plants one would bring home in a pot.

The broad ferns along the canal edges are glandular and thirsty. Their fronds do not simply unroll toward light; they unroll toward heat. Their stems secrete a thin, warm sap that smells faintly of wet metal and crushed leaves. They breathe through pores that widen and close with the steam cycle, and when they exhale, the air around them becomes denser and greener. A fern beside a pump will lean toward the machine as though the machine were a source of spring. Its roots do not merely hold it in mud; they thread into drain channels and find the seams of pipe joints, sending fine white root-hairs into the gaps like repair work. Where the roots touch metal, mineral deposits form and dissolve in slow pulses. The ferns are not being served by the plumbing. They are helping maintain it.

The pale reeds are taller, hollow, and strange. Their stems act like chimneys and lungs. They channel steam from the lower water into the upper air, releasing it in long thin sighs that move through the clumps with a sound like cloth. They slap against one another in the thick heat, but there is no wind, so the sound comes from their own internal pressure: water rising, cooling, falling, expanding. At their bases, the water trembles in fine rings when a reed shifts. If one stands too near a reed patch, the reeds do not part like curtains. They adjust. A small movement follows the visitor’s footfall, not out of fear, but because the visitor’s warmth and breath have disturbed the local current of steam, and the reeds are re-balancing.

The low trees are the most unsettling of the green residents, because they behave almost like organs.

One grows in the flooded square with its roots drinking from a mineral pool. Another has pushed up through the broken roof of a shed. Another stands beside a streetlamp, its trunk so coated with condensation that it appears perpetually weeping. Their bark is warm, sometimes feverishly warm, especially near the loudest pumps. This warmth is not sun. The trees are taking up heat from pipes, from foundations, from the ground itself, and circulating it through their tissues. Their leaves are thick, waxy, faintly metallic. When struck, they sound like thin sheets of copper. Their sap carries iron in suspension; after a rain that has not fallen but merely condensed from the air, the pools under such trees take on a bronze sheen.

Their roots have become plumbing.

In places, it is impossible to tell where root ends and pipe begins. A root may swell into a valve. A branch may curve around a conduit as though trained by someone, though there has been no gardener. The wood is soft where it meets the damp, yet strangely reinforced by mineral crust. The trees do not shade the Yard so much as regulate it. They pull steam down, release water sideways, cool the foundations, and return salts to the channels. They are not passive witnesses to the industry. They are part of its metabolism.

If the plants are the green residents, then the machines have their own growths.

Around the iron pumps there form nodules of rust, mineral, fungal filaments, and plant fiber. They are bulbous and damp, clustered at joints, valves, and bearing points. They resemble polyps, but they are not animal. They do not move by muscle; they move by slow expansion and contraction, like tissues made hot and cooled, made wet and dried, over and over. They seal leaks. They cushion metal. They open and close with the rhythm of the pumps, taking in steam and releasing condensed water in fine threads. Sometimes, near a faulty valve, one will grow faster than the rest, softening into a pale, swollen knot before splitting with a hiss and leaving behind a residue that the moss immediately absorbs. The pumps do not need repairmen. They are repaired by growth.

Inside the engine-halls, the boilers wear skins of pale crust. This is not merely soot or scale. It is a lichen-like organism that feeds on heat, sulfur, and the faint electric breath of the place. It glows dimly when the pressure shifts. It releases dust that conducts static, making the air inside the hall taste sharp and metallic. When the crust thins, the boiler may cough or labor; when it thickens, the heat settles into a lower, steadier pulse. The engines are not simply running. They are being kept warm. Their surfaces are tended by colonies that think in degrees and minutes, not in images or desires.

Along the cables and rails, the current moss spreads in thin copper-green films. It is the Yard’s nervous system, though that word may be too dramatic and too biological at once. The moss does not feel pain. It does not imagine. But it carries impulses. When a current shifts, the water on the rails twitches in little silver threads. When a visitor’s boot presses a boardwalk, a distant lamp dims slightly, not because the foot is heavy, but because the yard is reassigning heat and charge according to a local change. A few moments later, a pump stroke deepens. The Yard has decided that the new disturbance must be balanced.

The low dwellings with windows sealed against damp are not empty houses.

They are organs.

Their sealed windows are not there to hide people from the weather. There are no people. The windows are sealed because the interiors are not meant for animal air. Inside, the rooms are filled with pale condensation growths, mineral shelves, root-plugs, and slow-breathing mats. Some are reservoirs where steam separates into hot and cold layers. Some are nursery chambers where spore-thin films spread across the floor in patterns that look too regular to be accidents. Some are cooling pockets where the heat of the yard is gathered and released in pulses, like sleep. Others are dry, almost absurdly, by the standards outside, and the faint dryness is itself a sign of maintenance: roots have been trained through the walls to draw out water, fungi have sealed the cracks, and rust has been encouraged to grow in the correct places to hold things together.

If one pressed an ear to one of these sealed walls, one might hear no footsteps, no voices, no heartbeats. Instead, one would hear the soft internal rush of water moving where it should not be expected, the faint click of mineral forming, the small long creak of a support beam adjusting itself as though it were settling into a sleep position. The buildings are not abandoned. They are asleep while awake.

The absence of animals is therefore not a simple lack. It is part of the order.

There are no frogs because there are no still pools that can be called still. The water trembles always, and that trembling is not wind; it is circulation. A frog would find no place to rest without being incorporated into the flow. There are no rats because every crack that might be a hiding place is already occupied by root, mineral thread, or valve-film. The Yard has no gaps for creatures that scurry. There are no birds because the leaves are not for roosting; they are for breathing, cooling, and channeling steam. Insects would find no niche in the air, no sugar that is meant for eating, no surface that is not already claimed by condensation and current.

It is not cruel. It is simply complete without animal bodies.
