No one lives in Mirestone.

At least, no one in the ordinary sense: not a person standing in a doorway, not a household moving along the stone paths, not a herder checking reed bundles in the morning, not a child crossing a bridge over a warm channel, not a watchman walking the rim when the mist thickens. There are houses, but they are not occupied. There are rooms under low roofs, terraces for drying, basins for holding water, platforms for standing, plazas for gathering, and walls for keeping certain shapes stable, but there is no body in any of them. There is no lamp, no cooking fire, no sleeping mat pressed into the dust, no broken pot swept to one side, no handprint on the lintel. The town is complete without inhabitants, as though it were built around the principle of habitation but not around the actual presence of beings.

Nor do animals live there.

There are no frogs in the wet sedge. There are no birds on the reed roofs or stone rings. There are no fish in the shallow basins, no insects in the steam, no beetles under the moss, no small mammals burrowing beneath the peat. There is no sound of wings, no call from the water’s edge, no rustle of hunting or fleeing. The absence is not sudden, not a ruin left after some catastrophe, not the silence of a place emptied by drought, fire, disease, or war. It is a deeper absence: the silence of a landscape that never required animal life and never permitted it to disturb the final balance.

And yet the place is not dead.

If you ask not “who lives in Mirestone?” but “what lives in Mirestone?”, the answer changes. The basin is alive, but its life is quiet, attached, slow, and communal. It is inhabited by reeds, sedges, rushes, mosses, algal films, bacterial skins, fungal threads, roots, lichen, mineral crusts, and the living patterns of water and stone. Its residents do not walk. They do not hunt, migrate, nest, sing, bite, feed, or flee. They occupy niches, hold edges, receive warmth, condense moisture, and persist in forms that seem already decided.

The most obvious inhabitants are the plants.

Along the water channels, in the saturated mud, on the peat shelves, and in the margins where wet stone meets shallow soil, there are rings of reed, dense bands of sedge, low clumps of rush, cushions of moss, and small shrubs with glossy, flexible leaves. These are not the aggressive, sprawling growth of a wild wetland being grazed, broken, trampled, or continually reopened by animal life. They are more like settled communities. A reed patch occupies its strip of mud and remains that patch. A moss cushion takes its place on stone and keeps its shape. A sedge mat thickens only to the edge of what the water and soil allow. The vegetation does not form a food web in the ordinary sense; it forms a pattern of surfaces, heights, and boundaries.

The reeds are perhaps the most visible residents. They line the channels like pale green colonists, their stems carrying beads of condensation, their blades bending not from hunger or wind alone but from the gentle disturbance of warm air. They do not stand as individuals so much as in households: a cluster here, a strip there, a screen around a pond, a fringe along a drying terrace. Their roots bind the black mud, their fallen stems add fiber to the peat, and their living tips drip single drops into the water. They make the basin’s silence visible. Where the reeds grow, the air is softer. Where they thin, the mineral shine of the stone appears again.

The mosses live in more private places.

They occupy the damp underside of stone ledges, the shaded side of platforms, the crevices where water seeps, the tops of fallen peat, and the bases of posts that have been sealed by humidity. A moss cushion in Mirestone does not behave like a plant trying to conquer the stone; it behaves like a resident who has found a suitable corner. It holds moisture. It keeps the rock cool and dark. It receives tiny drops from condensation and slowly returns dampness to the air. On the fresh young basalt, moss and lichen cling like the first breath of life on a new face. On older surfaces, where the stone has learned its colors, moss becomes a fine green seam along the wet parts, leaving the dry mineral crust shining pale above it.

The small shrubs are more isolated. They grow where the ground holds slightly more soil, where peat has thickened enough to support woody tissue, where water reaches the roots without drowning the crown. They are not trees. They are not a forest. They stand alone or in small thickets, their leaves glossy and darkened by mineral moisture. They mark the limits of the plant community: places where life can build upward, but only modestly, only where warmth and water agree to stay near the surface.

The water ferns and aquatic plants live at the edges of the channels.

They spread slowly along the shallow shelves, taking a place, filling a niche, stopping when the water changes. They are not lush in the sense of a jungle; they are lush in the sense of a perfectly fed wetland. Their fronds float on warm, grey-green surfaces. Their roots drift into sediment. They do not crowd. They do not take over. They live in the spaces allowed by equilibrium, and those spaces are carefully defined by the basin’s own rhythm.

Below and beside the visible plants live less visible but equally real residents: algae and bacteria.

In the shallowest pools, where mineral warmth rises from underground, algal mats spread across the mud. They may be green as copper, brown as tea, or faintly blue-grey where mineral deposits mix with microbial life. They do not move, but they breathe, photosynthesize, decay, and build. They are part of the lake’s skin. They darken the water’s floor. They make the mud smell faintly of warm stone and old water. In some places, they form the first living coat over raw sediment; in others, they become a fine film over stone, feeding the quiet chemistry of the basin.

Bacterial films live where water and peat meet, where oxygen thins and warmth rises. They are invisible except by their effects: the slickness of stone, the smell of the mud, the color of mineral crusts, the slow transformation of wet sediment. In Mirestone, decay does not become rot in the noisy, insect-filled sense. It becomes a quiet settling, a softening of plant fiber, a darkening of peat, a return of minerals to water, and then a re-formation of the same stable surface. Bacteria are residents, but they do not build homes. They live in the surfaces of things.

Fungi thread through the peat.

They are not mushrooms erupting after rain. The rain here is not violent; it is condensation, seepage, mist, and slow dampness. Fungal life in Mirestone is therefore subtle: pale threads beneath the moss, fine networks binding organic matter, faint blooms on old reed roots. They help the soil remain soil. They help the dead become part of the living without disturbing the balance. They do not send up loud fruiting bodies. They do not break the surface. They work beneath the town’s floor, as if the town itself rests on a quiet, living understructure.

Even the mineral crusts seem alive, though they are not.

The stone of the basin wears films of iron, silica, calcium, and microbial precipitate. These crusts color the rocks rust-red, ochre-white, black-glassy, pale grey, or blue-tinged where the water is shallow and warm. They seal channels, smooth rough faces, and coat edges so slowly that the effect appears permanent. If one looks closely, it becomes difficult to say where the mineral deposit ends and the biological film begins. The rocks become skins. The stones are not merely objects placed on the land; they are surfaces that have been living with water and air long enough to acquire color, texture, and moisture as a kind of identity.

So what lives in Mirestone is not a collection of creatures but a community of surfaces.

The living things of the basin are defined less by movement than by location. A reed cluster lives where the mud holds moisture. A moss cushion lives where stone remains damp. An algal mat lives where shallow warm water lets light reach the sediment. A bacterial film lives where organic matter is slowly dissolving. A fungal thread lives where peat is dark and humid. They do not search. They do not choose territories through struggle. They occupy the conditions that allow them to occupy conditions.

This makes the basin’s ecology strange.

It is not a food web, or at least not one that can be seen. There are no animals eating the reeds, no birds eating insects, no fish eating larvae, no foxes hunting at the marsh edge, no insects pollinating flowers. Without animals, there is no grazing pressure, no trampling, no burrowing, no seed dispersal carried by beak or fur, no waste returning nutrients in sudden concentrated bursts. The vegetation is therefore not being shaped by hunger. It is shaped by water level, mineral availability, humidity, light, and the physical limits of each plant’s own growth.

The plants reproduce, but only locally. Seeds fall near parent stalks. Runners expand to fill gaps and stop when the gap closes. Spores land on wet stone and form films that persist without spreading beyond what the balance permits. There is no migration of life through the basin in the ordinary sense. No animal carries pollen. No bird drops seed from somewhere else. No fish moves nutrients from one water to another. Life continues, but only by maintaining the existing form.

That is why the place feels both young and settled.

Young, because the stone has not yet been softened by deep time. Settled, because the biological community has already decided not to grow wild. The basin does not advance from bare rock to forest. It does not become dense. It does not thicken into jungle. It does not fill with life in every direction. The vegetation has reached its final arrangement. The reed beds have their borders. The moss has its moisture line. The algal mats have their shelf. The bacterial films have their oxygen threshold. Everything is alive, but nothing is in a hurry.

Then there is the town.

It is not inhabited, but it behaves as though it is inhabited. The platforms, walls, terraces, channels, roofs, bridges, and drying racks are not living things, yet they are part of what lives in the basin. They have become integrated into the ecological balance. They shelter the water. They redirect it. They raise certain surfaces above the mud. They create dry corners for movement that is not human. They provide edges for reeds, foundations for moss, supports for bacterial crusts, shade for mineral deposits, and clean paths for warm vapor to follow.

If one were to describe Mirestone as a living place, the built environment would be its skeleton.

The stone platforms are not merely constructions. They are surfaces that the basin accepts. The dry-laid basalt foundations, fitted with hand tools and set against the wet peat, become places where mosses, lichens, and mineral films adhere. The reed-thatch roofs, weighted with stones, are not rotting into ruin; the stable humidity preserves them. The wooden posts are not consumed by insects or weakened by sudden storms; the warm, even dampness keeps them flexible. The channels are not silting shut because life does not rush into them uncontrolled; mineral films smooth their edges and water carries just enough sediment to line them. The drying racks stand with no cloth on them, but they still serve: they break wind, catch falling drops, collect condensation, and mark the places where the town’s logic persists.

The town lives as a pattern.

It is a pattern of separated houses, separated pools, separated mounds, separated paths. The separation is not emptiness; it is the town’s method. Each structure keeps its own relationship to water. Each channel has its own route. Each terrace holds its own dryness. Each spring has its own surrounding life. The settlement does not need crowds. It does not need movement through its streets. It has become a kind of architecture that can exist without inhabitants because the water performs the maintenance.

This is perhaps the most important way to answer the question.

Mirestone is inhabited by equilibrium.

The equilibrium has no face, no voice, no body, no mind. But it behaves like a resident. It decides what grows and where. It decides which stones remain wet and which remain shining. It decides how the reeds bend and how far the moss spreads. It decides whether a path stays dry, whether a channel remains clear, whether a house remains standing. The town is not maintained by hands, but by the same order that keeps the lake from surging, the air from cooling, the stone from collapsing, and the vegetation from filling every open space.

What lives there, then, is a stillness with structure.

A stillness that has roofs.

A wetness that has streets.

A plant society that has no predators.

A microbial city beneath the mud.

A mineral skin on the stone.

A human-shaped settlement without humans.

The houses are not abandoned in the way a burned village is abandoned, because no disaster took the people away. They are not empty because someone fled. They are empty because the place does not need them to be occupied. They are empty in the way a stone channel is empty before water enters it, except here the water never fully leaves. They are empty in the way a terrace is empty before a body stands upon it, except here the wind and mist stand in the body’s place. The houses are niches in a larger balance, and the balance does not require persons.

If there were once hands, those hands have vanished so completely that their absence has become architectural.

No footprints remain on the wet paths. No fingerprints remain on the stone rims. No sleeping mat remains on any raised floor. No fire scar remains in any hearth. No broken tool lies forgotten. No pot holds water for a single mouth. The town is not a place where people recently left; it is a place where the idea of people has been absorbed into the landscape until it no longer needs them. It is as though habitation became a principle, and then the principle continued without beings.

What lives in Mirestone, therefore, can be described in layers.

At the smallest layer, life is bacterial and algal: films, mats, microscopic communities processing warm water, minerals, dead plant matter, and light. They are the basin’s quiet metabolism.

At the next layer, life is fungal and root-bound: threads in the peat, roots in the mud, hyphae in the moss cushions, organic networks holding the ground together. They are the basin’s binding tissue.

At the larger layer, life is vegetal: reeds, sedges, rushes, mosses, shrubs, water ferns, lichens, and sparse trees. They form the visible body of the marsh, its green outlines, its soft edges, its breathing surfaces.

At the layer beyond life, there is architecture: stone platforms, reed roofs, channels, walls, bridges, drying racks, and paths. These are not alive, but they participate in the basin’s living order. They create conditions for life. They shelter water. They direct steam. They preserve moisture. They become substrates for moss, algae, mineral crust, and bacterial film. They are inhabited by their usefulness.

At the final layer, there is absence: the absence of animals, the absence of people, the absence of noise, the absence of change. This absence is not mere nothing. It is a presence in the landscape. It keeps the reeds from being eaten. It keeps the moss from being trampled. It keeps the channels from being disturbed. It keeps the roofs from rotting into chaos. It keeps the whole basin in its strange, completed form.

If one were to name the inhabitants of Mirestone, the names would not sound like names.

They would sound like locations:

The reed of the east channel.  
The moss cushion on the north lintel.  
The algal film beneath the steam vent.  
The black sedge ring around the spring basin.  
The willow-like shrub on the high terrace.  
The lichen stain along the fractured basalt.  
The root mat under the drying rack.  
The bacterial skin over the warm mud.  
The mineral crust sealing the old channel.  
The empty house facing the pool.  
The stone platform above the green-black basin.  
The bridge where condensation drips into the water.  
The path that stays dry because no one walks it.

Or the names would sound like functions:

The condensation-reed.  
The peat-holding root.  
The stone-sealing biofilm.  
The channel-lining crust.  
The humidity-preserved thatch.  
The wind-tested post.  
The water-guided terrace.  
The light-balanced pool.  
The quiet, maintained settlement.

None of these would be individual creatures. They would be aspects of a system.

This is the deepest answer:

No animal or person lives in Mirestone. What lives is a complete wetland order without animals. It is a town-sized ecology of plants, microbes, fungi, minerals, water, and built forms, all held in a balance that does not require disturbance. Life there is not active in the sense of movement and hunger; it is active in the sense of persistence, adhesion, precipitation, photosynthesis, condensation, and slow replacement. The basin is alive, but it is not inhabited by anything with a voice.

So Mirestone is a home to no one.

It is a home to surfaces, thresholds, patterns, and equilibria.

It is a town whose streets are wet but not trodden.

A settlement whose houses stand but are not occupied.

A marsh whose reeds grow but are not eaten.

A lake whose water breathes but does not surge.

A stone plateau that wears life like a second skin but does not know it.

And if, in all that stillness, something can be said to be alive, it is the arrangement itself: the water rising, the steam curling, the reed bending, the drop falling, the moss holding, the mineral sealing, the roof staying dry, the path staying clear, the town continuing to exist as though someone had built it once and then left it to become part of the land.
