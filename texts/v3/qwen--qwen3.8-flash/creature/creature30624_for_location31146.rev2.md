## Inhabitants of the Weeping Caldera Cities

What lives there is not one thing. It is a nested population, a slow ecosystem of water, heat, stone, film, breath, and machine. The plateau has no forests in the green sense, no fields, no root systems, no canopy, but it is no less inhabited. The “vegetation” of this place is chemical. Its animals are mineral-adapted. Its people—if people can still be called that without alteration—are practical, quiet, and fused so deeply with the city’s wet industrial rhythms that they seem less like settlers on a landscape than organs inside a body.

The inhabitants can be sorted only by function, not by neat species, because everything here shares the same water cycle, the same heat, the same silence.

---

### The Tenders of the Drip: human inhabitants

There are human communities on the plateau, or near enough to human to be called so by other humans. They are not loud, not numerous, not sprawling, and not easily mistaken for pioneers conquering a dead land. They belong to the land the way salt belongs to a crust: slowly, thoroughly, and with a kind of patience that looks like surrender.

They are tall, lean, broad-chested people, with the stillness of those whose bodies have learned not to waste heat. Many live at elevations where the upper air is thin and the ground heat is close, so their lungs are large, their ribs deep, their breathing slow and deliberate. Their skin is pale in some basins, smoked or bronze-grey in others, depending on how much sunlight reaches their terraces and how much time they spend beside glowing steam fields. At noon, many of them appear almost ashen; at dawn, they look like living mineral dust touched by amber light. Their faces are often hard to read because the eyes are narrowed against glare, the mouths covered by breath-masks, and the skin marked by salt lines, fine cuts, and pale scarring from handling hot stone.

They do not sweat as lowlanders do. That would be a waste of water. Their bodies conserve moisture, excrete mineral salts in faint yellowish crusts, and rely on external evaporation for cooling. They are almost always damp—not sweaty, but rinsed, beaded, condensation-stained. Their skin feels warm, not cool, unless they retreat into subterranean chambers. Their hair is worn short beneath masks, or braided tight with strips of mineral-cured fiber. They have a habit of touching surfaces constantly: walls, railings, pipe joints, basin rims, the broad backs of animals, the edges of collection sheets. This is not nervousness. It is reading. A touch tells them whether a pipe is carrying water, steam, brine, or slurry. A hand on a wall tells them whether a building is sealed, breathing, overheating, or beginning to leak.

Their clothing is pale, waterproof, and stained. Outer layers are made from the hides of the six-limbed grazers or from dense, woven sheets grown from fungal-like fibers. These hides are cured in mineral baths until they become flexible but water-resistant. Underneath, they wear linings of porous sound-damp matting, grown in shallow channels and trimmed to fit. Their boots are soled with pumice dust, compressed fiber, and fragments of obsidian glass, roughened to prevent slipping on wet stone. Their gloves are thick where they touch hot surfaces, thin where they handle tools, filters, eggs, larvae, or mat-threads. Many wear broad-brimmed hoods or angled shoulder plates to shed mist and direct it away from the neck. Their belts carry glass hooks, ceramic vials, filtration pads, scraping knives, and small signal tokens made of polished iron or dark crystal.

When vent cycles rise, they put on masks. These are not decorative. The mask is a “breath-shell,” a porous ceramic facepiece lined with cultivated fungal wool that filters sulfur gases, metallic dust, and aerosolized microbes. During heavy steam pulses, they wear smoked glass over their eyes, because the mist can carry hot mineral particles that turn the air into a stinging, glittering haze. Children learn to fit their own masks before they learn to read; adolescents receive their first personal shell after their first solo maintenance walk across a cooling terrace. To go through a vent cycle without a mask is not bravery; it is foolishness. It means breathing too much of the plateau’s breath.

They live in compact hubs on elevated terraces, sunk into cool ground where possible, and raised on pilings where the land weeps too freely. Their dwellings are not individual houses with yards, but nested cells: storage niches, sleeping chambers, collection alcoves, drying lofts, repair bays, bathing platforms, and communal pump halls. Every building has channels. The roofs are broad and angled, designed to catch mist and direct it inward. In the morning, the upper terraces are white with condensation. In the afternoon, the stone beneath is warm and damp. At night, the lower city smells of wet calcium, rust, salt, and faint chemical sweetness.

Their daily life is governed by vent cycles, wind shifts, water pressure, and the slow movements of animals. They wake before full sunrise, when the temperature dips and condensation thickens. Their first task is listening: to pipes, reservoirs, collection sheets, and the ground itself. A pump that has begun to “run hot” may be heard before it is seen. A mat bed that has dried too fast will change color. A basin where brine is about to erupt may give off a sharper mineral scent. They move through the city slowly, carefully, speaking little. Rushing is dangerous. The stone is slick, the light is bright, and the machines have learned silence over centuries.

Human social organization is not based on wealth in the ordinary sense. Water, heat, and chemical stability are wealth. A family may be respected not for owning gold, but for maintaining a clean filter channel, for knowing how to feed a mat bed without poisoning it, or for being able to predict which basin will swell during the next cycle. The towns are separated by long distances, so each hub develops its own character. The city in the wide caldera basin is broad, industrial, and layered with reservoirs. The settlement climbing the salt ridge is narrow, terraced, and filled with crystal harvesters. The town resting in the brine-carved canyon is built around pipes, cooling towers, and old refining yards. They trade, intermarry, argue, and share migration calendars, but they do not see each other often. Distance is real here, even when a town looks close through the shimmering haze.

Among the human settlements, the important roles are clear:

- **Mistwrights** maintain roofs, collection sheets, capillary channels, and condensate systems.  
- **Pumpwrights** tend the sealed hydraulic machines that move water, steam, and brine without loud clatter.  
- **Filter-tenders** care for the porous mats that clean water and break chemical impurities.  
- **Mat-gardeners** cultivate the silver, white, yellow, and orange chemically active organisms that function like agriculture.  
- **Herd-tenders** follow the six-limbed grazers and keep them away from toxic pools.  
- **Crystal-harvesters** prune, collect, and read mineral growth on basin rims and abandoned reservoirs.  
- **Listeners** walk the pipe galleries and thermal baths, detecting faults in the city’s body before they become failures.  
- **Breathwrights** make masks, replace filter wool, and seal the tiny cracks where vent gases can leak indoors.  

They speak softly. Some of this is practical: the cities are built to be quiet, and shouting in stone-and-water corridors becomes painful, bouncing between wet walls and metallic pipes. Some of it is cultural: a loud voice feels like waste, like splashing water where it is not needed. Their speech is low, vowel-rich, and often accompanied by gesture. Because masks hide the mouth and much of the face, a hand-language exists in parallel. Fingers tap the wrist, brush the chin, close against the palm, or make small circular motions indicating water direction, pressure, heat, or animal movement. At distance, they signal with obsidian mirror flashes, slow hand arcs, or the soft striking of ceramic chimes hung from tower edges.

Their food is not pleasant to outsider imaginations, but it is deeply familiar to them. There are no grains, no leaves, no fruits, no orchards. Bread, if one can call it that, is made from dense bacterial-fungal mats, fermented in warm ceramic vessels and baked in stone ovens or steamed under mineral lids. It tastes chalky, sour, slightly sweet, and warm. Protein comes from glassback beetles, ribbon-swimming pool animals, grazer flesh, beetle larvae, scavenger eggs, and cultivated microbial pastes. Fats are rendered from animals that live near the thermal pools. Salt is everywhere, so much salt that meals must be carefully balanced or the body dries out. Drinks are condensed, filtered, sometimes flavored with sulfur-sweet mineral syrups or fermented into a thin, sharp broth that burns faintly on the tongue. Children drink their first mineral water from a family reservoir after naming. A name is given not at birth, but after the child has been kept alive through the first vent cycle.

Their religion—if it is religion—is ecological. They do not worship the caldera as a person. They do not pray to the sun, because the sun is harsh and distant, or to the wind, because the wind is fickle. They honor the breathing basin, the dripping stone, the living pipe. The dead are placed in crystalline shelves or evaporator niches, where their bodies slowly become part of the mineral crust. Over time, calcium, salt, and iron grow over them. Eventually, a dead person may be indistinguishable from the wall they helped build. The Tenders of the Drip do not see this as erasure. They see it as becoming city.

Their greatest taboo is waste of water. Not because they are poor, but because their world is too finely balanced to be careless. A cracked channel can flood a mat bed. A neglected valve can poison a reservoir. An idle mouth can let a town drown in its own condensation. Children are taught to close, collect, listen, and return. They learn early that nothing belongs fully to one person. Water is not owned. It is borrowed from the plateau, held briefly, and sent on.

### The mineral fields: fungal-like organisms as forests and farms

If the people are the visible inhabitants, the true population of the plateau is chemical. The most common life-forms are not animals at all, but vast mats of vegetation-free organisms that fill the place of forests, grasslands, and crops. They are fungal, bacterial, crystalline, and chemical all at once. They have no stems, no petals, no seeds, no roots, no leaves. They are crusts, threads, foams, skins, veils, lattices, wool, and slime.

They appear as:

- **Silver crusts**, thin as glass film, spreading over wet obsidian where iron and sulfur meet.
- **White wool**, damp and fibrous, growing along mist channels and condensation traps.
- **Pale yellow veins**, branching beneath hot stone, pulsing slowly as gases shift.
- **Dark orange threads**, clinging to acidic edges where steam meets mineral residue.
- **Black mineral skins**, dense and rubbery, growing in shaded thermal pools.
- **Blue-grey films**, floating at the surface of still basins, filtering light and breath.

These organisms do not merely live on the plateau. They make it habitable. They stabilize moisture, filter toxins, produce oxygen in localized bursts, break down sulfur compounds, trap metal salts, insulate machines, and feed both animals and humans. Without them, the cities would drown in brine, choke on steam, or become coated in unusable mineral scum. With them, the plateau functions like a vast, slow organism whose organs are buildings, whose blood is water, and whose skin is matting.

Some of these forms are wild. They grow wherever water seeps through stone, wherever heat rises, wherever chemical gradients meet. In the open terrain, they are indistinguishable from mineral deposits at a glance. A field of white wool may look like salt crust until a gardener kneels and separates fiber from mineral with a thin knife. The wild mats are harvested by hand, raked carefully, cut into bands, or scraped into shallow trays and fed through processing vats. The harvested material can be dried, fermented, compressed, grown into insulation, used as filter cloth, pulped into food, or returned to the soil as seed-matter.

Some of these forms are engineered. For centuries, the city’s mat-gardeners have cultivated strains to serve particular functions:

- **Sound mats**: thick, spongy, mineral-filled fibers grown around pistons, turbine chambers, and pipe junctions. They do not just dampen noise; they transform vibration into tiny movements of water and heat. A machine surrounded by sound matting exhales instead of clanking.
- **Filter mats**: pale, layered sheets that trap arsenic, sulfur compounds, excess salt, and fine metal dust. They are grown in sloping channels where dirty water can pass through slowly. Over time, a filter mat darkens in bands, each layer marking what the water contained.
- **Capillary mats**: dense fibers that pull water upward against gravity. These are grown on rooftops and collection walls, where they drink steam from the thin air and carry it down into reservoirs.
- **Insulation mats**: porous, lightweight, mineral-stiffened foams used in building walls, cooling vaults, and underground passageways. They feel warm to the touch but do not conduct heat.
- **Structural mats**: organisms that secrete calcium, silica, or iron-rich cement. Used carefully, they can bind cracked stone, seal pipe joints, or reinforce walkways. Left unchecked, they can seal a door shut within a season.
- **Food mats**: cultivated strains with milder chemistry, grown in controlled vats where mineral content can be regulated. These become the foundation of many human dishes.

The mat-gardeners speak of them not as crops but as “wet families.” A healthy mat bed is judged by smell, texture, water speed, temperature, and color. If it turns too yellow, the bed is acidic. If it blooms in sudden white tufts, the water is too pure, too lacking in mineral. If it grows black along the edges, the basin is suffocating. The gardeners adjust the feed: a trickle of sulfur broth here, a cooling sheet there, a small scraping to open pores, a release of warm water to wake dormant threads. They do not plant. They invite, guide, harvest, and prune.

These organisms are not intelligent in a human sense, but they are responsive. They remember water in their growth rings. They answer heat by changing color. They communicate through chemical gradients and mechanical tension. When a basin begins to rise, the mats at its edge may curl inward before a human sees the change. When a vent is about to open, the silver crust can tighten like skin. The people have learned to watch them. The city listens through its fur.

### The glassback beetles

Among the animals, the glassback beetles are the most numerous and perhaps the most characteristic. They are crustacean-insect-like, with shells polished black like obsidian, legs splaying wide across hot stone, and bodies adapted to heat that would blister an unprepared animal. They range from thumb-sized scuttlers in pipe seams to broad, slow creatures the size of large dogs on the open obsidian flows.

Their shells are not merely protective. They are functional mirrors. In the dazzling light, a glassback’s carapace throws a white flash that can blind a predator or confuse a human walking too fast. Their legs have wide pads of rubbery chitin, allowing them to walk across slippery glass and hot mineral mud without sinking. When the temperature rises too high, they raise their bodies on stretched legs, creating a small air gap beneath. When steam bursts from a fissure, they fold flat against the stone and let the cloud pass over them.

They feed on microscopic life: bacterial films, fungal crusts, algae-like chemical mats, mineral slime, and organic residue left near springs. Some species specialize in eating calcium dust; others grind sulfur deposits with obsidian-edged mouthparts. They are essential to the plateau because they keep microbial overgrowth from clogging surfaces. Without glassbacks, many stone terraces would become slick with unmanaged mats, and small channels would seal themselves shut.

Humans harvest them carefully. Shed shells are collected and polished into tiles, mirror fragments, armor plates, and roof facets. Whole carapaces can be curved into condensation trays that direct water without rusting. Ground shell is used as abrasive for polishing stone. Beetles are also bred in troughs along the reservoir edges, where they consume biofilm and produce edible larvae. The larvae are rich in fat and can be rendered into lamp oil, cooking paste, or waterproof sealant. Glassbacks are not pets; they are too strange and too easily startled. But they are tolerated, encouraged, and protected. A town without them becomes slippery, foul, and eventually choked with mineral growth.

At midday, fields of glassbacks on open obsidian can look like a scattered constellation of black mirrors. They click softly when their leg joints move, a sound like tiny hammers tapping ceramic. In the quiet city, that click is one of the most familiar sounds of life.

### The six-limbed grazers

On the white salt flats, where the ground gleams and the air is warm enough to shimmer, the six-limbed grazers move in slow lines. They are tall, thin, and almost delicate-looking, with long legs, broad flat heads, and wide-set reflective eyes. Their bodies are mostly translucent under a pale outer film; beneath that, one can sometimes see the dark blue or grey threading of fluids. They have no fur, no grass-hides, no ordinary mammalian coat. Their skin is smooth, slightly damp, and covered in a microscopic film of mineralized hair that gives them a faint sheen.

They do not eat plants. They eat chemical films. Their heads are low as they move, tasting the crust with broad, flexible lips that work like filter scoops. A healthy grazer herd can strip a microbial mat down to its mineral base, then move on, leaving the substrate clean enough for new growth to begin. Their digestion is built around sulfur compounds, bacterial proteins, salts, and iron. They can smell water from a distance, not by moisture alone, but by chemical sweetness: which basins are ripe with mat growth, which springs are becoming acidic, which vents are about to open.

Herds are migratory. They follow condensation, wind direction, and the slow blooming of microbial fields. Some herds remain near human settlements year-round; others cross long distances between towns, following animal paths between basins. Human herd-tenders do not own them in the old sense. A herd is considered shared by the towns it passes through, and the tenders move with them, or send messages ahead along the pipe routes. A herd that arrives too early or too late can damage a mat field. A herd that stays too long can strip it bare. Grazers are valuable, but dangerous if misunderstood.

Their meat is pale, mineral-rich, and tender when cooked slowly. Their hides make strong waterproof cloth. Their fat renders into a thick, faintly sweet oil. From certain healthy animals, a fermented broth can be drawn from crop-pouches where microbes have broken down mineral matter into a sour, protein-rich liquid. Children are sometimes given a thinned version of this broth as part of a growth rite. The animals themselves are calm, slow, and deeply herd-oriented. They communicate by low-frequency hums and body positions. When alarmed, they press close together, six legs interlocking, and turn their reflective hides toward the threat so that the herd glows as one pale shape.

When they cross a salt flat at dawn, their bodies are wet with condensation and dusted with salt. Seen from a distance, they look like moving ridges of mineral crystal. Seen close up, their wide eyes reflect the high sun like small disks of black ice. They do not flee easily. They have learned that humans are mostly useful: they clean the reservoir edges, protect certain pools, and know where the toxic steam will rise. Still, a panicked herd is a force to be respected. At speed, their legs strike the ground with a soft hydraulic rhythm, like a slow rain falling on tile.

### The ribbon swimmers of hot pools

In the shallower mineral pools live ribbon-like animals, transparent except for a central blue line of oxygenated fluid running from head to tail. They swim through warm water with a slow, sinuous motion, filtering microscopic life from the current. They are not fish, not eels, not worms, but an independent lineage of pool-dwelling organisms with a soft, segmented body and frilled fins that look like pale membranes. Some are thin as thread; others are broad as cloth. They gather in schools that move like drifting ribbons, turning with the thermal gradient.

They are extremely sensitive to water quality. If the chemical balance of a pool changes, their blue lines dim or brighten. If the water becomes too acidic, they crowd toward the rim. If the water begins to boil from a hidden vent below, their membranes flutter and tighten before any human notices the heat. For this reason, filter-tenders and pumpwrights keep ribbon swimmers in observation basins. They are living gauges. A healthy ribbon pool is clean, oxygenated, and chemically stable. A troubled pool is silent, still, and full of dim ribbons pressed against the bottom.

Humans harvest ribbon animals sparingly. Their bodies contain useful mucus and chitin-like membranes used in seals, filters, and waterproof coatings. The blue oxygen-rich fluid at their center is extracted in small quantities and used in some chemical processes, but overharvesting is forbidden by most basin laws. The animals are more valuable alive, keeping pools clear and warning of danger. Their eggs are laid in cool upper pools, where they resemble pale beads of glass. The young feed on microbial fluff until they develop their central blue line.

At midday, when the sun strikes a pool, a school of ribbon swimmers can look like liquid smoke moving through water. They are beautiful in the way that useful things are beautiful here: not ornamentally, but functionally. They make the pools clearer. They make the city safer. They remind people that water has temperament.

### The glasswing kites

Above the plateau drifts the most graceful of the animal inhabitants: the glasswing kites. They have no feathers, no fur, no leaves, no ordinary skin. Their bodies are narrow and ribbed, their wings stretched membranes of thin, translucent tissue that refract the high sunlight into faint green and amber glows. Their eyes are broad and reflective, adapted to the glare of wet stone and bright salt. They ride thermal columns rising from vents and condensation plumes, and they feed on airborne microbial spores, mineral dust, and tiny drifting particles of organic film.

They are living weather vanes. If kites circle above a basin in tight spirals, a vent is likely to open. If they gather along the leeward edge of a ridge, mist is pooling there. If they abandon a tower, the structure may be overheating, leaking, or becoming chemically hostile. Humans read their flight. Not with certainty, but with familiarity.

Kites nest on abandoned cooling towers, observation spires, and the upper edges of processing yards. They build shallow cups from shed membrane fragments, mineral fibers, and strands of wind-carried mat wool. Young kites hatch blind and pale, then open broad mirrored eyes within days. They learn to fly by riding the thermals above hot pools. They are not domesticated, though some towers are maintained partly because kites nest there. Their presence helps disperse microbial colonies, fertilizes certain rooftop mats, and clears airborne particles. In return, the city gives them heights, wind, and safe ledges.

Their wings are too thin for ordinary sailcloth, but when shed, the membrane can be treated into waterproof sheets, lamp covers, and flexible lenses. Hunters may gather these membranes, but killing the adults is considered unlucky. A town with no kites is a town that has lost something from its upper air: its warning system, its ornament, its proof that the city still breathes with the plateau.

Seen against the glare, they are almost invisible. Only the flicker of light through their ribs reveals them. Sometimes a whole flock passes over a reservoir at dawn, and the water below shivers with a net of reflected wings.

### Reservoir scavengers and pipe-dwellers

At the edges of water basins, long-legged scavengers pick through the biofilm. They are not birds, exactly, though they have the height, patience, and sharp movements of herons. Their bodies are slim, their legs tall, their beaks or mouths adapted to scrape mineral crust and eat dead microbial mats. They feed on discarded biofilm, dead ribbon embryos, shed glassback fragments, and the accumulated grime of reservoir edges. They are tolerated as cleaners. Without them, reservoir rims would become crusty and slow to release water.

They are also useful as indicators. If scavengers crowd near a particular edge, it may mean the water there is rich in a certain microbial growth. If they avoid a pool, the pool may be poisoned, too hot, or chemically wrong. Some scavengers have become partly urbanized, nesting on storage platforms and walking the upper tiers at dusk. Their eggs are sometimes collected, though not in large numbers. Their bones, when found, are used as chimes because they ring clearly when struck.

In the warm damp spaces between industrial seams, small darting creatures live by accident and necessity. They drink condensed water, lay eggs in pipe joints, hide behind mineral crusts, and feed on biofilm, mite-like insects, and shed glassback eggs. Humans call them pipe-dwellers. They are pests when they block filters, but their presence also reveals moisture. If a pipe-gallery is suddenly full of them, there is a leak. If they vanish from an area, the area has become too dry or too hot. Some people encourage them in abandoned sectors because their nests indicate water movement. Others breed them for emergency fat or use their eggs in food scarcity, though this is looked down on except in lean years.

They are quick, pale, and nearly invisible. A flicker in a pipe joint, a soft scrape of tiny legs, a sudden silence where there had been movement. They are the most human-like of the plateau’s smaller inhabitants in their habits: they live in cracks, thrive in industrial warmth, and belong to the city without being admitted to it.

### The basin lilies: soft-bodied crystal growers

The strangest living things are not mobile at all. Great soft-bodied organisms lie half-submerged in abandoned basins, old reservoirs, cooling pits, and cracked processing yards. They look, from a distance, like mineral formations that have forgotten how to be mineral. Their bodies are broad, pale, and layered; some resemble flattened lilies, others shelves of gelatinous crystal. They are not one animal, but colonial organisms, communities of chemically active tissue that convert mineral water into pale crystal lattices.

They grow slowly, over years and seasons. Water passes through them. Sulfur, iron, calcium, and silica enter their tissues. Chemical reactions within their bodies precipitate crystal. They build lattices at their edges: white shelves, blue rims, yellow veins, translucent domes. These lattices are harvested by crystal-harvesters. Some are used for storage. Some for architecture. Some for filtration. Some are ground into mineral powders or processed into heat-resistant glazes. The largest basin lilies can take over abandoned cooling towers entirely, their bodies fusing with cracked metal, their crystal rims spreading across the platforms.

They are not sentient in a way humans can easily test, but they are responsive. Their edges change shape with water chemistry. They swell or contract with heat. If a vent cycle raises the acidity of a pool, a colony may bloom with orange crystals. If water becomes too clean, it may stop growing and begin to dissolve. The city uses them as slow recorders. The patterns of a crystal shelf can show where water came from, what it carried, and how long it stayed. In this sense, a basin lily is a book of the plateau’s recent memory.

Some settlements are built partly from these organisms’ remains. Walkways may be anchored in old crystal beds. Bridges may rise from shelves no one remembers planting. A town may appear to grow out of the stone because, in a sense, it does. The basin lilies are the city’s slowest citizens: patient, mineral, and almost invisible in their activity, yet they shape the built world as surely as human hands.

### The machine ecology

The most important thing to understand about the inhabitants of the Weeping Caldera Cities is that the boundary between biology, industry, and geology is false. The city is not separate from life. It is one kind of reef.

The machines are not merely maintained by humans. They are colonized by mats, sealed by fungal secretions, insulated by grown materials, and stabilized by microbial chemistry. Pipes develop skins of calcium and rust. Turbines turn in chambers lined with fibrous mineral foam. Pumps rest in sealed basins filled with fluid that both moves them and feeds the organisms around them. Metal and fungal life exchange substances: one gives structure, the other gives absorption, filtration, damping, and repair.

The city must be managed as a living thing. If a section becomes too dry, its mats die, filters fail, and pipes corrode. If a section becomes too wet, crystal growth seals channels and machines lose heat control. If the chemical balance changes, the sound mats rot, the food mats sour, the glassbacks vanish, the grazers leave, and the kites fall silent. The Tenders of the Drip do not simply repair the city; they feed it, trim it, cool it, clean it, and listen to it.

This is why the world feels both industrial and biological. The plateau has not fallen into ruin, nor has it been perfected by technology. It has matured into a symbiosis. The city breathes because the machines must not speak too loudly. The machines are quiet because the ecology has grown around them. The ecology survives because the humans regulate the water. The humans survive because the water, stone, and microbial film have learned to hold them.

There is no single master of this system. No king sits at the caldera’s center. No priest controls the steam. If there is a governing intelligence, it is emergent: the sum of human decisions, animal movements, vent pressures, mat metabolisms, crystal growth, pipe maintenance, and seasonal wind cycles. Some of the people call it “the breathing.” Others simply call it “the schedule.” But everyone lives inside it.

### The wider peoplehood

The human towns do not see themselves as isolated. They are linked by pump contracts, water migrations, grazing routes, and shared knowledge of vent timing. A child in the salt-ridge town may learn a word from the caldera-basin towns for a certain kind of steam fog that forms before an eruption of hot brine. A herd-tender may travel for weeks between reservoirs, sleeping in way-stations and observation spires. A filter-tender may cross a canyon to consult an elder who remembers the growth patterns of a mat bed that has not existed for a century.

They are practical, subdued, and deeply observant. They are not dreamers in the soft sense. Their dreams are made of pressure, water level, mineral taste, and silence. When they speak of home, they may mean a particular reservoir, a terrace, a pipe gallery, a bath, a herd path, a tower where kites nest, or a basin edge where the ground weeps in a particular rhythm. Their world has few boundaries that can be crossed quickly, but it has many thresholds: the line where steam becomes droplet, where droplet becomes brine, where brine becomes crystal, where crystal becomes architecture, where architecture becomes geology, where geology becomes memory.

They are not happy in the way a coastal city might be happy, nor sad in the way a ruined city might be sad. They are settled. The place has become ordinary to them. The silence is not oppressive. The brightness is not cruel. The heat is not unwelcome; it is the warmth of the body beneath the skin. They live inside a land that sweats, drips, breathes, and crystallizes, and they have learned to be quiet enough not to disturb it.

### What lives there, in short

If one asks, “Who lives in the Weeping Caldera Cities?” the answer is layered:

- **The human Tenders of the Drip**, quiet, water-wise, mineral-stained people who live by condensation, vent cycles, and maintenance.
- **The mat-gardeners**, who cultivate fungal and microbial organisms that function as forests, fields, filters, insulation, and food.
- **The wild and cultivated chemolithotrophic life**, silver crusts, white wool, orange threads, black skins, and pale veils that stabilize the plateau’s wetness.
- **The glassback beetles**, black mirror-shelled animals that keep stone surfaces clear and provide materials, larvae, and movement.
- **The six-limbed grazers**, tall translucent herd animals that feed on microbial films and move across salt flats in slow migration.
- **The ribbon swimmers**, transparent pool-dwellers that filter water and reveal chemical danger.
- **The glasswing kites**, membrane-winged drifters that read the air and mark the thermal cycles.
- **The reservoir scavengers**, long-legged cleaners that feed on biofilm and help maintain the edges of water basins.
- **The pipe-dwellers**, small creatures that hide in industrial seams and signal leaks through their presence or absence.
- **The basin lilies**, soft-bodied colonial organisms that grow crystal shelves and fuse abandoned machinery into the city’s living crust.
- **The city itself**, a biological machine, a mineral reef, a wet industrial organism that must be fed, cooled, pruned, and listened to.

They all live in the same breathing body: heat below, pale sky above, water cycling endlessly between stone and air. They live where rocks weep, where salt grows, where engines exhale, and where silence is not absence but the shape of cohabitation.
