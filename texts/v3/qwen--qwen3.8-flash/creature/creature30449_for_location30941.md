Some roots form nodules that look like black almonds half-buried in pale gravel, and when the wind thins enough to make them audible, they give a small click. These nodules are not seeds in the ordinary sense, nor bulbs swollen with stored dampness. They are dry condensers. Within their black shells lie fine lattices of mineral fiber and powdered crystal, and they hold, in a still and patient way, the electrical history of the season that made them. When the plant above is mature, when its broad horizontal leaves have charged through many bright afternoons and discharged through many cold nights, one of these nodules splits along a line already prepared for splitting. No liquid runs from it. No pulp appears. It opens like a small box made of slate, and from inside comes a faintly luminous dust that drifts upward in the local air current.

The dust is not smoke, and it is not true pollen, though it serves something of pollen’s purpose. It is seed-matter, waste-matter, and charge-matter all at once. Each particle is so small that it can be lifted by the plateau’s gentlest thermals, yet each carries enough mineral residue to fall, after a time, onto a patch of ground that may or may not accept it. Acceptance is not a question of moisture. It is a question of compatibility. A particle settles on a salt plain and remains inert, too far from the electrical rhythm of the living crust. Another settles in the shade of a basalt shelf and begins a slow transformation, threading itself into existing lichen films until, over years, it becomes a pale green filament that feeds on the field generated by the wind and the stone. Another falls between the roots of a dry tree and is drawn into the root-network like a key into a lock, because its mineral signature matches the pattern already present in the grove. Then it begins to live, and if it lives, it becomes part of the grove’s continuing self.

The groves are not only collections of separate plants. They are slow organisms of shared tension. A single fan of dry leaves may be a tree, but the whole stand of such trees behaves like one vast, half-conscious instrument. Their roots do not simply anchor them. They braid together through cracks and mineral seams, forming dense mats of pale filaments that conduct charge across the ground like hidden wires. When one part of a grove charges strongly under noon sunlight, the others draw strength from it, and when shade falls over one side of the grove, the rest lend charge back, so that the whole remains balanced. This is not thought, not as a human might recognize it, but it is memory enough for survival: the grove remembers which winds bring dust, which basalt shelves hold heat longest, which salt flats are too bright for young filaments, which old dunes can be colonized after three years of stable charge.

During the brightest hours, the groves hum faintly if one presses an ear close to a trunk or rests a hand near the roots. The sound is not mechanical. It is electrostatic, a thin crackling that lives just beneath hearing. The broad leaves tilt themselves so that their nearly horizontal surfaces catch the pale sky. Their edges, where light passes through, take on a translucent quality, like parchment made of glass. Their centers are copper-green, blue-gray in certain angles, deep olive in others. No water moves through their veins. Instead, ions move across dry membranes, and the plant is always, in its way, breathing electricity. It does not drink. It tunes.

Between the groves, the land is not empty. The white salt basins are covered with films that shift from silver to ochre as they charge, and these films are alive. They are so thin that they can be mistaken for weather stains on stone, for mineral blooms, for the ghost of some long-vanished shore. Yet they feed. They grow. They reproduce in fine bursts when the charge in their surface becomes too great for them to hold. They form vast mosaics across pale ground, broken by cracks and wind-scoured channels. Some of them are pale as ash; others glow with a dull copper sheen; some become so dense in certain years that, viewed from a high ridge, they look like fields of tarnished metal. They are the beginning of almost every other life, for where they grow, dust settles in patterns that the larger organisms can read.

There are smaller lives than the salt films: microscopic crystals that live within their crevices, filamentous colonies that extend invisibly through vapor-filled pores, creatures no larger than a mote that feed on the chemical breath of mineral vents. These beings do not form eyes or jaws or limbs. They form surfaces. They build themselves into lattices that can open and close with the changing charge of the air. They are the quiet laborers of the plateau, turning raw mineral into usable gradient, making the dry world more receptive to larger things. Without them, the great plants would have less to draw from; with them, the plateau is never entirely inert.

Even the basalt shelves are not dead. Wind and heat have worn them smooth, but beneath their dark skins live creatures adapted to the long, slow ticking of stone. They are flat, segmented beings, no longer than a hand, with bodies made of overlapping plates like broken pottery. They feed on the heat differentials between sunlit stone and shaded stone, pressing their broad undersides against the basalt and moving from warm patch to cooler patch in a rhythm that resembles walking. When frightened, they do not flee in a panic. They flatten themselves completely, becoming almost indistinguishable from the rock, and their plates shift until they mimic the texture of the shelf beneath them. They are slow, patient, and nearly silent. A traveler crossing the basalt might not notice that the ground itself has been breathing under his steps.

If there are herbivores on the plateau, they are not creatures of lush mouths and soft tongues. They are broad-backed animals, low to the ground, built like dry seed pods fitted with legs. Some have wide, fan-shaped forequarters covered in mineralized bristles that comb the dust and channel charge into the body. Their mouths are slit-like openings set low in the head, taking in powdered lichen, salt film, and airborne mineral fragments. They do not eat green tissue so much as they eat the energy pattern stored within it. Where a rain-world herbivore would browse on leaves, a plateau grazer stands in the shadow of a grove and allows its body to be charged by the falling seed-dust, by the electric field rising from the roots, by the mineral breath of nearby vents.

Herds move not in lines, but in slow expansions and contractions. When one grazer senses that a distant salt basin has brightened with charge, it moves toward it, and others follow, not because they hear a call but because the leader’s passage disturbs the same field they all read with their broad sensory plates. In this way, movement becomes contagion without urgency. A herd can travel for days without making a sound except the dry rasp of their feet on gravel and the faint crackle of dust falling from their backs. They do not sweat. They do not need water to cool them. Their heat regulation is architectural: plates open, lattices expose themselves to the wind, and excess charge is bled away into the ground through special pads on their feet. When they rest, they often press those pads against the bare stone and become, for a time, extensions of the plateau’s own electrical body.

There are predators, though not in the manner of fast teeth and warm blood. They are slender, flat-bodied things, built with long necks and forelimbs that seem too large for their weight. Their surfaces are hard and glossy, like fired clay, and their eyes—if they can be called eyes—are broad, dark plates that see polarized light and electrostatic disturbance rather than shape alone. They hunt by reading the charge fields of herds. When a grazer bends low to feed at a basalt shelf, its body field changes. When a young animal is separated from the group, its connection to the grove-network weakens. The predator does not need to tear flesh to eat. It presses its long forelimbs against the prey, or bites into a node of thick mineral tissue, and drains a portion of the victim’s charge. The prey does not bleed. It may go rigid, then shudder, then lie still with a fine powder sifting from its joints. The predator takes not meat, but the organized potential within it, along with the dry nutrient film that coats the inside of the shell.

Death among such creatures is not wet, not bloated, not foul. A carcass becomes a dry sculpture. Its shell cracks in the thermal wind. Birds or kites of the plateau pick at its outer plates. Salt films grow across its hollows. Within a season or a year, it is no longer a body but a mound of mineral fragments, and if its chemistry is right, the groves will draw it back into themselves, root by root, film by film. Thus the plateau recycles not by decay, but by reintegration. Nothing rots. Everything returns as powder, as film, as charge.

Above all this, the sky itself is alive, though it is difficult to see at first. The upper atmosphere is thin, and flight here requires wide wings and endless patience. Some creatures resemble living kites, bodies compressed into small capsules with long trailing filaments that catch the wind. They never flap. They rise in columns of warm air, drift in spirals, and ride the long mineral-laden currents that sweep down from higher ridges. Their wings are not feathered. They are membranes of spun salt fiber, translucent in one direction, iridescent in another, ribbed with fine filaments that stiffen or relax according to charge. When a flock passes high above a grove, it makes no caw, no cry. It makes a sound like many small shells touched at once.

Other flying beings are like veil-hawks: longer, narrower, with heads small and sharp and tails broad as leaves. They live almost entirely aloft, touching ground only to feed at the tops of the dry trees or to ride upward through the warm air rising from dark basalt. Their eyes catch the faint blue flicker along the leaf channels, and they know where groves are strong from great heights. They do not hunt with claws so much as with precision. A veil-hawk can dive at a grazer’s back and scrape away a plate of mineral skin with its beak, taking only enough dust and charge to sustain it. This is not cruelty, not malice, not waste. It is grazing from above, a way of harvesting without killing, and the herd animals tolerate it as they tolerate the wind because the birds are also part of the same electrical field.

Some of the flying creatures are not born alone. They emerge from crystalline pods attached to the high branches of the dry trees, pods that look at first like pale nuts or shards of quartz. When the pods are ready, they do not crack from heat or from a parent’s bite. They open by a sudden reversal of charge, releasing a dry powder into the air and, with it, a small folded being that unfolds in the sunlight. It lies for a while motionless on the branch, its membranes stiff, its body too light for the wind to carry. As it absorbs enough charge from the tree, its filaments loosen, its wings expand, and it lifts without effort. It will not return to the same branch. It may return to the same grove years later, drawn by the particular hum of its root-network.

In the hollow places of the plateau, in old lava tubes and beneath wind-carved arches, there are other lives still stranger and quieter. The tubes are dark, but not cold. Mineral vapor seeps from cracks in the deep rock, carrying salts and gases that feed dense colonies of filamentous organisms. These colonies build themselves into layered curtains across the tunnel walls, gray-brown and faintly metallic. They do not see. They do not move. They respond to pressure, to temperature, and to the faint electrical pull of stone under strain. When a dust spiral gathers above ground, the pressure inside the tube shifts, and the colonies respond by opening tiny pores and releasing puffs of dry spore-dust that drift upward through hidden shafts. These releases are not for flight alone. They are for tuning. The spores carry information, encoded in mineral grain and electrostatic residue, about the state of the surface world below.

The wind through these tubes creates the low hums mentioned from the open ridges, and it is not the tube itself that sings so much as the organisms within it. Their layered walls have grown to a thickness and spacing that resonates with certain frequencies. Some tubes hum only in storms. Others hum for days after the air has calmed, because the vibration is stored in the elastic memory of their dry membranes. To walk near such a place at dusk is to hear the plateau breathing through a single pore after long centuries, and to know that breath is not only animal but architectural, not only life but landscape.

If one speaks of the plateau’s inhabitants, it is necessary to include the beings who might, by their own definition and not by the definitions of wetter worlds, be called people. They are not many, and their numbers are hard to guess, because they do not build permanent cities as other lands would. They build dwellings that can be dismantled, grown over, or left to the wind without harm. They live in the spaces between groves, on the edges of salt basins, along the shelves where heat and light change by degrees. They are low-bodied, broad-chested, with limbs long enough for reaching but not for running. Their skin resembles pale, dry bark, layered with fine mineral plates at the joints. Their eyes are wide and reflective, set in faces with little visible mouth movement, for their speech is not made primarily of air and tongue.

They speak by touch and by static. Two such beings standing close may communicate by resting their hands together, or by drawing long fingers along the mineral plates of one another’s arms. Small sounds are produced: clicks, sparks, faint chimes along the wrist. Their ordinary spoken language sounds like a dry branch passing through a screen of leaves. It is not loud. It does not carry far. When one wishes to speak across distance, one does not shout. One uses a resonance stone, a hollow piece of basalt or crystal that has been shaped until it can hold a particular vibration and release it in a pulse. These stones are carried on the shoulder or worn at the waist. They can send a message like a bell struck once in a vast, quiet room.

Their bodies require no drinking. This is difficult for a mind shaped by wet worlds to understand. They have no need of thirst, no organs for water, no fear of drowning because drowning is not a concept in their vocabulary. They take mineral vapor through slit-like passages along their chests and forearms. They take charge through direct contact with ground, stone, leaf, and sky. A person sitting beneath a dry tree may be eating in the most essential sense of the word. A person standing in a dust spiral may be gathering strength. A person sleeping on a warm basalt shelf may be charging as deeply as another sleeps beneath food in a lower world. Their injuries are not gushing or soft. A wound is a crack. It rings faintly. It may spark if the body’s field is disturbed. Then it seals with powder, leaving a pale scar like a vein of quartz.

They do not wear heavy clothes. They wear garments grown as much as made: fronds of the dry trees softened and flattened into sheets, salt-filmed membranes woven into bands, mineral fibers spun from the waste of seed-dust. Their clothing is pale and metallic, often shifting color in certain lights. It protects them from the abrasive dryness and helps them read the fields around them. Some wear necklaces of cracked nodules that chime softly when they move. Others bind their heads with bands of fine crystal that can receive faint changes in air pressure and charge. Their tools are carved from stone, bone, or the hardened stems of plants, and nearly all of them are piezoelectric: they make charge when struck, when bent, when rubbed. A tool can be a knife, a scraper, a musical node, and a message-stone at once.

Their dwellings are low and wide, built into the lee side of ridges or grown around the roots of groves. A house is not a sealed box so much as a series of membranes and screens, open to wind but shielded from direct dust. When the light is strong, the outer panels reflect it and become silver-white. When the charge is high, they faintly glow with a blue-gray sheen. Inside, the air is stiller, darker, and filled with soft sounds: the tick of stone adjusting to temperature, the rustle of dried leaf-litter, the occasional click of a stored nodule releasing its charge into a container. There are no hearths for cooking, because fire as a central domestic art is rare or unnecessary. Instead, there are charging shelves, where tools, garments, and sometimes persons are left to balance their fields. There are resonance bowls, where wind is allowed to pass over carved edges until the room fills with a low hum that is not decoration but orientation.

Their social life is not loud. Because the plateau is vast, because distance is ordinary, because emptiness is a medium rather than an absence, the people of the high land move in small groups and gather in larger ones only when the winds and fields are favorable. A gathering may last days, held at a place where several groves meet at the edge of a salt basin. There the charge in the air is strong, and the ground beneath the roots provides natural connection. People stand in circles or half-spirals, touching one another with long fingers, speaking in pulses. Children run between the legs of adults with a sound like small stones in a stream—dry, sharp, and bright—but they do not shout. On the plateau, shouting is not the loudest way to be heard. Stillness is.

They are hunters, herders, and gardeners. They do not fence fields, for there is no soil to fence against erosion. Instead, they tend groves by moving charge: bringing warm basalt into shade, arranging nodules at the edges of root-mats, releasing seed-dust into the right spirals. They also move the herds of broad-backed grazers, not by driving them with noise, but by altering the field along the trails. A person carrying a resonance stone can stand on a ridge and send a pulse into the ground that makes one basin seem more attractive than another. The herd shifts its path over hours, never knowing it has been guided. This is not domination so much as conversation. The plateau’s people understand, perhaps more than most beings can, that control in a dry and charged land must be gentle, because force dissipates quickly and the wind carries it away.

They also keep birds. Not caged birds, but veil-hawks and kites that roost on tall poles of basalt outside their dwellings. These birds are trained by charge, not by hunger. A person touches a bird’s sternum until its field matches the household’s field, and the bird learns to associate that pattern with rest, food-dust, and safety. Some birds carry messages across leagues, riding currents no runner could follow. Others are used to locate groves in crisis, because they can see the blue-gray flicker of stressed leaves from high air and return to tell of it by scratching at the outer membrane of a dwelling until let in.
