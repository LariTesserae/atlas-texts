To ask who lives there is to expect, somehow, that the water has doors and that the doors have owners. They do not. No one knocks through the rain-ceiling. No one keeps the lamps in the deep hollows. No one has ever walked those straight lanes between the columns, no one has ever laid the stair-ridges of root-plate against root-plate, and no one has ever decided that one space should remain open while another should arch itself into a room. The Terrace of the Upper Rain is not empty, but it is not occupied in any human way. It is inhabited the way a forest is inhabited, the way a tide pool is inhabited, the way a body is inhabited—by processes that move, eat, shed, thicken, glow, and become part of the place so thoroughly that their presence appears as architecture.

If a name is wanted, no creature will answer to it. If a species is sought, every species is already half dissolved into the larger thing: the drowned bowl itself. The life there is too interdependent to be counted as residents. Each organism is a temporary organ of the Terrace’s continued being, and the Terrace itself is the only large thing that seems to live.

From the underside of the surface, the highest life begins with a kind of suspended silver. The rain does not fall through empty air. It enters the basin as fine motion, a cold and ceaseless pressure, and it carries within it everything the water can catch: mineral dust, dissolved salts, microscopic spores, particles of shed frond-skin, fragments of glowing bacteria, and the faint oily film that forms wherever the air meets water. This uppermost layer is not soil, not vegetation in any ordinary sense, but a living film clinging to the underside of the ceiling of the world. It spreads over the membrane-like frond-tops as they flare beneath the rain. It drifts in curtains of root-thread that descend from those broad, translucent umbrellas. It is populated by tiny things that might be called motes if motes could swim.

These motes are not animals in any obvious way. They are less than animals, and yet they behave as a single slow shimmer across the dark water. They feed on suspended particles. They gather in the thin turbulence created by the endless impact of rain on the surface. Where currents meet, they cluster in faint, sparkling threads. Where the root-hair curtains hang down into the open water, they settle like silver dust on the filaments, making the curtains visible as lines of moving light. They are not the whole of the Terrace’s surface life, but they are its first skin, the living film that makes the ceiling of the basin more than a mere boundary between water and air.

Just beneath them, larger things drift: pale, round-bodied floaters with translucent veils between short arms. They are not fish, nor jelly, nor exactly plant, though they resemble all three. Their bodies open and close with a softness like leaves moving in shallow current. They filter the rain-borne particulate through frond-like lips and trailing fibers, and when they turn in the dim light they glow faintly at the edges, the glow coming not from themselves alone but from colonies of smaller organisms inside their tissues. They rise and fall without urgency, carried by the pressure waves of the surface and by the slow vertical currents that form beneath the broadest root-umbrellas. When one dies, its veil sinks in a quiet spiral, and its body breaks apart into a fine white haze that is immediately consumed by the next generation of motes and micro-filters. Nothing is wasted. Nothing becomes rubble. Death is only another way of feeding.

These surface floaters are the highest of the Terrace’s tenants, and they belong to the rain. Their bodies are shaped by the ceaseless impact overhead. They do not know the surface as anything separate; to them, the surface is the ceiling of their ocean, the boundary from which pressure and vibration come, the source of the constant white roar. They live in the uppermost layer of water where the glow is strongest and the falling rain has not yet been translated into the deep, dark quiet of the open lanes below.

Beneath them, the lanes begin.

The open water between the great plant-columns is not empty. It is full of moving light and moving bodies, but the bodies move as though they belong to the architecture. The small fish come first: clouds of them, clouds within clouds, each one no larger than a finger, pale as wet bone, with eyes wide enough to gather the thin blue-green light. They are called by no one, but they move as if called. Their bodies are transparent at the edges and silver at the center, so that when a school turns in the dark water, it does not seem to change direction but to reconfigure itself, the silver becoming a different figure. They are not afraid of one another because fear is too slow for this place. There is no chaos, no scattering panic, no dramatic hunt. What predation exists is absorbed into the larger motion. A passing body of fish does not flee so much as reorganize, narrowing and widening around obstacles as a stream does around a stone.

These fish are not merely swimming through the Terrace; they are one of the ways the Terrace breathes. They carry spores in their gill-mists. They disturb the suspended particles on which the filter-feeders depend. They scrape the thin biofilm from the sides of the root-columns and leave behind faint, shining lines. Their eggs attach to the curtains of hanging root-thread, and when they hatch, the fry drift downward among the frond-hairs and feed on the motes and microfauna that make those curtains look like living lace. The fish are not builders, but their passage polishes the surfaces and prevents the open lanes from choking with sediment. Their numbers are so great that the lanes do not feel empty because the emptiness is always being moved through. The lanes feel inhabited by motion itself.

Some of the larger fish are less obvious as fish. They are broad and flattened, with fins that ripple from the body like wet leaves unfolding. They drift with a heavy, placid confidence, as though they do not need to hurry because the water has already learned to move around them. These are the grazers. Their mouths are wide and soft, lined with plates that scrape and nibble at the edges of root-curtains, the frond-margins, the undersides of leaf-canopies, and the fine hairs that line the walls of the living columns. They do not tear the plants open with violence. They take small bites from growth that is already old, already dying at the edge, already ready to be returned to the system. Their feeding shapes the architecture. Because they eat the loosest, most outward growth, they keep the lanes open. Because they follow the same slow routes from one generation to the next, their grazing rounds certain stems into buttresses and hollows others into channels. They are not gardeners. They are mouths, and mouths are pruning tools.

The grazers are often accompanied by smaller organisms clinging to their bodies: tiny shelled things, translucent polyps, and crustacean-like cleaners that eat the dead skin from the grazers’ flanks. The grazers tolerate this cleaning with the same patience they tolerate the current. Over their generations, the relationship becomes so settled that the grazers almost seem to have been made to carry the cleaners, and the cleaners almost seem to have been made to move among the grazers. In truth, the grazers are mobile substrates, and the cleaners are just another way the Terrace keeps itself clean.

Between the columns and the walls, there are also eel-like creatures, but not eels as the lowland water would know them. Their bodies are dark and slick, segmented faintly, like lengths of living root pulled into motion. They move through the plates of bark with a strange familiarity, slipping between ridges and into the small hollows formed where one root-plate overlaps another. They are not large enough to be mistaken for anything important at first, but they are among the most intimate inhabitants of the architecture. They live inside the walls without being walls. They crawl through the spaces between stalks, through the tiny channels that open into the trunks, through the seams where growth has fused old tissue into new.

These eel-weavers have bodies shaped by their passage. Their skin is smooth and faintly adhesive. They carry mineral particles from one part of the Terrace to another, often in the mucus that coats their bodies. They consume decay at the edges of root-faces, but they also deposit things: tiny grains of calcium, fragments of silica, spores, and bacteria that help certain plant tissues knit more tightly together. Their movements encourage the roots to seal, to thicken, to divide into channels that resemble gutters or stair-rails. When they gather in a hollow stem, they move in spirals, their bodies brushing against the inner wall in ways that leave fine, repetitive scratches. Over generations, these scratches become patterns, then channels, then arches whose interiors have been smoothed and shaped by countless visits. They do not know they are making a building. They only know the shape of the space, the taste of the current, the warmth of the inner wall.

In the deepest and quietest parts of the living columns, where the water becomes still and the blue-green glow is softest, the eel-weavers reproduce. They lay their young inside chambers formed by overlapping root-shields, and the larvae drift out through the same channels their parents use. The young are transparent and threadlike at first, and for a time they are barely distinguishable from the hanging root-hairs. But as they grow, they darken and stiffen, and when they begin to crawl against the walls, their presence announces the same old process: the body that moves through the structure becomes part of the structure’s making.

Near the floor of the Terrace, where the ground is a felt of rootlets, calcified fronds, shed plates, and living skin, the smallest shelled animals feed without pause. They are numerous enough to look like sand at a distance. Their shells are flat, rounded, and pale, often tinged with blue or green from the light around them. They crawl in lines that curve and bend around the larger roots, never quite crossing the central ridge of one, and this avoidance gives the impression of streets, boundaries, and rules. But the lines are taught by chemistry, not by law. Certain root-saps discourage them; certain exudations attract them. The geometry of the place is not imposed on them; it is written into the taste of the ground.

These shell-crawlers polish the floor. Their mouths work ceaselessly over the living pelt, eating the thin layer of dead tissue, bacteria, and suspended mineral that has gathered there. Their excreta adds calcium to the root-felt, thickening the places where new growth will take hold. Their molted shells do not simply disappear; they settle into crevices and become embedded, eventually joined by rootlets that grow around them. The floor becomes paved with the remains of its own inhabitants. In one place, a patch of white shells may look like a deliberate mosaic, all arranged by some unknown intention. In truth, it is only where the current narrows, where a grazer has passed, where a school of fish has pressed the shells into a pattern, where the small shelled animals have fed for generations along the same chemical boundary. The mosaic is not planned. It is the accumulation of many repeated, ordinary movements.

These shelled animals are also hosts to light. Tiny colonies inside their tissues or beneath their shells produce the faintest phosphorescent dust, and when they move, the ground seems to ripple with a pale, broken glow. They are one reason the floor of the Terrace is not dark. Their presence transforms the root-felt from a mere living mat into a surface that holds light the way wet stone holds moonlight, not by reflecting something above, but by carrying a slow internal shine.

There are larger things on the floor, too: broad, low-bodied creatures that resemble turtles, slugs, and armored fish mixed together. Their backs are covered with plates of fused organic matter, moss-like growths, and small attached fronds. They move through the felt as though the ground is soft beneath them, which it is, but also as though they are returning the softness to itself. They consume the thicker mats of root-growth, especially the places where the felt has begun to become too dense. By doing so, they prevent the ground from rising too far into the lanes. Their waste becomes new cement for the root-plate floor. When they die, their bodies collapse into the ground and the living felt grows over them, incorporating their shells into the structure. They become part of the path they once crossed.

This is one of the most important things about the life of the Terrace: the inhabitants are not separate from the building. They are not merely housed by the architecture; they are one of the means by which the architecture is maintained. The plants are the builders
