No one can be pointed to and named as the inhabitant without making the room smaller than it is. The place does not hold a single creature, nor does it hold a population in any ordinary sense. It holds a society of surfaces, pressures, metabolisms, and rare moving bodies, all of which agree—without agreement, without design, without ceremony—to remain. To say “something lives there” is already too narrow. The correct answer is plural and slow.

The first citizen is the rind.

It is not a plant, though from far off, in a beam of weak glow, it could be mistaken for one. It is not an animal, though it feeds. It is a living crust, or rather a loose and shifting consortium of life: bacterial mats, archaea, fungal-looking filaments, mineral precipitates, electroactive films, and residues too complex to classify without instruments no diver would bring into a place like this. It spreads across basalt, concrete, glass, iron, tile, cable insulation, turbine blades, and the inner faces of conduits. It blooms in radial fans around vent mouths. It beads along pipe joints. It grows in soft, pale fur on surfaces that receive heat but not direct force. Where currents are fastest, it thins into a hard ironed glaze. Where water is slow and chemical gradients pool, it thickens into white or rust-brown spongy masses that look like the inside of an abandoned lung.

This crust is the room’s first metabolism. It eats heat, rust, current, sulfur, dissolved metals, and the remains of dead organisms. It turns leakage into growth. A cracked pipe does not merely fail here; it becomes an invitation. From the crack comes a warm chemical tongue, and within days or weeks—centuries make such distinctions meaningless—the rind reaches toward it. Filaments branch into the current. Bacterial colonies settle where turbulence is least hostile. Minerals precipitate around them. The result is not healing as humans would understand it, but adjustment. The structure learns its own wound and becomes compatible with it.

There is no brain in the rind, no center, no self that could survive removal from its wall. Yet it has a kind of civic memory. It records flow by growth rings. It records heat by color. It records electrical instability by the formation of metallic veins and flickering films. A patch of rust-nerve growing in a certain direction indicates a current that has persisted long enough to teach mineral life to orient itself. Another patch, bright with conductive residue, marks a place where cables sweat weak current. The room’s walls are therefore not silent. They are written upon. The writing is not symbolic, but it is informative. If one could read chemistry as easily as language, one would discover the history of the space in the layers of its crust.

Sometimes the rind acts almost like a collective organism. A disturbance—a pressure wave, a chemical plume, a new vibration from a failing engine—travels through the connected films and mats faster than water alone carries news. Glows shift along veins of conductive rust. Tiny pockets of gas form, then collapse. Mineral particles rearrange. A surface that seemed inert becomes briefly, faintly reactive, as if the room has been touched. This is not thought in any human way, but it is a form of responsiveness. The crust thinks by depositing, by sealing, by corroding, by reorienting, by growing toward heat. It is a slow intelligence made of chemistry and pressure.

It is also the foundation of everything else that lives there. Without the rind, there would be no grazing surfaces, no hidden nutrients, no microhabitat, no glue for brood chambers, no armor, no food web, no reason for rare animals to be rare. The crust is the true first population of the pocket. It is everywhere. It is almost everything. It is so numerous and so diffuse that it is mistaken for the wall itself, which is perhaps not a mistake.

The second citizens are the walkers.

They are not common. They do not swarm. But they are persistent enough to suggest that even in a drowned industrial room, small bodies can occupy niches carved out by current and chemistry. They are the filigree creatures, the tap-legged, the wire-skinned, the calcium-and-rust climbers. At first they look like fragments of the infrastructure detached by some earlier violence: a curl of corroded cable, a broken clamp, a clump of mineral fur, a tangle of hairlike growth. Then they move.

Their bodies are low and armored, made of overlapping plates that resemble concrete or baked clay. Some are pale as bone; others are stained the color of iron or volcanic glass. Their legs are too many and too thin to appear stable in a normal environment, but in water under pressure they can grip, pivot, and distribute weight with the uncanny sureness of things that do not fall but anchor. They climb vertical walls, cross ceilings of broken pipe, and hang beneath ledges where the current would otherwise tear them away. They do not walk as land animals walk. They test the surface. Each step is an inquiry into pressure, temperature, texture, and chemical taste.

They graze the rind. That is their main work. With small scraping mouths, or with appendages that function like trowels, they remove mineral film from walls, pipes, turbine casings, and conduit mouths. Their feeding is not destructive in the way we usually mean. They thin the crust where it is too thick, expose fresh surfaces to chemical exchange, scatter mineral fragments into the current, and leave behind polished scars. In this way they are part of the room’s editing process. They maintain channels. They prevent some surfaces from sealing themselves too completely. They also create small waste pellets that become habitat for bacteria, seed mineral growth, and feed other, smaller lives.

Some of them are solitary. Some move in pairs, not social pairs, but because two bodies can survive a shear current better than one can. Others gather only where heat plumes meet electrical leaks. They are rare enough that each individual seems accidental, as though it has wandered into a place not made for it. But they are not accidents. They are adapted to the noise. Their bodies are shaped by vibration. Their legs sense cavitation bubbles before they collapse against a surface. Their shells incorporate tiny metallic particles that make them resemble corroded wire bundles until they shift.

Their reproductive lives are hidden in architecture. A crevice behind a beam is a nursery. A hollow tile becomes a chamber for eggs sealed in mineral paste. The eggs are not eggs in the bright sense of a bird’s egg. They are pale capsules, often attached to warm surfaces, surrounded by a fungal mat that protects them from current and from predators. When the young emerge, they are transparent and thin, barely visible. They climb onto the legs of adults, or ride bubbles, or settle immediately onto the nearest safe wall. Many are lost. Those that survive grow by incorporating materials around them: calcium, iron, broken silica, bits of old conduit skin.
