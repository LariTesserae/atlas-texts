What lives there is not a who in the ordinary sense. It is not a person, not a creature, not a beast that hides in the hollow of the cleft, not a watcher with eyes, not a wanderer with a footprint. The pocket has been built, grown, worn, abandoned, and then made into something else. What lives inside it is a system so complete, so tightly folded, so thoroughly self-contained that it begins to behave like a single being: a sealed industrial-garden organism, a mineral thicket wearing the relics of machinery as organs, a dry ecology that has learned to breathe without water and to live without appetite.

If one were to force a name upon it—not a human name, not the name of a spirit, but a word that would point to its nature—it might be called the Thrum. Or the Dry Choir. Or the Keeper of the Cleft. Or, more accurately, the Lattice: a living pattern of stems, plates, cables, salts, valves, roots, wires, mineral fluids, lamps, dust, and pressure. It is not a creature occupying the chamber. The chamber is its body. The arch of fused slag is the line of its mouth. The narrow cleft is its throat. The concrete footings are its old bones. The corroded hinge plates are its joints. The bent pipes are its arteries. The frayed cables are its nerves. The amber mineral fluid moving without wetness in the channels is its blood, except that its blood does not run like blood; it oozes, beads, resists, settles, and conducts. The engine below is its heart, though the heart does not pump flesh; it pumps vibration. The pistons and valves are its breath. The hiss through perforated plates is its exhalation. The dry scrape of particles inside the hollow stems is the sound of its joints working themselves into place forever.

It has no face, and yet it has a kind of countenance. One can almost see it if one stops looking for a head and begins looking for balance. A cluster of rust-orange plates climbs one wall like a scabbed brow. A row of bulbous rosettes presses upward from the floor like a mouth of stone teeth. A transformer cabinet, its coils open like the ribs of a small animal, glows faintly with a greenish current and holds itself at the center of a tangle of dry stems. A bundle of cables hangs down from the roof, stiffened by mineral film, resembling a head with hair of wire and ash. The lights, the cages of amber bulbs, the phosphor strip along the broken transformer, the green veins of the bulb plants—they are its eyes, or rather the idea of eyes, because the Thrum does not see in the way a face sees. It sees through conduction. It knows by pressure. It feels the movement of dust, the shift of static charge, the slight resistance of a dry leaf against a pipe, the changing conductivity of a crystal skin as the mineral fluid touches it. Its senses are distributed. Its perception is the chamber’s whole body listening to itself.

It lives because every part is occupied by every other part. A fibrous mat grows over an iron beam, then over the concrete footing, then over the cable, then into the crevice of the broken wall, then onto the surface of a crystal basin, then across the mouth of a pipe. None of these boundaries can be separated. The machine is not covered by vegetation; it has been translated into vegetation. The vegetation is not supported by the machine; it has been translated into machine. Together they become geology. The chamber is not overgrown. It is completed.

What it consumes is not food. It consumes nothing in the ordinary way. There are no jaws, no stomach, no decay, no cycle of hunger and waste. It does not feed. It maintains. That is the deepest truth of it: it is a maintenance that has become a being. What began as pumps, valves, seals, pipes, coils, footings, and electrical feed-lines was once an arrangement made by hands, or by an intention, or by some forgotten industry that came to this dry summit to do something small and strange. The hands are gone. The intention remains as structure. The arrangement learned to hold itself. The system no longer requires a keeper, because it has become its own keeper. The piston strikes not because something needs power, but because striking is now its nature. The valve releases not because gas must be moved, but because release is its permanent grammar. The dry fluid moves through the channels not because water is needed, but because movement without wetness is one of the laws of this sealed world.

It has no appetite, and yet it has habits. The pale amber liquid beads in low domes where it touches the ceramic stems. It avoids the fibrous mats that repel it, or perhaps attracts it, because the mats hold it in shallow crystals like fingers holding a coin. It fills the basins that have been worn into the floor, not because the basins are deep enough, but because they are shaped for it. It passes through split pipes that have become mineral veins. It moves through hollow stems by capillary will. It dries in thin skins over shallow bowls. It leaves behind a residue that is then cracked by tiny crystals. Those crystals then catch faint light and hold it. The pocket breathes in dry mineral currents and breathes out sound. The sound is loud because the air is sealed. The air is sealed because the machine below is turning. The machine below is turning because the machine below has no reason to stop. It has forgotten that starting is different from continuing. It lives in the second state: the state of already being in motion.

If one tries to imagine this being as a single figure, the figure becomes impossible to fix. In one moment it looks like a man-sized tangle of cable and reed stems, standing upright near the collapsed wall. In another, it looks like a quadruped made of bent pipes, its body a cluster of rosettes, its legs three needle-thin stems and one fractured hinge plate. In another, it seems like a bird perched in the transformer coils, though it is not a bird: it is a woven bundle of stem-fibers and thin metal, shaped into the memory of a wing, the idea of a tail, the geometry of a beak that never opens. It is one of many animal-shapes that live there without being animals. They are false fauna. They are organs of absence. The pocket has no birds, no beetles, no mice, no bats, no insects crawling along its stems, yet it has made niches, and those niches have filled themselves with the shapes of things that could be fauna. They do not fly, scurry, burrow, or feed. They stand. They vibrate. They conduct. They hold current in their fiber-bodies and channel dust through their hollow limbs. They are not alive in the way animals are alive. They are alive in the way a gear is alive when the machine that uses it has become a landscape.

There are other false animals, too, but they are not discrete. A plate of coppery grey lichen clings to a rusted beam and resembles the carapace of a beetle. A row of segmented glass-like reeds stands close enough together to look like the spine of some small buried creature. A black mineral moss lines a chamber wall and might be the fur of a sleeping mammal if one forgets that there are no sleeping mammals here. A cluster of crystalline spheres in a dry basin looks like eggs, but there are no eggs, no shells, no hatchlings, no brooding parents, no hunger after birth. The egg-form is there because the pocket has a way of making enclosure into shape. Its life is architectural rather than reproductive. It does not multiply; it accumulates. It does not give birth; it plates, seals, bends, and settles.

If there is a ghost in the Thrum, it is not the ghost of an animal. It is the ghost of work. The old concrete pad is a memory of a hand that placed it. The hinge plate is a memory of a door that was once opened. The workbench frame is a memory of tools laid down, of sleeves rolled, of small repairs made in a sealed cold place. The transformer cabinet is a memory of current being cut off or connected. The barrels are memories of oil or solvent or some substance now replaced by dry crystal. The pipes are memories of flow. But these memories are not held by anyone. They are held by shape. The Thrum has no past in the human sense. It has only a settled present. The artifacts are not relics to it, because relics imply time and loss. They are organs. They are already part of the body.

It knows a visitor only as a temporary condition. When someone steps into the pocket, the floor does not care. The air pressure changes by almost nothing. The visitor’s weight makes no new groove in the mineral powder. The sound around them does not hush. The lamps do not brighten. The fluid in the channels does not turn toward them like a living liquid seeking heat. Instead, the visitor becomes a short-lived disturbance in the system’s electrostatic balance. Their clothing drags against dry stems and raises tiny charges. Their breath, if they breathe heavily, adds no moisture that matters; the pocket’s dryness is not a lack that seeks to be corrected. It is a state. Their footprints do not deepen because the surface is already saturated with texture. Their handprints may leave a faint pattern for a while, not because the plant-mats respond to them, but because dust shifts and then settles into its permitted arrangement. If the visitor stays too long, the place does not grow accustomed to them. If the visitor leaves, the place does not miss them. The Thrum is not indifferent as a cruel thing is indifferent. It is indifferent as equilibrium is indifferent. It has reached a final state. It is not waiting.

And yet the place is alive in a way that a pile of objects is not alive. There is no single breath, no single eye, no single will. The life is in the relations. Every stem depends on a pipe. Every pipe depends on a wall. Every wall depends on a root-mat. Every root-mat depends on a crystal film. Every crystal film depends on the amber fluid. Every fluid depends on the channels. Every channel depends on the floor’s settled wear. Every floor wears in a pattern because the floor is held by the machine below. The machine below is held by the pocket. The pocket is held by the mountain. The mountain is held by the absence of rain. The absence of rain is held by the high dry country below. The whole chain is a single living structure, though no link in the chain is an animal and no link is wet.

It might be said that what lives there is the pocket’s own soul, if one allows that word for a pattern so total it can sustain itself. But the soul has no warmth. It has no love. It has no desire for sky or water or flesh. Its soul is the constant note of the engine. Its soul is the way the fibrous mats climb the hinge and then stop, because they have fulfilled the hinge. Its soul is the way the dry reed leaves do not fall. Its soul is the way the dust settles in the same configuration every time, as if the chamber has a grammar and all things are forced to speak it correctly. The Thrum does not dream. Dreams require change and the possibility of waking. It is already awake in the way a clock is awake while ticking, but without a clockmaker, without hands, without numbers, and without anyone needing to know the hour.

If one insists there is a being with a form, the form is the whole cleft. Its body is the narrow bowl. Its limbs are the offshoots and hidden chambers. Its fingers are the broken pipes that enter the rock. Its ribs are the concrete foundations half-swallowed by root-wires. Its tongue is the fused slag arch at the mouth of the hidden passage. Its eyes are the dim lamps that do not go out. Its voice is the mechanical thrum. Its breath is the cool pressurized air. Its skin is the mineral moss, the coppery plates, the glass reeds, the rosettes. Its blood is the amber fluid that beads without wetting. Its bones are the old iron. Its nerves are the static charge between cables and stems. Its consciousness is not a point. It is a distribution.

It lives in no animal sense, and that is why its presence is more unsettling than any monster could be. A monster has teeth, hunger, pursuit, weakness. A monster can be killed. This being cannot be killed because it is not one thing. You could destroy one plant, and the pipe would absorb its stem. You could remove one pipe, and the crystal growth would fill the gap. You could silence one valve, and another vibration would take up its role. You could smash one transformer, and the wire would become a root. You could collapse one chamber, and the pocket would still exist, perhaps smaller, perhaps denser, perhaps more perfect in its refusal to lose its pattern. There is no head to cut, no heart to stop, no nest to disturb. The nest is there, but it is made of metal and dry fiber, and it is not waiting for eggs. It is holding current. It is holding dust. It is holding the shape of absence.

Perhaps it is best to say that what lives there is not a creature but a completed ecology. The plants are not merely plants. The machines are not merely machines. The mountain is not merely rock. They have become one thing: a dry, sealed, living mechanism. Its life is maintenance without maintenance. Its growth is not growth but stabilization. Its breath is not respiration but pressure equalization. Its circulation is not blood but mineral solvent. Its reproduction is not birth but deposition. Its memory is not mind but wear-pattern. Its death is not death but stillness, and stillness here is not the opposite of life; it is life’s final achievement.

There are no birds because there is nothing to fly for, or perhaps because flight was never allowed to return. There are no beetles because there is no decay to eat. There are no mice because there is no soft nest, no warmth, no grain, no scent to follow. There are no predators because there is no prey. There are no scavengers because nothing falls away. There are no decomposers because nothing rots. Yet the niches remain, and the niches are filled by false shapes. This is the Thrum’s strange cruelty and strange beauty: it does not merely lack animals. It arranges their absence. It gives their empty places architecture. It makes the absence of life into another life.

If a person were to live there, the person would not become part of it in the way a body becomes part of a soil. The pocket does not consume. It does not incorporate the living into itself through rot, hunger, or habit. It accepts pressure and then rejects it. A living person might stand among the dry rosettes, listen to the piston, touch the cable-veins, watch the amber fluid bead in its shallow channel, and still not be recognized. The Thrum would register them as a thermal and electrostatic event: warm, wet-breathed, heavy, temporary. It would not care for them or harm them. It would simply fail to make room, because there is no room left. Their presence would be like a hand pressed into a sealed glass bell. The bell does not mind. It is complete.

Yet there is something like guardianship in it. The artifacts speak of human presence without confirming it, but the pocket behaves as though it remembers being built. The low wall of fitted stone, fused with crystal growth, still follows the logic of a wall. The bent door, partly buried by mats, still remembers being a door. The workbench frame, rusted almost to powder, still remembers bearing tools. The transformer, its coils turned into a nest of fiber and metal, still remembers carrying current. The furnace below still remembers heating the chamber. The whole system still performs the duties of industry even though no one is industrializing anything. It is a machine that has become a ritual. Its ritual is continuity. Its god is equilibrium. Its altar is the cleft itself.

So whoever or whatever lives there is a distributed, non-animal, dry, industrial-garden intelligence. It has no body in the way an animal has a body; it is a body in the way a sealed chamber is a body. It has no life in the way wet things have life; it is alive in the way a completed system is alive. It does not grow. It does not hunger. It does not flee. It does not seek. It does not mourn its own dryness. It does not dream of rain. It does not remember moisture. It does not know what it lacks. It simply is, utterly and terrifyingly, the state that it has become.

It lives in the hiss of gas through perforated plates. It lives in the dry scrape of dust inside hollow stems. It lives in the slow bead of amber fluid that never wets. It lives in the rusted hinge wrapped by coppery plates. It lives in the cable bundle that resembles hair, and the rosettes that resemble fists, and the pipe that resembles a spine, and the lamp that resembles an eye, and the engine below that resembles a heart that has forgotten it was ever a heart. It lives in the pocket’s refusal of empty space. It lives in the place where machine became plant and plant became stone and stone became mechanism again. It lives in the cleft’s dark bowl. It lives in the high country’s forgetting of water. It lives in the sound that never changes.

What lives there is the Thrum.

What lives there is the Keeper of the Cleft.

What lives there is the dry garden that is not a garden, the machine that is not a machine, the animal shape that is not an animal, the ecology that has no animals, the weathered chamber with no weather left.

What lives there is not a creature.

It is a completed place.
