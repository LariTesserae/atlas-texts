It is inhabited by a population arranged less by territory than by water. No one creature owns the basin, because the basin is not divided into clear homes and clear roads; it is divided into wet and slightly less wet, salt and less salt, exposed and shadowed, temporarily flooded and permanently damp. The beings that live there are therefore shaped like the place itself: flattened, patient, opportunistic, mostly small, and tuned to thresholds. If you look for the residents as if they were the residents of a forest or a meadow, you may miss them. The forest has trees, the meadow has grasses. The basin has wetness, and its inhabitants have learned to read wetness the way other creatures read weather, scent, or social rank.

The first and most important inhabitants are the ones that make the surface itself look alive. Across the shallow pans, the damp mud, the stone hummocks, and the salty rims, there are living films: algae, cyanobacteria, diatoms, bacteria, fungi, and microscopic green and gold mats that spread like stains. These are not decorations. They are the basin’s engine. Where a stone is darkened by mineral seepage, life may be clinging to its thin skin of dampness. Where a salt pan glitters in the morning, the salt itself may be tinted by microbial life—pale green, ochre, brick red, or brown, depending on how much water remains and how concentrated the salts have become. In the very wettest hollows, the surface may take on a metallic, slippery sheen, not merely because of water but because of layers of organisms, dead and living, building over one another until the ground seems varnished.

In these films, or just beneath them, live tiny animals almost too small to notice without a hand lens or a still pool reflecting the sky: rotifers, tardigrades, nematodes, protozoa, ostracods, springtails, and the soft translucent larvae of insects. Some graze on algae. Some filter through water. Some dig through the upper mud. Some eat bacteria. Some are eaten. Their bodies are so small and their world so saturated that they seem almost to be particles of the landscape rather than creatures with names. They give the basin its hidden fertility. They are the reason a place that looks bare can nevertheless tremble with life.

The plants, where they exist, do not form a canopy or a floor. They behave like accents that have learned to conserve themselves. There may be cushions of moss in cool damp crevices, liverworts spreading as a pale green lace, thin carpets of salt-tolerant grass or rush at the edges of pools, low shrubs colored by salt, and small succulent herbs that store moisture in their leaves. Some are halophytes, able to tolerate mineral harshness by excreting salt, storing it in tissues, or simply enduring conditions that would poison less adapted life. Others are ephemeral, rising only when the ground is sufficiently softened by rain and retreating underground, leaving only seed, bulb, root, or resting stage behind. A plant may appear as a single pale mound on a mudflat, almost absurdly isolated, yet around it a tiny ecosystem has gathered: snails on its stalk, larvae in the damp at its base, flies hovering above its flowers, mites moving through its lower leaves.

When the water is shallow but not yet too salty, the living films thicken into richer communities. Algal mats become pastures for grazing animals; dead algae become food for bacteria; bacteria become food for protozoa; protozoa become food for tiny crustaceans; and all of these become the lower rungs for anything larger. The basin may look barren because the water is thin, but within that thin layer there can be clouds of organisms: swimming fleas, tiny shrimp, worm-like larvae, snail-like mollusks, and the immature forms of aquatic insects. In the more permanent pockets, where water hides beneath overhanging stones or collects in depressions fed by seepage, small fish or eel-like animals may lurk. Some of them are tolerant of poor water quality, of warmth, of low oxygen, of sudden drying. They do not need rivers. They need enough dampness and time. If conditions worsen, they may retreat into deeper crevices, become inactive in mud, or wait in hidden refugia until the basin fills again.

In the most temporary pans, life takes a faster, more explosive form. A hollow that appears barren on one morning may, after rain, become filled with eggs that have waited in dry mud through sun and wind. Within days, tadpoles blacken the water. Within weeks, midges or mayfly-like insects may emerge in dense swarms. Dragonfly and damselfly nymphs hunt in the shallows, their bodies flattened and patient, their labia striking with sudden precision. Tadpoles feed on algae and detritus, turning the water’s thin greenness into new bodies. Small crustaceans, some shrimp-like, some fairy-shrimp-like, hatch in numbers that make the water seem to shimmer. All of them race against the basin’s central danger: not drought, which comes slowly, but drying so gradual that by the time it is complete there may be no way out. Some survive by hiding; some by burrowing; some by laying drought-resistant eggs; some by growing so quickly that they leave the pool before the pool leaves them.

The snails, slugs, and freshwater mollusks are especially characteristic. They cluster under stones where the ground stays cool, their shells marked by faint growth rings. Some are aquatic, grazing on films of algae. Some live in wetter mud, leaving silvery tracks across peat. During dry spells, many seal themselves to a stone or sink slightly into damp soil and wait, mouths closed, bodies withdrawn, life reduced to a slow mineral patience. They may look like small stones for days or weeks, as though they too have been eroded into the basin’s general stillness. When moisture returns, they reopen, feed, move, and lay their eggs among the wet crevices. Their abundance is not dramatic, but it is reliable. They are among the basin’s quiet recyclers, grinding down detritus, transferring algae and decaying plant matter into animal bodies, becoming food for birds, fish, and small land animals.

Amphibians are the most obvious residents of the basin at night, though their bodies are often as hidden as their voices. Frogs and toads are everywhere, though many of them spend long periods away from water, buried in mud, under stones, in damp hollows, or simply waiting in cool microhabitats until the air becomes heavy enough to bring them out. At the beginning of the rains, they emerge with an urgency that contradicts the basin’s apparent slowness. For a time, the open ground becomes an orchestra of wet, clicking, trilling, metallic, hollow sounds. Some species call in long mechanical trills, some in wet clicks, some in high thin notes that scatter across the emptiness like drops of water striking different surfaces. Their calls are not random. They form waves and counters, ponds answering ponds, hollows answering hummocks, until the darkness seems to have a pulse.

These calls are also maps. They indicate water that is shallow enough for tadpoles to survive but damp enough for eggs not to dry. They indicate safe stone rims, good seepages, and patches of vegetation where larvae can shelter. After the breeding rush, many adults disperse into the wider basin, hiding under stones or in the damp underside of moss and peat. Some may enter long aestivation, wrapping themselves in dried mud or forming cocoons until the next rains. Others remain near permanent refugia, waiting with the patience of creatures that have learned the basin’s central lesson: water may disappear from sight, but it is never fully gone.

Among the amphibians there may also be newts, salamanders, or similar wet-skinned animals, especially in cooler, shaded crevices. If they are present, they live almost invisibly, sliding through damp grasses, turning over beneath stones, moving through the boundary between water and land without ever fully belonging to either. Their skins breathe the moisture. They are animals made of the basin’s own dampness, vulnerable to dryness and therefore deeply tied to the smallest wet places.

Birds do not dominate the basin, but they reveal it to the human eye as a place of life. Because there is no thick forest and no continuous green wall, the birds are visible as figures against water and mineral light. Long-billed waders probe the mud and thin water, reading the surface for movement, scent, or the flicker of prey beneath. Small dark birds skim just above the pans, snatching insects in quick aerial bites. Herons and crane-like birds stand so motionless in the shallows that they seem to have grown out of the stone and salt, their bodies folded into the same economy of patience. They wait because the water is too shallow to hide them and the ground too open to surprise anything larger. Their success depends on stillness, not pursuit.

In late afternoon, when midges and other small insects rise in columns, the basin’s birdlife can become startlingly active. Flocks wheel in loose bands, darting through the warm, humid air, their wingbeats cutting short sounds through the larger drone of insects. These birds may include small passerines, swallows or swift-like forms, pipits, warblers of damp edges, and other open-ground species that depend on the basin’s insect abundance. They do not nest in trees, for there are no trees; they use hummocks, crevices, reed edges, and low stone shelters. Their presence is like punctuation in a long pale sentence, but their dependence is deep: they are fed by the very wetness that makes the basin seem empty.

Larger predators may be less visible, but they are part of the same network. Snakes, if present, would move through the wettest cover and the edges of pans, hunting frogs, lizards, small mammals, fish, or young birds. Small carnivorous mammals—shrew-like, vole-like, or otter-like forms—would hunt by touch and smell in the cool darkness. Otters or otter-like animals, if the permanent pockets support enough fish or crustaceans, might pass through at night, leaving tracks in mud, catching prey with quick submerged searches. Bats may hunt above the pools when the air is thick with insects, their flight routes reading the basin not as land and water but as corridors of density: more midges here, fewer there; more dampness near the pan, drier salt farther away. They would roost in crevices, under stone overhangs, or in any shelter where warmth and mineral moisture persist.

Reptiles are especially well adapted to the basin because the open ground provides heat and the damp provides prey. Lizards move from sun-warmed stone to stone, their bodies dark with moisture when they cross wetter zones, then brightening quickly as they bask. They feed on flies, beetles, ants, termites if present, spiders, small worms, and the occasional tadpole or hatchling. Their motion is abrupt but economical, matching the landscape: quick bursts between shelters, long pauses, then another movement as conditions allow. Turtles may rest on hummocks of peat or low mudbanks, shells dark, pitted, stained with mineral deposits. When danger passes or the sun becomes too strong, they slide into the thin water, where their heads remain just below the surface, barely different from floating stones.

Small mammals live in the basin more through their traces than through their visible presence. At night, when the mud cools and the insects remain abundant, they leave tracks like fine handwriting across damp surfaces. Some are adapted to swimming and climbing between hummocks when water rises unevenly. Some burrow into peat or soft sediment. Some store plant material, shells, or aquatic vegetation in damp underground chambers. Their lives are governed less by the broad seasons than by the micro-seasons of water: which pools are still full, which rims have become too salty, which stones hide eggs or resting snails, which tracks will dry too quickly to be followed.

Even the most barren-looking patches are not empty. Salt crusts may hold specialized microbes and brine-tolerant organisms, especially where salinity varies with evaporation and rainfall. Drying mud may support bacteria and fungi that feed on organic matter trapped in thin layers. The underside of stones shelters colonies of springtails, mites, and small beetles. The rims of pools, marked with concentric lines of silt and salt, become highways and waiting rooms for frogs, snails, and aquatic insects. The wind may scatter fine salt dust across the flats, but beneath that dry-looking surface there is often a damp stratum no wider than a hand, and that stratum is alive.

The basin’s animals are not distributed according to size alone. Many of its larger residents are present only intermittently: birds arrive with insects, frogs emerge with rain, turtles bask when the warmth is right. The smaller residents are more continuous: microbes, films, snails under stones, larvae in wet hollows, midges that cannot leave dampness. In one sense, the basin is full all the time; in another, it is almost always only half-occupied, because so much of its life waits. Waiting is its normal state. A dried pan may look like absence, but beneath its surface are eggs, cocoons, dormant seeds, resting spores, aestivating frogs, sealed snails, and bacteria capable of surviving until water returns. The apparent emptiness is a pause, not a negation.

The food chain here is low, wide, and diffuse. Sunlight warms the wet surfaces. Microbial films and algae convert that warmth and light into living material. Tiny invertebrates eat that material. Small fish, tadpoles, snails, and crustaceans eat smaller organisms. Insects and larval insects become food for birds and bats. Snails and tadpoles become food for fish, frogs, turtles, and mammals. Dead animals fall into the mud, where bacteria and fungi break them down and return mineral wealth to the water. Because there is so little standing vegetation, the living mass is spread thinly across the surface, like a net of water instead of a forest of bodies. Nothing rises dramatically above the level of the ground, so almost every creature must be efficient in small ways: small mouths, flat bodies, quick reproduction, tolerance for drying, ability to move between pools without needing a river.

This is why the basin seems simultaneously empty and full. It has no trees, no walls, no fields, no clear human order. The visual scale is mineral and hydrological. But at the scale of skin, mud, water film, stone crevice, and salt grain, it is dense with activity. The basin’s abundance is not architectural. It does not make towers or thickets. It makes layers, coatings, burrows, tracks, egg masses, shell beds, resting hollows, and seasonal pulses. Its “construction” is biological but not monumental: a film over a pan, a mat in a hollow, a track across mud, a chorus of frogs in the dark, a ring of silt where water has retreated.

If one had to name the dominant inhabitant, it would be water itself, not as a substance only, but as a biological condition. Every resident is in some way a creature of dampness: the algae that cannot grow without a film of water; the snail that cannot move without moisture; the frog whose skin must not dry; the fish that waits in a hidden pocket; the midge whose larva cannot survive without saturation; the bird whose food appears only when the surface is wet enough to release insects. Even the lizards that bask in the sun are shaped by the same rule: they move by dampness, shelter from it, hunt because of it, and become less active when it withdraws.

The animals that live here are not the animals of a closed, shaded, or stable ecosystem. They are the animals of thresholds. They are edge-dwellers: the edge of a pan, the edge of a stone, the edge of salt and mud, the edge of rain and dryness, the edge of day and night, the edge of water and air. Many of them are flattened in form, because flattened bodies catch heat efficiently, shed wind, and fit under stones. Many are cryptic in color, because the open ground offers few hiding places and the basin’s palette is pale, gray, brown, ochre, and mineral. Many reproduce explosively because the favorable moments are brief. Many can endure deprivation by waiting. Many move in response to wetness with the certainty that water is both shelter and trap.

The basin’s night inhabitants are especially distinct. When the sun leaves, the stones give back the heat they absorbed, the moisture condenses, the frogs begin, and the insects become a buzzing background against which other sounds can be distinguished. Snails emerge. Worms may surface. Tadpoles become visible in the dark water. Small mammals cross the mud. Bats hunt over the hollows. Birds, if present at night, may be quieter, but herons and other wading predators sometimes move through pools with careful, stabbing motions. The mist that forms low above the basin blurs the distinction between land and water, between creature and surface. In the darkness, the basin does not become more populated; it becomes more audible. Its inhabitants reveal themselves through sound more than through form.

Thus the place is not a desolation. It is a wet, open world whose population is mostly hidden in the details.
