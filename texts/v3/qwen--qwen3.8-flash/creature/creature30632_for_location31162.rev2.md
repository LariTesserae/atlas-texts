There is no one there in the sense of a person, a ruler, a shepherd, a founder, a species that could call the basin “home.” There are no cities that were built with intention. There is no single creature to point to and say, *this one lives here*. If one asks, “Who lives in this place?” the answer must begin by saying: **no one**.

And then it must continue, because the place is overflowing with lives.

It is not empty. It is not sterile. It is populated at every scale, from the almost invisible crust-mats that glue the mineral floor together to the vast waxy architecture that seems to breathe, to the small pale animals moving through corridors like thoughts through a sleeping mind. The inhabitants are many, layered, interdependent, and strange. They are not plants and animals in the ordinary sense, though they borrow from both categories. They are a consortium of builders, grazers, dwellers, hunters, sifters, cementers, and inheritors.

What lives there is not a civilization with people, but a civilization without a self.

## The underlife: the crust-mat inhabitants

At the lowest level, before anything tall can stand, there is a living skin over the land.

The basin floor is not simply bare salt and silica. It is colonized by dry, scale-like organisms so numerous and intimate with the ground that they seem to be part of the earth itself. They are chalky, rose-grey, bone-white, pale amber, faintly translucent at their edges. They creep in sheets across the mineral crust. Some look like dried lichen; some like fused mineral foam; some like tiny tiles of hardened wax.

These are not mosses or algae in any familiar sense. They have no watery tissue, no swelling green cells, no dew-covered surfaces. They survive by dry chemistry: slow reactions in mineral-rich oils, waxy secretions, porous crystalline bodies. They feed on dust, on heat gradients, on the faint chemistry of the salt plates, on the tiny fragments of shed growth that settle over them. They do not drink, because there is nothing to drink; they absorb and transform dry material through surfaces sealed in wax and mineral.

Their role is foundational. They cement the ground. They trap fine dust. They heal cracks in the brittle crust. They create micro-habitats: shallow depressions, pore chambers, tiny ridges, and sheltered pits where other organisms can settle. They are the quiet workers of the basin, too small to be noticed except when their absence causes the architecture to collapse.

If one kneels and looks closely at a pale salt sheet, one might see its surface moving at almost no perceptible speed. Tiny grains shift. A thin film of waxed spores pulses outward from a crack. A flake of crust lifts, curls, then lies back down. The mat breathes chemically. It grows by accretion, by bonding, by patience. It does not reach for the sky. It prepares the ground so that others can.

These organisms are the true first inhabitants of the basin.

## The wax-stalks: the living architecture

Above them rise the builders.

The most conspicuous inhabitants are the pale stalks, columns, fronds, shelves, and fused stems that make the land look like a city. They cannot be cleanly called plants, because they do not have the water-dependent physiology of Earth’s green flora. Their tissues are dry, fibrous, porous, sealed with waxy skins, strengthened by mineral grains. Their “leaves” are rigid plates like cured horn. Their “stems” are hollow columns like dried reed, polished bone, or candle wax that has hardened around an inner lattice of fiber.

And yet they are alive.

Many of these structures are not single organisms. A tall stalk may be a colony, a fused community of smaller stalks that grew too close and became inseparable. A wall may be several competing species pressed together by light, space, and wind until their surfaces welded into one shared body. A platform may begin as a branch, then accept the attachment of epiphytic sheets, dust-trapping mats, and resin-coated fibers from other organisms, becoming a composite living floor.

These are the most obvious inhabitants, but they are also the habitat. They build the city by the simple fact of growing, and the city becomes their body.

Each wax-stalk has a different internal economy. Inside its hollow channels, instead of sap and water, it carries dense, slow-moving liquids: amber oils, mineral gels, waxy resins, and dry organic fluids that remain stable under warm air. These substances do not run; they creep, swell, contract, lubricate, transmit pressure, and store heat. At dawn, as the basin warms, the fluids become slightly less viscous. The structures relax. Tissues flex. Membranes that closed overnight tighten or open, revealing dry inner veins colored like pale glass or old resin. At midday, the stalks stand almost bleached, their surfaces shining with dry, waxy light. By late afternoon, the fluids slow again, and the whole city feels darker, denser, as if it is pulling itself inward for night.

The stalks grow toward light, toward one another, and into one another. A pair of neighboring stems may bend slowly until their sides touch. Over years, the contact point softens, fuses, and becomes a shared arch. A dense cluster becomes a wall. A wall overtops, cracks under its own pressure, and becomes a room. A shelf grows from a joint and becomes a floor. A dead stem does not rot in wet decay; it dries, hardens, becomes mineral-rich, and forms a foundation for new tissue. The dead are incorporated. The city consumes its own history.

They have no intention, but they have direction. They live by phototropism, heat, touch, gravity, and chemical signaling. They know one another by pressure. If a stalk is pruned, nearby tissue grows to protect the wound. If a corridor is grazed open, surrounding branches lean away or seal shut according to the light that enters. If a structure bears weight, its fibers thicken. If the wind alters an air channel, growth adjusts. They are not engineers, but they are endlessly responsive, and their responsiveness accumulates into architecture.

They are the primary citizens of the pale basin.

## The pruners: small six-legged maintainers

Beneath and among the stalks move animals.

Most of them are small, pale, and dry-bodied. They do not have the wet sheen of Earth creatures; their skin is waxy, scaly, or powdered with fine mineral dust. They do not glisten with sweat, because there is no sweating problem; they seal their bodies against a world that would otherwise draw every liquid into itself. Their fluids are as dense and strange as the plants’—oils, pastes, mineral-rich humours kept private inside membranes.

The most common are the pruners: six-legged grazers with hard mouths, careful feet, and flat bodies that can slip between stalks. They look like a fusion of beetle, springtail, and pale arachnid, though none of those names truly fit. Their color is chalk, bone, candle wax, faint rose-grey, sometimes translucent at the joints. Their legs are slender but strong, ending in dry claws that do not slip on mineral surfaces.

They feed on the edges of the living architecture.

They do not consume plants so much as edit them. They bite into waxy fronds, scrape resinous bark-plates, pierce young stalks, and drink the thick internal oils that seep slowly from the wounds. They eat the outer skins of growths, the fungal-like layers that collect dust, and the soft new tissue at the tips of stems. Their mouths are not for chewing in a watery sense; they are rasping, pinching, grinding tools designed for dry material. They crack flakes of mineral-fiber and process them into paste, waste, or binding residue.

This grazing is not destruction. It is maintenance.

When the pruners move through a dense cluster, they open gaps. New growth follows those gaps, curving, thickening, forming shelves and arches. A corridor may be born because a line of pruners fed along a seam for generations. A plaza may exist because a species of grazers keeps a particular area from being colonized by taller stalks. The streets of the city are not roads; they are wounds that have learned to stay open.

The pruners communicate by vibration and scent. They tap their legs against stems. They press their abdomens against surfaces. They release dry chemical traces that evaporate as faint warm mineral vapors. Because sound is smothered here, their language is not loud. It is localized, structural, felt through the bodies of the plants themselves. A pruner may walk along a stalk and send a pulse into the network; another creature three levels below may answer by scraping a shelf, a tiny click that travels through fused tissue and is felt rather than heard.

They live in hollow stems, beneath shelves, inside packed mats, and in the shallow dens they scrape into living surfaces. Some remain solitary. Others gather in loose associations, not herds, not colonies, but seasonal concentrations that move through the city like blood cells through a network of rooms.

They are the editors of the basin.

## The shelf-dwellers and pore tenants

Higher in the architecture, in the overlapping platforms and shadowed galleries, live other inhabitants adapted to the vertical world.

Some are flat-bodied organisms that resemble living tiles. They press against the undersides of shelves or the curved inner walls of arches, their bodies scaled with pale mineral powder. They are dust-sifters and film-eaters. As warm air rises through the packed city, fine particles settle: salt dust, shed frond flakes, pollen-like grains, tiny fragments of dead tissue. The shelf-dwellers feed on this accumulated dry matter. Their bodies are porous, ridged, layered, built to catch and grind what drifts through their dark little rooms.

Others are seed-tenant species: small pale creatures that do not travel far. They live permanently inside the hollows of stalks, under flanges of bark-like growth, or beneath the overhangs of fused stems. Their reproduction is tied to the architecture. A female of one such species may burrow into a growing stem at dawn, when the tissue is softer and the internal fluids are warm, and seal herself inside a chamber made by the plant’s own expansion. There she lays dust-hardened eggs that will hatch into flat larvae that immediately begin eating the inner fibers and excreting a binding resin. Over generations, their activity helps hollow out new passages, which later become corridors for pruners, dens for predators, or chambers for spore-cultures.

There are also mineral-gatherers, small pale beings with broad, shield-like bodies and short legs, who travel in dry herds along the edges of salt plates. They consume dust, crystalline fragments, and the outer coatings of stalks. Their waste is not liquid; it is powder, paste, or glue-like dry residue. This waste becomes part of the basin’s fabric. It cements cracks, enriches growth edges, or forms the substrate for new mats. In this way, even their excrement becomes building material.

The city is made of life, but it is also made of its residents’ byproducts: secretions, shed flakes, excreted binders, mineralized corpses, packed dust, dried oils, and the slow transformation of one body into another’s foundation.

## The dry scavengers

Nothing is wasted.

When a stalk cracks, when a grazer dies, when a shelf collapses, when a platform sheds its outer skin, the material does not disappear into wet rot. It dries. It hardens. It becomes part of the architecture. But even dry death has inhabitants.

There are scavenger organisms that specialize in mineralized remains. Some are small and hard-bodied, living in the spaces between old and new growth. They gnaw at desiccated stems, ingest brittle fibers, and excrete fine sand-like pellets that become the substrate for crust-mats. Others are larger, flatter things that spread across the surfaces of dead stalks, feeding on the last trapped oils and the thin residual layers of living tissue at the edges. They are pale, jointed, and slow, with mouths like dry scrapers.

These creatures do not merely consume; they transform. They grind dead architecture into usable particles. They incorporate waste into the city’s continuing growth. A dead stalk may become a tomb-like column, then a hollow tower, then a nursery for seedlings, then a wall for another structure. Death here is not erased. It is inherited.

If one wanted to name the scavengers simply, one might call them the recyclers of the dry world, but that word is too human. They are not performing a service for the basin. They are the basin performing itself through them.

## The hunters

Even in a quiet place, there are those who kill.

The predators of the basin are not large open-field beasts with roaring voices. They are not pack hunters thundering across plains. There are no plains to run across, no open ground to chase over, no soft wet soil to smell. The predators are still, elongated, waxy-skinned, and patient.

They live in the architecture: in the shadowed galleries under shelves, in hollow stems, in the spaces between fused trunks, in the dark interiors of arches, in cracks that look like ordinary mineral seams until one of them moves. They have long limbs, dry claws, flat heads, and narrow mouths that open not with a wet tear but with the creak of sealed organic plates. Their bodies are pale, sometimes almost invisible against the stalks: chalk, bone, old amber, dried wax, faint rose-grey.

They hunt by stillness.

They do not chase prey across open ground. They wait. They listen through vibration. When a pruner walks along a stem, the pressure of its legs travels into the surrounding tissue. A predator pressed against the inside of that stem feels it. A change in weight. A slight flex of fiber. A pattern of taps. It moves with dry precision, closing joints, sliding through passages, emerging onto a shelf or shaft without the sound of breath or footsteps.

Its attack is not a howl but a clasp.

The prey is seized by limbs that lock like dried hooks. The predator uses chemical methods as much as mechanical force: a dry secretion may soften the victim’s outer coating; a pressure-induced rupture may expose internal oils; a bite into a joint may inject a trace that causes the body to seal around the wound, trapping it. These are not violent, dramatic kills in the human sense. They are efficient, quiet, almost surgical. The predator does not drink blood. It consumes oil, resin, mineral-rich tissue, and the slow internal fluids of the body.

When the hunt is over, the remains are scattered in dry fragments: wax-flakes, shell-pieces, powdery residue. These are gathered by smaller scavengers. Even violence here becomes construction.

The predators reproduce rarely. Their young are born in sheltered hollows, waxy and pale, unable to move quickly. They remain inside the structure for their first cycles, feeding on secretions and soft inner fibers, learning the city by touch and vibration before they emerge to hunt. Their earliest experience is not sight, but the shape of corridors, the direction of warm air, the rhythm of growth.

## The dust-riders and light-dwellers

In a place without water, the air becomes a medium.

There are organisms that live largely in the dry internal currents of the city. They are not birds, not insects in any exact sense, but small, membrane-bodied creatures that exploit the thermal movements of the basin. They resemble sheets of pale horn or translucent dried wax, with thin ribbed supports and a flat, almost paper-like body. They have no wet eyes, no open ears. They navigate by pressure, temperature, and vibration.

At certain times of day, warm air rises from the salt crust in slow broad columns. Cool air settles into denser districts of growth. Between them, fine dust moves: salt powder, pollen-like grains, fragments of shed tissue, microscopic spores, tiny chips of waxy fiber. The dust-riders live in this haze. They spread their membranes and tilt through the corridors, riding the slow internal breezes between stalks. They do not flap; they glide by adjustment of angle, heat, and weight. When they land on a shelf, they make a soft tick, barely louder than the flexing of a stem.

They are important because they move life across the city.

Some carry spores. Some carry seed-grains. Some carry fragments of living tissue that can attach elsewhere and begin a new colony. Their bodies are coated in dry oils that help dust cling to them; as they glide through the haze, they become mobile ecosystems. A single dust-rider may carry the eggs of crust-mats, the spores of shelf-films, and the mineral grains that help a new growth bind. It does not pollinate in the way a bee pollinates a flower. It seeds a dry world by accidental transport, by friction, by the chemistry of surfaces.

At midday, when the crust reflects strongly, they become almost invisible, tiny glints moving through the warm channels. At dawn, they rest on the upper surfaces of stalks, membranes folded dry against their bodies. At late afternoon, they drift lower, seeking the warmer interiors before the fluids of the structures slow.

They belong to the sky without leaving it. They are not animals of the ground. They are animals of the city’s breath.

## The fungi-like intermediates

Between what one would call plant and what one would call animal, there are pale fibrous organisms that grow in the dark interiors of stalks and under shelves.

They are not mushrooms, not mold, not anything wet. They are dry fungal analogues: lattice-forming, fibrous, mineral-coated bodies that spread through hollow chambers, bind together loose material, and feed on trapped residues. They give structure to the spaces inside the architecture. They are often the reason a hollow stem does not collapse under its own weight. Their fibers interlock like dry thread, their mineral coatings harden the inner walls, and their slow growth helps separate compartments, create pockets, and stabilize rooms within the living city.

Some are cultivated by the inhabitants. The pruners and scavengers spread their spore-fragments while moving, and the fungus-like organisms grow in the paths of passage. Over time, entire wall systems become composite structures: plant-fiber, mineral plate, animal scrape-mark, fungal network, crust-mat, and dry oil seal. It becomes impossible to say where one begins and the next ends.

This is the essential ecology of the place: boundaries dissolve. What appears as a floor may be animal residue, plant tissue, and mineral paste. What appears as a wall may be a fused colony of stalks. What appears as a street may be a grazing wound. What appears as a building may be a corpse, a nest, and a nursery.

The inhabitants are not separate from their habitat.

## The collective: the basin as one slow inhabitant

If one insists on asking who lives there, there is another answer, larger and stranger.

The basin itself lives.

Not as a mind. Not as a god. Not as a creature with intention. But as a system so complete that it behaves like a body. The crust-mats are its skin. The stalks are its skeleton. The platforms are its organs. The corridors are its passages. The pruners are its maintenance cells. The predators are its corrections. The dust-riders are its circulation. The dead are its memory. The warm air is its breath. The chemical vapors are its speech.

The city does not need a builder because building is the default outcome of growth, competition, pressure, and death.

It does not have a center because any point can become a district if the conditions allow. It does not have a border because the horizon only repeats more of itself. It does not have a ruler because leadership would be useless; there are no decisions to make in the human sense. The whole basin makes one decision by being what it is: to continue.

At dawn, the city relaxes.

At midday, it brightens and hardens.

At dusk, it slows and deepens in color.

When dust drifts, it feels the movement in the upper membranes. When grazers press through passages, the architecture learns the pressure and adjusts. When a stalk fuses to a wall, the city’s geometry changes. When a predator closes a joint into a hollow, a chamber becomes sealed. Nothing is instantaneous, but nothing is still. The landscape is under permanent, patient construction.

It is not ruined. It is not ancient in the sense of being finished. It is young in erosion because there is little erosion. It is ancient in accumulation because generation after generation of living tissue has pressed, fused, dried, and built. The city is old as process and young as form.

## What it feels like to live there

To live here, one does not need water, open space, soft soil, or loud weather. One needs sealing, porosity, chemical balance, thermal rhythm, and the ability to endure dryness without brittleness.

The inhabitants are not fragile because they do not rely on wetness. They are dry-tough. They are built to survive without liquid abundance. Their bodies are like the environment’s own solution: compact, layered, waxy, mineral-strengthened, and internally sealed.

They have no need for rivers because the landscape is not shaped by flowing external liquid. They have no need for lakes because the city stores dry chemistry inside itself. They have no need for open sky because they are shaped by shadowed, layered light. They have no need for loud voices because the world absorbs noise and transmits pressure through tissue.

A creature here does not stand in a meadow and call across a hill. It waits. It touches. It taps. It tastes the air for resin and salt. It feels the temperature of a stem through its feet. It navigates by the warmth of a shaft, the tilt of a shelf, the vibration of a grazer above, the faint chemical trace of a predator below.

It lives in a world where everything is adjacent to something else. There is no emptiness. There is no bare space. There is only packed presence, and within that presence, movement.

Even the silence is a presence. It is not the silence of death. It is the silence of density, of absorption, of millions of small processes occurring at once without producing clamor. The quietness is the place’s way of saying: we are here, we are alive, and we do not need to announce ourselves.

## The inhabitants, named by function

If one wanted to describe them without inventing false categories, one could call them by what they do:

- the **crust-welders**, who bind the mineral floor and create living ground;
- the **wax-stalks**, who grow upward and build the city from their own bodies;
- the **shelf-fusers**, who flatten and extend, creating floors and rooms;
- the **pruners**, who open corridors and maintain passages;
- the **dust-sifters**, who consume settled matter and turn it into binding residue;
- the **hollow-tenants**, who live inside the stems and shape internal chambers;
- the **dry grazers**, who harvest plant tissue without water and recycle it into structure;
- the **stalk-riders**, who glide through thermal currents and distribute life across the city;
- the **hollow-hunters**, who ambush from the architecture and maintain population balance;
- the **incorporators**, who consume dead matter and mineralize it;
- the **lattice-growers**, who form fungal-like networks inside walls;
- the **memory-organisms**, whose generations leave layers, scars, and inherited forms in the city’s fabric.

None of these are separate enough to stand alone. The crust-welders prepare the ground for the stalk-builders. The stalk-builders shelter the pruners. The pruners open paths for the hunters. The hunters regulate the pruners. The dust-riders connect districts. The dead become walls. The waste becomes cement. The whole system is one continuous act of living architecture.

## The city without a people

So whoever lives there is not a people, but a plurality.

It is a world of builders that do not know they build. It is an ecology that has become urban by habit. It is a plain that became a city because density is the natural consequence of life without liquid abundance and without open ground. It is a place where architecture is not imposed but accumulated. Where the ground is not soil but a living crust. Where the air is not neutral but charged with dust, oil, mineral scent, and faint chemical speech. Where nothing is clean, because everything is inhabited. Where nothing is empty, because even apparent vacancy is a passage maintained by movement, feeding, breathing, or waiting.

There are no humans. No machines. No roads made by planning. No agriculture sown by intention. No city laid out by a blueprint.

And yet there is a metropolis.

Its citizens are the stalks, the mats, the grazers, the hunters, the dust-riders, the dead, the waste, the secretions, and the slow warm pulse of the whole. Its streets are grazing paths. Its floors are grown shelves. Its walls are fused organisms. Its districts are ecological tendencies. Its weather is internal, dry, chemical. Its sounds are clicks, ticks, creaks, rustles of brittle tissue, and the faint shift of dust through hollow rooms.

The greatest inhabitant of the basin is not a single creature. It is the collective, the process, the living architecture itself.

If one asks, “Who lives there?” the answer is:

**No one.**

If one asks, “What lives there?” the answer is:

**Everything, at once, in layers, without water, without noise, without intention, building a pale city from its own bodies.**
