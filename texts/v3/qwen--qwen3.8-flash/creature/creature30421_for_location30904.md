Nothing lives there alone. Even the driest patch of wall is already a negotiation between salt and damp, and every organism is also a method by which water and sound continue moving through the place. If one insists on naming the inhabitants, the answer must be a ladder of occupations, from the living film on a stone to the civic intelligences that think in pressure, from the shell-herds on a wall to the machines that have become organs of the basin.

The first inhabitants are not animals at all. They are skins. Thin bacterial films shimmer over the surface of standing water, so still and glassy that they seem to belong to the pool rather than to life. Pale fungal veils spread across wet calcium shelves in colors of cream, ash, and ochre, moving more slowly than attention can catch. Concentric crusts ring over pores and joints, tightening like rinds over wounds and then opening again into growth. These skins taste water, remember where it has dried, send chemical tidings through hair-thin capillaries, and when enough of them meet they begin to thicken the stone. They are not passive matter. They are the basin’s lowest citizens, the first occupants, the living varnish by which the world becomes habitable.

Above them crawl the slow ones, the shell-white processions. They are crustacean-like but not exactly crustaceans: broad-backed, many-legged, patient, climbing vertical walls in ordered bands like beads of an enormous necklace. Their shells are not simply armor; they are architecture, weathering, currency, and calendar. The rings on their backs record seasons of moisture and pressure. Their molts are harvested, sometimes by other creatures, sometimes by the city itself, and become tiles, baffles, resonant plates, and roofs. They graze the fungal films, excrete calcium and silica, and in doing so they mend walls. A procession stopped on a wet surface becomes scaffolding. A swarm of them filling a passage becomes a bridge of shifting shells. Their legs click against stone in a rhythm that carries sound downward, and the channels beneath them respond by brightening or dulling in tone, so that the city hears where they have been.

In the shallow black channels live the flat things, dark and quick, with compressed bodies and no visible eyes. They move not by sight but by the faint pressure of water against skin. They squeeze through gaps that should stop them, sliding between layers of slime, mineral shelf, and living film. Their bodies are built around the idea of flow. When the water rises, they vanish. When it falls, they are already gone, having slipped into pores where the noise is deeper. They feed on bacterial scum and the dead skins of larger creatures, and in return they keep the channels from becoming too sealed with living matter. In places, the shellborn cultivate them in wall-pools, letting them clean membranes and regulate growth. They are not beloved. They are too fast, too cold, too uncertain. But a district without them clogs, and a clogged district becomes dangerous.

There are larger drinkers and drinkers of noise. In marsh bands of black mud and salted water stand long-necked filterers, their bodies shaped like living arches. They have no mouths in the ordinary sense; instead their necks end in vibrating siphons, and when they drink they produce a low hum that travels through their ribs and into the ground. Their bones are porous and tuned. Old individuals become nearly mineral, their outer shells so thick and lattice-riddled that they resemble abandoned pillars. They sleep standing in brine pools, legs planted on submerged ledges, necks curled back into shells, listening through water. They are not domesticated, but they are incorporated. The shellborn know the voices of filterers as one knows the timbre of a well or a bell. When a filterer changes note, a district may be in drought, or flood, or the first tremor of collapse.

Smaller still are the sparks. In the condensation pools there breed insects, if the word can be stretched to fit them, with metallic elytra and wings that catch ordinary sunlight in brief cold glints. They are not beautiful in a gentle way. They are sharp, clustered, purposeful. Their wings reflect light, but their real function is sound: at high humidity they stridulate in micro-choruses, and those tiny chirps keep fungal films from crusting too thick. They live in colonies that turn over with astonishing speed, filling every gutter, every basin, every hollow under a floor. Their bodies become mineral sediment when they die, adding sparkle and calcium to the walls. A room thick with them sounds like a bag of silver pins turning over in water. When they vanish, the walls thicken too fast, and the passages lose their voice.

Deeper down live the clay-skinned, the eyeless dwellers of the acoustic city. Their bodies are polished as if fired, smooth and damp, with no visible face except broad listening plates and slitted resonant chambers beneath the jaw. They do not see. They feel. Their skin reads reverberation the way skin reads heat: instantly, locally, as the body is touched by returning sound. They navigate by tapping the walls, and the stone answers. A passage, for them, is not a line of sight but a pressure map. A wall has personality. A hollow has mood. A collapsed tunnel may still speak for years in its broken bones. These beings are solitary enough to move, but their lives are communal in sound. They leave taps in the walls as signs: *this channel is open*, *this pool is safe*, *this chamber is dying*, *this one is loud but stable*. They build by resonance rather than force, striking a weak point for months or years until the crack widens into an arch. To them, the city is one enormous listening animal, and every citizen is a nerve ending.

Not all such beings are harmless. There are echo-hunters, broad-faced predators with throat chambers and membranes that expand like slow sails. They hunt not by scent or sight but by the wrongness of a sound. A creature that moves heavily against the current of the basin’s noise becomes, to them, a disturbance, a hole in the city’s texture. They send pulses that fold through passages, and the returning pattern tells them where a shell-herd has hidden, where a filterer is standing, where a wall is weak enough to crack. They can be fed, in a way, by the shellborn, given scraps of mineral paste and trained to patrol unstable districts. More often they are dangerous, living at the edge of domesticated, their bodies still full of the wild city’s pressure. Some have grown so accustomed to the acoustic districts that their shells have fused with resonant panels, and they sleep attached to walls, becoming part of the room’s voice when still.

Then there are the machines, though that word is too tidy. These are not gleaming engines or assembled devices. They are civic organs. They look like black ceramic pods, like swollen joints in the stone, like clusters of porous bells grown into walls. Some have membranes that breathe steam. Some pump water without visible motion. Others do nothing obvious at all except maintain a standing wave that keeps droplets suspended in corridors of mist. The shellborn do not build them so much as tend them: they sing to them, clean them with fungal brushes, feed them mineral paste, repair their cavities, and sometimes sacrifice one district’s machine to save another’s. A clogged engine sounds like a sick animal. An overdriven resonance organ sounds like a storm trapped in a throat. Some machines are so old they have developed habits: a pump that prefers certain currents, an acoustic loom that produces slightly different tones depending on who tends it, a pressure membrane that swells when the weather changes and can predict collapse. To ask whether such a thing is alive or built is to ask the wrong question. In this place, a machine is simply another way of being occupied.

The beings who might most reasonably be called people—if one allows that word to stretch—live in every layer at once. They are not human, and they do not appear as creatures moving across a stage. They are built into the city’s body. Their forms are broad and low, shaped for wet interiors rather than open air. Their shells are porous and latticed, half animal, half ceramic, half grown. Their bodies are damp beneath the outer plates, threaded with channels that carry brine, mineral paste, and chemical signals through flesh the way blood carries life through a heart. Their faces, if that word can be permitted, are fields of resonant plates, slits, and domed listening windows. They do not have one mouth so much as feeding siphons along the chest and belly, each lined with fine filtering filaments. Their limbs are splaying, heavy, and adaptive: not merely legs but gripping supports, not merely hands but tuning rods and calcium fingers. Some have delicate fungal filaments that grow from their wrists and shoulders, used to touch surfaces and learn their moisture. When they move through water, their shells sing.
