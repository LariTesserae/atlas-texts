Who lives in the Latticed March?

At first: no one.

There are no footfalls in the wet courts. No nests tucked beneath the roof-leaves. No burrows through the fibrous plates. No trails worn by the crossing of bodies. No droppings. No chewed edges. No feathers, no shells, no fur, no bone. The architecture may look as if it waits for a traveler, a laborer, a priest, a child, a beast of burden, a citizen—but no such form appears. The empty distance between buttresses remains empty. The channels remain unwalked. The arches carry only air, light, and moving water.

If that is what “inhabitant” means, the March is uninhabited.

But the March is not empty.

The emptiness is not an absence. It is a condition of the life that is there: a life so continuous, so distributed, so architectural that it cannot be found as a body moving through a place. The place itself is the body. The citizen is not one person, nor many persons, but a living pattern that has learned to keep itself alive. The Latticed March does not contain a creature. It is a creature-like process made of plants, water, heat, pressure, and time.

If a single word must be used, the resident of the Latticed March is **the March itself**: not a city, not an ecosystem in any ordinary sense, but a vast colonial being whose organs are buttresses, whose blood is warm water, whose breath is steam, whose memory is growth, and whose thoughts are slow rearrangements of stress and fluid.

## A Body Without a Center

There is no head. No heart. No torso. No limbs set aside from the ground. If one walks onto a broad platform and sees a hundred-pace tower of woven stem-fiber fanning into a roof-disc, the tower is not a building beside the ground. It is a protrusion of the ground into air. If one stands in a court of pale sealed fiber, the court is not a floor. It is a living surface, stretched and hardened by growth, supported by hidden chambers below. If a channel cuts across the plateau, it is not a river carved through soil. It is a vein in a living wall.

The March has no central place where “itself” resides. Its identity is spread through gradients.

A buttress may know itself as weight.

A pool may know itself as contained water.

A membrane may know itself as tension against pressure.

A channel may know itself as direction.

A fracture may know itself as urgency.

A dying plate may know itself as substrate for new growth.

None of these is a separate animal. None is an individual with boundaries the way an insect or bird has boundaries. They are parts of a continuous, self-correcting whole. The whole is composed of many plant forms—structural, waterproofing, draining, storing, sealing, growing rapidly, growing slowly, dying into scaffold, reborn as surface—but it is not a simple colony where one species does one thing. It is a negotiated landscape. The plants do not cooperate through thought. They cooperate through water pressure, chemical films, shading, root contact, mineral exchange, mechanical strain, and the time-scale of growth.

Still, the result is a unity so strong that any single feature can be read as an organ.

The roof-leaves are its skin against rain and sun.

The weeping columns are its glands.

The hollow stems are its pipes, bones, and resonating cavities.

The sap-sealed floors are its membranes and scar tissues.

The channels are its circulatory system.

The reservoirs are its bladders.

The buttresses are its tendons and ribs.

The pressure chambers are its valves.

The wind through the lattice is not merely weather. It is breath moving through a body that has learned to exhale in chords.

## How It Wakes

The March changes its state with the day, and those changes are not passive. They are the closest thing it has to waking, resting, drinking, stretching, and attending.

In the cool early hours, water has gathered in low pools and swollen underground reservoirs. The upper plates are firm but moist. The fiber surfaces are pale and tight. The air is still or moving only in shallow drafts pulled through the hollow cores of columns. Condensation beads along the undersides of broad leaf-roofs. Each bead is a tiny stored weight.

As the highland sun strengthens, the March becomes louder.

Heat moves through the lower wet layers. Turgor pressure changes inside living walls. Stems swell. Channels narrow or widen. Membranes stiffen, then relax. A platform may rise almost imperceptibly, its edge curling as if lifted by a finger beneath. In other places, old parts fail: a sealed plate cracks with a dry report, a hollow column shifts, a support takes on new strain and begins depositing fresh fibers around the point of pressure.

The March does not walk through these changes. It **passes through them**, the way a breathing chest passes through breath.

At midday, when heat builds in the lower chambers, water can force itself upward through narrow valves and burst out with sharp rhythmic thumps. These thumps are not mechanical accidents, not random geysers. They are the March relieving itself. The chambers fill, pressure rises, water seeks release, and the structure answers by opening, expelling, cooling, and redirecting. After a thump, the nearby pools ripple. After several thumps, the pattern of flow may change. Channels that were quiet may begin carrying warm, dark water toward new margins. Reservoirs that were full may lower their surfaces, revealing pale growth rings of mineral and plant film.

The March’s “morning” is swelling.

Its “noon” is tension.

Its “evening” is condensation.

Its “night” is repair.

None of these is metaphor only. Each is a physical state of the whole. The living plate rises and falls slightly. Water moves between storage and surface. Fibers tighten and loosen. Chemical gradients shift. Light changes the angle at which membranes stretch. Wind changes the pitch of the hollows. If the March has a daily mood, it is not emotion as an animal would have it. It is systemic tone: hydrated, strained, leaking, cooling, hardening, healing.

A visitor who stays long enough may begin to notice that the landscape has habits.

A particular roof-leaf releases water at the same hour, with a groan and a splash that echoes across a court.

A certain arch hums when the wind turns east.

A channel that ran high yesterday becomes sluggish today.

A new fissure appears along a pale seam, not because the ground was weak, but because the body underneath shifted.

A pool that was empty at dawn is full at sunset, not because rain fell there, but because hidden pores released their stored water.

These are not signs of individual animals. They are signs of a living system maintaining itself.

## What It Senses

The March cannot see, smell, taste, or hear as animals do. It has no eyes, no nose, no mouth, no ears, no nervous system in any familiar form. But it is not without response.

It senses **pressure**.

That is its primary sense. The entire construction is a conversation about pressure: the pressure of water beneath the plates, the pressure of heat in sealed chambers, the pressure of air in hollow stems, the pressure of light on growing surfaces, the pressure of one buttress resting against another, the pressure of a roof-leaf holding stored condensation. Every structure is an answer to a force that would otherwise break it.

It senses **strain**.

Fiber tightens under tension. Stem walls thicken where weight bears down. Cracks form where stress concentrates. New growth appears where old tissue is pulling apart. The March knows where it is overloaded. It may not “know” in the way a person knows an injury, but it responds: strengthening here, dying there, redirecting fluid, splitting along the path of least resistance, rebuilding along the new margin.

It senses **moisture**.

The weeping columns do not merely exude water by accident. They are tuned to the state of the substrate. When the lower reservoir is full, they flow. When the plate dries from above, they open. When pressure rises, they may release in sudden glassy beads that scatter into the channels. The living membranes beneath the roof-plates swell or contract with dampness. The surface film changes from matte to glossy. The whole March is a humidity meter made of growth.

It senses **light**.

Not through vision, but through growth direction, heat, and chemical change. The upper leaves angle themselves according to available light. Pale ribbed stems expose themselves to brightness. Hanging fibers shade certain pools while leaving others open. The roof-structures cast moving shadows across the courts because the leaves are never still, but that shadow-play is not merely atmospheric. It alters temperature. It alters evaporation. It tells the plants below where moisture will collect and where it will dry. Light is a command.

It senses **vibration**.

The March is loud. Wind moving through open lattice creates deep shifting moans. Hollow stems resonate. Water surging through channels produces percussion. A cracking plate sends a report that travels across wet floors. A sudden release of pressure can echo for miles. These sounds are not only sounds. They are signals. They travel through solid fiber, hollow air, and liquid water, and they alter the structure’s response. A vibration may open a valve. A pressure change may move a membrane. A crack may be the announcement that a new edge will become a new home for growth.

It has no face, but it has fields.

It has no voice, but it has tones.

It has no attention, but it has sensitivities so distributed that it seems to turn toward the air and water around it.
