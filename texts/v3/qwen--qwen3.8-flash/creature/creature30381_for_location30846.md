Because the sea is young, the first residents are not large enough to name. They begin as film, as bloom, as a thin living varnish over the manufactured stone. What the maintenance engineer intended as corrosion control has become, by now, something closer to a nervous system of the trench. It spreads along ceramic plates, threads the expansion seams, creeps over flanges, and settles into the micro-grooves where water pressure is always trying to pry the world apart. In the bright cycle it turns a pale oxidized bronze, the color of old pennies and wet skin; in the dim amber hour it bruises into violet-copper, and in the deepest shadowed galleries near the exhaust vents it takes on a faint green glow, because the sea has learned to make its own phosphorescence, or because the people have encouraged it.

This film is not a single organism. It is a community: iron-feeding bacteria, salt-dwelling yeasts, mineral-etching lichens, photosynthetic cells adapted to the false daylight, microscopic grazers, viral clouds, spore motes, and a dozen smaller lives that have no common word in the old surface languages. It eats the made surface and it also protects it. It seals hairline fractures with mineral cement. It coats the serial marks stamped into the basalt composite until the letters become soft as old handwriting. It traps drifting silt and turns that silt into a thin artificial soil, and where that soil collects on a flange or a buttress shelf, other things begin to live.

The walls, therefore, are forests without trunks.

Flanges form thick overhangs. Expansion seams become dark vertical caves. Conduit channels are corridors. The cast steps descending to the water are ledges, plazas, shelves, and thresholds. To an untrained eye they remain engineering, but to the life that has taken up residence they are habitat. The trench does not have beaches; it has maintenance geometry, and that geometry has been colonized so thoroughly that it now looks like geology seen through a strange, bolted lens.

The first visible animals are small, plated, and deliberate. They live on the number-hermits, though they are not hermits in any human sense. They are crustacean-like things, perhaps descendants of laboratory stocks, perhaps accidental arrivals sealed in cooling water and maintenance supplies, perhaps engineered from the beginning and only now allowed to be wild. Their bodies are segmented in shades of grey and rust, with jointed legs that move over wet alloy with surprising softness. They do not grow shells of calcium so much as they collect shells of the sea’s own construction. They attach broken pieces of ceramic plate, chips of composite, thin strips of riveted alloy, fragments of insulation, and especially those small plates stamped with serial marks. The marks give them purchase; they also give them identity. A number-hermit may wear the same half-lettered plate for decades, growing around it, sealing it with its own mineral mucus, until the creature becomes a moving fragment of the trench itself.

They graze the seam-film. They clean the flanges. They climb the cast steps in slow processions when the lamps dim and the water cools. They tap one another’s shells with their claws, not as speech but as a kind of counting—pressure, rhythm, repetition. If you stand on a maintenance ledge and look down at the wet step where the water touches, you may see dozens of them arranged like living rivets, their plates catching the broken ladder of reflected light.

Higher on the walls, near the intake rings, other things have learned to make a living from the water’s motion. The engineered sea is full of currents because it is built to move; the turbines, pumps, ballast systems, and flow channels are not merely machines to the animals that live there. They are weather. They are hunting grounds. They are the places where food appears.

Around the intake rings grow the ballast anemones.

They are colonial polyps, pale when expanded, amber at their roots, with tentacles so thin they look like strands of stripped wire. They open during strong flow and close in stillness. Each one resembles a small sunburst of wet metal. They feed on the drifting microbial rain of the sea—spores, detritus, mineral flakes, dead skin from larger animals, eggs, fragments of film. But they do not merely wait. They pulse. Their bodies draw water in and expel it, and because there are thousands of them clustered along the rings, they create their own small current around the intake, filtering not only food but also the taste of the sea.

They are dangerous in their own way. A person not adapted to this water may feel their tentacles first as softness, then as a prickling burn, then as a numb cold that travels up the limb. The anemones secrete a mild paralytic into their sting, not to kill but to stop prey from being swept away before digestion. In the warm exhaust plumes, they grow faster. They cluster on the lower ballast platforms like pale fur. Their dead bodies sink and become mats of mineralized tissue that other animals later scrape clean.

Inside the submerged galleries and pipe farms, where the fabricated terrain is shaped into channels for flow, live the insulation worms. They are blind and segmented, no thicker than a finger and longer than an arm, with pale skin the color of old cable foam. They nest inside conduits, along expansion seams, in the hollows behind buttresses. They eat the living film and the mineral cement it produces.
