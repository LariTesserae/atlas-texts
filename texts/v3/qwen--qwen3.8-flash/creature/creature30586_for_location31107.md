No one lives there in the way a heron lives in a wetland, or a deer in a forest, or a fish in a river. That would be too simple. The marsh does not host populations so much as intensities, habits, pressures, slow accidents that become necessary. If something is alive here, it usually looks first like geology, then like weather, then, only after long watching, like a creature. And even then it may not be a creature. It may be a way of staying together.

The smallest residents are the first to understand.

Across every wet stone there is a skin: not moss, not slime alone, but a bacterial fur, a mineral lace, a colony so thin it could be mistaken for sheen. These skins feed on salt, on iron, on the faint heat in fog, on the sound that falls into water and does not leave. They grow in whorls that look like fingerprints. They form on the backs of shells that have never had animals. They make the pale stones seem tired. Some of them are so old they have begun to behave as records. A rock may be covered not merely by microbes but by their descendants’ descendants’ descendants, layer pressed against layer, each one eating the last one’s waste and leaving a new taste in the stone. If the plain seems aware when a footprint is taken, this is why. The ground has a skin, and the skin remembers pressure.

Between the grains of mud, in the dark syrup that is neither water nor soil, there are pale threads. They are not plants. They are not fungi in any comfortable sense. They move by swelling, then retracting, then swelling again, and they carry dissolved minerals from one stone to another in rhythms so slow that a human lifetime could pass across a single network without noticing a change. They are the marsh’s nerves, or perhaps its digestion. They bind. They separate. They listen to vibration through the crust. They may have no intention at all, but they react with such precision that intention is beside the point. A crack opens, and within hours the threads reach into it. A reed falls, and the air around its damaged stem becomes warmer, as if the ground were leaning closer. This is not spirit. It is wet biology of the lowest order, so abundant in its invisibility that it rivals the animals for the right to call this place home.

The insects are the loudest silence.

They are small, black, and hard-shelled, crossing the mud in ranks that look like moving shadows. The noise around them is impossible, but they move as if it has no relation to their bodies. Their legs are broad and flat at the tips, padded with a microscopic lattice of pits that cancels vibration before it reaches the ground. They leave tracks that do not fill. They do not disturb the standing water. They cross the wet plain and seem to subtract themselves from the world’s roar. If a wave passes, they may ride it without appearing to ride, because their shells flex with the pressure and they become temporarily invisible, not by stealth but by agreement with the surface they traverse.

What they eat is difficult to name. They scrape bacterial films from shell and bone. They drink condensation from the air. They lay eggs inside bubbles that will not last. But they also seem to feed on interference: where two echoes meet in the fog, they gather in clusters. Where a mineral crust hums with trapped current, they settle there, motionless for hours. They are not drawn to sound because they hear it. They are drawn to sound because it changes the chemistry of the surface. The marsh’s noise is not empty. It is food.

There are also shell-creatures with too many legs, pale as milk and soft as a forgotten tooth. They live in the thin water over mud, and they have no clear heads, because the concept of a head is inefficient here. They are radially arranged, like a small cathedral of wet limbs. Some resemble mollusks, but their shells are made of compressed silt and echo; they trap sound in their whorls and carry it through the marsh. When one is picked up, it may make a noise that did not exist yet in the local water. This is not magic, only biology under pressure, but the effect is unsettling.

Some pale things in the pools seem to have no limbs at all. They drift with too many eyes or with none, and move not by swimming but by allowing pressure differences to pass through their bodies. They look like droplets that have learned to want. They are often the size of a coin, sometimes larger, sometimes so faint that they are only a slight darkening of the water, a hesitation. They reproduce in waves. During a stormless day, a patch of surface water may suddenly fill with hundreds of tiny translucent bodies, rising from the film as if the mist had condensed into animals. By the next day they are gone. The pool forgets them, but the mud does not.

The larger life begins as stillness.

A reed clump is never merely a reed clump. The black stems are hollow, brittle, and stained so darkly with tannin that they seem charred. Their roots extend far beneath the water line, branching into the mineral crust like veins. They have no obvious flowers, but they swell at intervals, and when they do they release spores that hang in the fog for decades. The clumps have authority. A single patch can hold a broad area in its shape, not because it is aggressive, but because the water agrees to behave around it. The reeds drink slow pressure. They take up the groaning of stone, the static in the air, the faint chemical breath of the bacterial skins. They also house animals in their stems.

Inside these black reeds, tiny crustaceans live in droplets trapped at the nodes. Some salamander-like larvae shelter there, curled in the dark sap-water, with gills like white threads. Some insects bore through the walls and leave doors so perfectly angled that no rain enters. The reed clump is a city, a nursery, a instrument. When the wind passes, it makes a wet whistle that is not exactly a whistle, because the reeds are partly open at the base and partly blocked by growths of mineral foam. They sing in pitches that seem to belong to older animals.

The mineral grass is even stranger.

It chimes when the wind touches it, but the wind rarely cares enough to be regular. Each blade is edged with crystalline dust, and when two blades strike, they release tiny sparks of chemical light that vanish before the eye can decide whether they were seen. The grass roots are not roots but conductors, carrying moisture through narrow bands of mineralized tissue. Some tufts are thousands of years old, though they do not look old. They look patient. Their seeds are hard, pale grains that settle in the shallow water and wait for a particular kind of turbulence before splitting. When they germinate, the young shoots rise like small gray needles, and for the first few months of their lives they make no sound. Then they learn to chime.

There are broad-leafed plants, too dark and translucent at the edges, standing in the shallow water as if holding up the low light. They grow without visible effort. Their leaves are wide enough to seem excessive in this dim world, but they are not wasteful. They gather fog, not through stomata in the ordinary way, but through fine hairs that draw droplets along internal channels toward roots full of stored water and mineral salts. At the heart of each plant is a swollen bulb of pale flesh, which attracts soft-bodied animals that crawl inside it and are, in return, digested very slowly. The plant does not eat them quickly. It lets them live inside it for months, while it absorbs their movement and their breath.

There are twisted stalks with no leaves, only knotted stems and roots like clenched hands. They stand in the pools for so long that their surfaces become host to shells, moss, and tiny bone growths. Some may be plants, some may be animals that have made a decision to stop becoming. They do not move in any way a watcher can immediately see. But after enough years, their posture changes. A curve becomes sharper. A fork becomes more angular. They are the marsh’s slowest hunters. Their roots extend under the mud, waiting for a pulse of vibration, and when one comes, they contract by a millimeter. This is all the warning anything gets.

The truest animal of this place may be the one that does not seem alive at first.

It sits in a dark pool for days. It is broad, low-backed, and blacker than the water around it, with skin that appears to breathe through tiny pits along its flanks. Its head is too small for its body, or perhaps too large for the body it suggests; it is difficult to tell. It may be a salamander, but the word feels insufficient. It may be a creature that has outgrown the need for locomotion most of the time, a being that has decided that motion is expensive and the marsh is not in a hurry. Its eyes are closed or nearly so, covered by a film as thin as fog. Its gills are not external feathers but internal cavities that fill and drain with pressure changes. When it breathes, the pool becomes momentarily warmer.

It lives alone. Not because solitude is noble, but because its presence is too intense for company. A single one may shape the water chemistry of a mile of channels. Its skin sheds mineral layers that the microbial mats consume. Its stillness teaches the mud how to settle. When it moves, it moves like the land agreeing to a single sentence. One limb lifts, and the mud accepts it. The body slides forward, and the reeds bow. The water closes behind it with such softness that no ripple appears. If it has a predator, it does not seem afraid of it. If it has prey, it does not seem to hunt. It lives by being so profoundly present that other forms adjust around it.

Beneath the larger pools, there are things too long to be seen at once.

Once, a dark lake may be smooth, its surface not reflecting the sky, then a shadow passes under it, long and smooth, blacker than the water. This is not always a single creature. The marsh produces many bodies that appear as a single line because their movements are synchronized: ribbon-like colonies, segmented worms as wide as logs, colonial fishes whose individuals swim fused in a shared current. Some have no eyes, only pits along their skulls that taste pressure. Others have mouths like valves, taking in water and letting it pass through chambers lined with bacterial gardens. They do not chase. They travel through the underground channels of the plain, pushing before them the water’s habit of standing still.

One kind, perhaps, has a back like a stone shelf. It is not stone, but covered by a crust so old that it has its own moss. This is the Underback, or whatever name the wet world gives to it when the fog thickens. It moves slowly through the deepest channels, and when it passes, a ridge of pale stones may lift and resettle elsewhere.
