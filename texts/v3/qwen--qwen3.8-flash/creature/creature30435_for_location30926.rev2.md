What lives in the Glass-Hollow is not a single creature. If one answer is insisted upon, the best answer is that the chamber itself has become an animal, and the beings within it are its moving surfaces, its breath, its organs, its slow and silent appetite.

At first the eye is deceived. The floor seems to be black glass, the walls seem to be basalt, the crusts seem to be salt, the shadows seem to be mineral stain. Then the floor begins to adjust itself. The salt crusts bloom in patterns too deliberate to be weather. The shadows unfold jointed limbs that were lying flat enough to seem accidental. The black domes, which seemed like shards, begin to crawl with the patience of stones pretending to be slow. The room is not empty with creatures living inside it. The room has been taken over so completely by life that life has disguised itself as geology.

What lives there has no faces, or has faces so far from a face as to be unreadable. No eyes are needed where there is no sunlight. No mouths gape in obvious hunger where feeding is scraping, tasting dust, pressing mineral pastes into tiny rasping slits, drawing heat from the floor through shells. No soft wet things belong in this chamber. Their bodies are sealed against desiccation in a different way, because the thing that would kill them is not dryness but water. A pool here would be an impossibility, a poison, a collapse of the dry chemistry that lets these organisms grow. Their blood, if that word can be tolerated, is not liquid in the ordinary sense. It is a suspension of metallic salts, sulfur-bearing powders, dry fats hardened into resin, warm as tar, moving through channels no wider than hair. Heat is their breath. Mineral vapor is their food. The stone beneath them is not inert; it is a slow source of power.

They are a dry, packed, crawling world.

The broad dark shells are their cattle, their turtles, their boulders with appetites. They are not one species, or if they are, they are a family of species so fused by habit that one observer cannot tell them apart. Their domes are black, glossy, edged with a pale apron of salt where old shells have cracked and healed. They crawl low, barely lifting their rims from the glass, so that they seem like fragments of the floor that have learned to choose their places. Under each shell is a paler body, segmented, ribbed, lined with thousands of tiny feet that grip by dry adhesion and microscopic friction, not by moisture. Those feet do not leave wet prints. They leave faint scratches in the salt crusts, and these scratches are the first signs that what seemed like texture is intention.

These shell-animals are the room’s primary grazers. They eat the pale mineral crusts. They eat the blooms of crystalline salt that rise in shelves where heat and vapor meet. They eat the powder left by dead creatures. They eat the skins shed by the standing colonies. They may also absorb tiny motes of dust through the porous rims of their shells, letting the air itself pass over them and leave a residue behind. Their mouths are not open in the way a mouth is normally open; they are rasping slits pressed against the floor, lined with crystalline teeth that are renewed from the edge of the shell. They grind no roots, chew no leaves, drink no nectar. They scrape a dry world into themselves and turn it into heat and body.

They are dense because the chamber rewards density. Space is not empty. A shardback does not wander in the way a deer might wander through meadows. It occupies ground. It holds a place. It presses its broad back to the floor to receive warmth. When the heat from below pulses, the herds orient. The shells tilt. The edges lift slightly. The undersides turn away from cooler pockets. The room rearranges itself like a field of stones hearing an invisible call. When the heat fades, the shells settle again, but never in the same configuration. The herd has changed position. The balance of warmth has shifted. A dead shell, cracked open by predation or by heat exhaustion, becomes an archway, a shelf, a crevice, a house.

Their reproduction is not liquid, not egg in a nest, not birth into water. It is heat and pressure. Small nodules, hard as seed-glass and pale as salt, are dropped into cracks and hollows. For years they may lie there, inert-looking, mistaken for grains of crystal. Then a pulse comes from below, or a colony collapses above them, or a shardback presses them into a warm seam. The heat enters through the nodule’s skin and the nodule unfolds. It opens like a shell, then like an animal. The young is not soft and wet. It is dry, tight, armored before its eyes would be needed. If there are eyes at all, they are pits, lenses of heat-sensitive crystal, not organs for seeing light. The young crawls away with the certainty of something that has never known a world in which stillness was safe.

Their shells are not merely protective. They are memory. Each shell records the chemistry of the place it was grazed. A shardback born near the salt shelves carries paler edges than one born near the black glass. Molts leave rings and fractures like tree trunks, but these are not wood-rings. They are layers of mineral deposition, of heat damage, of growth slowed by cold pockets, of rapid bloom when the room breathed warmth. If one were foolish enough to touch a shell that had been abandoned, one would feel not only smoothness but a faint vibration left in the floor, a last tap or scrape, a signal that had not finished dying.

Below and among the shardbacks lives a smaller, faster, more numerous population. The tiny blind animals that move in glittering sheets are not spores, though they might be mistaken for them by anyone hoping for plant life. They are organisms with legs, plates, joints, mouths, and purposes. They are the dustlings, the mica-tides, the salt-sand mites, the crawling frost. No larger than a grain of iron shot, each one is armored in flakes that catch faint heat-glow from the floor. Alone, they seem random. Together, they behave like a nervous system.

They feed on dead skins, mineral powder, microscopic crusts, the waste of the herds, and on one another. They clean the chamber with relentless patience. A dead shardback does not remain a carcass for long. It becomes a landscape of movement. Mica-tides sheet over it, prying at shell edges, consuming soft membranes, scattering powder over the floor. Their bodies break the dead into a fine white dust that the crusts reabsorb, turning decay into a kind of mineral fertility. Death is not an interruption of life here. It is a form of distribution. The mica-tides are the room’s recyclers, its tiny accountants, its slow fire in the dry.

Their movement is patterned without being hurried. They crawl in waves, not because they are frightened, but because they are communicating through pressure and electrostatic trace. A patch of dustlings may suddenly lift, scatter, and fall again in a different configuration when a predator passes. Or they may rise in a dense carpet over a warm stone because the stone has exhaled vapor. Or they may crawl backward into the edge of a shell because the shell has turned too sharply toward heat. Their patterns suggest a mind that is not centralized, or a mind so distributed that the difference between instinct and thought is not visible from the outside.

In the shadows beneath the tilted panels of stone and glass, there are creatures that seem too delicate to live among such hardness. They are pale, jointed, folded, clustered in crevices like salt formations with limbs. They are not mineral deposits until they move. When they move, they reveal themselves as animals built for concealment. They fold flat. They tuck legs beneath themselves until their bodies become thin fans of white shell. They fill gaps so completely that they look like the room is furnishing itself.

These are the fold-limbs, the chalk-folds, the under-shelf dwellers. Their bodies are pale because they do not need darkness-proof shells; they live in shadow and stillness. They have many thin legs, and those legs are not for running but for anchoring. They grip stone, panel, shell, and salt shelf with the tenacity of creatures that have learned that falling is fatal. They feed on dustlings and on the fine mineral vapor that drifts from heated cracks. They may filter dust with feathered legs that resemble crystalline branches. They do not make webs, because wetness is absent, but they can extrude dry mineral filaments that harden into threads, scaffolding their shelters or binding their young into cracks.

They are prey, but their bodies are so well adapted to disappearance that being seen is difficult. A fold-limb can detach a limb and still hold with the rest. A fold-limb can become inert for hours, looking like a broken piece of salt shelf. If disturbed by a heat pulse, it unfolds and presses itself deeper into the crevice. If disturbed by a predator, it may shed pieces of itself and slip away as though the body were a puzzle with too many removable parts. They are creatures of the spaces between things, and in the Glass-Hollow there is no space without creatures occupying it.

The predators are the room’s most elegant threat. They are not loud. They do not have the posture of pursuit. They are flat animals with heat pits along their flanks, bodies like slivers of obsidian with a flexible underside, low-slung and silent. They patrol the warmer edges, sliding between the shells of the grazers without seeming to disturb them. Their movement is not slithering, exactly. They undulate along the floor using ribs of muscle and plates of chitin, and because the floor is glass, their passage is almost soundless. Only a faint tap tells that something is moving when the prey should not have been still.

These pit-slates have no need for eyes. They hunt by temperature, vibration, and chemical trace. A shardback that has moved too close to a vent becomes visible to them as a warm object on a cool floor. A fold-limb that has unfolded into a crevice leaks a faint mineral breath. A young nodule cracked open for birth is a small thermal event. The predators wait beside shell-edges, or between tilted stone panels, or under the bases of the standing colonies. They do not charge. They adjust. They position themselves according to the room’s heat cycle, because the heat cycle determines where the prey will be forced to move.

Their strike is intimate. Not a savage bite at a distance, but a slip into a gap, a pressure against a vulnerable membrane, a crack along a growth line. A shardback’s shell is strongest at the center, weakest where old molt edges meet new mineral deposit. The pit-slate finds that seam with its heat-pits and forces its flat head under the lifted rim. It may not tear in the way a wolf tears. It may sever the membrane beneath, or inject a dry enzymatic paste that softens mineral tissue, or pry the shell apart with a body built like a wedge. The death is quiet. The herd may close around the disturbance for a while, then resume the same dense crawl, because the place is too packed for tragedy to be dramatic.

The predators also eat smaller creatures. They catch fold-limbs, crush dustling carpets, take small scavengers, and sometimes feed on the shed shells of larger animals if no better prey is available. They are not numerous, but they are enough. Their presence shapes the herd. Shellbacks learn to edge away from them, not through fear as we might picture it, but through adjustment of position. The herd does not scatter screaming. It becomes a pattern in which vulnerable shells are hidden and stronger shells are exposed. The predators read that pattern, too, and choose accordingly.

Beneath the predators are the scavengers, the powder-feeders, the dust-rats, the cinder-borers. They are heavy-headed, many-legged, blind or nearly blind, with mouths built for grinding. They come after death and after molts. A dead pit-slate is scavenged. A broken colony limb is scavenged. A shed shell cracked by heat is scavenged. The powder-feeders are not gross in the ordinary way; they are precise. They pick apart dead tissue, separate mineral crust from organic shell, carry grains of salt into their own bodies, and excrete a fine pale dust that feeds the growth of salt shelves. They are the reason the chamber does not accumulate waste. They make death useful again, but they also make the dead seem less like corpses and more like raw material.

Some of these scavengers mimic. They carry bits of shell and mineral on their backs until they resemble fragments of the floor. Others imitate molted husks, lying still until approached too closely. Some crawl inside the broken shells of shardbacks and use the shell as a mobile house. They are neither herbivores nor carnivores in any simple sense; they are consumers of endings, and endings in the Glass-Hollow are abundant.

The most remarkable inhabitants are the larger forms that seem tree-like at first glance but are not trees. They are standing colonies, living towers of chitin, mineral accretion, shell fragments, salt crust, and the fused bodies of thousands of generations. Their bases are not trunk but foot, a cluster of many jointed legs that move the whole structure with glacial patience. Their bodies rise in layered columns, each ring a year, each decade, each pulse of heat recorded in mineral strata. They are slow. They are enormous. They are animals, but they behave like architecture that has learned to breathe.

A standing colony feeds by raising frond-like branches into thermal vents. The branches are porous, layered, furred with tiny filters that catch dustlings, salt vapor, mineral grains, and the faint chemical exhalations of other creatures. They do not photosynthesize, because there is no sun. They chemosynthesize, or something adjacent to chemosynthesis: they use heat and mineral gradients to drive slow reactions inside their tissues. Their bodies are warm with retained heat. Their surfaces may glow faintly red when a pulse from below reaches them, as though their mineral bones are remembering fire.

When they crack, the room reacts. A colony limb breaks and falls, and the smaller animals around it move in waves. The shellbacks near the shadow change their basking angle. The dustlings swarm over the exposed interior. The fold-limbs retreat into the new crevice. A fallen branch may become a shelter, a grazing platform, a bridge, a trap. If the colony leans, the heat flow changes. If a colony collapses, a new architecture of death is built. Their remains become shelves, walls, and homes. They are the slowest engineers in the chamber.

These standing colonies may be the closest the Glass-Hollow has to memory. Their layers record what the herd cannot: which vents burned hottest, which winters brought dry ash from the throat of the mountain, which pulses arrived too late or too strong. They grow by adding, not by replacing. Their lives are accumulated. A colony may be part parent, part ruin, part nursery. Young organisms may burrow into its base and fuse to it, becoming part of the body. Old organisms may die within its layers, their shells becoming walls inside the living structure. They are not intelligent in a way that can be easily tested, but they are not stupid either. They are creatures that have turned time into body.

The scattered built things of the chamber are not ignored by these lives. They have been incorporated. The ring of low stones near the center is not an altar to us, but to the animals it is a geometry of heat. Stones hold warmth differently from glass. A ring of stones can trap a pulse, slow a cooling, create a shade, make a cooler pocket for molting, or force the dustlings to gather in one place for the fold-limbs to harvest. The half-collapsed wall of dry-laid blocks is a ridge of shelter. The flat slab of glass is a basking plate. The three upright stones are too deliberate to be accident, but too old to be a house. They have become bones.

No animal in the Glass-Hollow has a village, because no animal needs one in the way a human mind imagines need. There are no roofs to keep rain out, because rain is a myth. There are no walls to keep wind out, because wind is mostly absent. There are no soft beds, because softness is suspicious. There are no doors, because the room is not a sequence of private spaces; it is one packed surface. But the old stones may have been built by some earlier mind, or by creatures with hands that no longer exist, or by a stage of life that has since hardened into mineral silence. If so, those builders are not here. What is here are their inheritors. The standing colonies grow toward the cairns. The shellbacks sleep in their shadows. The predators patrol their edges. The dustlings write pale trails across their surfaces. The built world has become another kind of geology, and geology, in this place, is never dead for long.

If a person entered, the first thing they would notice is not fear but rearrangement. No animal would run away, because there is nowhere empty to run to. The chamber is too crowded for flight. Instead, the floor would answer the visitor’s heat. A person standing on the warm glass becomes a new thermal object, an intrusion of shape and temperature. The shells nearest the visitor lift slightly. The dustlings ripple and close. The fold-limbs fold deeper into cracks. The predators pause and orient. The standing colonies may lean almost imperceptibly, their joints adjusting to the changed pressure of the room. Nothing flees. Everything incorporates.

If the visitor remained too long, the place would begin to use them. Dust would settle into their boots. Mineral powder would cling to their clothing. Mite-tides might explore the seams of their garments, attracted to heat, salts, and whatever organic trace they carried. The visitor’s shadow would create a cold island on the floor, and the animals would treat that shadow as weather. If the visitor knelt, their knees would become warm objects that shellbacks might approach for heat. If the visitor touched a shardback, the shellback would not bite. It would simply change position, adjusting the visitor into the calculus of pressure and temperature. If the visitor died there, the scavengers and dustlings would not mourn them, nor celebrate. They would begin the slow work of turning the wet alien thing into useful mineral powder.

The ecology has its own language, but it is not made of words. It is made of taps, scrapes, shifts, exhalations, and chemical traces. A shell tapping the glass may tell of distance, weight, heat, or danger. A dustling carpet forming a sudden line may warn of a predator’s passage. A colony limb cracking may instruct the whole herd where to move during the next pulse. A predator’s stillness in a warm pocket may become a grammar of absence: the other animals know to avoid that shape without seeing it. The chamber is quiet, but its quiet is full of instruction.

There is no green thing, yet there is no emptiness. There is no water, yet there is no dryness in the ordinary sense; this is a dryness that has become life. There is no sun, yet the room is not without light. There is only enough glow to show that surfaces exist. A shell passing near a hot fissure may catch a faint inner redness. A mineral crust may flake to reveal a dull heat below. A shardback turning its edge may reflect a shimmer so slight that the viewer doubts their own senses. The darkness is not an absence of light but a condition of intimacy. Everything must be close to be known.

The creatures of the Glass-Hollow do not depend on the outside world for softness, warmth, rain, or shade. They depend on one another and on the mountain. The heat pulse is their weather. The glass floor is their pasture. The salt shelves are their forests. The predator’s shadow is their night. The molt is their birth and death. The colony is their architecture. The dust is their soil. The chamber is not a desert with rare survivors. It is a body with organs. The shellbacks are its moving plates. The fold-limbs are its hidden nerves. The dustlings are its breathing skin. The predators are its teeth. The scavengers are its digestion. The colonies are its long memory. The old stones are its scars.

What lives there, then, is not one creature wearing the place, but the place wearing creatures. The Glass-Hollow has become alive not by growing leaves, but by hardening into animals. Not by flowing with water, but by storing heat in mineral bodies. Not by filling silence with noise, but by making silence into a medium through which pressure, warmth, and vibration speak. It is a cathedral of obsidian and salt, and its congregation is not human. Its congregation is the slow, dense, patient world of shells, limbs, dust, teeth, towers, and bones, all of them occupied, all of them rearranging themselves around the breath of the mountain.

If asked who lives there, the answer must be: they do, and it, and the boundary between them is false.

The shellbacks crawl in their broad black herds, eating crusts, basking, molting, dying into shelves.  
The dustlings move in glittering sheets, cleaning death into powder and powder into salt.  
The fold-limbs lie in crevices like pale mineral flowers that refuse to bloom.  
The pit-slates wait with heat-sensitive bodies, quiet as stone learning cruelty.  
The powder-feeders break the dead into dust and call that dust life.  
The standing colonies grow into the dark, adding layer after layer, becoming architecture, ruin, parent, house, corpse.  
The old stones hold heat and shadow and give the animals reasons to gather.  
And the whole chamber breathes in slow pulses, not because it has lungs, but because every creature in it has made its breathing out of movement, consumption, deposition, and rearrangement.

They are not gentle, except in the way all quiet things can be gentle by accident. They are not cruel, except in the way a body must be cruel to another body in order to continue. They are not intelligent by human measure, unless the slow accumulation of a colony is thought, or the herd’s adjustment to heat is knowledge. But they are alive with a completeness that makes the word “barren” look foolish. They live where nothing seems to live. They build without tools, eat without plants, hunt without sight, and die without disappearance.

The Glass-Hollow is inhabited. Its inhabitants are not people. They are not animals as the mind first imagines animals. They are a packed, dry, mineral civilization of shells and salts, a society of pressure and heat, a room that has learned to move.
