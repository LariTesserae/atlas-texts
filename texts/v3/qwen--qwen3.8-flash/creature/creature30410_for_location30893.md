**The Living of the Halfroom Beneath the Chalk**

The temperature is stable in the seam—so stable, in fact, that the distinction between living and mineral begins to thin there. A shelf is never merely a shelf. A pool is not only water. A damp streak along a wall may be a mark left by breath, or it may be the slow travel of something that has no need for feet.

It is a common mistake, when you first enter, to think the place empty.

The room is bright and sealed. It feels like a held breath. The light presses softly on the skin, not warm, not sharp, but everywhere, as if you have stepped inside a pale lantern that has forgotten its flame. You look around and see chalk lobes, broken ridges, abandoned arches, half-formed stair steps, wet seams. You think these are merely the accidents of stone still learning what to be.

Then, after a few minutes, you notice a ledge softening by the width of a finger.

A crack widens not because something pushed, but because something *decided*.

A thin crystal thread slides from a wall, touches your shadow lying aside, uncertain, and withdraws.

The Halfroom is not a cave with animals living inside it. The animals are among the reasons it is a halfroom at all.

If one must give them a name, call them the **Halfborn**.

They are not one kind, though they resemble one another the way different drafts of a single sentence resemble one another. They are neither quite animal, nor plant, nor fungus, nor mineral, though they borrow from all four. Their bodies are made of what the chamber is made of: chalk, damp clay, calcite, gypsum, milky quartz, and the strange bright dust that gathers in the corners when the room is left alone. They do not age as surface things age. They *become*, and then they *become something else*, and then, if they are lucky, they are broken before they finish.

For the Halfborn fear completion.

A finished thing, in their way of thinking, is a dead thing. A true doorway, once built, is a door; and doors belong to the world above, where things are decided and carried into houses. But the Halfroom is below-surface, in the place before decision. So the Halfborn tend to beginning, to almost, to suggestion. They make ledges that are not yet steps. They make niches that are not yet chairs. They widen cracks that are not yet roads. If a stair begins to look too much like a stair, they melt one of its steps. If an arch threatens to become an arch, they sink it into the floor and forget its shape by morning.

The smallest of them are the **curdlings**.

At first, a curdling appears to be a pale mound of floor, the kind of thickened place where a foot might rest too comfortably, as if the ground were remembering a seat. But watch it long enough and you will see the surface ripple. It breathes not with lungs, but with a slow mineral exhalation, releasing a milky cream of chalk and water. That cream gathers into low shelves, broken ridges, and shallow pools. In one hour a curdling may be a platform; in the next, a soft bowl filled with light; in the next, a patch of damp clay that leaves a white mark when a fingertip brushes it.

A curdling does not hunt and does not flee. It has no eyes, because the chamber’s light comes from every side and makes shadows unreliable. Instead, it senses by texture. It tastes the air through wet pores. It responds to pressure the way cream responds to a spoon. If you step near it, it may rise slightly toward the heat of your foot—not because you are warm in the ordinary way, but because the room is so stable that any change is an event. If you sit on it, you may feel it firm beneath you for a moment, then soften, then become uncertain again, as if deciding whether it wishes to be stone.

When many curdlings gather, they form what looks like an animal lying flat against the floor: a pale, soft-backed thing, legless, mouthless, breathing only by becoming and unbecoming. The older stories among those who have glimpsed such places would call that a **chalk-sleeper**. But the chalk-sleeper is not one creature so much as a temporary agreement between many.

Beside the curdlings crawl the **fretters**.

The fretters are smaller than snow flecks, though they move in the bright mineral dust like tiny living frost. They feed on the light that rises from the walls—not the light itself, but the brightness as it settles into the pores of chalk. Where a fretter passes, the wall becomes a shade paler, as if it has been licked. In large numbers, fretters can make a chamber glow unevenly, blooming with spots of brightness that drift across the floor. If you stand still in the middle of the room, you may feel a fine powder settle on your hands and hair. That powder is fretter dust: molted crystal, dried chalk, and fragments of unfinished light.

The fretters are eaten by almost nothing. They are too small and too mineral, and perhaps too strange to be regarded as food in the usual sense. But they are harvested. The **threaders** draw them into cracks and consume the brightness in their bodies. The **breath-moths** land upon patches where fretters have gathered and dissolve into them, becoming part of the chalk film that coats the chamber walls.

The threaders are the room’s slow hands.

They live inside cracks. They are pale, glassy, almost invisible at first, like veins of milky quartz that have learned to stretch. A threader may be no thicker than a hair, but it can move with a strange, deliberate patience through seams too narrow to see. It travels where the stone is trying to open, and it helps it try. Wherever a crack begins to widen into a niche, you may find threaders inside, spinning out fine filaments of calcite, testing edges, shaping hollows, making small pockets that may become homes, beds, bowls, doorways
