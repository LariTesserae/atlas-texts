What lives there cannot be answered with a single name. At this elevation, “inhabitant” is a word that has been thinned, stretched, and made damp. It applies to the people, certainly, but it also applies to the mosses, the steam, the water, the rust, the pale birds, the low grey creatures that slip under terraces, the beetles in the crevices, the moths that batter themselves against oil-glass, and the slow mineral life of the plateau itself. The place is not owned by its residents. It is more like a negotiation between them.

If one insists on starting with human beings, the answer becomes immediate: they are there, but not in the manner of travelers passing through, nor conquerors erecting a bright flag. They are there the way lichen is on a stone. They have grown into the settlement’s wet bones. They are compact, quiet, patient people, built by altitude and damp into a form that seems made for this world’s pressure. Their chests are broad but not proud; their movements are economical, as though the air itself had taught them not to waste breath. Many of them have high, clean lines of bone beneath weathered skin, pale faces, deep-set eyes adapted to grey light, and hands that have learned the texture of stone, iron, rope, and cold metal. They do not move with hurry. Even where the city is dense, the people move through it with a sideways, unhurried attention, as if they expect surfaces to change under their feet.

Their bodies show it. Fingers are often cracked, then sealed again with wax or oil. Knees and elbows carry old stains where they have knelt on wet rock. Shoulders are rounded from working under low roofs and leaning into steam. They know how to stand for long periods without seeming to rest, and how to breathe in a way that looks, to an outsider, almost like listening. Their eyes are often narrowed, not because of sun, which is rare, but because the world there is composed of fine drifting water. They learn early that moisture is not an accident. It is an atmosphere. It is a tenant.

They dress for this tenant. Wool is not a luxury; it is infrastructure. The inhabitants wear thick, close-fitting layers, many of them patched more than once, often dyed in muted shades of brown, grey, black, and dull moss-green. Oilcloth and treated canvas keep the worst of the damp from reaching the body. They favor short coats, heavy boots with deep soles, and wraps that can be tightened at the neck and wrists against the seepage that finds every seam. In maintenance yards and engine rooms they wear leather aprons, metal-fastened sleeves, and thick mitts that allow them to touch hot pipes or cold iron without losing feeling. Some wear small glass-lidded boxes around their necks or belts: dryness carried with them like a private shrine.

Their city is not built to be looked at. It is built to be survived. The human inhabitants live in stacked, interlocking rooms that follow the logic of the shelf and the damp. A family may occupy a ground floor that is half cellar, a middle floor that is mostly drying loft, and a roofed upper space where the air is colder but the light is slightly less exhausted. Children learn the names of channels before they learn all the names of streets. They grow up knowing which walls sweat, where moss grows too fast for safety, which stairs turn slippery before the rest of the world notices, and how to tell from the sound of a drip whether a joint is loose or a cistern is near full. They learn that water has opinions, and that some rooms should not be too dry if one wishes to keep the stone content.

The settlement’s human economy is one of maintenance, extraction, and continuity. It is not flashy. There are no grand ceremonial industries, no shining halls of invention, no great monuments to progress. Instead, there are pipe-readers and steam-tenders, channel-carvers, roof-sweepers, furnace-nurses, condensation farmers, belt-fitters, bell-keepers, and women and men whose job seems almost invisible: the careful preservation of dryness. These people move through their tasks with a competence that looks, from the outside, like devotion. They know the temperament of their machines. They do not command the plateau so much as they keep a long-running appointment with it.

Some tend the great tilted plates in the upper terraces, where dew and mist gather in slow sheets and are scraped into basins. Others walk the cooling towers and abandoned platforms, checking for frost at the joints and clearing steam outlets when a new seam has begun to breathe. There are those whose whole working life is spent listening to iron. They press their ears to pipes, note the difference between a dull knock and a bright one, and decide whether a line is blocked, cooling too fast, or carrying heat from too deep a source. Others live near the engines, feeding small fires beneath damp stone, adjusting belts, wrapping joints in wool to keep the chill from seizing a shaft. Still others maintain the cable tracks and stairways that link the settlement to lower terraces and outlying works. Every trade seems to require the same skill: an awareness of what is wet, what is dry, what is hot, what is about to change.

Human life here is communal, but not in the bright, outgoing way of open-air cultures. The closeness of the settlement creates a social texture more like moss than like polished wood. Privacy is scarce; familiarity is constant. Neighbors know one another not through conversation alone but through the rhythms of dripping and tapping, through the smoke from particular roofs, through the faint heat that rises from a shared engine room, and through the small debts of maintenance that make the city function. One household keeps a dry box; another knows where to borrow a sealed cloth. One person can fix a bracket; another can tell from the color of lichen whether a wall has begun to shift. These exchanges are not performed as charity. They are performed as ordinary law.

Their speech, too, is shaped by the place. It is usually low, careful, and unhurried. There is little need for volume when the wind is patient and the water speaks softly, but there is also little room for the kind of boisterousness that would break against all this wet stone. People talk as though they have learned to conserve heat. Jokes are dry and brief. Insults, when given, are precise. There is no cheerful grandeur in their social life, yet it is not joyless. Joy here is quiet and practical: a warm room after a cold patrol, a sealed cloth still dry, a bell-hour that means work has stopped for a breath, a lamp that holds steady when the fog thickens, a child finding a pale feather on a wet ledge and naming it aloud with surprise.

The human inhabitants are not the only citizens, and in many ways they are not the most numerous. If the plateau has a native language, it is spoken by moss. Moss lives over the walls, inside seams, across roofs, and in the dark corners beneath staircases. It is a resident in the fullest sense: it takes space, excludes other things, holds water, softens edges, and announces weather. It grows differently on different stones. It thickens where heat leaks, thins where air moves too hard, and turns a strange deep green where steam has made a sheltered pocket. Some people scrape it away. Others encourage it. They do not always know the difference, but they respect it. In the older quarters, a particular patch of moss on a particular lintel may have been kept, or allowed to return, for so many years that it seems less like plant life and more like a family crest.

Lichen is the next most patient resident. It paints the stone in blues and yellows, in crusty rashes and slow mineral stains. Unlike moss, it seems less alive in the ordinary human sense, yet it is always working, always eating the surface of the world, always changing color according to water and heat. It is the first thing that notices a new vent, a cracked channel, a shift in steam, or a wall that has begun to drink differently. The people learn its grammar. They know that when lichen turns in a certain direction along a courtyard, water will come from one side and not another. They know that a patch can appear overnight if a pipe has loosened somewhere unseen. It is not magic, exactly, but it has become a kind of civic sense.

Ferns and moss-covered trees are also inhabitants, though their lives are more solitary. The trees near the settlement are short, heavy-limbed, bowed away from wind and weather for so long that they seem to lean into the city’s mood. Their branches hold pale moss like old ragged scarves. They give shelter, collect dripping water, and provide a place for small life to hide. In abandoned sections where groves have closed around cooling towers and platforms, one gets the impression that the trees are not merely growing there; they are occupying, filling the gaps left by human withdrawal with slow green confidence. They are not decorative. They are part of the settlement’s second architecture.

Even more numerous, and easier to overlook, are the insects and tiny water-life. In such a place one would expect scarcity, but the damp makes a different argument. Midges drift in cloudy spirals near condensation fields, their bodies too small to be called a nuisance and too persistent to be ignored. Beetles live under stones and in iron seams, their shells shining with an oily sheen from the constant moisture. Moths gather near warm glass panes in the evening, their dusty wings pressing briefly against the light before they vanish back into the dark. In shallow channels and black reservoirs there is a whole smaller economy: larvae, crust-like films, pale soft creatures that feed on mineral residue, bacteria and fungi working at the edge of sight. These are the city’s hidden metabolism. They keep water alive, or at least moving, and they make the settlement feel less like a machine than like an ecosystem that has learned to use machines.

The most visible animals are the pale-eyed birds. They are not large, and they are not colorful in any way that would brighten the grey-green world. Their plumage is dull slate or ashy brown, their eyes pale and watchful, their wings long and stiff. They circle the upper terraces at dawn and move between abandoned cooling towers and roof cavities with an ease that suggests they were here before the people. They nest in iron housings, in open brick joints, in the damp shadows beneath chimneys. They are silent most of the time, but when the wind drops, one hears a thin metallic click in the upper air—perhaps the sound of a wing adjusting, perhaps a warning, perhaps simply a bird speaking to its mate from a ledge too wet for human comfort.

These birds are not tame, but they are accustomed. They do not flee from a person unless the person moves too quickly through a shaft of steam. They know the city’s rhythms. They seem to understand where warmth rises and where moisture falls. In the mornings they circle the settlement in loose, disciplined patterns, sliding across the rooflines like something written in a faint hand. Sometimes a single bird sits for a long time on a ventilator cap, its pale eye fixed on the drifting steam, and one gets the uneasy feeling that it is not merely resting but reading the place.

Beneath them, at the lowest level of the terraces and the outer margins of the moss beds, live the small ground creatures. They are not rabbits exactly, nor mice, nor any animal one can comfortably name after only one glimpse. Their fur is dense and grey, their ears short, their feet broad for walking on wet stone.
