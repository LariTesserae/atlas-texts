Whoever lives there lives first as a skin.

The oldest inhabitants are not animals with eyes or hunger or named bodies, but the low, slow, almost mineral life that has taken the high stone and made it hospitable. Moss, lichen, fungal crusts, and pale threadlike growths are not merely attached to the plateau; they are the plateau’s first citizens, its first architects, its first workers. They are not a forest, and they are not even obviously a community in the way one imagines a herd or a hive. They are more like a distributed being, stretched thinly over black stone and dark meltwater, holding together by moisture, cold, heat, and time.

The black mosses grow like iron velvet over basalt, their fibers fine as hair, their surfaces able to capture the least breath of vapor. The blue-gray lichens spread in whorls and rings, not because the stone demands rings, but because that is how such a partnership spreads: chemically, patiently, by breaking down the rock grain by grain. The fungal shelves and mats are stranger still. Many of them seem to behave less like plants and more like organs. They open and contract around the warm mouths of vents. They collect water and mineral dust. They transmit faint charges through damp filaments. They release spores that glow with a faint chemical light. Some of these fungi are so woven into the moss, lichen, and bacterial slime that it is impossible to say where one life ends and another begins.

These ground beings are the true factory floor. They grind stone. They trap water. They absorb heat from vents. They exhale vapor. They build tiny wet layers that become the habitat of everything else. Without them, the plateau would be only cold rock and steam. With them, it becomes a place where living things can feed, shelter, move, and reproduce.

Beneath the visible moss, there are even smaller lives. The porous dark soil is filled with bacteria and archaea that live on heat and chemistry rather than sunlight. They feed on sulfur, iron, ammonia, dissolved gases, and the thin films of mineral water that move through the stone. Some of them form slimy layers in the black pools. Others colonize the undersides of fungal shelves, turning condensation into a faint metallic taste. In the warmest zones, where steam loosens the frost, their colonies pulse with invisible metabolism, changing color, thickening, thinning, responding to the rise and fall of heat. They are the food of larger things, though larger things often do not look like animals at all but like pale threads drifting through water.

In the tiny hidden pools, there are blind, delicate creatures no longer than a eyelash. They may be larvae, or worm-things, or slow aquatic relatives of insects. They move through mineral slime, graze on bacterial films, and live in a world of chemical gradients rather than light. Their bodies are translucent, so faintly colored that they seem almost like thoughts made visible. They are eaten by small glassy beetles that crawl into the moss mat at the pool’s edge. Their carcasses are then broken down by fungi. Even death here is a step in the process.

The larger, more visible inhabitants are few enough to preserve the emptiness of the land, but they are the beings that give the place its most recognizable faces.

The first are the grazers.

They are wide, low, heavy-bodied animals with broad heads and padded feet made for moving over moss without tearing it into ruin. They do not stand tall. They do not need to. There is nothing above them to reach. Their bodies are pale in the darkness, often semi-translucent, so that under the faint blue glow of moss or the dim light of an aurora, one can sometimes see the dark shapes of their internal organs shifting slowly with breath. This translucence is not vanity or display; it may be a result of long life in cold, dim, mineral-rich places, where skin is thin, fat is layered in strange pockets, and warmth is stored deep near the body’s core.

Their backs rise in segmented ridges, not like the humps of familiar animals, but like low dunes or stone slabs arranged for a purpose. Along these ridges grow stiff filaments, short and thick, sometimes almost metallic in appearance. These filaments trap condensation. They catch windborne spores. They absorb faint glow from the mosses and fungi on which the grazers feed, so that when the animal stands still in the dark, its back may hold a dim blue or pale amber light, like a field of tiny coals that are not burning. This is not camouflage, exactly. In this land, it may be a language.

The grazers move in long, spaced lines. They are not herds in the noisy, crowded sense. There is no clatter of hooves, no sudden panic, no thunder of bodies. They move as if the land itself has chosen a rhythm. One stands by a moss bowl. Another waits near a vent rim. A third is already halfway across a black stone shelf, its broad muzzle combing the lichens. They are far apart, but not aimless. They follow the warmth, the moisture, the mineral deposits, and the invisible electrical cues left by the fungal networks.

They are built for patience. Their lungs are large and slow, drawing in thin air with a kind of practiced calm. They do not gallop. They do not sweat much. They conserve moisture and heat within their fur and skin. When they drink, they lower their broad mouths into shallow pools and let the water be drawn in by the texture of their lips and tongue. Their tongues are long, flat, and slightly rough, adapted to scraping moss, lichen, and fungal threads from the ground. Their teeth are not the sharp blades of predators, but broad grinding surfaces, suited to breaking down fibrous mats and mineral-heavy plant matter. Inside their bodies, there are whole communities of symbiotic organisms that help digest what ordinary digestion could not manage. In one sense, each grazer is itself a small moving factory, full of microbes, heat, chemistry, and conversion.

They are also gardeners.

The grazers carry moss fragments, fungal threads, and lichen bits in the filaments along their backs. They drop them where they rest. They spread spores through their droppings. The routes they follow, though not roads, become lines of colonization. A patch of stone that was once bare may become shaded with moss decades after a line of grazers crossed it, because the animal carried the ground with it. The grazers’ bodies therefore do not merely feed from the land; they also help build it. They return warmth-bearing material from vent margins to colder flats. They bring mineral-rich water into moss patches. They disturb the surface just enough to let new growth take hold.

Their young are born rarely, and they are born quiet. A newborn grazer stands within hours if the ground is warm and dry enough, its body pale and damp, its back ridges not yet fully formed. Its filaments are soft, almost like new fur. For many days, it stays close to the mother, though “close” here does not mean pressing against her. It means within sight, within scent, within the faint glow of her back. It learns to identify moss pools, vent rims, and the places where frost withdraws first. It learns the spacing. It learns not to crowd. It learns that the land is not a resource to be seized, but a current to be followed.

A grazer’s life may last decades, or longer. Their bodies wear slowly. Their hides thicken in places. Their backs may collect lichen rings so old that they look like weathered stones. Some grazers spend years in a single vent basin, becoming part of its ecology, their bodies covered by moss and fungal films. When they die, they do not simply rot in the ordinary sense. In the cold, dry air, their remains may mummify, darken, and become colonized. Fungi spread over them. Beetles arrive. Millipedes with glassy shells burrow through dried tissue. The minerals in their bones and hides are slowly returned to the water and stone. Their death is not an ending but a redistribution.

The predators are even easier to miss.

They are not large in the way the grazers are large. They are long, low, and close to the ground. They move like spilled frost. Their fur is black and silver, fine and dense, with a texture that resembles the frost that forms on stone at dawn. Their paws are broad and soft, so that they do not break the surface of moss mats. Their faces are not obviously feline or lupine, but there is something in their stillness that suggests both: the patience of a cat waiting by a hole, the quiet coordination of a pack that does not need to bark.

They live in the spaces between vents, moss pools, and shallow depressions where condensation gathers. They know where warmth will make prey come. They know where steam will hide scent and sound. They know which fungal shelves glow brightest, because that light often means moisture and life, and where there is moisture and life, there are grazers to drink and small animals to eat.

Their eyes are wide and pale, almost without reflection in the dark. They do not rely on sunlight. They may see heat shimmer, faint electrical changes, the glow of moss, and the subtle blue pulses that move through the ground. Their ears are low and broad, able to detect the tiny crack of frost and the softest footfall in wet moss. Their noses may be adapted not only to smell, but to taste the vapor itself, detecting changes in sulfur, salt, water, and breath.

They do not chase. That would waste too much oxygen in the thin air. They wait. A predator may stand beside a vent rim for hours, its body so still that moss breathes around it and a thin film of frost may gather along its back. When a grazer steps too near, the predator moves in a single low rush, not a leap but a compression of distance. It kills quickly, often with a bite or claw strike to the neck or flank. There is no shriek. There is no dramatic collision. In the cold, the event is almost too quiet to believe. A grazer may simply stop moving, and the predator will be beside it before the breath has left the body.

The predators raise young in warm hollows beneath vent shelves, where the air is damp and the stone retains heat. The dens are not burrows dug by tools but natural pockets enlarged by generations of moss growth and animal bodies pressing them into softness. The young are born blind or nearly blind, pale as worm-things, and they grow quickly because warmth is available and the cold waits for no one. They are nursed in silence. They are taught by example. A young predator learns to stand with its body low, to breathe through the thin air without panicking, to hear the difference between falling water and moving animal, to distinguish the glow of a healthy moss pool from the faint, dangerous shimmer of an electric charge.

They do not form noisy packs. Their cooperation is subtle, based on spacing and habit. Two predators may share a thermal valley without touching. They may circle a basin from opposite sides, not because they have a plan, but because generations have taught them which directions bring prey into a corner. Sometimes they meet, and if both are old and satisfied, they pass each other without violence. Sometimes they fight, though rarely to the death, because death is cheap in this cold and unnecessary conflict is wasteful.

They also help the land. A predator’s kill is a concentrated return of nutrients to the ground. It creates a point where beetles, fungi, mosses, and mineral-eating bacteria can work together. It draws small animals from farther away. It leaves scent, heat, and blood in the cold air, all of which become information for the next hunter and the next grazers. The predator’s presence keeps movement from stagnating. It prevents any single basin from being overgrazed. It forces animals to remember that the land is dangerous even when it is quiet.

There are smaller creatures between the grazers and predators, and these smaller creatures are perhaps more numerous than all the rest.

Beetles crawl through the root mats, their shells glassy and dark, their legs adapted to walking across surfaces too slippery for ordinary insects. They eat dead fungal threads, chew bits of moss, carry dust from one stone to another, and leave behind waste that helps nourish the ground. Some of them have bodies that catch the faint electrical charges of the plateau. On certain still nights, when the air is dry and the moss is cold, one can see tiny blue sparks leap from moss tip to moss tip, and among these sparks there are insects that seem to wear the charge like dew. They do not fly with buzzing noise. They drift.

Millipedes, thin as thread, crawl along the undersides of fungal shelves. Some are pale and blind, moving by chemical sense alone. Others are segmented so finely that they resemble the ribs of a small wing. They eat the slime that forms beneath fungal growth, helping to keep those surfaces clear. In their passage, they may carry spores or electrical residue from one shelf to another. They are cleaners, carriers, and connectors.

Then there are the drifting things.

They may be called insects, but they are not exactly insects as one knows them. They are too small, too soft, too strange. Some resemble blind worms stretched into silk. Some resemble tiny pale birds that have forgotten how to fly and instead glide on filaments charged with static. They live in the air above the moss where vapor rises from vents. They ride thermal threads. They drink condensation. They feed on floating spores, mineral dust, and the faint electrical fields that accumulate near the fungal filaments. Their bodies may be so light that they seem less like animals than like motes with intention.

They form the plateau’s airborne nervous system. When they gather near a vent, their bodies can touch and charge one another. They may transmit tiny impulses between moss mats that would otherwise be too separated to communicate. They carry spores across basins. They feed the predators and the beetles and the larger drifters. They are the dust that breathes.

Around the vents themselves, there are other beings more specialized.

At the warm margins where steam meets cold stone, one finds tube-like creatures, pale and slender, growing in clusters where the mineral water is richest. They are not quite plants and not quite animals. They may have no eyes, no legs, no visible mouths except a thin opening at the top. They extend into the steam, drawing warmth and mineral content from the air. Their surfaces are plated with salts, making them shine faintly in the dark. Around them, small organisms live in the damp crevices, feeding on their shed fibers. In time, when the vent cools, these tube-things die, and their mineral shells become part of the crust that lines the vent rim.

There are also flat, glassy creatures that resemble slugs, but larger, living beneath moss shelves near the pools. They graze on slime and fungal threads. They leave trails that become chemically attractive to other organisms. In this land, a trail is not a mere line; it is a message written in salt, acid, and water. Some predators follow these trails not because they are hungry for the small creature itself, but because the trail leads to moisture, warmth, or the remains of a recent kill.

Even the lichens are not passive. Some of them appear to behave with a slow kind of agency. Their rings expand toward mineral sources. They crack the stone in patterns that direct water flow. They form polished depressions that collect meltwater, and in those depressions, life concentrates. In this sense, the lichens are not merely living on the plateau; they are shaping it, preparing small basins for other organisms to inhabit. They build bowls, channels, and microclimates. They are the slow masons of a world without buildings.

The mosses also behave in ways that blur the line between plant, fungus, and animal. Some of the black moss mats are so dense that they hold water like sponge and release it slowly as mist. Others grow in ridged bands that direct the wind and trap vapor. Where the air turns warm near a vent, the moss unfurls; where frost returns, it contracts. This movement is so slow that it feels geological, but if one watches from one thermal pulse to the next, one can see the land opening and closing. In that sense, the moss is the plateau’s most visible breath.

The fungal networks are perhaps the most important inhabitants, not because they are the largest, but because they connect. Their threads spread through the upper soil like pipes and wires. They carry water, nutrients, and signals. They may transmit electrical impulses from one part of a basin to another. When the air is dry and cold, static builds in their filaments. A tiny blue spark can jump from one tuft to the next, traveling along a mossy path like a message too slow for lightning but too fast for ordinary growth.

These sparks may be more than weather. In some places, they seem to correspond to biological events: a fungal shelf opening after a pulse of vapor, a moss pool warming and releasing spores, a line of grazers shifting toward a new vent field. It may be that the sparks help germinate some spores, or that they regulate the chemical balance of moss mats. It may be that they carry information from one part of the land to another, not in the manner of language, but in the manner of a nervous system. The plateau may not speak, but it can be disturbed.

There are also things that have no single form.

Some parts of the plateau seem to be occupied by a living film so widespread that it is impossible to find its edges. A pale sheen over a stone may be one organism, or a community of many organisms working together. A black mat may be one moss, one fungus, one bacterium, and one mineral slime all blended. If one lifts a small piece, it holds together like tissue but crumbles like dry earth. It can be moved by wind, carried by animals, dissolved by frost, and reassembled by warmth. These composite beings are the land’s true residents. They do not need homes. They are the homes of others.

There is no culture here in the human sense. No rituals. No fire. No story. No name. But there are habits, and habits repeated by generations become something like memory. A grazer line follows a path not because each animal remembers every bend, but because the path is embedded in the land itself: moss worn thin, pools kept open, lichen rings arranged by centuries of disturbance. A predator waits where a thousand predecessors have waited before, because the thermal currents have been shaped by generations of bodies. The animals carry the land’s memory in their bodies, and the land carries the animals’ memory in its growth.

If one asks what kind of being lives at the center of this place, there may be no one being at all. But if one watches long enough, the distinction between creature and land begins to soften. The grazers are not merely living on the plateau; they are parts of its metabolism. The predators are not merely hunting through it; they are organizing its movements. The insects and drifting thread-worms are not merely passing through the air; they are connecting its pools, vents, moss mats, and stone shelves. The moss is not merely covering the ground; it is keeping the ground alive. The lichen is not merely decorating the rock; it is teaching the rock how to become soil. The fungi are not merely growing near heat; they are learning how to use it.

So what lives there is this:

It is the black velvet moss and the frost-amber lichen, the fungal shelves that breathe with the vent, the pools that collect water from both above and below, and the blind pale things that live in those pools. It is the dustwing motes that shimmer in the dark, the glass-backed beetles crawling through damp root mats, the thread-thin millipedes walking under fungus like creatures crossing a ceiling. It is the wide grazers with segmented backs and glowing filaments, moving slowly through the high stone, carrying moss like seeds and heat like memory. It is the silent predators with frost-textured fur, waiting beside condensation until the air shifts and prey steps too close. It is the tube-dwellers around the warm black mouths, the drifting silk-worms in the vapor, the mineral films, the bacterial slime, the tiny sparks between moss tips, and the slow cycles of frost and steam.

And beneath all of it, there is the living plateau itself, not dead ground but a vast, patient, distributed body. It has no heart, yet it moves. It has no brain, yet it responds. It has no hands, yet it shapes the stone. It has no roads, yet its creatures follow paths older than their names. It is a factory without builders, a city without citizens, an organism without a single face.

Whoever lives there is many.

Whatever lives there is one.
