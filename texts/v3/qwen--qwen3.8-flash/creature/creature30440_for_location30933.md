If one asks who lives in the Warm Shelf, the question must be bent sideways. This is not a place crowded with individuals the way a finished valley, forest, or shoreline might be crowded. Its inhabitants are mostly thin, slow, pale, translucent, or flat as if pressed into the landscape. Many of them are not clearly one thing rather than another: a membrane, a colony, a floating root-mat, a drifting cloud of particles that happens to be alive. Others are animals, but they behave as though being an animal is a tentative arrangement, not a permanent identity. They live in the intervals between water and stone, warmth and altitude, light and reflection. They do not announce themselves. They do not claim territory with noise. They seem to be trying out a way of living in a world that has only recently arrived at its own existence.

The first and most numerous residents are the thin ones. They cover the surfaces of pools, channels, and flooded shelves like living film. These are not quite moss, not quite algae, not quite lichen, though they share qualities with all three. They form green-gold sheets beneath the water, spreading in slow radial patterns, curling over edges of fresh basalt, creeping along pale limestone shelves with no visible feet or front line. They feed from light and from dissolved minerals rising through fissures. Some are photosynthetic, catching the sun in broad, watery membranes and turning it into a kind of green breath. Others are chemosynthetic, eating the heat and chemistry of the young stone itself. They live at the border where the plateau’s fire-heat meets the sea.

These membranes are the place’s own skin. They polish and stain the water. In still pools they split sunlight into shimmering floors, and at their edges they leave bright crystalline rings where the waterline rises and falls. They are not individuals in any way a person would recognize, but neither are they mere scenery. They respond to warmth, to current, to mineral gradients, to the daily breathing of the water. They expand, contract, divide, merge, and over centuries may form thin crusts that help new stone become something that can hold life. If the Warm Shelf has a true population, this is where its numbers lie: not in the pale animals rare enough to be noted, but in these patient films that cover almost every submerged surface.

Beneath them are the green citizens, the plant-like inhabitants that have learned to live without committing fully to land. They are the most abundant visible life, and the place feels populated mainly by them. There are strap-leafed floaters with waxy leaves that ripple in currents too slow to see. Their stems are hollow and swollen with gas, so the meadows appear to hover just under the water’s surface rather than root into stone. They do not stand still. They drift a little, settle a little, lift a little, as though the sea is gently turning them over in sleep. Their leaves are deep green near the base but flushed bronze, rose, or pale copper where light strikes them. In some basins the water is so crowded with them that it seems less like sea than like a slow green field growing upside down.

Then there are the cup-hand plants. These live along the margins where water rises and falls. When the shelf is submerged, their leaves close into smooth, waxed cones. When the water recedes, they open like hands releasing heat and light, then begin to breathe more directly from the air. They are not merely plants adapting; they seem to keep the conversation between sea and land in their bodies. Some leave behind translucent husks when they change form, thin windows of dried leaf floating on the water or snagged on stone. These husks are not waste so much as discarded habits, the empty shape of a decision already made.

The fern-like growths are stranger still. Their fronds are nearly transparent, their veins pale rivers catching the sunlight. They grow in clusters on shaded ledges and in crevices where moisture lingers. Some stand rooted in shallow water; others lie across the surface as though they have forgotten down. Their movement is almost invisible, but over a day one can see that they have leaned, or curled, or opened toward warmth. They host microscopic life in the spaces between their leaflets, tiny animals that never leave their botanical neighborhoods.

Among the mosses and floaters live smaller creatures, but not swarms. This is an important fact about the place: its abundance is botanical, not animal. Still, there are mobile lives. Some are so slow they seem like extensions of stone. Small plate-backed grazers move across submerged shelves, thin as coins, pale as eggshell, wearing little plates that may not be shells in the ordinary sense but mineral crusts drawn from the warm water. They scrape the membranes that grow on stone, leaving clean crescents behind. Their motion makes no sound, and their trails are visible only when the light is angled just right. They do not hurry. They do not need to. The films regrow around them as they feed, and they are consumed in return by bacteria so tiny that death in their world is mostly a quiet redistribution of minerals.

There are also threadlike swimmers in the spaces between submerged stems, creatures that might be called larvae though they are not always larvae of anything particular. They are translucent, flexible, almost impossible to separate from the water. They feed on drifting particles and the edges of algae, and when they are disturbed they do not dart so much as fade, their bodies becoming less distinct against the green. Their whole existence is a series of pauses. They drift, they pause, they attach to a leaf, they pause. If they have predators, those predators are equally rare and cautious.

The pale amphibians are among the most visible animal residents, though “visible” here means one might not notice them at first because they look like stones that have grown soft. They are frog-shaped only in the sense that water, gravity, and a body with legs have agreed on a general form. Their skin is thin and pale, often translucent enough to show the faint dark shapes of organs beneath. They lie on floating mats of moss or strapleaf with their bellies pressed flat, and their legs spread wide as if they are listening to the surface through their hands. They do not burrow. The ground is too sharp, too wet, too young; there is no soft soil to vanish into. Instead they rest in the living cushions of vegetation, where the warmth of the stone and the water holds them like a palm.

They move slowly because speed would be wasteful. They also move slowly because the water supports them almost as well as the land does. They do not leap or dive. They step or sink from one mossy island to another, letting buoyancy take half their weight. When frightened, they do not cry or thrash; they flatten themselves, become less distinct, and allow the surrounding greenery to hide their outline. If a fly lands on a leaf above them, they may watch it without reacting. If a warm current shifts, they may turn their bodies to follow it, not because they are playful, but because they read the water with their skin.

They reproduce in still pools and hidden channels. Their eggs are laid in gelatinous masses that float near the surface like clear grapes clustered around a green seed. The tadpoles are almost invisible except in backlight, long-tailed threads with wide heads, grazing on film and drifting pollen. Their development is slow. They do not burst into the world in noisy springtime explosions. They emerge over days, weeks, seasons, in the same measured way that water rises and falls. The adults do not sing.
