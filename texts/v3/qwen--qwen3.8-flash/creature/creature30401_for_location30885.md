There is no one living there in the old sense. There is no footstep to disturb the skin of water, no breath that would be visible in the cold, no shadow that could pass across the reeds. No child has been born on the wet terraces. No elder has sat down where the stone is smooth enough to hold the heat of another body. No dog sleeps curled against a wall, no horse stands blinking at the high light, no flock moves across the flats. If a person once made the built places, that person is not here now, and if any people were ever here at all, they have been taken back into the same quiet economy that takes back all other living things. The basin does not mourn them. It simply continues without them.

But what lives there is still very alive.

The inhabitants are a distributed society of dampness. They are not creatures of movement but of wet contact, of surface tension, of slow expansion across stone. They live in the spaces between the large, the small, and the barely visible: in the moss cushion, in the black peat, in the green film on a pool, in the thread beneath the soil. They have no faces, no hunger in the sense of pursuit, no territory defended by sound or violence. They defend themselves by being able to live where very little else can: on bare wet rock, in acid peat, in shallow mineral water, on the thin skins of stone that will not become soil until they have been worked, grain by grain, by the patience of living things.

The most evident citizens are the mosses. They do not form a continuous carpet. They are more sovereign than that. Each moss patch is its own small country, a dense cushion of pale or deep green, standing apart from the next by yards of wet stone and standing water. These are mosses of high places and damp air, the kind that do not need true rain so long as mist remains, so long as the stone weeps, so long as water can be held in the tiny architecture of their leaves. Their leaves are not leaves in the sense of broad sun-hungry things; they are tiny overlapping scales, translucent at their edges, capable of drawing water inward from the air. A moss cushion can hold several times its own weight in water. Press it, and it gives up a clear bead, not because water lies on the surface only, but because the moss has become a vessel, a sponge, a living reservoir. Its threads of rhizoids do not dig deeply into soil so much as grip mineral dust and thin peat, anchoring the patch while it feeds mostly from wetness and light.

Around these moss patches are the sedges and rushes. They are the builders of the rims. Where a depression collects water, they send pale green spikes upward, not tall, not showy, but firm, fibrous, patient. Their roots do not make trees, but they make something as essential: they bind peat. Beneath the visible blades, their rhizomes run through black fibrous matter, turning wet silt into structure, holding the edges of pools together. In dry places they would fail. Here they are not merely surviving; they are constructing. A clump of sedge is both organism and landform. Its dead stems lie down around it and become the base of a small island. New shoots rise from older crowns. Over years, this produces little terraces, slightly raised, slightly acid, slightly stable. A sedge island in the water is not a tree growing from soil; it is a living accumulation of itself, its own future ground.

The reed-like plants along the pools are still more architectural. Their broad leaves, some waxy, some soft and fibrous, hold drops of water for long after the air has moved. The leaves are often shallow cups, not because they collect rain, but because their surfaces are made to bear wetness. Small streams of water run across them and gather at their points. Where they die, their stems fold into dark thatch. The thatch does not rot with any dramatic smell; it is taken back by moisture and microbial labor into the base of the patch. The living reed continues at the outer edge, so that the colony grows outward in a slow ring, advancing where moisture is sufficient and receding where the pool shrinks or the peat dries. In this way, the reed edges move without walking. They creep in the way a tide creeps, in the way a stain creeps across stone, in the way silence creeps after a sound has stopped.

There are also isolated shrubs. They grow on raised platforms where the water is only capillary, just enough to dampen the feet of the plant without drowning it. Each shrub stands alone, leathery-stemmed, low to the ground, its leaves small and thick, its form open rather than crowded. It does not reach toward the light as if competing with a forest. There is no forest. It does not grow fast. It has no need for speed. It sends roots into the thin wash of mineral powder and humus beneath it and remains there for what would be, by human reckoning, many lives. Perhaps it reproduces by dropping seeds into the nearest water film, where they might float a short distance before lodging in the silt. Perhaps some of them can be replaced by broken stems rooting where the damp stone holds them. Perhaps their pollen is carried by nothing more intelligent than air. No creature carries it deliberately from bloom to bloom. There is no buzzing, no landing, no insect leg dusted with gold. The work is done by chance and moisture and wind, and so the shrubs grow alone, but not lonely. Loneliness requires a social expectation. Here, isolation is the social expectation. To be far from another plant is not to be estranged; it is to have been given the right amount of space.

Beneath all these visible forms lives a more extensive population: the fungi. They are not mushrooms in this land, not large caps or pale gills rising from soil to fruit for the notice of something that might eat them. They are mostly invisible, white threads running through peat and mineral dust, branching and rebranching, connecting moss to sedge, dead leaf to living root. Where a moss cushion dies back at its base, fungi work. Where a reed stem collapses into the water, fungi and bacteria meet it there. They do not consume in the sense of devouring; they dissolve, transform, and hand the dissolved substances back. The dead does not vanish into chaos. It becomes available to the living in the smallest, most orderly increments. The fungal threads are the quiet economy of the basin. They are not a forest floor, because there is no forest, but they create something more fundamental: a soil thin enough to be almost imaginary, but real enough to hold life.

In the pools, the citizens are still smaller and still more complete. Algae form bright green skins over still water, not because the pools are rich, but because the water is held in such a way that light and moisture and dissolved mineral remain in balance. These skins are not scum. They are living surfaces. They shimmer slightly when the air moves. When a pool dries a little at its edge, the algal film can remain in a thin green crust that seems to belong to the stone more than to the water. Cyanobacterial mats may be present too, in colors not quite plant green, darker, older, more mineral in their habit. Some of them take nitrogen from the air and offer it to the wet ground. They work in silence, turning thin water into nourishment. Around them, bacteria live in layers, each layer doing its narrow chemical task. Some may feed on iron in the basalt. Some may work on sulfur in the shallow volcanic pores. Some may simply break down dead sedge into soluble things. None of them need mouths. They live by contact, by osmosis, by chemistry. A single drop of water on a stone can be an entire neighborhood.

The built places have become as inhabited as the natural shelves. If there are low stone walls, their inner faces are green with moss and algal film. If there are terraces, they hold peat in the joints. If there are former roads, they are now channels in which moisture migrates from one pool to another. If there are buildings whose doors or windows have long since been gone, the openings have become north-facing niches where damp collects and mosses spread in velvet tongues across lintel and sill. The architecture does not look abandoned. It looks absorbed. The wetness has made the built things continuous with the stone. There is no difference between a rock ledge and a terrace wall once both hold moss. The towns, or what were towns, are not empty. They are filled by the only citizens who can live here: the slow ones, the rooted, the floating, the creeping, the film-making, the thread-running ones.

If one were to name the inhabitants collectively, one might call them the green skin of the basin. But that phrase is too simple, because the life here is not skin-deep. It is also fiber-deep, thread-deep, pool-bottom deep. It includes the dark mat of peat, which is neither soil nor plant nor animal but a living archive of all these things compressed into water and air. A handful of peat lifted from the basin’s floor would show fragments of old moss, broken sedge stems, white fungal threads, brown fibers of half-decayed leaves, tiny voids where water had lived. It would smell wet and faintly iron-tinged, not sweet, not rotting, but patient. The peat is a library written in slow chemistry.

The most surprising fact is that none of this requires an animal. The world seems complete because it is not missing any essential piece of its own kind of life. A basin with no grazing is not starving. A pool with no fish is not silent in the wrong way. The still water is not empty because it is not waiting for something. The shallow films of algae and bacteria use light and dissolved minerals without hurry. The mosses spread by spores released in damp air. The sedges multiply by runners and fragments. The shrubs stand where they can stand. The fungi carry the work of return. The wind carries the smallest things from one wet terrace to another. There is no need for movement with intent. Movement by drift is sufficient.

Reproduction here has a different character. It is not noisy, not urgent, not seasonal in the sense of explosion and completion. A moss patch may grow by producing tiny gemmae, cup-shaped structures that release a mass of cells when water enters them. Sudden water, even only a film, can open a tiny chamber and scatter a cloud of potential moss plants. They land nearby, on damp stone, and begin to grow if the stone holds them. Other mosses release spores in slow capsules; the spores drift a little and then fall into water or onto wet surface. The pool edge may send a fragment of reed across the surface; where it grounds in shallow silt, it takes root. The basin does not need animals to plant it. It plants itself by accident, by current, by the gentle carrying power of wetness.

Death is equally quiet. When a sedge stem dies, it does not become carrion. There is no scavenger to tear it, no fly to colonize it in a flash, no maggot mass, no sudden collapse into bone and smell. It becomes dark fiber. It becomes part of the mat. It becomes the edge of the next island. A reed tip may fall into the pool, lie there for a time, and then settle into the silt, where it is taken apart by threads and bacteria. Nothing is wasted because waste, in the animal sense, does not exist. There is only transformation. The green persists.

The light is clean, but the life does not use light in the dramatic way of a tropical canopy. There are no great trees reaching for sunlight, no vines strangling other trunks, no flowers opening in bold color for pollinators. The vegetation is low and moist, and the light filters through thin mist. Some algae grow directly on stone. Some mosses spread on shaded faces. Some sedges stand in pools, their leaves angled to catch what brightness there is. Their green is not a flash of color but a condition of saturation. They have learned to make living out of wetness and mineral dust.

The whole place can be understood as a series of moist habitats nested inside one another. A wet stone has a habitat of bacteria and algae. A moss cushion has a habitat of moss itself, and within its damp spaces, tiny films of other life. A sedge clump has a habitat of roots and dead stems. A pool has a habitat of floating and submerged films. A peat basin has a habitat of decomposers and builders. The islands of vegetation are not scattered randomly; they mark the places where water can be held long enough for life to continue. The emptiness between them is not absence. It is waiting. It is water held in a thin way, not in a living way. It is stone not yet claimed. It is potential ground.

So whoever lives there is not a “who” at all. No one has a name. No one makes a mark. No one looks up at the pale sky. No one feels the wetness as wetness. The inhabitants are the forms of wetness itself becoming green. They are the beaded leaf, the black thread, the white spore, the bright skin on water. They are the moss that holds a pool, the sedge that holds the peat, the fungus that holds the dead, the algae that hold the light. They are isolated but not disconnected, because the basin’s moisture is a network, and the peat’s threads are a network, and the wind-borne spores are a network, and the tiny films of life on stone are all bound to one another by the same unbroken dampness.

The high place is not empty. It is occupied in the oldest, quietest way. It is a living stone. It is a breathing basin. It is a country where the only movement is growth so slow it looks like the land itself has become alive.
