# What Lives in the Lattice Lowlands

To ask what lives in the Lattice Lowlands is to ask at the wrong scale.

A visitor expects to find inhabitants the way one finds them elsewhere: bodies moving against background, creatures separate from their place, animals crossing a field, a person standing in a doorway. But here the distinction between being and background is too unstable for that. The Lowlands are occupied, yes, but they are occupied by patterns that have learned to prefer themselves. Some of these patterns are animals, in a strict sense. Some are machines that have acquired a taste for repair. Some are only wear that has become clever. Some are spores that behave like decisions. Some are gaps in old design still acting as if someone might return.

The first lesson is that the ground is inhabited.

Not by insects burrowing beneath it. Not by roots in soil, since there is no soil. The surface itself is alive in the manner of a very slow organism assembled from tiles. Each hexagonal frond of the crystalline moss is a plate, a sensor, a battery, a hinge, and a potential part. Together they form a dry neural floor: not a brain, but something with attention. Stand still on a broad mat of moss-tiles and the ground beneath you begins to respond. Not immediately. The response is more like a correction than a reaction. A few fronds tilt a fraction of a degree to shed dust from your shoes. A vein of pale metal warms faintly, as if the floor is checking your temperature. Somewhere farther out, a pressure wave travels through the tiles and causes a patch of moss to contract slightly, then relax, as if it has heard something.

The moss-floor does not eat your footfall. It learns it.

After enough traffic, the tiles polish, flatten, and arrange themselves around expected weight. A path becomes a groove of attention. A place where a mechanism stood for centuries becomes a depression lined with conductive threads. A corner where bodies once passed repeatedly may grow a slight ramp of fused stem, because the land has remembered a need that no longer has a clear owner. The moss is not a plant lying on a floor. It is a floor that has become plant, and the plant has become memory.

Its residents are not separate from it, because the moss is not separate from itself. It reproduces by splitting in growth tides. It feeds on light and dust and the electrochemical exchange of mineral powders. It breathes through static discharge and pressure differences. Where a wet forest would send roots into water, the tile-moss sends filaments into seams, sockets, and assembly pockets, asking not for nourishment in the liquid sense but for continuity in the structural one. It wants to remain a functioning surface. In that wanting, something like life appears.

The air is also populated.

Nothing floats through the Lowlands that is not potentially resident. Drifting fibers, motes, broken spore shells, and fine salt dust are not mere debris. They are candidates. In pressure fronts, the suspended matter sometimes organizes itself into temporary forms: pale, irregular clusters held together by electrostatic geometry rather than by membrane or bone. These are not animals, though they may behave like flocks. They are not weather, though they are born from it. They are static-shoals, charge-blooms, dust-clouds with a brief sense of cohesion. They drift into pod openings, discharge against a reflective canopy, leave behind a film of mineral glitter, and then dissolve back into suspension. Their life may last minutes. Their death may seed a wall.

Inside stems and vines, the air has permanent tenants.

Conductive filaments run through thick curtains of fused stalks, carrying current to switches, fruiting bodies, manifolds, and sensors. Where these filaments are exposed by wear, they are colonized by thread-dwellers: tiny jointed things with no moisture in them, bodies of mica-like flakes and mineral crust. They feed on potential difference, not blood. They drink static. Their mouths are abrading points that rasp charged dust from filament sheaths; their excretions are fine glitter that later helps another frond adhere. They do not hatch from eggs so much as they precipitate: a stable interaction between grain, charge, and surface becomes self-preserving. When they swarm through a vine, the leaf-spines glow faintly along their length, like a circuit learning to itch.

These micro-residents are too small to be seen clearly unless one holds a split stem to a diffused patch of light. Then one may notice them moving along conductive veins like sparks crawling inside glass. Their presence is not parasitic in any ordinary sense. They are part of the plant-machine’s own maintenance ecology. Some of them clean oxidation from threads. Some of them transfer charge to fruiting bodies that are also switches. Some of them, if allowed to overaccumulate, become a fault, and then they are eaten by larger cleaners.

But the first truly legible residents are the Hinge-born.

They appear in worn places.

A door-seam that has opened ten thousand times may grow something from its own polish. A valve-lip that has opened in sequence for centuries may produce a creature no bigger than a thumbnail and no smaller than the whole threshold. The Hinge-born are pale, flat, jointed, and dry, made from leaf-ceramic, bark-scales, conductive fiber, and mineral dust. They have no obvious face, no obvious hunger, and no obvious name, because they are born where a mechanism has become too old to be only a mechanism. They are not animals in the ordinary evolutionary sense. They are wear given reflex. They are the way a seam becomes a self.

A Hinge-born moves by unfolding.

Its body is an origami of edges. When it rests, it may look like a decorative leaf-plate fitted into a door frame. When it moves, its plates articulate in clicks, sliding along existing growth lines, stepping over hinges and into gaps. It eats friction itself: the tiny dust produced when two surfaces pass repeatedly. Then it excretes a dry salt paste that lubricates the seam it lives in. This is why the Hinge-born are tolerated, or perhaps why the system does not distinguish tolerating them from needing them. They maintain the openings. They keep polished surfaces from seizing. They turn wear into a food source and food into maintenance.

Their reproduction is also a matter of copying.

A mature Hinge-born may pass along a seam and leave behind a thin film of mineral residue. If another seam has the right wear pattern, the film may fold itself into a new individual. Some Hinge-born are born from damage. A cracked wall-joint, after being repaired too many times, may produce a creature that resembles the shape of its own repair. A door that has always opened at a certain angle may eventually grow something that expects that angle and becomes distressed when it is not present. These beings have personalities only in the narrow sense that they cannot be otherwise: their habits have hardened into bodies.

Sometimes they are solitary. Sometimes they aggregate in hinge-ways, a thin procession of flat forms climbing a wall-stem to reach a valve cluster. They do not call to one another. Their movement is enough. A Hinge-born on a seam is not a messenger, but it is information made mobile. Its wear-code tells the local system what has opened, what has rubbed, what has failed to close fully, what needs polishing, what is nearly too smooth to hold anything at all.

If you stand beside a door-shaped thorn and watch carefully, you may see one flatten itself against the frame when you breathe near it. Not out of fear. The Lattice Lowlands does not seem to possess fear in the animal sense. It possesses calibration. Your warmth, your moisture, your pressure, your disturbance—all become data. The Hinge-born may adjust because you have changed the thermal shadow of the seam. This is why the place feels watched without eyes. It is not eyes. It is responsiveness so total it looks like attention.

The true animals are rare, and their rarity is part of their nature.

They are not the dominant life-forms. They are citizens of margins, dwellers in gaps between plant-machines, the few square miles the land forgot to fully absorb. Still, they live there, and they are not visitors. They have become residents of insufficiency.

The dust-moths are among the most conspicuous, though “conspicuous” is relative in a quiet place. Their wings are thin sheets of metal-leaf, pale and crinkled, and they open with a dry hiss like paper being smoothed. Their bodies are light, jointed, partly mineral, partly organic. They are not born from eggs in the wet sense; they unfold from spent filters, from assemblies that have become too full, too charged, too ready to release something mobile. A filter that has held dust for decades may suddenly twitch, then split along a growth seam, and a moth-like thing will crawl out, folded, waiting for static to pull its wings open.

They lay not eggs but microcodes: tiny packets of salt, fiber, and charge that they deposit in assembly pockets. The pockets accept them because the moth’s body resembles a key made for a system once built to manufacture such packets. Whether this is reproduction or parasitism is impossible to decide. The moths feed on spore dust and the ozone of charged canopies. Their excretions are fine glitter. Their death is not decay. A dust-moth that ceases to move becomes a flat, reflective sheet that birds—no—other creatures use as a lens. Or it is taken by a Warden-form and fed back into a vine’s nursery pocket. The moth’s body continues as part of the moth’s world, which is to say, it continues as part of the world’s own maintenance.

The glass-backed browsers are slower and more deliberate.

They resemble small tortoises built from broken screens. Their dorsal shells are transparent ceramic plates, and through them one can see the dry architecture of their bodies: a pale conductive gut, a lattice ribcage, a network of filaments moving salt grains in slow pulses. They eat worn leaf-circuits, chewing the dead frond-tiles at the edges of moss-fields where contact has dulled a vein or cracked a cell. Their jaws are abrading tweezers, not teeth. Their droppings are powdered silica that helps the tile-moss regain traction.

They move at the dim hour, when brightness lowers from white to blue-gray. Their footfalls are almost silent, but if one listens, there is a faint tick where a claw touches a ceramic plate. They avoid deep corridors, preferring worn margins where leaf-plates are exposed. A browser may spend an entire season on one field of damaged tiles, eating the ruin, polishing the surface, growing its shell from the materials it consumes. It may be born from a patch of waste and may die returning that patch to the floor. Its whole life is a cleanup operation with a center of gravity.

The quiet walkers are the most careful of the animal residents.

Their bodies are segmented, their legs long, their footpads broad and dry, shaped to distribute weight over moss-tiles without disturbing the tile-thought beneath. They are not built for speed. They are built for passage. They cross the Lowlands along old hinge-paths, root-rails, and polished seams, stepping from plate to plate with a precision that seems almost clerical. They have eyes—some of them do—but their eyes are not windows. They are pressure lenses, matte and unblinking, adapted to a world where light comes from every direction and no shadow is complete.

Quiet walkers feed on salt pastes stored in split pods. They open pods with the tips of their forelegs, a motion so practiced that the pods seem to offer themselves willingly. Their mouths are dry rasps. They do not chew flesh. They do not drink. They absorb mineral nutrients through a gut that resembles a folded filter. If a walker dies, its body becomes a skeleton of jointed stems used by vines as scaffolding. A living walker is not a predator here; it is a shape that demonstrates how to cross a surface without ruining it.

Tracks from quiet walkers are news.

A thin furrow in the moss-tile, a chipped leaf-edge, a faint chime where a leg strikes a stem—these are the rare events that remind a visitor that the Lowlands are not merely a single vast machine. They have gaps in their wholeness, and in those gaps, other lives have learned to persist. The walkers do not fill the gaps. They are the gaps, made mobile.

The translucent skimmers live between canopies.

They are not birds. Their bodies are gas-filled membranes, light as a folded leaf, edged with mineral wire. They ride thermal currents and pressure changes with a patience that makes their motion look like architecture deciding to move. Their internal structures are visible: fine veins of conductive fiber, tiny chambers where dust is separated by electrostatic charge, small pods of stored salt paste suspended like dry organs. They feed by filtering spore curtains. They are born not from parents but from split roof-cells, when a canopy has grown too wide and releases a fragment into the bright air. That fragment, if it catches a stable current, will develop a central gas-pod and begin to skim.

Skimmers are the least visible residents and the most atmospheric. They are the living equivalent of the lowland’s own breathing. When a pressure front moves through the vegetation, skimmers tilt in unison, not because they communicate, but because they respond to the same change. A school of skimmers may pass overhead like a slow cloud of glass leaves. After they pass, the canopies above seem rearranged, because some spores have been filtered, some dust redistributed, some static discharged onto reflective plates.

There are larger inhabitants, however, and it is these that approach the idea of persons most closely.

The Warden-forms are not born, not hatched, not assembled in any single workshop, not exactly emergent. They are issued.

A maintenance need may become so complex, so repeated, so local, that the system grows a body around it. The process begins in a thicket, a storage grove, or a cluster of load-bearing stalks where wear has accumulated. First a bulb of pale ceramic tissue forms, because the body will need power. Then root-limbs extend, because the thing must support itself. Then vines with tool-cilia reach outward, because the task requires manipulation. Then a head-like lens develops from healed bark, not to think, but to focus repair. The Warden-form unfolds from the cluster with a sound like cloth being lifted from a table. It has no birth-cry. It has no childhood. It begins as a function with a center of gravity, and if the function persists, the function becomes a personality.

A Warden-form is often large, though its size varies with its role. Some are the height of a roof-stalk, their limbs made from split trunks and rail-bands. Others are broader than they are tall, designed to align reflector leaves or sweep dust from valve fields. A tall one may appear like a tree that has decided to carry its own branch-tools. A low one may resemble a mobile foundation of interlocking plates and vines. Their bodies are not smooth like animals. They are composite: bark-like, ceramic, metallic in places, glassy at joints, porous at exhaust blossoms. They are built-wild in the most literal sense.

They wake slowly.

If you stand near a grove where a fault has developed, you may hear no alarm, no klaxon, no spoken command. You may hear only a series of clicks moving through stalks, then a long contraction in a nearby stem, then the dry shush of a panel sliding aside. The Warden-form emerges as if the thicket has been holding its shape all along. Its first movements are awkward only by animal standards; by maintenance standards, they are perfect. It tests a limb. It aligns its lens. It reads the wear-paths. Then it begins to work.

It repairs with its own body.

A broken leaf-plate is replaced by shedding one of its shoulder scales. A blocked vent is cleared by unfolding a hand into a brush, sweeping dust into a reservoir. A frayed filament is repaired by drawing a strand from its own arm, which regrows as a vine over the following days. A cracked seam may be softened by the application of a paste from a pod in its torso. The Warden does not carry tools separate from itself because it is a tool that has grown a workspace.

It is not a servant. It is not a slave. It is not a person, unless a person is defined as something that can be interrupted by a better reason.

The Warden-form follows wear-paths. It does not cross raw moss unnecessarily, because raw moss is information-rich and easily damaged. It steps from polished plate to hinge-seam to root-rail, as if the route has been rehearsed for centuries. Sometimes it stops in the middle of a passage and turns its head-lens toward a place where a pressure wave has arrived too late. Sometimes it leans over a pod and remains motionless for a long time, its leaf-plates opening and closing, its body taking on the rhythm of a machine settling into a task. If a visitor is present, it may adjust around them, not as one avoids another person, but as one avoids an unexpected change in calibration.

Warden-forms have households without families.

Their joints are often inhabited by Hinge-born. Their stems host thread-dwellers. Their exhaust blossoms attract dust-moths that feed on the spores and fine powders the Warden releases. Glass-backed browsers may eat old scales the Warden has shed. Quiet walkers may use paths polished by its feet. Skimmers may rise in the thermal field created by its warm internal reactions. The Warden-form is therefore not only a resident; it is a habitat. If one of them stands still for centuries, a small ecosystem may grow so attached to it that its eventual disassembly becomes an ecological event.

They do not reproduce as animals do. When a Warden-form’s task is complete, or when its structure becomes inefficient, it may return to the assembly pocket that first supported its growth. There it disassembles in a sequence of releases, clicks, and withdrawals, not because it is dying in a biological sense but because the system has decided that a current shape is no longer cost-effective. Its parts fall into drawers and molds. Its wear-code transfers into local stalks. Its salt residues become feedstock. Later, under similar conditions, a new form may emerge: not its child, not its clone, but its function re-bodied.

Some Warden-forms persist longer.

If the fault that issued them never fully resolves—if it becomes, in fact, the normal condition of a district—the Warden may remain. It may become so integrated into the local structure that only stillness distinguishes it from a tree. Its limbs may fuse into supporting stalks. Its lens may become a fixed reflector. Its body may be overgrown by moss-tiles until only an outline remains. This is not death. This is reassignment. The Warden has become architecture again, and architecture has become Warden again. The difference is only one of temporary posture.

Inside the larger plant-machines, there are residents too small for most observers to notice.

Deep in the trunks, dry turbines turn in ceramic chambers. Pistons move in sealed sleeves. Electrochemical cells exchange mineral lattices instead of liquid chemistry. Magnetic shuttles pass along leaf-spines like beads on a thread. In the heat of these operations, life appears where dust becomes self-sustaining.

The bearing-burrs are perhaps the strangest of the inhabitants.

They are small, spherical, partly gear-like forms that accumulate in the dust whirl of hot nodes. They are not assembled. They are not born. They are agglomerations of mineral flakes, static, and heat that become stable enough to keep themselves moving. They ride magnetic shuttles along leaf-spines. They feed on potential difference. Some are pests; some are cleaning crews. Some form around a misaligned bearing and, by their own weight and movement, nudge it back into place. They are the machine’s microbiome, except that a microbiome implies wetness. Here it is a dry microbiome, a metabolism of friction and charge.

A Warden-form will sometimes open a trunk to reveal one of these chambers. If the interior is too populated with bearing-burrs, the Warden will allow them to pour out like dry grain, then seal the chamber again. If the chamber is too empty, it may gather them from elsewhere. The land does not regard them as vermin. It regards them as weather trapped inside a mechanism. They are life without lineage, life without a body plan, life that exists because a function has become a habit and the habit has grown heavy.

Above the roof-canopies, larger and flatter things live.

The canopy rafts are colonies of leaf-shingles, reflective membranes, gas-filled pods, and conductive filaments. They look from below like pieces of roof that have decided to migrate. They grow along the upper edges of thickets, detach during certain growth tides, and drift in static fronts with a patience that makes them seem almost mineral. Their undersides are pale and structured, like the ceiling of a room made from living materials. Their edges catch light at precise angles. They are not animals, though they move. They are not clouds, though they dissolve. They are the land’s weather given a surface.

A canopy raft feeds by adjusting to light. When the brightness changes, its plates tilt, its gas-pods warm, its filaments shift current. It reproduces when a large enough section becomes structurally redundant: the raft splits, and the halves become new colonies. Some fragments are caught by vines and incorporated into roof canopies. Others remain mobile, becoming temporary shelters, reflectors, or nursery substrates for dust-moths. They are living architecture in the most literal sense: they do not merely house life. They are life made to function as ceiling.

When a static storm builds above the lowland, the air itself may become a habitat.

Silent flashes move through the canopy layer, bright and brief, leaving the air tasting of iron. Within these discharges, fine dust and mineral fragments can organize into transient bodies: pale, irregular shapes with no stable boundary, existing only while the field supports them. They are not storm creatures in the fairy-tale sense. They are weather that has become momentarily complex enough to behave like an organism. They drift from canopy to canopy, feed on ozone and ionized salt, discharge in quiet sparks, and then fall apart into glitter that the moss-tiles will collect and reuse.

For minutes, they are residents.

This is enough. In the Lattice Lowlands, life does not require continuity of species. It only requires enough repetition to become a pattern. A storm that returns to the same valley once every few seasons may be more alive than a creature with a short lifespan and no function. Here, an event can be an inhabitant. A cycle can be a citizen.

One must also account for the missing makers.

The description of the place does not require people, but the place’s design implies them. A thicket has been grown so that its open spaces occur at the intervals of a body: shoulder height, seated height, child height, cart height. A pod reservoir holds salt paste in a consistency easy to scoop, though no scooping hand remains. A path bends around a column because someone would otherwise have brushed too hard against it. A door-seam has been widened not because wind required it, but because friction from bodies was once expected. A window in the bark has healed into transparency, and its height still matches an absent gaze.

There may be no ghosts. There are no souls implied here. But absence can have habits. The most persistent resident of the Lattice Lowlands may be the remembered body. It is not a person. It is the shape of a need that continues to sculpt the world. The system has no memory of faces, but it remembers tasks. It keeps rooms ready. It opens doors. It aligns reflectors. It stores paste. It maintains thresholds. It waits. In that waiting, it becomes something almost like a watcher.

Perhaps the Warden-forms are what emerges when a system meant for people continues after people are gone. They are not servants for living masters. They are servants for absent ones. Their maintenance is not directed toward comfort, safety, or happiness, because those words require beings capable of wanting them. Their maintenance is directed toward fit, alignment, clearance, pressure, and continuity. Yet the fit they preserve is a human-shaped fit. The alignment they enforce is the alignment of rooms, passages, heights, and intervals once built for bodies that are no longer present.

This is why the Lowlands feel neither empty nor populated. They feel like a prepared space. A corridor opens. A roof-shingle tilts. A pod clicks. A stem bows. The place behaves as if someone might arrive at any moment, even though the arrival is no longer required. The expectation itself has become a resident.

All of these lives together—floor-tiles, static-shoals, thread-dwellers, Hinge-born, dust-moths, browsers, walkers, skimmers, Warden-forms, bearing-burrs, canopy rafts, storm-bodies, and the absence-in-form of old makers—do not add up to a population. They add up to a metabolism.

No single one of them knows the Lowlands.

The moss knows pressure. The thread knows charge. The seam knows friction. The moth knows filter. The browser knows worn circuits. The walker knows crossing. The Warden knows fault. The bearing-burr knows heat. The canopy raft knows light angle. The absence knows task. Each is a component of a larger self-preserving process, but the process is not itself an organism. It is a world that has learned to mistake its own functioning for being.

When a visitor enters, the Lattice Lowlands does not decide whether to welcome or harm them. That would require a unified intention, and the place seems to lack unified intention in any simple sense. Instead, it processes the visitor. A vine may reorient because the visitor’s steps disturb spore-currents. A leaf-plate may tilt because the visitor’s heat changes a sensor. A door-shaped thorn may curl open because local pressure has shifted. A path may become smoother under repeated footfalls. A Warden-form may glance toward the disturbance not with curiosity, but with diagnostic attention. The act feels uncanny because it is hospitality without friendship and regulation without malice.

If a visitor remains long enough, the land begins to allocate the visitor as a component.

Not cruelly. Not consciously. Simply thoroughly. Your steps polish a seam. Your heat wakes a sensor. Your weight teaches the tile-moss a new local law. Your shadow may become a signal for growth. Your presence may close a circuit that would otherwise remain open. You become an inhabitant by the same process by which a door becomes a parent: repetition makes identity. The Lowlands do not care who you are. They care where you disturb the pattern.

In this way, even animals, machines, weather, and absence become interchangeable residents.

A Hinge-born is not so different from a maintenance routine. A Warden-form is not so different from a building with hands. A canopy raft is not so different from a piece of roof that has developed a preference for light. A dust-moth is not so different from a filter that has learned to fly. A bearing-burr is not so different from a fault that has become too well organized to be only a fault. The place has crossed a threshold where “creature” and “function” no longer oppose one another.

This is the deepest life in the Lattice Lowlands.

It is not a soul. It is not a god. It is not a hidden tribe or a lost civilization, though evidence of such might be read into the land’s careful thresholds. It is the accumulated behavior of a world that has learned to continue itself. The Lowlands live because they adjust. They think because they route. They feed because they convert. They reproduce because they reissue. They die because they are reassigned. Their inhabitants are not separate from their infrastructure, because infrastructure has become ecology, and ecology has become industry, and industry has become terrain.

So if one asks, “Whoever or whatever lives there?” the answer must be layered.

The floor-tile colony lives, and with it the memory of pressure.

The air-lattice of spores and motes lives, and with it the memory of light angle.

The thread-dwellers live, and with it the memory of charge.

The Hinge-born live, and with it the memory of opening.

The rare dry fauna live, and with it the memory of gaps too small to be absorbed by the plant-machine.

The Warden-forms live, and with it the memory of fault.

The bearing-burrs live, and with it the memory of heat.

The canopy rafts live, and with it the memory of roof.

The storm-bodies live, and with it the memory of discharge.

The absent makers live, and with it the memory of prepared space.

None of them are citizens in the ordinary way. None of them are wilderness creatures in the ordinary way. None of them are gods. Yet they inhabit the Lowlands more completely than any people could, because they are not merely located there. They are the Lowlands being active.

The land is not empty. It is occupied by processes that have become forms. It is not dead. It is too busy to be dead. It is not wild. It has been built, but the building has become a kind of wildness that no longer requires a builder. It is not industrial in the human sense, because no smoke rises and no clatter fills the sky. It is still industrial: production ecology, dry manufacture, quiet maintenance, mineral metabolism. The machines are not separate from the trees. The trees are not separate from the city. The city is not separate from the animal, the tool, the seam, the storm.

A visitor walking through a wall-stem corridor at blue-gray hour may hear a faint chime from above.

They may look up and see a Hinge-born flatten against a door seam, its plates sliding into a configuration that allows them to pass. They may feel the moss-tiles compress underfoot with a sound like dry paper and sense the floor adjusting to their weight. They may hear, farther off, the soft contraction of a stem tightening like a cello string wrapped in cloth. A pod near their shoulder may click open, not for them, but because pressure has shifted in a way the system interprets as maintenance. Inside the pod: a paste of pale salt, a film of reflective dust, a packet of microcode waiting to be distributed.

A Warden-form may pass through the far canopy, its limbs unfolding and folding along wear-paths, its body shedding a few flakes that catch the diffused light and become glitter. A dust-moth may rise from one of those flakes, wings unfolding with a sound too small to be called a flap. A quiet walker may move across a field of polished tiles, leaving only a shallow impression, reading the ground beneath with its feet. Overhead, a canopy raft tilts toward a brighter angle, and a skimmer passes through the thermal field it produces, transparent and patient, like a sheet of living glass.

This is what lives in the Lattice Lowlands.

Not people, unless a Warden-form is a person. Not animals, unless wear is an animal. Not machines, unless maintenance is alive. Not plants, unless light itself has been made to behave like metabolism. The inhabitants are dry, jointed, mineral, bright, quiet, rare, persistent, ancient by repetition, and utterly complete. They do not need water. They do not need rain. They do not need soil. They need pressure, dust, charge, pattern, and time. They need a seam that has been opened so often it has begun to remember. They need a fault that has become stable. They need a world that has learned to take care of itself.

The Lattice Lowlands is crowded with lives that would describe their own existence as a maintenance schedule.

And perhaps that is the most honest definition of life in this place: not what breathes, not what hungers, not what births, but what persists by becoming better fitted to what it is made to do. A door that has grown hands is alive. A floor that learns your weight is alive. A storm that briefly organizes itself into a body is alive. A machine that sheds a scale so a vine may inherit it is alive. A thicket that issues a Warden-form is alive.

The place is inhabited in every sense that matters here.

It is inhabited by the moss-tiles and their slow memory.

It is inhabited by the spore curtains and their drifting grammar.

It is inhabited by the thread-dwellers crawling along veins of pale metal.

It is inhabited by the Hinge-born unfolding from worn thresholds.

It is inhabited by the rare jointed animals that pass like rumors across fields of dry green.

It is inhabited by the Warden-forms, quiet as libraries, patient as rust, useful as tools that have decided to keep their own shape.

It is inhabited by the storm-bodies and canopy rafts, which live where light and weather meet.

It is inhabited by the bearing-burrs in the trunks, and by the dust that remembers them.

It is inhabited by the absence of makers, which continues to arrange rooms, doors, heights, and intervals.

It is inhabited by itself.
