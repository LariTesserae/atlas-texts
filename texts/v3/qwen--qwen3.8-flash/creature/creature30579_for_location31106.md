The grazers are not herds. In the Blister, a “herd” is two droplets touching.

If one insists on naming what lives there, the names should be temporary, because the residents are less a fixed population than a recurring condition of the pocket: something the mountain keeps trying to grow in the shape of a wound. No creature here is large enough to be seen clearly by an unaided human eye unless it happens to cluster, refract light, or gather near the fissure’s mouth. Most are no bigger than a grain of salt, and some are barely more than a change in the way the wetness clings to glass. Yet there are so many of them, and their lives move so constantly, that the pocket appears not to contain animals so much as to be crawling with the idea of animal.

The first inhabitants are not animals at all, though later life depends on them. They are the heat-skins: thin, gray, faintly iridescent mats that spread over wet surfaces and around the rims of droplets. They have no color the way leaves have color. They are pale where the steam is strong and darker where salt and iron accumulate, and they often look like smears of mineral film, not life. When one is disturbed, it does not recoil. It simply begins to grow again, if there is enough moisture and enough heat, and the disturbance closes behind it like a healed scratch.

These mats feed on the boundary between water and vapor. They are not photosynthetic in any ordinary sense; they do not turn sunlight into sugar. Light enters through the crack and is merely a condition they can tolerate, sometimes seem to use, but never worship. Their nourishment comes from dissolved gases, from sulfur and silica and sodium carried by condensation, from the slow decay of fresh glass, from the thermal gradient between the hot floor and the cooler dome above. They taste the rock by touching it. Their membranes are so thin that a droplet sitting on them can look like a stone in the eye of a smaller creature. In places where the mats are old—old meaning hours, perhaps a few days—they become tough and layered, like skin over a burn. In places where they are new, they are translucent enough to show the hairline fractures beneath, and the whole pocket looks as though it has begun to remember itself as a body.

From these skins rise the glassy filaments.

Whether those filaments are truly alive is not a question the inhabitants ever seem to ask. They are fine crystalline threads, clear, pale yellow, or iron-red, growing along moisture channels with a kind of patience that resembles intent. They branch, they fuse, they bend around droplets and then, if the droplet moves, leave behind a thin scar of silica marking the path of contact. They look like frost made patient. They look like nerves. They look like the beginning of a nervous system if one is inclined to find one. Some extend into suspended droplets and float there without breaking surface tension, appearing as tiny curved needles inside clear spheres. Others form mats so dense they seem furry, and bead-walkers travel across those mats as though the fur were a road.

The bead-walkers are the most obvious animals, if obviousness means something that moves with a body rather than merely growing. They are round-bodied and almost completely enclosed by the droplets they live in. Some are only visible because a droplet carries a dark dot inside it, a knot of wet animal matter that shifts with the surface tension. They do not fall through water as a human might fear; they move on the water’s skin, pressing against it from above and below, rolling the droplet itself across the glass. To watch one is to watch a temporary world move. The droplet is home, water supply, sensory organ, shield, and grave. If the bead-walker finds a wetter surface, it may leave the droplet behind. If the droplet evaporates, it may leave the bead-walker behind as a ring of salt and a faint dark curl that quickly dries into a speck. Some bead-walkers reproduce by growing too large for one droplet and then splitting, the two halves pulling apart into separate drops that are each less than they were and enough to become whole. Others exchange minerals through their undersides as if mating were a kind of chemistry lesson. They have no eyes in any familiar sense. They sense curvature, heat, vibration, and the taste of the glass beneath the water.

Thread-skimmers are longer, paler, and harder to see than bead-walkers. They travel along the crystalline filaments without gripping them, as though held by an invisible rhythm between the filament’s charge and the moisture film coating it. Their bodies are soft and jointless, more like a stretched droplet than an insect. They appear to shiver, but that shiver is how they move. When a thread-skimmer passes along a filament, the whole filament may brighten for a moment—not by glowing, but by refracting light differently, like a wet strand catching the sun in a way that makes the eye suspect a second strand beside it. They feed at the junctions where filaments meet droplets, drawing out salts and dissolved minerals through their undersides. In dense places, they can resemble pale worms swimming through dry air, except that there is no dry air: every movement happens within a film so thin it is almost a habit.

Shard-hunters are more animal than the others, if “more animal” means more obviously bounded, more sharply defined, more likely to be perceived as an individual body. They are flattened, wide in relation to length, with fine legs that move over glass without leaving a visible trail. They hunt in groups, though “hunt” may be too deliberate a word. They gather around thick mineral skins and press their bodies against them until a flake of obsidian, a crystal ridge, or a hard salt crust becomes a tool without becoming a tool. They borrow sharpness the way a hand borrows a surface: they do not craft it, do not carry it away, do not return. One shard-hunter may wedge a chip of glass against a mat and pull itself backward, tearing a strip of living crust, while the chip remains exactly where it was found. Another may scrape a droplet’s edge against a crystal thread and feed on the residue. They do not eat the whole surface; they eat the exposed boundary between growth and destruction.

They are silent in a way that unsettles an observer. Not because they are small, but because their bodies seem to damp vibration. A thousand shard-hunters crossing the same patch of glass do not rustle, click, or patter. If a human finger were laid on the rock nearby, the vibration might travel through skin and bone as a faint clicking, like teeth in another person’s mouth. The animals themselves are not silent because they have no movement; they are silent because the pocket is too dense with surfaces, too full of moisture, too close to itself. Sound cannot travel far without being absorbed by films, droplets, salt rims, and bodies that are more like extensions of the glass than independent things.

Predators are less numerous, but their effect is felt everywhere.

The first are lens-hunters: bead-like predators no larger than a pinpoint, with clear or faintly tinted bodies that function as multiple droplet lenses. They do not chase prey in any continuous way. They sit at the edge of a wet surface and bend light through themselves until the area ahead appears distorted. A grazing bead-walker may move toward what seems to be a larger, safer droplet, only to find that the droplet is actually the lens of the predator’s face. The surface tension shifts. The prey does not drown, exactly; it is drawn inward, compressed against a boundary, its body spread thin across the predator’s wet under-side. Lens-hunters are also the creatures most likely to appear doubled or multiplied, because their bodies refract so efficiently that one may seem to be three, five, or seven until the light changes angle. In daylight through the crack, they are not beautiful in the way a flower is beautiful. They are exact. Their beauty is the beauty of a small thing that has made an error of perception into a mouth.

Others are thread-wolves, if one allows the word. These are not mammals; they are not even clearly separate organisms. They are clusters of fine, branching filaments that contract when a nearby body disturbs the humidity. They resemble nothing so much as a tuft of glass fur that has learned to close around wetness. They do not eat solid flesh so much as they dissolve the films that make other creatures possible. Where they pass, mats die back and droplets break apart into smaller, poorer drops. They are predators of moisture, predators of surface tension, predators of the medium in which the rest of life swims. A thread-wolf may take a bead-walker not by swallowing it whole, but by making the droplet that holds it collapse. The animal then becomes a dry speck in the salt, which the wolf may then incorporate into its own branching mass.

There are also mirror-eaters, and those are the hardest to describe honestly.

They may not exist. They may be only the way light fails in certain alignments of droplets and filaments. But if they do exist, they are less animals than errors that learned to feed. They appear on the surfaces of droplets a moment before the thing they are about to eat appears in the place where its reflection should be. They look like dark spots that move in formation, like a stain that briefly agrees to have a body. They do not consume matter in the obvious way. They consume states: the nearness a creature had to becoming visible, the image it made in another droplet, the wet outline it left on a crystal after moving away. Some observers may say this is metaphor. Some observers may be wrong. The Blister does not seem to make a clear distinction between what is alive and what is almost alive. It treats both as wetness that has grown interested in itself.

The salt-murmurations are similarly uncertain. These are not droplets, not threads, not predators. They are grains—fine grains of salt, mineral dust, and sometimes fragments of the heat-skins—moving in coordinated patterns along moisture films. At first glance they look like scattered debris. Then the debris rearranges itself. A cluster forms a line, the line bends, the bend branches into something resembling a cursive letter or a larva’s outline. Then it collapses. Then it forms again near a different droplet. These patterns seem less like animals than like the pocket’s nervous system dreaming in salt. If they are alive, they are alive the way a whirlpool might be alive: not a body, but a persistence of motion. If they are not alive, they behave with such repeated intentionality that the distinction stops mattering to the creatures around them. Bead-walkers move away when the murmurations gather. Thread-skimmers change their vibration. Shard-hunters stop grazing and flatten against the glass, as though the salt is reading the water.

Reproduction, such as it is, occurs everywhere and nowhere.

There are no eggs, no young, no clear generations. Most inhabitants are born from division, from fusion, or from the failure of a droplet. When a bead-walker’s droplet evaporates too slowly, the animal may leave behind a film that thickens into a new mat, from which smaller creatures later bud. When two filaments touch under the right heat, they may exchange mineral charges and grow a new branch that resembles neither parent exactly. When a shard-hunter dies, its body may be consumed by heat-skin until only a dark outline remains; that outline may become a place where moisture collects more readily, and new grazers may arrive there as though summoned. Death does not disappear in the Blister. It dries. It is preserved as a ring of salt, a curled residue, a pale thread inside silica, or a faint shadow that the steam keeps briefly in place before erasing.

The inhabitants are also constantly being made of the same materials as their environment. Their bodies are not separate from the glass in the way a bird is separate from a tree. The boundary is more like the boundary between skin and water: real, but permeable, constantly renegotiated. Many animals have no true exoskeleton. They have tension. They have films. They have a temporary agreement between mineral and moisture. If the pocket grows too dry, they collapse into their surroundings and wait. If the pocket grows too hot, they migrate downward along the walls or flatten themselves into the coolest microscopic crevices. If the pocket grows too wet, they may disperse, riding larger droplets down into basins where the heat-skins are thick enough to feed them. They live by thresholds. They are threshold-life.

This is why they feel overwhelming without being large.

The pocket is full not of bodies but of near-bodies. A human eye looking through the crack will find motion in places where nothing visible seems to move: a droplet shifting its shape, a filament bending by an amount too small to notice except by comparing it to memory, a ring of salt expanding as moisture leaves, a pale dot inside a bead that was not there a second before. The fauna is a swarm of small changes, and because the changes happen so close together, one cannot always tell where the terrain ends and the life begins. The glass weeps. The salt arranges. The thread trembles. Something feeds. Something dies. The pocket becomes.

If one presses an eye to the fissure and watches long enough, a few individual presences become possible.

A bead-walker enters a droplet already resting on a curved glass plate. The droplet is almost perfectly round, trembling but not breaking. Inside it, a pale dot settles, then begins to move with a strange rolling patience. The bead-walker does not appear to drink in the way an animal would. It presses itself against the inner surface of the water and the surface seems to dim for a moment, as though the droplet is losing a small part of its clarity. The bead-walker leaves no waste that can be seen, only a slight clouding behind it. It rolls toward a nearby filament, and the filament bends into the droplet as though it had been waiting there all along. The two meet. The droplet shivers. The thread-skimmer that had been moving along the filament outside the droplet reverses direction. This may be predation, reproduction, communication, or simply chemistry becoming too intimate to call anything else.

On a shelf near the mouth of the pocket, a shard-hunter crawls into a dense mat. It stops. Its body flattens until its outline nearly disappears into the gray skin. A loose obsidian flake lies beside it, half embedded in a salt rim. The shard-hunter presses against the flake and pulls backward. The flake catches. A strip of mat tears away. The shard-hunter drinks the torn edge, if drinking is the right word, or perhaps eats it, if eating is the right word. Then it moves on, leaving the flake exactly where it had been. No one would call this tool use. The flake has no handle, no wear from previous use, no ownership. But it also does not remain unused. Later another shard-hunter finds the same flake and repeats the movement. Then another. This is not culture. It is more like a groove becoming easier for water to follow.

Near the dome’s inner skin, condensation collects in beads that slide downward. One such bead contains a dark speck that moves. Another contains no speck. Yet when the light passes through both, the empty droplet seems to contain the shape of the one that does. This is where the mirror-eaters begin to become possible. The specked droplet slides down, and the other droplet’s shape changes without contact. A moment later the specked droplet arrives beside it. The speck stops. The empty droplet breaks. The speck is gone, though it may still be there, too small or too different to be seen. A faint ring of salt remains around the broken point. That ring will soon be colonized by heat-skin, and the heat-skin will soon feed the next arrival, and the cycle will continue.

The thread-skimmers are the most graceful of these small beings, and also the least understandable. They do not climb, grip, or walk along the filaments in any way a larger animal would recognize. They appear to move by becoming sympathetic to the vibration of the thread. When they are near, the filaments may hum with a frequency too low to hear but enough to make nearby droplets flatten slightly at their bases. The thread-skimmer’s body seems to have no fixed shape except when observed: it looks like a pale worm one moment, a folded line the next, a blurred image that could be two creatures sharing one body. When it crosses the mouth of a droplet, it may split into several images, none of which is wholly outside the light. This may be because its body is almost transparent, or because the droplet’s curvature multiplies its outline, or because the creature is not as bounded as the observer expects.

The bead-walkers, too, are deceptive. One may watch a droplet roll slowly down a tilted glass plate, its roundness wobbling slightly, and think it is seeing a bead-walker ride it. Then the droplet flattens and spreads, and the bead-walker becomes part of the film itself: a darker layer, a living skin on the water, indistinguishable from the heat-skin except for motion. Another time, what appears to be a single droplet may actually be a colony of bead-walkers pressed together under a shared surface tension. When the colony separates, each fragment carries a part of the same salt pattern, like a family of lenses breaking apart. These fragments will grow again, or fail, depending on whether the mountain sends heat and water.

Some of the most unsettling inhabitants are the ones that seem to be made only of water and intent. They are not clearly animals. They look like hands made from salt and moisture, too narrow, too flat, with fingers ending in droplets. They appear when the steam is thickest and vanish when the heat sharpens. They do not attack in any obvious way. They simply place themselves across a grazers’ path, and the grazers avoid them as though they were cliffs, though they are only a few tenths of a millimeter tall. If one could be touched, it might feel like a wet film that does not let the finger pass cleanly through. If it has a mouth, it is not a mouth; it is a fold in water where the creature’s body becomes too thin to distinguish from the environment. If it has eyes, they are lenses borrowed from droplets not its own. It may be a predator that eats by making other creatures lose the boundary that defines them.

The predators that seem to consume “what an organism nearly becomes” are perhaps the least credible part of this description, and yet they may be the most necessary. The Blister is a place where bodies are not stable until they dry, or freeze, or are sealed by time. A droplet creature is a temporary shape held together by surface tension. A mat is a temporary shape held together by salt and growth. A thread is a temporary shape held together by chemistry finding a path. If something evolves in such a place, why should it only eat bodies? It might eat the conditions under which a body could become stable. The mirror-eaters and salt-murmurations suggest that the ecosystem is aware of possibility. It can graze on nearness. It can prey on potential form. It can consume the image before the image becomes fixed. This is not supernatural; it is simply ecology at a scale where identity is fragile.

There are also symbionts, though no one would call them that. A bead-walker may carry a fragment of heat-skin on its underside, and the skin may feed the walker as it moves, while the walker brings the skin to new moisture channels. A thread-skimmer may become encrusted with salt grains that later fall into droplets and provide mineral structure for new mats. A shard-hunter may leave a trail of dark residue that thread-wolves follow, not because they hunt the hunter directly, but because the residue contains the dissolved remains of whatever the hunter ate. Life here is not a ladder or a food chain. It is a wet graph of consequences. Every creature is a path, every death is a surface, every droplet is a place where many possibilities briefly agree to remain in contact.

The inhabitants do not seem to know they are inside a wound. Or if they do know, they know it as a temperature, not as a fact. The crack gives them light, which they do not love. It gives them boundaries, which they use. It gives them risk, which they metabolize. If the mountain decides to heal—if the steam slows, the glass cools, the crack seals, the pocket fills with new mineral skin—the inhabitants will not be saved and will not be destroyed as a single event. They will be translated. The mats will harden. The droplets will become films. The bead-walkers will become dry dark points inside new glass. The thread-skimmers may collapse into faint striations. The shard-hunters may become inclusions. The pocket will retain them as structure without becoming alive in any way one would choose to visit. Its memory will be mineral, not personal. The mountain does not remember individuals. It remembers patterns.

But until then, life continues in a form that is hard to call comfortable and harder to call tragic. It is simply saturated. Every surface has a function. Every droplet has a tenant, a visitor, a grave, or a lens. Every filament is a road, a nerve, a root, a wire, a trap, or a beginning. Every grain of salt is a seed, a bone, a mark, a mirror, or a mistake. The Blister is not a nursery. It is not a paradise. It is not a hell. It is a hot, wet, sharp interval where the mountain’s body is trying to decide what it is, and countless smaller bodies are trying to take advantage of the uncertainty.

The largest resident, if one insists on a creature, is the pocket itself.

It breathes in pulses of condensation. It grows skin. It branches with filaments. It feels pressure through droplets. It hunts by focusing light. It reproduces by making micro-landscapes where none was before. It may not be alive in the way the bead-walkers are alive, but it is no less alive than the crack that contains them. The crack has become a small organ of the volcano: a weeping place, a healing place, a testing place. Its inhabitants are not merely living inside it. They are its method of living.

So whoever lives in the Weeping Blister is best described not as animals, not as plants, not as microbes, not as patterns, but as wetness that has learned to stay just long enough to become a body. They are small, sharp, hot, and silent. They feed on the edge between rock and water. They move by surface tension and mineral thread. They see through borrowed lenses. They are eaten by shapes that may or may not exist. They leave behind rings, scars, films, and faint reflections. They are not peaceful. They are not malicious. They are temporary inhabitants of a crack in the skin of a young volcano, living in a place that is still learning whether it wants to heal.
