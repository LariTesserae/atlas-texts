What lives in that place does not live as ordinary life lives. It does not arrive, do something, and then pass into memory. It has no childhood that can be followed by age, no death that can be followed by rot, no waste that can be followed by restoration. It is already complete in every motion. If you enter, you must learn not to look for inhabitants but to recognize the inhabitants as the living shape of a single held note.

The first thing that lives there is the floor.

The cut stone is not merely the thing the chamber rests upon; it is the chamber’s body. It breathes in the language of heat, taking warmth up from below and giving it back as resonance. Every footstep presses a low tone into the room. Every movement of a frond, every tick of an insect leg, every click from a predator’s throat is taken in and answered. The floor is not silent because it is waiting for something. It is not a foundation. It is an instrument already being played.

The smallest residents are the things that make the dust behave as if it has opinions.

There are runners with six pale legs and shells like blown glass. They move in arcs and loops that are not paths, because a path implies a destination, and here there is no destination. A runner crosses a patch of salt grit, pauses, rears, clicks, then resumes. If it is startled, it does not flee so much as unfold itself into a faster geometry. Its legs are slender rods of mineral cartilage, not bone in the wet sense; they have the hardness of cured reed. When it steps, the contact sound is tiny and bright, like a fingernail on the edge of a tuning fork. Its shell is translucent enough that you can see the heat of it, the internal lattice of pale strands where something like blood moves, oily and quick, in no hurry.

These runners are not animals in the ordinary meaning of the word. They are more like mobile crystallizations of the chamber’s own habit. They do not seek water. They do not sweat. They do not pant. They simply keep their pattern. If you watch one for too long, you realize it is not repeating a routine; it is being a routine. The routine is the thing itself.

There are beetles with backs carved in concentric rings. These rings are not growth rings, because nothing grows, and they are not mere decoration, because every surface here means something by its shape. Each beetle carries on its carapace a record of dust pressure, thermal drift, and mineral precipitation, the way a shell might carry a record of tides in a wet world, except that here there are no tides, only standing currents. They move in families, not of kinship but of similarity: a dozen identical backs passing under a salt bush, each one slightly different in angle, each one as exact as the first. Their mandibles click with a dry, papery sharpness as they scrape powder from the fronds. They eat in the way one eats music: by taking form into the body and letting the body answer with more form.

The moths do not fly in the sense that a moth above ground might fly. They do not push through air with wet wingbeats. They are suspended in the chamber’s heat currents as if the currents had been arranged just for them. They hover in fixed arcs, their wings hard as translucent lacquer, their bodies pale and segmented like pieces of blown sugar. They feed on salt fronds, but feeding does not deplete. A moth lands on a crystal leaf, inserts a fine proboscis into the facet-seam, and draws heat and mineral vapor into itself. The leaf hums. The moth shivers. A second later the leaf stands as it stood before, unmarred, whole. The moth remains exactly where it was, not because it has not moved, but because its movement is a held condition.

The plants are alive enough to count as inhabitants, perhaps more so than the animals.

They are not green. They are a dry, mineral life, a verdancy made of geometry instead of sap. Their leaves are thick and reflective, like painted glass, like waxy scales, like tiles fitted over some unseen furnace. Their stems are tubular, pale, split into facets, each facet holding a faint glow from the chamber’s warmth. They do not drink. They precipitate. They draw from the thin warm air and the dust beneath them, assembling lattice, edge, vein, and frond with a patient abundance that borders on showmanship. A salt bush may have dozens of curled fronds, each one a small architecture of angles. A single stem may branch into a hair-thin forest so dense and delicate that it looks like the thought of a tree rather than the tree itself. Tubers swell beneath the surface like pale fists, smooth and heavy, full of mineral potential, never spoiling, never rotting, never becoming anything other than themselves.

If you touch a leaf, it chimes. If you press too hard, it cracks. The crack is sharp, bright, and final, yet after a moment the frond returns to its exact prior angle. There is no wilt. There is no wound that darkens. There is only the sound and then the shape, repeated in the same permanent light.

These plants are not food in the ordinary sense. They are also instruments, temples, boundaries, and hosts. Their chiming creates standing tones in the air. Their branching forms mark out territories. Their mineral powder feeds the beetles, their heat-currents carry the moths, their curled fronds shelter the smallest runners from the room’s deeper radiance. The whole dry ecology is a collaboration of hardness, a concert of forms that do not consume themselves because consumption, here, is not loss. It is participation.

Among the larger residents are the low, wide-bodied grazers.

They have salt-colored hides, rough as kiln-fired clay, ridged with subtle veins of pale crystal. Their bodies are broad, almost architectural, built for warmth and stability rather than speed. They do not leap; they do not dash; they do not kick up dust in long gallops. They move by shifting their centers of weight from one set of feet to another, each motion deliberate, measured, almost ceremonial. Their heads are low, their mouths broad and dry, made for pressing against the stems of crystal trees and the thick fronds of salt bushes. They do not chew with the wet grinding of teeth in a mouth full of saliva. They shear, press, and dissolve heat into themselves. Their breath comes out not as fog but as a thin warm wave that makes nearby dust rise slightly and fall back unchanged.

Their eyes are hard, bright, bead-like. They do not blink in the way ordinary eyes do, because there is no moisture to clear. Their vision is not a wet lens in a soft socket; it is more like a polished facet set into dry, heated mineral. If one of them stands still while looking at you, it is not judging you. It is not welcoming you. It is simply including you in its field of permanence. A grazer may stand near a stand of tubers for what seems like hours, not because it is waiting for anything, but because standing near that particular cluster of pale fists is one of its essential postures in the world.

And there are the foxlike things.

These are rarer, more scattered, more alert. Their bodies are dry and narrow, built with a clicking precision that makes them seem almost mechanical. Their throats are dry chambers of bone and membrane, capable of producing a rapid clatter when they move, when they hunt, when they call. Their hides are thin, taut, pale as heated shell. Their limbs are long in the wrong places, too angular to belong to any animal you know, yet somehow suited to the sharp ground. They do not bark, do not howl, do not whine. They click. The clicking is not empty noise. It is a language of geometry, a series of sounds that seem to measure distances between stones, between fronds, between the moving points of heat that are the lives of other creatures.

They hunt, but the hunt does not diminish the hunted. A foxlike thing may strike at a six-legged runner, its jaw snapping with a sound like a shard of glass hitting a ledge. The runner may be caught, may twist, may emit a sudden metallic shriek. Yet afterward the chamber is no less full. The runner is elsewhere, or the same, or another exactly like it, standing in the same angle of light. The predator does not grow stronger from the act, nor does the act change the room. Predation here is not hunger moving toward fulfillment. It is a movement of the whole chord, a dark note within a sustained harmony.

Their blood, if pierced, does not run in ordinary streams. It is warm, oily, quick-silver, faintly resinous. It beads on the dry ground like a living polish and does not sink, because there is no soil to take it. It does not darken. It does not clot into crust. It sits, bright and strange, as if even its falling is part of a held arrangement.

Among all these creatures, the builders live too, though perhaps not in the way a traveler first expects.

There is no smoke. No worn footpath. No fire-ring blackened by seasons. No doorway rubbed smooth by years of passage. The human traces are not abandoned, because in this place abandonment would require decay, and decay has no entry. The circle of flat stones is not a ruin. The frame of crystal-ribbed hut is not a collapsed dwelling. The scraper, the pendant, the bone comb, the hollowed bowl are not relics. They are still engaged in their uses, even when no hand holds them. They are present as actions that have been released from time.

The human inhabitants are small in number, or rather they seem to be, because number itself is slippery in a place that never changes. They are hand-made beings in a hand-made ecology, their tools primitive, their gestures patient. Their bodies are dry in the chamber’s way: no sweat, no damp mouth, no soft skin, no wet hair. Their hides, if they have hair, are pale, fine, powdered with white dust that does not brush away. Their skin is matte, firm, warm, taking heat through the soles and palms. Their blood is the same oily quick-silver as the animals’. Their eyes are polished and bright, hard with reflected light rather than wet shine.

They are not ghosts. A ghost implies something lost, something no longer in its body. These people are in their bodies. They are simply bodies that do not move from one instant to another. They live in the acts they know.

One of them, if you follow the white dust seam far enough into perception rather than into distance, is crouched beside the circle of flat stones. The stones are set face-up, low, as if they are not merely markers but keys. This person is not praying, exactly. They are listening. Their palm hovers over one stone, not quite touching, and the stone hums. The humming is not heard so much as felt in the teeth, in the heel, in the base of the skull. This is one of their daily tasks: not to make, but to sustain. They are a keeper of tones. They know which stone, which crystal rib, which salt ridge, which thicket of reeds must stand in a certain relation for the chamber to remain full.

Another stands beside the fallen frame of the hut, or what appears to be a fallen frame. It is lashed with cord-like strands of woven filament, pale and tough, made from fibers split from the stems of crystal trees. The hut is broken, but it is always broken in exactly that manner. One rib has snapped near its base, and the other end rests in a ridge of mineral dust. This person does not repair it, because repair would imply a before and an after, a history of damage leading to restoration. They touch the crack instead, with careful fingers, and the touching is not grief. It is recognition. The crack is part of the hut’s form. The fallen rib is part of the chamber’s shape. To pass through the room is to know that a thing can be broken and yet remain itself, unchanging in its break.

There is a figure who uses the scraper. The scraper is sharp flake, chipped into a cutting edge by slow, deliberate strokes. The hand that holds it is broad, dry, dust-rimmed at the joints. It draws the edge across the base of a crystal tuber, not to harvest it into a pile, not to carry it away, but to release a bright flake, a sliver, a piece of the tuber’s body. The flake separates with a clear click. For a moment the tuber is wounded, but the wound is not a wound. It is a note. The hand turns the flake over, examines its edge, sets it down where it will remain. If it is picked up again, it is still the same flake. It does not dull. It does not become less. It is always ready.

There is another who combs their hair, or perhaps the hair of someone else, with the bone comb. The comb is small, its teeth worn only into their permanent form. The strands are pale, dry, brittle as glass reed but pliable enough to part. No knot tightens. No strand falls to the ground and stays there. The combing is a gesture of intimacy without change, a maintenance of the same soft hardness day after day, or not day after day, because there is no day. There is only the act of combing, repeated without repetition becoming tiredness.

There is another holding the bowl.

The bowl is hollowed from a crystal, clear enough to contain light, not liquid. It holds a faint amber glow at the bottom, or perhaps it holds only the shape of the room, the tiny folded chamber seen in miniature: a forest of salt bushes, a stand of tubers, a circle of stones, a figure crouched, a foxlike thing mid-stride, all of it suspended as if the bowl has swallowed not water but the idea of water. This person does not drink from it. They lift it to their mouth in the gesture of drinking, and then the gesture rests, forever just before fulfillment, forever complete without becoming waste.

These are the builders. They made their tools by hand, with patience, and they built their cairns to mark stable ground. The cairns are not graves, though they have the gravity of graves. They are not monuments, though they remember. They are signs placed by bodies that move through the room as the room moves through them. Each cairn is a small stack of chipped stone and broken shell, and each one is exactly as high, exactly as tilted, exactly as dust-covered as it has always been. They indicate paths not because the paths change, but because paths are ideas, and ideas need anchors in the world.

Some of the builders keep animals in the larger stands of crystal trees. They do not herd them. They do not drive them across open ground. They stand among the thickets and the animals stand among the thickets, and between them is a relation of heat and sound. They mark territory with their feet. They leave small piles of white powder, the residue of insect metabolism, arranged into ridges. They know which fronds ring highest, which tubers are sharpest, which currents of warm dust carry the moths in fixed arcs. They do not cultivate because cultivation implies change. They attend, as one attends a fire that burns without fuel, a fire that only asks to be watched.

Perhaps they sing the chamber, because the stone responds to voice. The builders’ songs are not melodies with endings. They are sustained tones that open into other tones. A voice enters the room and the room answers, not by changing its echo, but by revealing that it has been shaped to answer all along. The songs are like the cairns: small, hard, placed with intention. They do not move the listeners. They move the space through the listeners.

There is no metal gleam. There is no machine. The builders are primitive only in the way a stone is primitive: the beginning of form, not a lack of intelligence. Their minds are adapted to a world without entropy. They do not plan for tomorrow, because tomorrow would be a wound in the room’s body. They do not store food, because food does not spoil and does not run out. They do not bury their dead, because there are no dead. They mark stable ground instead. They build shrines to the standing wave. They shape combs, bowls, pendants, scrapers—not because they need objects, but because an object is a way of giving attention a body.

The pendant of polished black glass is another such body. It hangs from a thin cord, its surface so dark it seems not to reflect but to drink the dim light. Yet it does not drink. It only holds the room in miniature. If a builder wears it, it lies against a dry breastbone, and the room hums in the pendant. If it rests on the ground, it rests forever in the same glint. It is not jewelry. It is a tuning stone. It is a small black hole for warmth, a dark lens for the chamber’s fixed glow.

The artifacts are inhabitants too.

In a place without decay, an object does not become useless because no hand touches it. It continues its being. The scraper scrapes its own silence. The bowl receives light. The comb combs nothing and yet completes its part. The cairn marks a path even when no walker follows. The hut frames a dwelling even when no body rests within it. These things are not dead because they are dry. They are not abandoned because they are unchanged. They are alive in the way the floor is alive: by holding form, by answering pressure, by continuing the pattern.

If you stand still long enough, you begin to suspect that the entire room is a single creature.

The crystal trees are its lungs, though there is no air to be wet. The salt bushes are its skin, rough and bright. The insects are its nerve endings, clicking through the dust. The grazers are its slow limbs. The foxlike predators are its quickened pulses. The human builders are its hands, making marks and shapes and small acts of care. The cairns are its thoughts. The standing stones are its teeth. The hut is its rib. The bowl is its mouth. The pendant is its eye. The floor is its chest. The sound is its breath.

But that is too tidy. The chamber does not behave like a creature with edges. It behaves more like a choir whose individual voices are so perfectly balanced that the song seems to have a body. The runners, moths, plants, stones, tools, people, and warmth do not form a single organism so much as a single continuous act. They do not cooperate to survive. They cooperate to remain exactly as they are.

The room has a kind of consciousness, but it is not like human thought. It does not ask questions. It does not form intentions. It is a warmth that knows its own angles. It is a sound that knows its own body. It is a dry lushness that knows what it is. Its awareness is not in the walls, though the walls glow. Not in the dust, though the dust hums. Not in the creatures, though they move. It is in the spacing.

The spacing is the true resident.

Between the glass reeds and the salt bushes there is empty warm dark. That emptiness is not absence. It is shaped. It is the pause between notes. It is the air the moths hover through. It is the distance the foxlike thing measures with its clicking. It is the room’s own openness, preserved not because nothing fills it, but because the filling has been done carefully, with too much life left in reserve. If the chamber were dense with life, it would seem crowded. But it is not. Its life is stationed, placed, allowed to stand apart, and this apartness is the pattern that holds the whole.

There are places where the floor is bare and white, a field of clean mineral grit. The grit is so fine it seems almost liquid, yet it does not flow. It holds ridges made by insect excretion, small parallel lines like the tracks of tiny combs. It receives the footprints of the runners but the prints do not deepen. It receives the pressure of the human hands, but no crease remains. It is not mud. It is not ash. It is powder that has been arranged so many times into the same arrangement that arrangement has become its nature.

At the edges of these grit fields, the crystal reeds stand in small groups. They are thin, sharp, translucent, catching the chamber’s light and returning it as a pale blue-green sheen, not quite green, not quite mineral. Their tips are split like needles, their bases flared into star-shaped anchors. When touched, they make a sound that is not exactly ringing. It is closer to the voice of a stone remembering a strike. If you move through them too quickly, they do not bruise or wilt. They simply cease to be whole for a moment, and then become whole again, exactly as before. They are fragile and yet they cannot be worn out. Their fragility is their permanence.

Near the larger stand of crystal trees, the branches grow so complex that they seem to create their own small rooms. The trunks are pale, faceted, hollow in the way a reed may be hollow, but without softness. The canopy is not overhead in any ordinary sense; it spreads outward and downward, like a dry firework held still at the moment of full bloom. Each branch splits into dozens of smaller branches, each smaller branch into dozens more. It is extravagant beyond need, overdeveloped beyond usefulness. If you trace one limb, it ends in a cluster of hard leaves that glow faintly at their seams. The cluster hums. The hum is not from one leaf but from all of them at once, a chord of mineral edges vibrating at slightly different pitches.

These trees may be the deepest residents.

They are older than anything in the chamber, if “older” has meaning in a place where age cannot accumulate. Their roots do not seek water; they branch horizontally through the dust, calcified anchors, pale and hard, like coral made to grow in air. The roots do not tangle. They arrange themselves into symmetrical fans, and where a root meets a stone, it does not crack the stone. It joins it. The tree is not separate from the floor. It is an extension of it, a living continuation of the chamber’s dry geology. The tree does not grow upward because there is no sky. It grows outward because there is room for form.

The builders know this. They place their cairns near the roots, not to worship the trees, but to acknowledge the places where the chamber’s body is thickest, most responsive. They set their circles of flat stones where the hum is clearest. They rest their hands against the trunk-facets and feel the lattice life of the room move through them: heat, mineral, pressure, resonance. They are not outside the ecology. They are one of its most intricate forms.

There are also the absent creatures, or rather the creatures that seem absent only because you are looking for change.

No one is missing. No one has died, migrated, forgotten, left the door open behind them. The room does not require visitors to understand its inhabitants as a sequence. If a foxlike thing is gone from one patch of dust and appears in another, it has not traveled. It has relocated within the same held arrangement. If a moth is no longer hovering near one salt bush but is near another, it is not moving through time. It is completing a pattern too large for your attention to follow all at once.

The chamber may contain many more human bodies than are visible at first, but “visible” is the wrong word. It contains the visible and the invisible as if they are two facets of one mineral. A person may stand behind a screen of crystal reeds, their shape not hidden so much as divided into bright parts. Their dark eye may catch the light. The white dust on their shoulder may mark them as part of the ground. The pendant may flash once, as if a black star had moved through the dry trees.
