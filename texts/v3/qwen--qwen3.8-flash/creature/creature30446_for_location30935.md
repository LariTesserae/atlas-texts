From a distance, the High Kettles might look like a mountain marsh that should not exist: green at altitude, wet under heat, and black above. But from near enough, the illusion of emptiness disappears. The place is populated. Its inhabitants are not merely animals. They are also weather, mineral, fungus, fiber, architecture, and the slow habits of a landscape that learned long ago how to keep itself alive.

The most numerous residents are invisible. They live in the dust. Not ordinary dust, not the dry silt that settles in forgotten rooms, but the pale, glittering, engineered powder that drifts through every thermal column and gathers in every depression. Those grains are not alive in the way a frog or a reed is alive, yet they behave like an organism. They gather, carry, deposit, repair, and respond. They make the air thick with possible water. They make stone hold its shape. They make dead wood become ceramic without becoming inert. Where they collect on wet surfaces, they form a fine skin that may later become the substrate for lichen, moss, algae, bacterial film, or the shell of a snail. The whole valley is therefore inhabited first by a population of processes: condensation, mineral deposition, heat exchange, surface-tension, repair, and memory.

Beneath that process lives the true micro-ecology. Every rock face has a film of life on it. In the pools, black algae form velvet layers over stone. In the channels, bacterial mats smell faintly of copper and wet earth. Fungal threads move through fallen branches, not as ordinary rot but as a network of soft white filaments that seem to communicate with the powder in the air. On the mineral skins of dead trees, tiny lichens bloom in pale rosettes, each one a partnership between fungal body and dust-bearing substrate. The smallest animals graze these films. Mites, springtails, ciliates, nematodes, and larvae too thin to see clearly crawl through the upper millimeters of moisture, eating, excreting, and becoming food. In the High Kettles, the boundary between geology and biology is porous. Life does not merely live in this place; it uses its strange materials as tools.

The first plants are also the first residents that a human eye would recognize. Moss lives everywhere. On the shaded north faces of stone, it is thick and black-green, breathing damp. On the sun-warmed ledges, it turns yellow-green and curls at the edges when the heat presses too long. There are mosses that grow into soft cushions, mosses that spread as a skin, mosses that rise like tiny forests above a single drop of water. They colonize the bases of pillars, the tops of channels, the sides of fallen trees, the roofs of collapsed huts. They are not passive. They hold water, trap dust, and make a humid microclimate beneath themselves. Some species have learned to grow only where the lattice powder has settled in a particular concentration, so that patches of moss form a map of older repairs.

Above the moss, the horsetails stand in green towers. Their jointed stalks are filled with warm water and lined with a fibrous interior that animals gnaw. Their pale cones release clouds of spores into the still air. Ferns grow below and beside them, unfurling slowly, their young fronds curling like fists of soft green. Some ferns are the size of a child’s torso; others spread through the marsh in vast overlapping canopies, dripping with epiphytes. The largest ferns form vaulted chambers under which small animals can crawl without ever touching open water. Inside those chambers, the air is cooler, the light green and diffuse, and the walls of the rhizomes are coated with pale mineral powder, making the ground beneath seem more stone than earth.

The trees are the slowest inhabitants. They rise from shallow pools with swollen bases and black bark slicked with algae. Their roots form cathedral arches in the water, and between those arches the smaller animals nest. Their branches are draped with moss, lichens, bromeliad-like plants, and tangled vines that hold droplets in cup-shaped leaves. Some of the trees are only partly wood. The lattice has begun replacing their dead heartwood with a pale crystalline substance, so that when a trunk is split open, its interior resembles ceramic more than timber. Yet the living outer layer continues to grow. The tree still breathes. The tree still puts out leaves. It lives as a collaboration between the organism and the repair system: a tree made partly by seed, partly by weather, partly by old engineering.

There are also plants that do not seem to want to be plants. On certain wet cliffs, pale crusts of lichen spread so finely over the stone that they look like frost. At dawn, those crusts take up moisture so quickly that the cliff appears to sweat. In the marsh, floating mats of root-like vegetation brown and spongy in texture drift between pools. They are not islands, but not merely weeds either. They are platforms. Snails climb over them. Young frogs shelter beneath them. Tiny crabs burrow into the fibers. When the water level changes over a season, these mats lift, fold, and resettle without tearing. Over decades, they become part of the valley’s slow cartography, marking where the water has been held before.

The water itself is full of residents.

In the black pools, the most abundant animals are scaleless fish. They move in shoals so thick that the surface of the water seems to darken, as if oil had spilled beneath the mist. Their eyes are large and flat, suited to low light and cloudy water. Their gills are supplemented by sacs that trap moisture, allowing them to survive for long periods out of water. Some leave the shallows at night, climbing wet stone into the grasses with a strange, sinuous movement. They look, at first, like dark worms until their fins flick and their mouths take in a breath of air through a modified gill cavity. In the grasses, they eat fallen insects, snail eggs, and the tender shoots of horsetail. In the morning, they return to the pools with droplets clinging to their flanks and dust powder settled along their lateral lines.

Other fish are solitary, dark, and slow-moving. They inhabit deep hollows beneath basalt overhangs, where the water is warm and oxygen poor. Their bodies have a faintly iridescent mucus coating that helps regulate temperature. Some carry small epiphytes in the folds of their fins, not because they are plants but because the plants attach themselves to the mucus. These fish are gardens on the move. Their bodies shelter seedlings, moss spores, and tiny crustaceans. When they mate in the shallows, the mineral powder suspended in the water gathers in their spawning trails, forming pale ribbons that last for days after the event.

Crustaceans are everywhere in the silt. Small shrimp-like animals with translucent shells burrow through the top layer of mud, filtering warm water through fan-like legs. Their shells often wear a white coating from the lattice, so they look like living porcelain. Crabs move in the reed beds, broad-bodied and sideways-stepping, carrying grains of mineral powder in the hollows of their carapaces. They use the powder as a kind of mortar. Some build domes around their burrows, plastering the edges with mud mixed with dust, then sitting quietly at the entrance with their antennae tasting the air. In places, the reed beds become a maze of these low domes, and the valley floor looks less like a marsh than like a village of miniature roofs.

Snails live on stone, in water, and on leaves. Some have conical shells with ridged lips, perfect for scraping mineral powder from wet rock. They leave trails that are not merely slime but a fine glaze, and over time their movement across a wall can seal small cracks. Larger snails graze the black pools and the floating root mats. Their eggs are pale clusters stuck beneath leaves, each cluster coated in a chalky film. Where many snails gather on the same surface, the white dust becomes so dense that the stone seems to bloom with frost. A traveler who has never seen the valley might touch it and mistake it for lichen, only for a small tentacle to test the air from beneath the frost.
