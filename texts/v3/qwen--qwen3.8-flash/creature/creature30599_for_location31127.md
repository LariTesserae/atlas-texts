If you step into the water, your foot makes ripples, but the ripples fall into the existing pattern. If you do not move, the pattern begins to decide what you are. First you are a cold shape. Then you are a warm shape. Then you are a source of salt, skin, carbon, pressure, and breath. Then you are a street.

What lives here is not a people, not a single creature, not a species, and not, in the ordinary sense, a population. The inhabitants are too numerous to be counted and too interdependent to be separated. If you try to say what one of them is, the answer slides away: a mite that has eaten a film of rust, a dust that carries a charge, a patch that opens when heat rises, a swarm that remembers a gutter, a valve that breathes. Each is an animal in the way that a lock is an animal, a key is an animal, and the hand that turns them both is animal. They do not appear all at once. They appear as the place becoming self-conscious.

The basin has citizens.

The first and most abundant are not animals yet. They are skins. Lithic crusts spread across concrete and pipe and iron plate like slow fire, but without heat. They branch in fractal plates of silica, salt, iron oxide, and something more uncertain than biology. Some are fungal in habit but not in kingdom: pale threads that taste mineral currents and follow water films with blind patience. Others are crystalline, forming ridges so regular that they seem built. They harvest gradients. They do not photosynthesize. They do not drink light. They eat difference: hot against cold, wet against dry, salt against iron, charge against metal. They turn damp electricity into biomass. They are the basin’s ground-level metabolism. If you touch one, it feels like dust until it contracts, a faint tightening, a shiver of organized powder, and then it resumes its apparent passivity.

These skins are not scenery. They are fields of feeding. Their edges advance by microscopic decisions. A condensation ring becomes a boundary; a warm pipe becomes a frontier; a salt-skimmer’s passage leaves a road that the crust follows a day later, or a month later, or an hour later. They do not grow upward. They grow across. They grow into the place’s surfaces, making them habitable for everything else. They are the basin’s first city, built without architecture, in the thin grammar of dampness.

Then there are the motes.

From a distance they look like dust disturbed by breath. Up close, the breath has legs. They are tiny organisms of salt, metal oxide, and a film of charge, born in the warm static fields around motors and coils. They rise like pale ash from a pipe that has run hot for hours, hover at a height where the air is neither moving upward nor downward but merely tired, and feed by landing on the lithic skins. Their bodies are simple enough to be almost mineral and complex enough to be almost thought. They do not have eyes. They have sensitivity to field, moisture, temperature, and pressure. They are a swarm that functions like weather and like memory.

A cloud of them drifting across the floor is not random. It is reading. If a gutter has filled, they rise from it in a pale column. If a valve has begun to leak, they cluster above the leak, their tiny bodies charged enough to make the water bead in odd directions. If a section of pipe has cooled, they retreat into the warm underside of a junction box. When they die, they do not pile. They become the powder that looks like ash but feels like salt, and the powder becomes surface, and the surface becomes food. Their death is the basin’s way of making a record.

The motes are not the only small animals. They are merely the first to be noticed when the place begins to look back.

The glass-backed isopods live in the condensation channels as if they were made for them. Their shells are fragments of old glass, thin and sharp-edged, worn into forms that let them flatten into a channel’s curve. They scuttle along the wet grooves, scraping the mineral films that accumulate there. They are cleaners, grazers, maintenance workers. When they move, they leave a faint polished line behind them, a groove that will later be mistaken for wear caused by hands. Some are pale as bottle bottoms; some are green-black like oil; some have tiny flecks of copper in their joints, so that when light passes through the glass above, they glitter as though they are made of broken lamps.
