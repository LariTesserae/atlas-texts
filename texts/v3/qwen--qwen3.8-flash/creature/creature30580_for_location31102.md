They are there before they are seen.

At first the bowl offers only its wet surfaces, its low roofs, its white-lined gutters, its reed-dark walls. Then a hand lifts a tray of pale flakes. A shoulder moves in a gap too narrow for the eye to have expected a person. A ladder shifts, though no one appears to be standing near it. A child’s bare foot steps from one platform to another as if the space between has always been too small and too dangerous for anyone else to trust.

They are human. That much is plain once you are close enough to see how a mother turns a wet bundle, how a man tests a cord with his teeth, how an old woman’s fingers move over a crack in stone as if reading the line of a vein. But the bowl makes them into a particular kind of human, as if every body in the settlement has been asked to answer, in its own flesh, to water, mineral, density, and the constant need not to sink.

They do not stand tall often. The passages are low, the roofs press overhead, and a person who rises too straight may knock a drying rack or jar a shelf. So their posture has become a compact one: shoulders slightly rounded, necks flexible from looking down as much as ahead, spines accustomed to bending through doorways and crawling under overhanging platforms. They are broad through the hips and shoulders, perhaps, and narrow through the ribs, with legs shortened or long and springy depending on the family, but always quick at the ankle. Their feet are wide and sensitive, toes splayed from years of gripping notched timber and wet reed, heels calloused, arches low and toughened. A stranger would slip on the mineral-slicked shelves, but they move as if the town has a hidden rhythm and their feet are already listening.

Their hands are where their life is most visible.

A child’s hands are soft, but already stained with clay where the fingertips press into pots. A reed-weaver’s palms are ridged with cuts that heal white and pale because the mineral water never quite lets a wound close the way a dry wound would. A shell-knapper’s thumbs are thick with callus from pushing flint against stone, and often the edge of the nail is darkened by crushed chert. A channel-runner has fingers long enough to reach into narrow gaps and clean a blockage without disturbing a roof above. An elder’s hands may tremble from cold, but they still know, by touch, whether a slab is bearing weight or merely resting there for now.

Almost all of them carry white.

Chalky dust from the weep-shell settles in the creases of their knuckles, in the small of their backs, in the folds of their hair. The white is not ornament, though they might wear a single copper bead green with age, or a carved antler toggle at a shoulder seam. White is residue. It means the walls have given, or the body has been close to the walls. When a person has a particularly pale forearm, it may mean they have been scraping crust for hours; if the hair dusts white after rain, it may mean they have been up on a roof catching condensation. The town does not smell like perfume or woodsmoke. It smells like damp hide, boiled root, fish left to dry, warm clay, and the faintly bitter breath of mineral stone.

They are not clean in the sense of a dry town. No one is. But they are not careless about dirt either. They know that too much waste clogs a channel, that stagnant water rots a reed mat, that a wet sleeping platform left too long will soften and sink. They scrape themselves often with smooth shells, wash with strained water that tastes faintly chalky, rub their hands with fish oil or the fat of eels when there is fat to spare. Their clothes are layered because the damp has a way of finding the knees, the loins, the joints between fingers.

Their garments are made from stitched hide, woven reed, and sometimes the thin pale skins made from weep-shell spread over clay and clothed in fiber. These skins are useful for carrying water, protecting goods, or sealing seams, but no one wears them unless they must, because they feel like stone trying to be cloth. For daily work, they favor soft hides scraped thin and bound with cord. For cold mornings, they drape themselves in reed mantles that soak up mist and then dry slowly on a roof. A person without a mantle is vulnerable; a mantle soaked through is a weight that can turn a day of work into a day of shivering. So they learn to manage damp as other peoples learn to manage fire. They hang cloaks in the highest breezes. They dry hides over low pits lined with clay and stones. They turn a sleeping mat every few hours. They keep their hair tied because hair catches lint and reed fibers and becomes impossible to wash in water too mineral for hair soap.

Their faces are shaped by quiet.

There are many faces, of course. Some are round, some long, some broad-nosed, some sharp-chinned, some marked by pock and scar. But the faces that live in the bowl tend to wear a certain kind of patience. Their eyebrows often move more than their mouths. They look at one another sideways in the passages because there is not room to turn fully toward a person. Their expressions are smaller than the expressions of people from open places, not because they feel less, but because the town is dense and sound does not need volume to travel. A frown can be seen at the next platform. A nod can pass through a whole shelf of families.

Their voices are low, often roughened by damp. They do not shout, not because they are especially polite, but because there is nowhere for shouting to go. In a packed bowl of reed and clay, a cry rebounds into confusion, a scream can make someone think a wall has failed. So anger is spoken softly. Grief is often a long exhale. Joy can break into laughter, but the laughter is enclosed, as if contained in a cup, and those who hear it know immediately that something rare has happened.

Children are the most startling part of the settlement, because there are children, and they are not frightened.

A child learns the town’s water before learning to speak freely. Before the first full sentences come, there is the pointing finger and the naming of wetness.

This is where it is running.

This is where it will sit.

Do not let it sleep.

The children know the channels of the house before they know the names of their parents’ names. They learn to walk sideways in passageways before they learn to run. They learn to step on the strong part of a floor by listening for the hollow. They learn not to lean on a weeping wall unless they are told it is safe, because even a new-looking stone may be held up by a hidden brace. They are never left alone near a pool, yet they are not shielded from water. They carry small trays. They watch a leak. They are asked to hold a cord while an adult adjusts a ladder. Their first play is not running through fields but constructing tiny channels in damp clay. A child’s toy may be a snail shell, a split reed, a knot made of cord, a pebble with a groove worn smooth by fingers. By the time they are old enough to work in earnest, they already think like the town.

Naming is slow here.

A newborn has a sound made for it, usually by the person who first caught the umbilical cord or who heard the baby cry while listening to a particular drip. But this is not the name that matters most. A child earns a name when it first demonstrates a kind of knowing. If a child notices a hidden spring in the morning, the child may be called something like Drip-seer or Small-awake. If a child is steady on a soaked roof while older siblings are afraid, the child may become Roof-walker or High-foot. If a child is gentle with a fish run or quick to free a trapped snail, the child may be called Choke-clear or Live-water. Names are not given by priests. They are given by the community, and often they are changed when the person begins to do something unexpected. An adult may carry a childhood name all their life, worn thin by use, or may shed it once they become a better knot-holder, a better leak-listener, a better mother or father.

The families are not simple.

There are parents, and there are blood-relations, but the town organizes itself by load. A household is often a line of people connected by a shared platform, a shared roof, a shared gutter, or a shared memory of which stone must never be moved. This makes their kinship stranger to outsiders and more intimate than outsiders expect. One may be married, but the marriage may matter less than the fact that one’s mother and one’s spouse are both responsible for the same leaky ceiling. One may have siblings, but the strongest bond may be with the person across the passage whose shelf rests above your ladder and whose life may depend on whether you brace a beam correctly.

There are names for these bonds.

A person who helps hold up your sleeping platform is called, in the translated sense, a shoulder-under.

A person whose gutter empties into your basin is your water-giver, whether friend or enemy.

A person who knows how your floor will bear a load is your stone-teller.

A person who can be trusted to move a ladder at the wrong hour and not disturb the town is your cord-holder.

A person who has saved a family from sinking is called a raise-by-hand.

This is not law in the way dry towns have law. It is more like weather. The town knows where its weight lies, and its weight is a network of promises.

Leadership is not grand. No one sits on a throne, because no one has room for a throne. No one stands before a crowd, because there are no squares. The people who lead are those who know, before others know, what must be moved. They are the ones who can say, “Take the ladder from there and put it here,” and everyone listens, not because they are proud, but because the town’s body has learned to trust their hands. They are called things like gutter-speaker, old-load, roof-sender, water-rememberer. Their authority is practical, temporary, and constantly tested. If a leader forgets to check a support beam and a platform dips, their voice loses weight. If a leader saves a sleeping child by rerouting a channel during the night, their name will be repeated long after.

The most important people, perhaps, are the elders.

They are not frail because no one can afford to be frail, but some have stiff knees and coughing lungs and eyes reddened by smoke. Still, they are indispensable. They know where the walls have wept before. They know which cracks are harmless and which mean a spring is changing course. They know which old reed mats are woven into supports that cannot be pulled away. They remember the names of stones that cannot be removed. When a family argues over a repair, it is an elder who is called—not to bless it, not to declare a rule, but to touch the wall, smell the water, step back, and say, “No, there is a floor resting on that lintel,” or “Yes, but if you take the flake now, the next frost will split the face,” or “This is not a good crack. This is the bowl thinking.”

Children cling to elders. This may be because the elders know how to listen to stones without becoming afraid, or because the elders have hands that have learned gentleness in cramped places. They often sit on upper shelves, where the mist comes through and the smoke is thin, holding a reed toy or a small shell and telling the child how to know when the town is thirsty.

“The town is thirsty when the white dust is too thick on the floor,” they say.

“No,” says the child.

“Then it is breathing,” says the elder.

The work of the town is never finished, because the bowl is never finished.

In the morning, before the mist fully lifts, someone climbs to a roof and presses a palm to the mineral skin. If the hand comes away wet, the day will be for clearing and scraping. If the wall is dry to the touch, the day will be for carrying stored water and making new catchments. Then the shells begin to tap. The sound is not loud, but it is everywhere: a soft click-click on stone, the scrape of bone against chalky crust, the whisper of reed mats being folded and dried. People pass small flakes of weep-shell between hands as if passing seeds. They mix it with clay to seal a jar, pack it into molds, spread it over a reed basket to make a skin. A skilled worker can take only the right thickness, leaving enough crust for the wall to remain whole. This requires a kind of reverence, though they would not call it that. They would call it patience.

The water itself is their first teacher and their constant companion.

They do not think of it as a gift, exactly. A gift implies generosity. They think of it as a conversation. Water asks questions in the language of damp. It says: here I can enter. Here I will remain. Here I will soften the clay. Here I will pull the platform down. Here I will make the floor breathe too much. Here I will freeze your feet. Their task is to answer. They answer with gutters, with raised reeds, with clay pipes, with ladders moved from one side to the other. They answer by scraping, sealing, lifting, bracing, and watching.

They have many words for wetness.

There is the word for the first dark shine on a clean face of stone.

There is the word for the slow bead that gathers at the edge of a break.

There is the word for the run that has just enough momentum to enter a carved gutter.

There is the word for the water that has lost its nerve and sits in a hollow, going still and wrong.

There is the word for the damp that climbs a wall after night without visibly moving.

There is the word for the water that tastes of fresh chalk and is only safe for soaking clay, not drinking.

There is the word for the seep that has learned to carry the taste of iron and must not be allowed near the drying fish.

There is the word for a wall that is weeping too much, as if the stone has begun to cry.

There is the word for a wall that has stopped weeping entirely, which is worse, because it may mean the stone has begun to die.

They do not waste. A pot is a household body. It may crack, but no one discards it quickly. A cracked pot can be sealed with weep-shell, bound in cord, made into a lower basin, placed over a channel, used to collect drip, used to soak tubers, used as a weight. A broken reed mat can become stuffing, packing, a foot wrap, a patch over a floor. A bone pin can be reworked until its point is blunt and its curve has become familiar to a hand. The town is filled with objects that have outlived their first purpose, and this is not considered poor. It is considered skill.

Food is not abundant. It is enough, but not in the way that makes a person careless. They eat boiled tubers, bitter from the mineral soil but softened by fire and time. They eat snails, which are not many but are found in white clusters after a wet season. They eat thin worms dug from silt with bone hooks, rinsed, dried, ground, or boiled.
