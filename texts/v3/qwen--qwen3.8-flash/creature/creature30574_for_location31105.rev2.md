If “whoever” is taken to mean persons with histories, then almost nobody lives there. The basin has no people. It has no hearths, no dwellings, no families, no graves, no marks by which one might say: someone once needed shelter, someone once returned to a place, someone once left a trace and expected it to be remembered. It has no roads, because it has no destinations. It has no ruined walls, because it has no ruins. If a traveler came in from a wetter world, carrying damp breath and wet skin and the small constant need for water, the basin would not drive the traveler out with violence. It would simply refuse to become a home for them. The ground would not soften under foot. The air would not cool into night. The leaves would not open to receive drink. The traveler would move through the place the way a thought passes through a closed room: not unwelcome, but unnecessary, and already beginning to be forgotten.

If, however, “whatever” is taken to mean any form that participates in the living condition of the place, then the basin is full.

It is full of inhabitants, but they inhabit it in a way unlike ordinary life. They do not metabolize, not in the manner we recognize. There is no digestion, no sweat, no urine, no blood, no feces, no scent of animal warmth. Nothing in the basin is moist, and so nothing is nourished by moisture. The creatures that live there seem to sustain themselves by relation rather than consumption. They persist because their exact place in the configuration is required. They are less like hungry beings in a landscape than like functions expressed in form: the thicket needs an edge, and the edge is an animal; the repetition needs a witness, and the witness is an insect; the hollow needs a weight, and the weight is a broad, pale thing that never moves.

The first and most numerous inhabitants are the dry plants themselves.

The thickets are not merely vegetation. They are residents with the seriousness of cities. The grass-like clusters, each blade stiff and angular as a sliver of bone or tile, stand in dense ordered fields. They do not sway. They do not bow or rise. When you move your hand through them, the blades press against the skin like fine ceramic reeds, not soft, not wet, not yielding the way living tissue yields, but giving with that strange, dry elasticity that belongs to thin material fixed in a precise shape. They let you touch them, and then they are exactly where they were before. They do not remember the touch. They do not seem to object to it. They simply reassert themselves as though the touch had been an error of perception.

The pale rosettes cover broad shelves of ground with the patience of snow that has never melted and never will. Each rosette is a star of dry leaflets, their edges sharp, their surfaces smooth as fired clay. The rosettes touch one another, edge to edge, without crowding, as if their boundaries were defined not by growth but by agreement. When one looks closely, the leaflets appear to have veins, but the veins do not carry anything. They are structural marks, patterns held for the sake of the pattern, as if the idea of a leaf were being preserved in mineral. The centers of some rosettes cup shallowly toward the sky, but never collect water, because there is none. If one expects moisture to settle there, the expectation feels foolish. The hollow is meant to receive light, not liquid. Light pools in the rosette centers without weight, without warmth changing into heat, without shadow deepening into damp.

The vertical filaments are perhaps the most alien forms. They rise from the ground in dense curtains, straight and hollow, looking like reeds, like dried bamboo, like the ribs of some immense forgotten umbrella. They stand without bending, though there is no visible support for them. They seem to be upheld by a permission inside the landscape, by a hidden rule that allows height without weakness. They are pale where the light strikes them, greenish near their joints, sometimes with a faint gray dusting on their outer surfaces, but the dust does not smear and does not fall away. If one touches a filament, it is smooth and cold-like under the warm light, yet it has no wet coolness. It feels like the side of a cup, not the inside. If one strikes it lightly, it may emit a short, high, glassy tone, but the tone does not carry. It occurs, then is corrected. It remains local, as though the air were reluctant to share it.

None of these plant-like forms has sap. None has rot. None produces fallen matter that darkens into soil. None dies visibly. Yet they are not dead. They are not frozen, not embalmed, not merely decorative. They possess the fullness of living organisms, only stripped of the fluids by which life elsewhere announces itself. They seem alive in the way a held breath is alive: tense, complete, impossible to ignore, but without the ordinary release of ending.

Beneath them is another class of inhabitants, less visible and more difficult to categorize.

The pale branching structures that appear to enter the substrate are not roots, or not only roots. They are too fused to the ground, too regular, too certain of their geometry to be merely anchoring. They spread beneath the mineral surface in delicate, hard, coral-like networks. If one could see the ground cut open, which it cannot be, because it does not cut or break into wounds, one would not find wet soil or fibrous threads. One would find pale lattice beneath pale lattice, branch joining branch in arrangements that seem too deliberate to be accidental but too complete to be recent. These branching solids are old, or they are ageless; they look as if they emerged fully formed from the substrate and have not since been altered.

They do not drink. They do not search for moisture. They do not decay. Yet they behave as though they are responsible for the stability of everything above them. When dust shifts beneathfoot, it shifts only a little. When a stone lies near a plant, it lies in its exact place. When a rosette seems too perfect, or a thicket too dense, the branching network below makes no visible response, but one feels it: a faint pressure, a correctness, the sense that the ground is not merely ground, but a keeper of limits. The basin is not being grown. It is being held. The roots-like structures are perhaps the holding.

There are creatures small enough to be imagined only if one is already suspicious.

At the very margin of visibility, where the pale dust lies between stems, tiny forms may assemble for an instant: six angular points, a threadlike antenna, the suggestion of a carapace, the quick impression that a grain of salt has chosen to become a beetle. Then the arrangement dissolves, not by scattering but by becoming unnecessary. These are not ordinary insects. They are not clearly alive. They are more like the basin’s way of testing whether a small animal should exist in this location, finding that it need not, and returning the test to dust. They are inhabitants in the sense of drafts, sketches, momentary possibilities of form. They do not feed. They do not mate. They do not hide. They appear because a certain configuration requires the idea of appearance, not because a body has crawled into being.

Then there are the animals that seem to have been allowed to stay.

They are rare. They are few. Their number does not appear to matter. The basin does not seem to need a population of them, a breeding line, a migration, a predator, or a prey. If there are two of the angular hare-like creatures in the distance, they are not necessarily related, not a pair, not a beginning of a population. They are simply two instances of a permitted animal, each exactly as improbable as the other. The hare-like thing the size of a small child may stand at the edge of a thicket, motionless, long-limbed, flat-faced, its body too angular for any familiar quadruped. Its hide resembles dry ceramic, pale and faintly green, as if mineral and vegetation had been fused into a single skin. The green lines that appear along its flanks are not scars, not fur markings, not the color of living flesh beneath a wound. They are seams. They are places where one plate of the creature’s body meets another plate, with the exactness of assembled work.

It has no wet nose. It does not sniff the air. It has no visible breath. Its mouth, if it has one, is a thin line, closed and unused, not because the creature is asleep but because eating belongs to a world of change. Its eyes are small, dark, faceted, not round enough for soft life, not wet enough for any life one would immediately call animal. They do not appear to focus on movement. They appear to register position. They are organs for correctness, not for hunting.

The creature’s legs are too long for its body, not because it runs, but because it does not seem to need ordinary motion. Its joints are not knuckled like the knees of a mammal; they are angular planes that slide against one another with dry precision. Its feet are flat, broad, slightly pointed, and when they touch the ground they do not press into dust. They do not leave prints. The dust does not resist them; the dust simply knows where they are. If the creature shifts its weight, the ground gives no audible complaint. There may be a faint crystalline tick, like sugar cracking inside a sealed jar, but the sound is instantly absorbed into the basin’s chosen quiet.

It does not graze. It does not drink. It does not shelter from light, because there is no sun moving enough to make shade desirable. It may exist by some other rule entirely, as the description suspects. If one were to say what it eats, one would have to say it consumes relation. It takes in the fact that it is adjacent to the thicket, that it stands in the gap where a thicket needs a boundary, that its form preserves the exact distance between a rosette and a stem, between one pale terrace and another. It is a body that keeps the landscape from being too complete. Its presence is a margin, an edge, a slight imperfection that proves the perfection is being maintained.

If one approaches it, it does not flee. It does not posture or alarm. It simply remains, except that the relationship between you and it becomes impossible. The distance that should have decreased remains exact. The thicket in front of it is too dense to cross, but it does not cross; it is simply there again, not having traveled, not having moved, having become present at the edge without passing through the space that ordinary motion requires. One might conclude it teleports, but that would be too violent a word. It does not seem to act. It seems to be allowed to be elsewhere, as a place can be elsewhere if its coordinates are corrected.

A broken leaf would not stay broken; likewise, this creature could not stay damaged. If its leg were forced into a wrong angle, if its plate were cracked, if its body were interrupted, the basin would not heal it in the way living flesh heals. It would reject the interruption. For a moment the creature might flicker between whole and not-whole, but the not-whole would not persist. The creature would not recover, because recovery implies time passing through injury. It would simply never have been wrong. Its body would remain, to any witness who could trust their memory, unbroken, exact, and standing at the edge where a broken thing has no reason to exist.

There is also the still-winged insect.

It is small, translucent, and impossible to observe as motion. It hovers above the pale rosettes, but the hovering is a misperception. Its wings do not blur. They remain fixed, one above another, like glass leaves, like cut mica, like fragments of ice that do not melt. If one looks closely, the wings seem to have no veins, or if they have veins, they are decorative, geometric, exact repetitions of a structure that carries no fluid. The body is slender and pale, made of a material between insect shell and mineral shard. It has no warm underside. It leaves no wake. It does not seem to disturb the dust below it.

It is not flying. It is being positioned.

One instant it hovers over a single rosette; the next it is over another, ten paces away. The space between is not crossed. It is not erased; it simply does not apply. This makes the creature seem less like a living thing than like a cursor in a system too calm to show effort. It may pause at leaves that are exactly alike, at stems that repeat too perfectly, at shadows that fall almost correctly but not quite. When it does this, one gets the impression it is not looking for food but checking for sameness. It is a creature that visits repetitions. It does not pollinate. It does not carry pollen, because there is no softness to receive it, no wet stigma, no fruiting chamber, no sticky sweetness. Instead, it may carry the idea of identity from one place to another. When it passes from rosette to rosette, the leaves become no more or less identical than they already are; they only become more certain.

The insect-like thing does not cluster. It does not swarm. It does not mate, or if it does, mating cannot be distinguished from the appearance of a second one elsewhere. There is no egg-laying, no larva, no pupa, no emergence. If there are several such insects, they are not a family. They are several places where the basin’s quiet allows a small flying-seeming form to exist. Each one is a token of motion without event. Each one is a proof that the silence is not absolute, but still chosen.

There is another creature, rarer still, and harder to describe, because it seems to have been reduced to the memory of a larger animal.

In a shallow hollow between terraces, where the pale ground forms a slight depression edged with crisp mineral lips, one might find a broad, low, backless shape. It is not quite a tortoise. It has no shell, though the idea of a shell haunts it. Its back is a series of pale, faceted plates, smooth and flat, arranged as if they were meant to protect something beneath them, yet there is nothing beneath them but the body itself, dense and dry. Its limbs are tucked in, too short for walking, too heavy for burrowing, too precise for ornament. Its head, if it can be called a head, is a blunt wedge of mineral, with one or two small lenslike points where eyes might be, though they are not eyes in the ordinary sense. They are not wet. They are not mobile. They are fixed angles.

This creature does not move. It has not moved. It may never have moved. It seems not to be resting, because resting implies a body that will later rise. It is simply where its weight is needed. It makes the hollow a hollow. It gives the shallow depression a reason for being a depression. Without it, the hollow might seem accidental; with it, the hollow becomes a place, and the place seems to be inhabited. The broad thing is not a guardian. It does not protect the plants. It does not eat the dust. It does not wait for anything. It is a mass placed in the landscape to prove that the landscape is not merely a field of forms, but a field with some forms chosen for their stillness.

If one stands near it for a long time, one may begin to doubt whether it is an animal at all. It might be a stone that learned to be a body. It might be a body that learned to be stone. It might be a remnant of a larger ecology that no longer requires a larger ecology: a single example of a wide, heavy thing, kept in the basin as a note of mass, as a reminder that not all living things are light, and not all bodies are meant to walk. If approached, it does not withdraw. If touched, it is warm, but its warmth is not blood-warmth. It is the warmth of mineral under steady light. It does not pulse. There is no heartbeating. There is only the quiet pressure of its presence against the surrounding ground, a pressure that does not crack, sink, stain, or mark. It sits there as a fact. It is alive as a held weight is alive: without becoming.

These animals—angular, still, dry, residual, accidental, observers from a different set of rules—are not what most of the basin is inhabited by.

Most of the basin is inhabited by density itself.

The thickets, groves, rosettes, filaments, shelves, and hollows are the majority residents. They occupy the place with a continuity that does not allow the eye to count them. One approaches a line of shrubs and expects individual plants, but they refuse to resolve into a row. They refuse to resolve into a wall. They refuse to resolve into any countable arrangement, not because they are confusing, but because the concept of “one” has been softened. There are ten thousand leaves, but there is only one green. There are ten thousand stems, but the thicket is one density. There are ten thousand rosettes, but the field is one surface. The inhabitants are plural, but their plurality is not separable. They exist in a packed, living unity that does not require social organization. They do not form herds, colonies, or forests in the usual sense. They form a state.

Their “life” is not the life of processes. There is no growth toward light. There is no response to damage. There is no competition for water, because water is absent from the logic. There is no succession, no soil formation, no litter decomposition, no nutrient cycle. Yet there is an ecology, if ecology means a system of relations in which each form matters to the stability of the whole. The pale rosettes hold the ground in a pattern. The filaments hold the air in a pattern. The thickets hold density in a pattern. The branching subsurface holds the pattern from below. The rare animals hold exceptions to the pattern, so that the pattern does not become so perfect it forgets it is being maintained.

They are, in a way, a technology.

But not a technology with visible parts. There are no wires, no panels, no mechanisms, no engines, no towers, no roads, no tools. The machine is made of living-seeming forms. The circuit is the relation between one leaf and another. The signal is the faint pressure on the eardrums, the silence that does not decay, the light that falls with too much patience. The basin is not built by creatures who then inhabit it; the inhabitants are the building made organic, or the organic made to behave as infrastructure. The hare-like creature is a valve of distance. The still insect is a check of sameness. The broad hollow-dweller is a anchor of weight. The rosettes are surfaces of repetition. The filaments are resonances of height. The root-like solids are the holding beneath.

This is why the animals seem rare. They do not need to be many. The place does not need biodiversity as a wet world needs it. A wet world requires redundancy, cycles, birth, replacement, decay, mutation, resilience. This basin requires only the exact forms that justify the condition. Too many animals would imply hunger, movement, waste, interaction, change. The basin cannot permit those outcomes. So the few animals stand at edges, hover above repetitions, and sit in hollows. They are not a population. They are a permission.

Their sounds are few and strange.

When a hare-like animal stands in the thicket, its body may touch a dry blade. There is a faint tick, like paper rubbed across shell. If the blade bends slightly—if it bends at all—the bending is immediately overruled by the return of exact shape. If a still-winged insect passes through a curtain of filaments, there may be a small, high, chime-like sound, not musical enough to be pleasant, not sharp enough to be frightening, but enough to make the ear remember it was open. The sound does not travel. It does not echo. The echo would mean the air had carried the event, and this place does not like events. The broad, backless creature in the hollow makes no sound, except perhaps if one kneels near it and believes the ground is pressing the air toward the eardrums in a rhythm too slow to be called breath. It is not breath. It is the basin maintaining itself.

Their smells are not smells in the ordinary sense.

There is no musk, no dung, no urine, no wet fur, no glandular animal scent, no rot, no sweetness of flowers, no green smell of crushed leaves. What one might call odor is dryness: warm chalk, fine mineral, cold salt, stone heated by a light that is not hot, metal warmed by a hand that is not there. Sometimes, near the rosettes, there is a faint green metallic smell, like a new coin or like the taste of an electrode on the tongue, but it does not come from a mouth. It comes from the color green itself being made physical without chlorophyll, without water, without life as we understand it. The hare-like creature, if it has any scent, smells of clean dust. The still insect, if it has any scent, smells of nothing, because nothing seems to touch it enough to make it matter. The broad hollow-dweller smells of stone that has recently decided to be a body and has not yet changed its mind.

Their eyes do not seem hungry.

They do not scan for movement because movement is already constrained. They do not look for food because food would require change. They do not watch for predators because predators would require conflict. The angular hare’s eye is a dark facet, perhaps reflecting the thicket, perhaps reflecting the visitor, perhaps reflecting nothing but the fact that something is present. The still insect has no visible eye, or has many small points that could be eyes, or wings, or flaws in mineral, or places where light is being tested. The broad backless thing has eye-lenses that do not seem to look at anything but the ground, and even then not with interest, but with the patient certainty of a thing that knows where it belongs.

There is no mating, or if there is, it does not result in young.

There are no eggs in the hollows, no larvae in the rosettes, no juveniles with softer forms and brighter colors. The inhabitants are all fully formed, all equally certain, all equally impossible. If one were to speak of their reproduction, it would have to be reproduction by correction: the basin finds a place too empty, too variable, too close to becoming ordinary, and a new form appears there, not born but admitted. If one were to speak of their death, it would have to be disappearance, and even that would not be death, because nothing is buried, nothing decays, nothing remains. A hare-like animal might seem absent, but it is not gone; it is merely elsewhere, or its elsewhere is not visible, or the idea of elsewhere has been corrected.

They do not compete.

One thicket does not encroach on another. One rosette does not shade out its neighbor. The filaments do not crowd the low shrubs into thinness. The angular hare does not claim a territory. The still insect does not defend a flower, because there are no flowers as such, only cups of light and repetition. The broad creature does not fear predators. The rare animals do not cluster for safety, because safety implies threat. They are distributed by exactness, not by ecology as we know it. They are spaced like punctuation, not like individuals.

They do not cooperate.

There is no herding, no flocking, no symbiosis. The hare does not eat fallen plant matter because there is none. The insect does not pollinate because there is nothing sticky, nothing soft, nothing that can become fruit. The broad hollow-dweller does not till the ground. The root-like solids do not exchange nutrients. Yet their functions interlock, not because they communicate, but because the basin’s condition requires their co-presence. The hare’s standing creates a gap in density. The insect’s hovering confirms repetition. The broad thing’s weight completes the hollow. The plants’ fullness makes the animals seem residual. The animals’ rarity makes the plants seem inevitable. This is their only relationship: a mutual refusal to become ordinary.

The place itself lives through them, perhaps, as a body lives through organs that are no longer needed in the way they once were. Or perhaps they live through the place. The distinction is not stable.

What is stable is this: if one moves through the basin long enough, the idea of “inhabitant” changes. One begins to see that the pale ground is not a surface on which creatures stand; it is a body from which creatures emerge. The plants are not things growing in soil; they are forms of the soil becoming visible. The rare animals are not animals surviving in an impossible landscape; they are the landscape trying, at a few points, to resemble an ecology. The hidden technology is not a machine underneath life; it is life reduced to the minimum that still deserves the word.

No water lives there. But dryness is not the absence of life. In this basin, dryness is a kind of metabolism. It is the process by which the land refuses becoming. It is the way the leaves stand without bending. It is the way the dust knows where to fall. It is the way a hare-like animal can touch a thicket and leave no impression. It is the way an insect can hover without moving its wings. It is the way a broad pale thing can sit in a hollow and make the hollow mean something.

And there are, if one is very patient and not too certain, other forms that seem to live between categories.

A field of rosettes may appear, from a distance, to contain the outline of a creature lying down. As one approaches, the outline dissolves into individual plants, but the impression remains: the field has organized itself to suggest a body, not because the body is there, but because the concept of body is useful. A curtain of filaments may reveal, when touched, a pattern of vibrations that travels nowhere, yet feels as if something vast is listening. A shadow falling slightly wrong may seem, for a moment, to be detached not from a plant but from an animal; yet no animal stands nearby. The shadow remains correct and incorrect at the same time, as if its owner exists just outside the basin’s willingness to display them.

There are also absences that seem inhabited.

A pale hollow with no broad creature in it. A thicket edge with no hare standing there. A rosette field with no still insect hovering above it. Each absence feels like a temporary withdrawal, not like the permanent state of being unoccupied. The basin has not forgotten the missing forms; it is simply refusing to let the refusal show. It maintains its exactness by not displaying the gaps, and in that not displaying, the absence becomes another resident. It lives there the way a missing tooth lives in the mouth: not as an object, but as a shape in the arrangement.

Perhaps the true inhabitants are the relations.

The hare-like animal lives because the thicket must have an edge. The edge is not a line; it is a body. The insect lives because the rosettes must be confirmed; the confirmation is not a word; it is a wing. The broad backless creature lives because the hollow must be completed; the completion is not a tool; it is a weight. The root-like solids live because the dust must be limited; the limitation is not a wall; it is a network. The plants live because the basin must be lush without water; the lushness is not growth; it is density.

If one asks, “Who lives there?” the answer is: almost no one, and that is sufficient.

If one asks, “What lives there?” the answer is broader and stranger: the dry thickets, the ceramic leaves, the rosettes with their impossible identical green, the vertical filaments that stand without bending, the pale branching network fused to the substrate, the dust-motes that become briefly animal and then return to mineral, the angular hare that eats no grass and drinks no water, the still-winged insect that hovers by correction rather than flight, the broad hollow-dweller that is not a stone and not yet fully an animal, and the absences that remain even when the creatures are elsewhere. They do not live by hunger. They do not live by birth. They do not live by death. They live by being exactly where they must be, in a place that does not permit anything else.

And their existence is not violent, but it is oppressive in its calm.

The basin does not need them to act. It does not need them to survive. It does not need them to reproduce. It has no predators, no parasites, no waste, no corpses, no bones, no dung, no decay. It has no young. It has no old. It has no survivors. It has no founders. It has no descendants. It has no migrations, no seasons, no weather, no fires, no floods, no storms, no droughts, because a drought implies water that once was. The animals that stand, hover, and sit are not living in this place. They are the place living itself as a few carefully admitted forms.

If a human were to walk there, the human would feel it immediately: not danger, not thirst, not heat, not cold, but the subtle offense of having needs. The human’s body would demand moisture, rest, change, passage, shelter, relief. The basin would offer none, not from cruelty, but because the offering would alter the condition. The human might drink from a flask, and the moisture might disappear into the air without wetting it, or might remain exactly in the flask because the landscape does not understand spilling. The human might step over a broken stem, but there is no broken stem; any crack would heal before the foot completed its motion. The human might leave a footprint, but the footprint would vanish not as if erased, but as if it had never belonged to the sequence of ground. The human’s shadow would fall correctly, and then not quite, because the human is too changeable to be fully trusted by the light.

The human would not become an inhabitant. The human would be a visitor made strange by proximity to a life that does not need visitors.

The true inhabitants do not notice this. They are not indifferent in the way of beings who choose not to care. They are indifferent because choice is a movement in time, and time here is not moving. The angular hare does not watch the human with hostility. It does not decide to flee. It remains because remaining is its form. The still insect does not approach the human’s head. It does not land on hair or skin. It cannot make itself at home on a wet creature. It hovers near the human because the human is a moving configuration, and moving configurations are dangerous exceptions. The broad backless thing does not turn its head. It has no neck to turn. Its lenslike points may reflect the human’s outline, but the reflection is not recognition. It is a surface doing what surfaces do in a place where nothing becomes.

They live there as the garden’s dry citizens, but they do not tend the garden. They are the garden’s proof.

Their bodies are not wet enough to rot. Their bones are not soft enough to break. Their skins are not porous enough to dry out, because they were never damp. Their mouths are not open enough to speak, because speech would alter the air. Their eyes are not wet enough to blink, because blinking would mean time has passed. Their limbs are not supple enough to run, because running would require the ground to accept a different arrangement. Their wings are not alive enough to fly, because flying would require motion through space, and motion through space is the one event the basin tolerates least.

And yet they are alive in a way that resists simple replacement with stone or machine.

They are alive as a held note is alive while it has not yet faded, though nothing here is fading. They are alive as a mathematical structure is alive when it continues to prove itself, though proof implies a mind. They are alive as a perfect garden is alive when every plant is exactly where it must be, though a garden implies gardener. They are alive as the place is alive: not becoming, not breathing, not feeding, not resting, not dying, but remaining so completely that the remaining is mistaken, by any ordinary creature, for life.

The basin does not bloom.

Its inhabitants do not change.

The thickets do not spread.

The rosettes do not open.

The filaments do not bow.

The root-like lattice does not seek.

The hare does not leap.

The insect does not fly.

The broad thing does not stir.

The dust does not settle into new patterns.

The light does not leave.

The silence does not move.

What lives there is a life without event. It is dense, dry, warm, exact, impossible, and patient. It has no hunger, because hunger would disturb the equilibrium. It has no birth, because birth would disturb the instant of formation. It has no death, because death would disturb the youth. It has no memory, because memory would imply a past that could be carried forward. It has no future, because future would imply a permission to become otherwise.

And the strange thing is not that these creatures exist.

The strange thing is that they look so correct.

The angular hare at the thicket edge belongs there as if it had always belonged there. The still-winged insect above the rosette belongs there as if the rosette had been made for it. The broad, backless, pale thing in the hollow belongs there as if the hollow had been formed to receive it. The dust-creature flickers into being at the edge of the field as if the field requires the possibility of a tiny body, but only momentarily. The root-like branching solids beneath the ground belong there as if the ground were their body, and their body were the ground’s patience.

They are inhabitants because they complete the place.

The place lives because it is inhabited, not by things that change it, but by things that prevent it from becoming anything else.
