Where the mats are thick, the water smells sour and alive, and the first proof of habitation is not a door but a stain: a ledge worn into a shallow bowl by years of sitting bodies, a wall darkened by oil and soot from a cooking stone, a channel scraped too clean, a pool whose surface holds the oily rainbow of someone’s breath. The Sinterwork is inhabited from below and from above, from the smallest visible shimmer and the largest human movement. Life here is not a garden placed upon the land, but a set of habits the land keeps returning to.

The first citizens are too small to be called creatures. They are skins. They are blooms. They are the slow, breathing films that cover the salt and iron. In the warm shallows, microbial mats build themselves into felted sheets, some black as tar, some yellow with sulfur, some blue-grey like bruises over old water. They trap mineral dust, bind crusts together, feed on dissolved iron and salt, and exhale faint bubbles that pop with a smell like eggs and wet metal. On higher terraces, fungal threads spread through dust and shell debris, fruiting in pale buttons or warty lumps that look less grown than deposited. These living surfaces are not background. They are informants, pantry, glue, medicine, and warning system. A mat that turns black and bubbles means a vent has shifted. A blue film that thins means the water has gone sour. A ring of cream-colored growth means something has died in the channel. If you know the Sinterwork well enough, the mats speak in colors.

The people tend them. They do not farm as one would with fields or stalks; there is nothing to cut and nothing to replant. Instead, they encourage, move, rinse, and harvest. They scrape mats onto stone trays, wash them in clean channels, set them in shallow pans, and leave them in the humid air until the smell changes from sulfurous to sour to something almost breadlike, though no bread was ever made in the Sinterwork. They press mat pulp into cakes and dry them on warm ledges. They mix certain mats with clay and shell flour to make a paste that hardens when heated. They use other mats, bitter and black, to draw rust from stone or to clean sores, though the wrong preparation can close a throat or make a child blind with fever for a season. The mat-readers know which is which. They taste danger from color, not from curiosity alone, and they teach children to ask permission before touching anything that has begun to bloom.

Then there are the animals, or what passes for animals in a place without grass, without burrowing rodents, without herds. The Sinterwork is full of small clicking, skittering, snapping life. Brine flies hover in veils over the shallow pools, their bodies salt-frosted, their wings catching the dim light like scraps of mica. Their larvae wriggle through decaying mats and are harvested by hand, scooped into shells, boiled in stone pans until they curl white, then eaten with a lick of salt or pressed into oil. The people complain of the flies, but they depend on them. Without the flies, many of the smaller creatures would have no reason to come to the basins, and without those creatures, many of the channels would clog with rot.
