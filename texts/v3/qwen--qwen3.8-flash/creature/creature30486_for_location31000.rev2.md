# What Lives in the Dry Grove

Life in the bowl does not announce itself with juice or shine. It arrives first as sound: the soft crack of salt, the click of seed heads, the dry rustle of something moving through the reeds. It arrives as dust lifting around a footstep and not settling as if surprised, as if the ground has been expecting that foot for a thousand years and does not care whether it is human, lizard, or beetle. It arrives as color only when one stops and looks closely: blue sparks under a stone, copper on a moving shell, pale green-gray where the vegetation thickens, rust-colored smoke from the town’s low chimneys. The inhabitants are not scattered across a background. They are the background. The grove is not a place with lives in it; it is a living density made of many lives, all packed into a room left by a dead mountain.

The first of these lives is the ground itself.

A newcomer might say the crust is mineral and therefore inert, but in this bowl even mineral has habits. The skin of the floor is a community: salt and gypsum fused with pumice powder, flaky mica, fine sand, and chalky rime. It holds warmth and breathes dryly with the temperature changes of day. At noon it softens almost imperceptibly; at dusk it contracts and whispers as it cracks. Into this crust grow white threads that are neither root nor fungus nor stone, but something closer to all three at once. They spread beneath the surface in fine nets, catching the faint mineral vapors that rise from heated rock, storing moisture not as water but as crystalline habit. In sheltered hollows, these thread-creatures form pale films that look like frost but feel like flour. They are called *rime-skins* by the people of the grove, and no one thinks of removing one without reason. A rime-skin that is cracked can heal if left alone, but if it is destroyed, the patch may take a human lifetime to recover. In a place this slow, that is a small death.

Beneath the crust is a honeycomb of passages, and the passages themselves are a kind of body. The burrows are not only homes but lungs. They carry warmth downward and coolness upward. They conduct the faint sound of the bowl’s wind from terrace to terrace. They are lined with salt where the animals pack it with their paws, smoothed with fiber where insects have wove it, hardened where plant roots have stitched it. Some tunnels lead into the deep mineral veins where heat rises in slow waves and the air tastes of iron. Some open into hidden chambers where small mammals sleep in dust nests, their bodies almost dry enough to seem like seed-pods until one sees the quick pulse at their ears. The ground’s living form is not only on its surface but in its architecture. The bowl has a skeleton of stone, a skin of crust, and a nervous system of roots.

The plants of the grove are its most visible inhabitants, though they are not what one would expect of “lush.” They do not glisten. They do not bow with the weight of leaves. They stand stiffly, packed together, as if the whole bowl were a dried riverbed that had been emptied centuries ago and then filled with the memory of water. Pale shrubs rise in domes no taller than a person, their branches curving outward from wind and warmth into shapes that look deliberately rounded by patient hands. The people call them *thread-willows*, because in the dusk their branches shake with thin sound, like looms being worked. Their bark is smooth but gritty, coated in a fine powder that protects it from the dryness. Their leaves are leathery and pale, not green so much as chalky green, edged with mineral residue. If one crushes a leaf between the teeth, it tastes of salt, resin, and faint bitterness. It gives almost no moisture. Yet the shrubs are alive because they do not need open water. They pull heat from the floor, draw mineral breath from the cracks, and lock what little hidden moisture there is into wax, fiber, and sap-like threads that run slowly down into their roots.

The tall mineral-grasses grow in bands across the warmer parts of the floor. Their stems are hollow, smooth, and pale as bone. Their seed heads are papery and brown, and when they strike one another, they make a sound like old paper being rubbed. At certain angles, with just enough wind, the whole band clicks at once, and the bowl seems to be counting. The reeds are taller and softer in their dryness. They bend almost to the ground in the thin high wind but do not snap; they have learned flexibility through centuries of being beaten by air. Their roots drink from fissures where warmth rises. Their stems hold air and heat. Their seed heads feed birds and beetles. In the town, they become mats, ropes, walls, drying racks, and the frames that carry salt blocks. A reed field is not just a plant community; it is a granary, a forest, a roof-beam, and a song all at once.

Low cushion-plants spread over stones and hardpan in pale domes, their surfaces dusted with chalky rime. They are dense enough to sit upon for a short time, but they are not soft. They feel like a bundle of fibers wrapped around a dry kernel. They do not wilt because they are never saturated. Their cells are waxy and tightly built, their interiors holding the bowl’s heat as if each cushion were a small hearth. When evening cools, they give back that warmth in a barely perceptible radiance, so that the grove at dusk feels not only alive but incubated. In the centers of these cushions, ants build galleries of chewed mineral fiber. In the hollows beside them, lizards lay eggs dry enough to look like pebbles. In their crevices, small beetles carry grains of salt away, polishing the ground as they go.

Hanging lichens drape from the higher branches like torn thread, gray-white and brittle until a hand draws one gently from its stone. Then it unrolls slightly, a thin skin of life, feeding on vapor and warmth rather than rain. It is fragile, but not delicate; it has survived too much dryness to be considered soft. The people use it for tinder, for stuffing, for poultice, and for marking boundaries. A stone left unlichen’d may be claimed by one household; a stone already rimed is old enough to be left alone. This is one of the grove’s many quiet laws.

Beneath the visible plants are the bulbs: pale, waxy, swollen, and partly hidden. They grow where the floor stores the deepest heat. Their shoots appear only at intervals, short and stiff, as if the bulb is reluctant to show itself. If one digs too near one, it does not release water. It reveals a dry interior made of layers of fiber and mineral paste, sweet only after long chewing. The people prize these bulbs not because they are juicy, but because they can be stored for months. They are ground in saddle querns, mixed with seed-flour, and baked into flat cakes that sustain travelers across the thin high plain outside the bowl. The bulb is the grove’s hidden treasury: life folded inward, waiting.

All these plants grow together in a packed weave, and that weave is what makes the grove seem impossible at first. It looks like a marsh drained long ago and then replaced by a dry mirror of itself. There are no open channels, no reeds nodding above water, no mud gleaming after rain. Instead there are salt flats, wind-polished stone, and a tangle of pale green and ochre vegetation so dense that the eye fills it with liquid anyway. The effect is of abundance without overflow. Life here is crowded by structure, not by flood. Each plant occupies a niche created by heat, mineral, crack, wind, and the bodies of other plants. No stalk is wasted. No branch is allowed to grow where it will not feed something, shelter something, or become something useful. The grove is lush because it is packed.

The insects are the first small creatures to reveal that packing. Beetles are especially common, and in the dim light their shells catch the bowl’s glow and turn it into brief sparks. Some are the blue of old fire; some are copper; some are white with dust. They crawl along salt crusts, under shrub-bark, inside hollow reeds, and through the thatch of the town. Their movement is not frantic. They have time. Their shells are hard because soft bodies cannot live without open water, and their legs are long enough to keep their undersides above the heated ground. Some eat plant fiber. Some eat the dry paste of bulbs. Some eat other insects. Some gather salt grains and polish them into their own galleries. A beetle’s shell, when it dies, becomes a toy, a pendant, a trade token, and sometimes a blade handle. Nothing in the bowl is allowed to vanish uselessly.

Ants live beneath the crust and within the cushion-plants, but their bodies are pale and dust-coated, almost too dry to look like living things until one sees them moving. They build paper-thin walls from chewed rime and fiber. They farm tiny scale-like creatures that secrete wax instead of liquid, and they gather this wax in lumps no larger than a grain of sand. The people sometimes raid these galleries, not for honey—there is none here—but for the wax, the scale-casts, and the packed salt the ants have smoothed into their chambers. A child may know where to find an ant gallery better than where to find a bird’s nest, because the galleries show in the surface cracks like veins.

At night, crickets and rasping-winged insects fill the grove with a thin, continuous chorus. Their song is not bright like a bird’s; it is dry, metallic, rhythmic, like small stones shaking in a cloth bag. Some insects call from inside the salt tunnels; some from the tops of the reeds; some from the roof-beams of the town. The sound becomes so normal to the people that they forget it is noise at all. A child born outside the bowl, brought here as an infant, might sleep poorly without it, the way a person from a river city might miss the sound of running water. The crickets are the bowl’s heartbeat, small and numerous, never stopping completely.

The lizards live among the stones and the lower shrubs, and they are perhaps the most characteristic creatures of the grove. Their skin flakes in pale sheets, each scale edged with mineral residue, so that when they crawl over salt crust they leave a faint white trace. Their bodies are lean and angular, their heads broad enough to swallow beetles and small mammals in sudden bites. They move in short urgent starts, darting from shade to warmth and then freezing again, their color making them nearly invisible against chalk and pumice. A lizard does not hurry unless danger is real; in this place, most movement is a negotiation with heat. They bask with bellies flat against warm stone, then retreat beneath shrubs when the ground overheats. They store fat in their tails when the insects are thick, and when the seasons thin, they become motionless in burrows, living on what they have carried.

Some lizards are small enough to be caught in woven traps. Others are no larger than a finger, pale as a strip of bark, and they can close their nostrils against dust for long periods. The town’s children learn to catch them by patience, not speed. A child crouches near a warm stone, breath held, hands cupped around a tuft of reed fiber. The lizard comes for a beetle or a seed. Then the hand falls. The people call these animals *flakebacks*, and many families keep one or two in shallow clay pens near the hearth. They are not loved as pets so much as tolerated and valued: they eat beetles from the grain-stores, and their shed skins are scraped for powder.

The small mammals emerge at dusk, when the bowl’s heat begins to lift from the ground and the air takes on a faint metallic taste. They are rat-like or gerbil-like, dust-colored, with soft fur that collects powder until each animal looks like a moving tuft of rime. They run in nervous little waves across the open floor, pause, vanish into cracks, reappear elsewhere. Their ears are large because the thin air carries high sounds poorly, but the bowl’s wind gives them many frequencies to listen for. Their eyes are wide and dark, adapted to the low gold-gray glow of late light. They hoard seeds in salt-lined chambers, chew dry bulbs, and lick warm crystals for the mineral moisture that condenses in their mouths when their bodies need it. Their tails sometimes carry pouches of fiber, or are used to brush sand from the mouths of their young. They are abundant because the burrows are abundant, and the burrows are abundant because the roots have made the crust patient.

The people trap these animals in small woven snares set along their runways. They use their fur for linings and their fat for lamp-wax. They eat them roasted in salt pits, bones buried afterward in crevices where the roots will find them. To eat such creatures is not considered harsh; in a place with no open water, no river fish, no soft fruit, no easy prey, the small mammals are simply the nearest meat. Their bodies are light, dry, rich, and full of the hidden mineral energy of the bowl. To eat them is to eat the grove’s density.

Birds nest above all this, in the rounded hollows of the rim and in the domes of the taller shrubs. They are white-breasted seed-eaters with thick beaks, sharp voices, and restless habits. They move constantly from perch to perch, never for long. They argue in bright, harsh calls that cut across the wind’s low tone. Their nests are cups of fiber, lichens, and beetle shells, built into the salt crevices where the walls hold warmth. Some nest directly in the roof-beams of human houses, sharing shelter and food with the people without being fully tame. The town’s children do not disturb these nests without reason; a bird that returns to the same roof for seven years may be given a name and left unbothered even when its droppings stain the salt blocks. In this place, repetition is a form of kinship.

The birds’ gizzards hold pumice grit, gathered by the parent or by the growing chick. The grit grinds the seeds of the reeds and shrubs. Their bones are pale and thin but strong enough for the thin air. Their feathers are dry and edged with mineral dust, so that at dusk the flocks along the rim look like sparks lifting from a fire. Their calls become part of the town’s evening: bright, sharp, insistent. To live in the bowl is to wake not to sunlight alone but to bird-argument, wind-rustle, and the dry chorus of insects beginning again in the shadows.

High above the bowl, raptors circle in long lazy arcs. They are pale, long-winged, and silent except when they cry. They watch the rim and the open floor, looking for movement among the lizards and small mammals. They do not often land inside the grove; the bowl is too crowded with life for their liking, too many hiding places, too much heat rising from the salt. They belong to the edges, to the higher wind, to the world outside the room. But their shadows fall across the town at times, and children are taught to look up and name them: the circle-shape, the pale watcher, the high crack, the mother of thin air. They are not worshipped. They are respected because they come from beyond the bowl’s rim, and in a place so bounded, anything from outside is strange.

Then there are the people.

They call themselves the Bowl-kin, because to them the bowl is not a town within a region but the world itself. Outsiders—if any remain—call them Saltfolk, pumice-hands, dry-town people, but those names are not used in the grove. The Bowl-kin live in a settlement that began as a hamlet and became town-like by accumulation, by family pressing against family, wall against wall, roof beam beneath roof beam. Their city is tiny by any large-world measure, but within the bowl it has the density and specificity of a true settlement. It has streets, though narrow. It has quarters, though not by decree. It has a square of hardpan, a ring of stalls, drying racks, corral, hearth-lines, storage pits, salt-cutting yards, and a child’s world that moves among lizards and larder-beams.

Their houses are built from the ground and the grove. Salt blocks, cut carefully and sealed with heat and oil, form walls in the lower places. Pumice brick is used where warmth and lightness matter more than permanence. Hardpan plaster fills the gaps. Woven mineral-fiber mats cover roofs or line interiors. The pale dry wood of the shrubs makes door-frames, looms, and carrying beams. The doorways are low, the roofs rounded to shed wind rather than rain, because rain never comes, but wind is always listening. Some houses sit inside old hollows in the crust, only their mouths visible, their interiors cool by day and warm by night. Others stand on raised platforms of fitted stone, allowing the burrow animals to pass beneath, a courtesy that may be practical but is treated as custom.

Inside, the rooms are small and warm. Fire is precious, but not wasteful; hearths are set at the town’s warmest points, and houses lean into one another to save fuel. Lamps burn rendered wax from dry plants, their flames low and sweet-smelling. The light pools in doorways rather than spreading through the room. At night, the settlement looks like a cluster of amber eyes in a white landscape, each lamp a signal: someone is here, someone is working, someone is eating, someone is awake. The people speak softly inside because the walls carry sound well, and because shouting inside a salt room feels like waking the stone.

They are not separate from the grove. The shrubs grow against their walls. Beetles live in the thatch. Birds nest in their beams. Dust drifts through their doorways and is swept into the floor-cracks, where it joins the ancient crust. Their waste, dried and salted, becomes part of the ground’s slow body. Their dead are placed in sealed salt pits, where flesh loses its moisture and the bones remain pale and clean, later moved to crevices where roots take them. No one expects a wet burial; to sink the dead into earth here would be almost unthinkable. The bowl preserves, and the people, too, are preserved in the pattern of their streets.

Their bodies show where they live. They are compact and tough, with broad chests adapted to thin air, wide noses that warm breath before it reaches the lungs, and dark-rich blood that moves patiently under the skin. Their skin is dry and pale, often marked by faint salt-crystal patterns where the floor’s breath has touched it. Their hair is coarse and often coated with wax or powdered fiber to keep it from fraying in the wind. Their eyes are narrowed at the corners, protected by thick lashes and a quick reflex to dust. Their voices are bright but not loud, clear in the low light, shaped by years of speaking through rustle and wind. They do not sweat much; their bodies conserve moisture as if moisture were a secret.

They do not drink water openly. There is no stream to visit, no pool at dawn, no rain-catch basin that fills and glitters. Yet they do not die of thirst. The bowl has taught them other ways. Hidden moisture lives in salt, in gypsum, in the deep cells of bulbs, in the fat of small animals, in the bodies of insects. When a lump of gypsum or warm salt is wrapped in sealed fiber and held in a clay vessel over a slow hearth, it gives up a faint mineral breath that can condense on the cooler lid in beads smaller than tears. The people call this *stone-sweat*, and it is not regarded as ordinary water. It is precious, strange, almost a spirit. A child may receive the first drop from the lid. An elder may receive the second. The maker of the heat may receive the third. This is not law by force, but custom so old it feels like physics.

Their diet is dry, mineral, and patient. Seed-flour, ground from reeds and grasses on saddle querns, forms the staple. Bulb-paste, beetle larvae, lizard meat, small mammals, and rendered wax provide richness. Nuts or hard seed-cakes carry them through lean seasons. They chew resinous bark to keep their mouths from feeling too dry. They suck warm salt stones when traveling. They carry small pouches of fine powder that, when mixed with a little plant oil, can be swallowed to cool the throat. Their bodies have learned to make use of what the land gives without asking it to become what it cannot. A Bowl-kin child grows up understanding thirst differently: not as a demand for a stream, but as a need to find the correct heat, the correct stone, the correct fat, the correct breath in the body.

Their tools are simple but not crude. Flint is chipped into knives, awls, scrapers, and small blade-tips. Copper is hammered for needles, pins, and prestige ornaments. Obsidian edges are so fine they can shave dry fiber without tearing it. Bone is polished into hooks, handles, and child’s games. Saddle querns grind seeds and mineral grains into paste. Salt blocks are cut and sealed. Fiber is spun between palm and thigh until it becomes cord strong enough to haul stone. A house may be raised by generations of hands, each wall added by a family, each repair made by a parent or child, so that the buildings look less designed than grown, like shrubs that have thickened against wind.

The town has its craftspeople. Salt-cutters know which blocks will hold and which will dissolve into powder if the hearth is too hot. Fiber-spinners know which stalks yield soft cord and which become brittle after one season. Lamp-makers render wax from bulbs and lichen-casts, pressing it into small clay cups. Quern-tenders know the rhythm of grinding: too fast and the paste heats, too slow and the hands tire. Bird-callers imitate the seed-eaters to warn of strangers from the rim, because in a small bowl, no one can approach without being heard by birds or wind. Dust-collectors sweep the fine powder that drifts into lanes, not because it is useless, but because it can be mixed with salt and oil to seal cracks. Even dust has a role.

The corral fenced with thorn and reed is not a grand pen. It holds small captured animals—gerbil-like mammals, lizards too fat to run well, sometimes a trapped raptor injured by a fall from the outside plain. The people do not farm as a wide-world farmer might; they gather, trap, tend, and store. The corral is an interruption in the wild density, a pocket of human patience. Animals kept there grow accustomed to salt-licks and dry seed-piles. Children climb the fence posts. Beetles colonize the manure, which is already dry enough to be swept rather than shoveled. The line between wild and tended is thin here, because the grove itself is so thick that almost any enclosure only adds another layer.

Their market square exists only because enough paths meet there. It is not a broad plaza but a cleared patch of hardpan where the ground is warm and the wind less biting. On the warmest hours, children and lizards compete for space there, both basking. The children learn to share stones, to move slowly so as not to disturb the heat, to read the lizard’s flicking tongue and wait their turn. This is one of the town’s earliest lessons: do not crowd without need, but do not be offended by another body taking the same warmth. In a small bowl, such tolerance is survival.

Around the square stand the stalls where salt, fiber, dried meat, lamp wax, obsidian flakes, copper beads, beetle-shell pendants, and bulbs are exchanged. Trade is not complex, but it is precise. A family may give a sealed salt cake for three coils of cord. A quern may be repaired in exchange for a month’s supply of lamp-wax. Dried stalks may be cut and stacked in patterns that say whose family is holding claim to which reed patch. The rules of exchange are memorized rather than written, because writing would require surfaces and tools better used for making. Yet the people have a deep sense of fairness. They have no rivers to divide, no fields to plough, no large herds to graze. Their wealth is what can be packed: salt, fiber, stone, seed, heat, and the labor that binds them.

At dusk, the town becomes loudest. Not in anger or hurry, but in presence. People call to one another from low doorways. Children run between drying racks, knocking pale strips against walls with soft clacks. Women and men grind seed while talking in bright half-voices. Elders sit where the crust holds heat, wrapped in fiber mats, eyes narrowed against the low gold light. Smoke rises thin and sweet, not from wet wood but from resinous shrubs and dried reeds, its scent mixing with chalk, salt, animal fur, and the faint iron taste of the air. Lamps begin to glow in windows and hollows. The settlement looks less built than lit, as if the bowl itself had opened many small eyes.

Their language is shaped by the bowl. It contains many words for dry sounds: the click of seed heads, the crack of crust, the whisper of lichen rubbing stone, the thud of a burrow collapsing, the bright call of a seed-eater, the low note of wind focused by the rim. It has fewer words for wet things, and when it names them, it does so with a kind of respectful strangeness, as if water were an old myth that almost happened. A stream is not just a stream; it is a story-thing, a world-shape, a place where a river might be if the bowl were different. Their poems are often made of sound-names and stone-names. Children learn to say the names of surfaces before the names of people.

Their beliefs are not dramatic. They do not imagine a god who sends rain, because no rain comes here. They do not fear floods, because the bowl has forgotten them. Their spiritual life, if one can call it that, is centered on attention to form. The rounded rim is sacred because it holds the warmth and defines the world. The cracks are sacred because they show patience. The salt is sacred because it preserves. The dry shrubs are sacred because they are packed with others. A house is not consecrated by water but by salt placed in its lowest crack, a stone warmed at the hearth, and a lichen thread tied to its doorway by someone whose name will be remembered for that act. To bless a newborn, the family places the child briefly against a warm salt block so the ground knows it. To mourn, they do not pour water but rub chalk powder on their skin, covering the visible marks of thirst and dryness until the grief becomes mineral.

Their deaths are slow and often unremarkable. A person dies in a room where the walls have thickened around them. Their body is salted, then stored in a pit where the crust will seal over the years. Their bones may later be moved to a crevice beneath a shrub, and the roots will take them up. No one expects to be buried wet or deep. The dead return to the grove’s packed architecture. The living do not visit them with flowers, because the grove is already full of pale plants and no one needs decoration. They visit with dust-brooms, with lamps, with small offerings of seed. They talk in the low way one talks when the wind is listening.

Time moves through the Bowl-kin not as a flood of events but as layers. A year is not dramatic; it is thickening. The town expands by a wall, then another. A child grows into the height of a door-frame. A salt block develops a crack that will reseal over decades. A shrub thickens by a finger’s length. An old street becomes a courtyard as families close around it. A hearth becomes worn into the floor. A person’s hands become rough enough to grip obsidian without fear. They measure their lives by these changes. An elder might say, “That lane began when my mother’s wall stood,” or, “This crack has healed twice since I was born,” or, “The reed stand was not yet thick when my grandfather first fenced the corral.” Their histories are not written but embedded in the place itself, readable only to those who know how to look for additions.

The animals live in the same long rhythm. Insect migrations are timed to the slow warming of particular floors. Lizards store fat when the beetles are abundant. Birds nest on ledges whose shapes have not changed for centuries. Small mammals shift their burrows when the salt crust becomes too hot for their young. Even the town’s movements are animal-slow: a herd of dust-voles may take a season to choose a new tunnel. The Bowl-kin do not hurry them. To hurry in this place is to waste warmth.

What emerges, when one begins to look closely, is not a wilderness with a village, nor a village with plants and animals around it. The grove is a single complex creature with many organs. The crust is its skin. The roots are its hidden fingers. The shrubs and reeds are its ribs. The burrows are its lungs. The beetles, lizards, mammals, and birds are its quick blood. The people are its memory, its hands, its voice. The town is not separate from the ecology but an intensification of it: another packing of bodies, tools, heat, sound, and time. The Bowl-kin understand this without needing to say it plainly. They know that removing a shrub too close to a house weakens the floor. They know that cracking too many salt crusts changes the wind-tone. They know that if the lizards vanish, the beetles will swarm the grain-stores. They know that if the beetles vanish, the lamps will lack wax and the thatch will sour. They live within these consequences the way one lives within weather: not by controlling it, but by moving within its patterns.

If you crouch at the edge of a thread-willow dome in late afternoon, you can watch this whole world work. A lizard’s shadow slips across the salt crust. Its scales flake and catch the light, pale as broken chalk. A beetle passes beneath it, shell flashing copper. A small mammal’s head appears from a burrow, ears broad and dusted, then withdraws. A seed-eater lands on a low branch and calls sharply, not because it sees danger, but because the bowl is full of voices and silence would make no sense. The lizard freezes. The mammal hides. The beetle keeps its slow line. The bird tilts its head. The wind moves through the dry reeds, and for a moment the entire grove seems to take one long dry breath.

Then the human world continues around this small drama. From a doorway, someone calls to a child who is too close to the lizard-hole. Another voice answers from a higher terrace. Smoke lifts across a drying rack. A quern turns slowly in a courtyard. A lamp wick crackles in a wall-niche. The town’s sound is not background; it is part of the ecology’s chorus, inseparable from the click of stalks and the scurry of small feet. Nothing is alone. Nothing is merely scenery. Even the dust is occupied.

This is the deepest quality of the place: its lives are not arranged in a hierarchy but in a packing. The people are not rulers. The birds are not ornaments. The lizards are not pests. The salt is not mere ground. The shrubs are not just vegetation. Each being adds another layer of density, and the whole becomes more than the sum of its inhabitants. The bowl is a small world, but not a simple one. It is old, but not exhausted. It is dry, but not dead. It is warm, but not bright. It is crowded, but not confused. It is loud, but not hurried.

At dusk, when the rim catches the last faint light and the bowl becomes a single object, the inhabitants are everywhere. The salt floor holds warmth. The dry stalks rustle. Smoke drifts across the tops of the shrubs. Birds argue in the hollows. Beetles scurry along the crust. Small mammals return to their tunnels. Lizards settle into the last heat-stones. People call from doorways, their voices bright and close. The town’s lamps begin to glow. The grove’s sounds merge: crack, click, rustle, call, step, breath, wind. It is not the sound of a wild place untouched. It is not the sound of a human place alone. It is the sound of a world that has learned to hold life without open water, to make density out of mineral and patience, to keep a small cathedral full of movement.

Nothing has happened for a long time. Everything is happening.
