The people who live there are not one neat race, not one clear tribe, not a people carved by some ancient lineage that can be read from a single face or dialect. The Hollow is too young for that kind of story. At first, they look like ordinary men and women from the plain, but the plain has begun to rub off them, and the Hollow has begun to settle into them in return.

They come from many backgrounds, because the upper world has always had more names for its poor than room for them to live. Some of them were farmers who lost the best fields to wealthier relatives. Some were herders whose flocks were taxed until there was nothing left but skins and memories. Some were quarry workers who knew the sound of a good strike and could read a seam in a cliff as easily as a plain woman reads weather. Some were traders who had carried salt across too many miles. Some were families running from drought, debt, feud, conscription, or the slow pressure of a world above that was becoming crowded in exactly the ways that matter. Some arrived as children. Some arrived as old people with sore backs and no expectation of seeing anything new again. Over time, the Hollow mixed them, not by any grand law or ideology, but because a cistern cannot be cleaned by one household alone, and because a roof frame raised from below will not hold if every person insists on being a stranger in the stone.

So there are people of many shades and shapes, many hair textures, many remembered tongues. Some have the ruddy windburn of surface farmers, now softened by years under shaft-light. Some have the lean, watchful faces of travelers. Some have broad shoulders and heavy hands; some have narrow frames and quick, sure feet. Some still carry the customs of the plain and resent their absence from it. Some no longer want to return. A few are simply the children of this place, the first Hollowborn, and these are the ones who will make the outsiders most aware that a new kind of people is beginning here.

They are not yet a different kind of human being in the way a river eventually wears new stone into a new shape. The change is too recent, too mixed, too practical for that. But they are becoming a people of the Hollow, and it is written on their bodies first, and only later on their habits and stories.

Their hands are among the clearest signs. Almost all of them have hands that would look too large for a stranger. The palms are thick, the fingers often short and strong rather than elegant, and the tips are worn to smooth pads that no longer feel as much as they know. They grip ledges with an unconscious economy of motion, the way a cat grips a branch. They can press a thumb hard against a chisel and hold it there while the hammer wraps strike again and again. Their knuckles are knobby from cold and from constant scraping. Their nails are cut short and often split, because long nails are dangerous in a town of rope, wet stone, and tiny passages. Even the children have rough hands by the time they can run the alleys. In the market, when people reach for coins or a dried herb bundle, it is easy to see whose family is new and whose have already begun to belong: the new still carry the softness of surface work, while the older ones have a look about the palm as if chalk and water had entered the skin.

Their feet are no less altered. The soles of the surface poor are often hardened, but the Hollow does not simply harden feet; it reshapes them. Many wear felt slippers made from discarded cloth and wool, or wrap their feet in narrow bands tied around the ankle, because bare feet are dangerous on wet stone and loose slippers catch on stair edges. Even so, most have broad, low-set feet, with toes that have learned to spread for balance. The big toe is strong. The foot arches, while not dramatic, have become better at gripping a ledge when a ladder or timber scaffold sways.
