What lives here is not easy to find because the category of “creature” has been stretched.

The first answer is too small to see. It is a life measured in films of water, in microscopic cities inside moss, in swarms of pale nematodes through saturated peat, in rotifers turning in a drop on a leaf, in ciliate clouds breathing inside shallow pools, in bacteria warming the black mud with their slow metabolism. If a person kneels and presses a hand to the plateau, they are touching a warm living skin, and inside that skin are countless creatures for whom the entire landscape is a single wet continent.

These are the dust-walkers and thread-farmers: springtails no larger than pepper, mites with bodies like tiny polished grains, pale worms, white-filament fungi, and slow-moving glassy things that swell with moisture and shrink when water recedes, though here it never fully does. Some live only their entire lives in the surface film of a single pool, which to them is a universe with a bottom of leaf-rot and a ceiling of reflected green. Others climb hanging tendrils, ride falling drops, build cases from cut leaf fragments, or carry spores in the joints of their legs. They are not separate from the plant mat; they are its blood cells, its cleaners, its gardeners, its noise.

Then there are the lives a little larger but still half-hidden: beetle larvae curved in the black mud, their mouths grinding root and bone; caddis-like creatures dragging tiny boats of leaf-shingle across water; snails with lichen-crusted shells, slow as thought; pale fly-things crawling in long columns along dripping stems, their bodies reflecting the bioluminescent dust of leaves. Some insects have become so intertwined with the plant world that they are mistaken for it: a beetle that looks like a curled seed, a moth-like thing with wings like moldy cloth, a walking fruit with legs, a grub with the color of old water. They are everywhere, not as individuals, but as a constant tremor of hunger, growth, and exchange.

In the shallow, mirror-black pools sit the amphibious people.

There are frogs that look like lumps of wet stone until they blink. Their skins are green-brown, black, olive, or veined like leaf margins. Many have moss growing along their flanks, not as decoration but because they have learned to host it, to carry it from water to shade. Their bodies are cool and damp, and their throats pulse with small bubbles of call. A frog call here is not loud; it is a wet, round sound that seems to rise from the ground itself, a single note absorbed immediately by hanging curtains of fern. Some species sit in the same depression of the pool-edge for days, then vanish so slowly that one never notices their departure. Others have translucent bellies and blue-green veins that shine faintly at night, so that when they move through dark water, they become drifting lanterns.

There are also the mirror-frogs, which lie flat on the film above deeper water, their bellies pale and wide, their backs patterned to imitate dead leaves, moss mats, or the black surface of a pool. They can remain still until the water around them grows still with them. When a larva touches the membrane above them, they rise and gulp in one smooth motion, so softly that it seems the pool has simply taken the larva.

The tadpoles are not only young frogs. They are a life of their own, swimming through black, tannin-dark water like soft needles, filtering bone meal, root dust, fungal spores, and green slime. Some grow leaf-shaped tails; some have gills like pale ferns behind their heads. They school in dense clouds where warmth gathers, turning water into a living broth. When they become adults, many leave the pool slowly, climbing across moss in a silent procession at night, guided by moisture, heat, and the faint tremor of other bodies moving through the mat.

The reptiles of the plateau are not creatures of dry stone. They are creatures of saturated root.

Snakes move over the moss with barely a ripple, their bodies long, flattened, leaf-patterned, scaled in olive, brown, black, and pale green. They have no need for bright warning marks; the dim light is enough to hide them, and their movements are so quiet that they often appear to be branches falling upward through the ground. Their tongues taste the air, sampling moisture and warmth. They do not chase prey with speed. They lie beside hanging stems, in hollows, under stone lips, at the edges of pools, waiting for the tremor of a small warm body, the soft push of a frog, the ripple of a swimming thing below. Some are so thin they pass between threads of moss; others are broad and slow, their scales so worn and damp that they look like polished wood.

There are small lizards, too, though one sees them only in fragments: an eyelid blinking in a fold of bark, a toe gripping a wet stone, a blue-black tail vanishing into shadow. They live in the spaces between root arches and epiphytes, catching insects from the hanging curtains. Their skin is water-shedding, and beads of moisture run along their backs like strings of pearls. At night, their bodies take on a faint blue-green sheen from the fungi that glow beneath them.

The birds are rare but not absent, and the birds here are unlike birds elsewhere.

They are broad-winged, soft-feathered, slow-moving things that seem less to fly than to fall sideways through the mist. Their plumage is the color of lichen, wet bark, moss, and shadow. Some have feathers like thin leaves, with pale edges that collect condensation. They drift through the upper haze with large reflective eyes that gather the dim green light and the faint glow of hanging vines. They do not flock noisily; they rest in crooked root arches, so camouflaged that the tree appears to have grown additional branches. Only when one lifts its head does its eye catch the glow of a fungal ring and flash silver.

These birds feed on spores, young shoots, dripping nectar, insects, and small frogs. They do not build nests in the old sense. They use hollows in the canopy, curved leaves, cupped bromeliads, and the low rooms between hanging roots. One species carries a single egg in a folded leaf suspended above water, so that when the chick falls, it falls into a pool and is caught by the amphibian life below. Another taps the hanging tendrils with its beak, producing faint glassy notes, joining the plateau’s slow water-song.

The small warm-blooded lives are the quickening pulse of the mat.

They dart along root bridges, vanish into moss, reappear behind fern stems, and are seen as nothing more than a brief disturbance in the green. Their bodies are flattened and agile, their legs broad for moving over wet mats without sinking.
