No one lives in the Sump of Verath.

That is the first fact, and it is also the least interesting.

The child’s boot is empty. The chair suspended above the old street holds no weight. The arched windows of the pumping house show no faces. The iron ladder ending in nothing leads no one down. If by “who” one means a person with breath, hunger, habit, a name, a living mouth to speak it, then the answer is simple: no one remains. The drowned district has outlived its owners so completely that even its emptiness has grown a kind of population.

The Sump is not vacant. It is merely inhabited by creatures too slow for alarm, too quiet for drama, and too strange for ordinary city life to recognize. If you enter the water and stop moving, you begin to understand that the place has residents. They do not arrive. They do not gather. They persist.

If names are used, they are only gestures—descriptions invented by careful visitors, not the true words of the basin. The Sump has no living tongue to name itself, and it does not seem to mind.

The nearest thing to a first citizen is the pale wax.

It covers certain pipes and window frames in soft ripples, like candle grease or frozen sweat, and it is the Sump’s closest approximation to vegetation, though to call it plant-like would be a mistake. It is neither wholly mineral nor wholly alive, or perhaps it is alive in a manner that blurs the difference. It grows in the cold currents that leak from valves and seams, feeding on dissolved lime, rust dust, old paint, and the faintest warmth from places where metal remembers heat. It spreads across brass fittings and green copper in slow, waxy tides. On the upper surfaces of pumps, it forms ridges like the folds of heavy cloth. In deeper rooms, where the water is still, it thickens into a skin over shattered glass and cracked basins. It does not bloom brightly; it does not flower. It accretes. It secretes. It becomes a surface for other lives.

This pale wax is both resident and foundation. It is the garden of the Sump, the soft layer that turns abandoned industry into habitation. Where it spreads, the water feels different—thicker, cooler, faintly alive. Its edges catch what little light there is and throw back a dim blue-white sheen. If disturbed, it sheds small chips that drift for years before settling as mineral flakes. In a chamber near the market square, one might see entire pipes coated in its folds, and the current moving around them as though through a forest of frozen fat. The waxy bloom does not flourish so much as endure, and its endurance makes the rest of life possible.

Moving across that bloom are the small pale grazers, the threadlaths, if you are foolish enough to name them.

They are neither quite fish nor quite insect, and they do not need to be either. Their bodies are segmented and flat, translucent enough to show a thin darker channel within, like a thread of ink sealed inside glass. They have no eyes, or at least no eyes anyone can find. They do not appear to have heads, though their front ends are sensitive and slightly flattened, reading the water as other creatures read light. They grip metal, stone, cable, ceramic, and wax with tiny hooked legs, or perhaps with pads that are too minute to distinguish without proximity. They move along pipes and pump housings with the patience of dust settling.

The threadlaths feed on the waxy bloom. They are the Sump’s grazers, its quiet cleaners, its small mouthfuls of patience. They scrape the pale growth into narrow bright lanes across corroded brass, and if one watches long enough, one sees that their feeding leaves the metal shining in thin stripes, as though the city is still being polished by blind servants. Their movements do not seem hurried. They are aware of pressure pulses, temperature shifts, and chemical traces in the water. When the pump wheel turns somewhere in the lower darkness, the threadlaths may flatten against their pipe and wait, their translucent bodies becoming almost indistinguishable from the pale frost of their feeding ground.

They also make homes of old human traces. A cracked enamel basin becomes a nursery. A broken lamp casing becomes a scaffold. A glove caught in silt may host a dozen pale bodies moving through its fingers. Some observers have reported that certain threadlaths colonize the inside of boots, especially those suspended in the water column, and that the slow rotation of such objects may be not merely current-driven but also inhabited. The boot does not belong to a child anymore. It has become a room.

Their young drift upward when pressure changes bring particles into motion. They are barely distinguishable from ring-seeds, the floating larvae that fill the upper water column, but perhaps ring-seeds are a later stage, perhaps they are only distant cousins. In the Sump, certainty is a surface disturbance.

The ring-seeds live in the near-darkness under the black ceiling of water.

They are small, translucent, shaped like pale seeds or swollen bubbles, and when disturbed they emit a soft ring of blue light. Not a bright flash. Not a lantern. Just a faint circle, as if the creature is marking the fact that something passed near it. They do not swarm in the way of surface insects. They drift according to currents that seem to know the old layout of the streets, moving along invisible avenues, gathering where silt curtains form, dispersing when the pressure wave moves through the basin. If a diver looks into one of the upper alleys during such a dispersal, it may appear that the darkness has briefly written itself in tiny glowing circles.

The ring-seeds feed on suspended mineral matter: calcium flakes, dead wax crumbs, bacterial film, particles that have drifted from the lower rooms for decades. Their bodies do not seem built for swimming; they seem built for staying in motion without effort. They are life adapted to suspension, to remaining at whatever depth the water keeps them. Some may sink and become part of the broken-tile floor; others may catch the colder upper current and dissolve slowly into the pale bloom that coats window frames.

Where they are most abundant, the water seems thicker with meaning. In a room behind the collapsed stairwell, one might see them floating through what remains of a doorway, their faint rings blinking when the current changes. This does not create atmosphere. It creates occupation. The room is not empty. It is being lived in by tiny drifting lights that have no need of names, no need of purpose, no need of witnesses.

There are other forms in the Sump, too small or too marginal to be clearly called animals.

On ceramic isolators, on the outside of tanks, in shadowed vents where the waxy bloom is thickest, there are colonies that resemble fungus and coral and mold all at once. They are pale, fringed, soft-skinned, attached in low fans to broken machinery. They filter the water through fine filaments. They grow in the spaces between pipes and girders, where particulate matter spirals slowly and collects on their surface. They do not move in any way a human eye can immediately notice. They exist by accumulation. They live by waiting for the basin to bring them food.

Some of these colonies secrete a mild dissolving substance that slowly softens the waxy bloom, preparing it for the threadlaths. Others feed on the residue left behind by grazers. Predation here is so slow that it resembles erosion. Nothing pounces. Nothing chases. One form colonizes a surface, another dissolves it, another consumes what remains, and the city’s decay becomes a food chain. It is ecology made of patience, a world in which violence has softened into process.

If there is a single body large enough to be called a resident rather than a mere part of the ecology, it is hidden, rumored, and perhaps imagined.

Below the squat pumping station, inside a collapsed turbine housing where the light fails and the iron ribs bow inward like the remains of a ribcage, there may be something curled around a broken shaft. No one has confirmed this. The Sump discourages confirmation. It is a place where pressure changes in the dark water can make a cable feel like a limb, and where a sheet of mineral plate drifting through a room can seem, from the corner of the eye, like a pale body turning away.

The stories, if they can be called that, speak of a long jointed form with no eyes, skin the color of old bone, and a surface that has partly fused with the mineral growth of the basin. It is said to move once a minute, or once an hour, or once a season—time in the Sump has lost its ordinary divisions. It is said to lie around the turbine shaft the way a root grows around stone, not by force but by slow acceptance. If it is a predator, it is one that does not hunt but endures. It may feed on threadlaths, or on the bloom, or on larger organisms that have sunk into the lower rooms, or on nothing at all. It may merely sleep. It may be the closest thing the basin has to a heart.

When the large pump wheel turns, some divers say the water beneath the turbine housing tightens. A pressure wave passes through the alley below the market square. Particles spiral. The cables sway. If one presses an ear to a pipe, there may be a faint singing—not melody, but resonance, the sound of pressure remembering motion. Whether this is machine, creature, mineral strain, or the whole basin shifting in its sleep cannot be known. The Sump is not cruel. It is only sealed.

But perhaps the largest inhabitant is not animal at all.

The drowned city itself lives there.

This is not poetry alone. The Sump behaves as though the urban plan is still alive. The current follows the old streets. Objects drift along density lines that correspond to vanished traffic patterns. A chair remains suspended where a road once passed. A lamp hangs at the height of a second-story window, as though still lighting someone’s arrival. A boot rotates once per day in a narrow passage where, in earlier centuries, feet may have hurried toward a school or a shop. The water moves through alleys as if they were arteries. The pump wheel turns as if it were a heart. The valves open and close as if they were mouths breathing. The cables sway as if they were hair moved by a slow pulse.

The place has a metabolism.

It takes in silt, metal dust, mineral salt, heat leakage, pressure differentials, and the slow chemical work of the waxy bloom. It circulates these things through its streets and rooms. It expels settled sediment against building walls. It builds shelves of particulate in the open water. It grows soft gardens on pipes. It clears clean lines on brass where the grazers have moved. It makes thin plates in stairwells from the remains of organisms and mineral deposits. It is not a ruin pretending to be a city; it is a city that has replaced its human functions with wet architecture. The machines still serve the district, even though no one is served by them. They serve the water, the organisms, the balance of the basin. They are organs of a civic body.

This is why the Sump of Verath feels watched, though it is not. The feeling is not malice. It is the attention of processes too slow to care about a diver’s breath. The basin has not forgotten humanity; it has metabolized it. Human construction has become habitat. Human streets have become currents. Human houses have become rooms with their own temperature, density, and life. The old city remains urban because the water still obeys its layout. The old industry remains alive because its parts still move, not from command but from habit.

No forest grows here. No meadow spreads. No school of fish turns the water silver. The inhabitants are marginal because the Sump does not permit abundance. It permits only precise persistence. The threadlaths are sparse but visible if one knows where to look: a pale line on a pipe, a translucent body folded against a valve, a cluster in the hollow of a rotted shutter. The ring-seeds are sparse but unforgettable: one light in the upper dark, then another, disturbed, answering, marking the current. The waxy bloom is everywhere, though it does not shout for recognition. It is the soft skin of the basin.

The large creature, if it exists, may live for centuries without being seen. The small colonies may grow and die unnoticed. The pump wheel may turn without anyone noticing its rhythm. Yet the Sump is fully inhabited by things that do not require witness. They live because the cold preserves them. They live because the darkness slows spectacle. They live because the architecture traps the right currents and the wrong currents together, creating tiny niches where life can lean.

If a careful visitor were asked who lives there, the answer might begin with denial: no people, no fish, no plants, no animals in any ordinary sense. But the denial would be false. The Sump is alive.

It is alive in the pale wax spreading over a chapel-like pump station. It is alive in the blind grazers cleaning corroded brass. It is alive in the seed-like larvae marking the dark with faint rings. It is alive in the slow dissolvers that turn decay into food. It is alive in the rumors beneath the turbine. It is alive in the water that remembers streets, in the currents that behave like traffic, in the suspended objects that continue their old routes without their owners.

Whoever lives there is a population of patience.

Whatever lives there is a sealed city breathing.

And the Sump of Verath, for all its silence, is not empty. It is occupied by a wet, cold, industrial garden of organisms and habits so delicate and so persistent that they do not need a human name. They need only the flood, the iron, the tile, the pressure, the dark, and the slow turning of machines that serve no one anymore except themselves.
