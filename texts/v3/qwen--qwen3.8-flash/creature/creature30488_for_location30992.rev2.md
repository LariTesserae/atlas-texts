To describe whoever lives there, one must first give up the expectation that inhabitants will be separable from the place.

There are people, yes. There are also animals, machines, and rare visitors that move like weather. But the most persistent resident of the plateau is not a person at all. It is the city itself: a cultivated plant-body, a civic organism, a living municipal anatomy made of root, moss, bark, resin, copper, steam, and memory. The humans who dwell within it are citizens, workers, and caretakers of that body; the body, in turn, is the host, architecture, and perhaps something like a slow and alien mind. To live on the plateau is to belong to a place that has stopped pretending to be merely terrain.

## The human residents

The people who live here are not forest people. That would be too simple. They are not hunters moving softly beneath trees, nor gatherers reading the season, nor primitives living lightly upon a generous earth. The earth here is not generous in any ordinary way; it is organized, industrial, and strange.

They are city people.

They live in districts, streets, and terraces. They work in factories, warehouses, reservoir offices, and public squares. They pay attention to maintenance schedules, cable tension, pressure readings, and municipal boundaries. Their lives are shaped less by wilderness than by civic infrastructure—only this infrastructure happens to breathe.

They dress for the plateau as one dresses for a cold, damp machine-shop: thick wool, oilskin, canvas, gloves that are never quite dry, boots worn soft at the toes but strong at the heel. Goggles hang from collars or rest over brow-bands. Many have brass counters, gauges, shears, or cutting-hooks attached to their belts, not as decoration but as tools of daily negotiation with the living city. Their hands are marked by resin, oxide, cold wetness, and small punctures where copper wire has slipped or a stem has burst. Their clothes smell faintly of the same things that the city itself smells of: wet bark, metal oxide, steam, cold stone, and that low sweetness of fruit stored too long.

They are usually quiet.

The plateau is noisy, but it is noisy with machinery. That leaves little room for shouting. Their speech is often flat, patient, and technical. They speak to one another in terms of water, root-pressure, valve timing, dew-load, and structural movement. A mother may call a child in with a whistle that mimics a condenser release. Workers greet one another by checking the sound of a nearby engine and nodding if it is right. Their language has many names for modes of growth: the kind of swelling that means a house is safe to occupy; the kind that means it must be opened and vented; the kind that means a road needs reweaving; the kind that means a district has forgotten its shape.

The human population is not dense. The city is spacious, separated by long living green distances that are not emptiness but deliberate room. Households cluster in pod-halls, seed-chambers, and rounded stem-floors. Families live in bulbous dwellings whose inner walls contract faintly in the cold. Children learn early not to fear the small movements of architecture. A child who wakes and finds the door a half-inch out of true does not assume something has been broken. The child waits, listens to the root-creak, and tells an adult. The adult notes it, adjusts the latch, and reports it to a district warden if it persists. A door that moves is not a haunting. It is a public works matter.

They have the habit of attention.

They are always listening to the city: to the low groan of root-roads under wheel-weight, to the hydraulic pulse from below, to the hiss of water in condenser coils, to the soft popping of drying resin, to the creak of lignin adjusting to humidity. Experienced residents can distinguish a worker’s hammer from a growth-node splitting. A child can recognize the particular whistle of the district engine-hall from three terraces away. An old person knows by the way the moss swells near their feet whether underground pressure is building.

This attentiveness makes them careful, not nervous. They do not live in fear of the place, but in conversation with it.

## Their work

Their labor is maintenance, cultivation, construction, and repair—but each of those terms has to be stretched.

To build is to train. The plateau does not permit crude erection. A house is not assembled; it is coaxed into habit. A wall is not laid; it is folded, bound, watered, fed, and kept from growing in a direction that would crush it. A bridge-road is not poured; it is cultivated as a hardened root-lattice and polished by traffic until it can bear weight. Workers do not simply put things in place; they persuade living material to accept municipal forms.

There are pod-wrights who shape bulb-houses, seed-dwellings, and chamber-frames. There are root-lattice tenders who maintain roads and platforms so that they do not soften into useless moss or harden into brittle failure. There are condenser-wrights who install brass cups, leaf-channels, and cold coils, ensuring that the city catches whatever water the sky and plants are willing to surrender. There are cable-wardens who check the tension of wires running between seed-poles, listening for the particular wrongness in a cable before it snaps or sags. There are engine-tenders whose hands are black not only with oil but with plant residue, because their engines sit in pith, bark, and living wood. There are market-keepers who arrange goods on slabs of hardened pith: bolts of fabric, coils of copper, jars of resin, dried fruit, machine parts, small caged condensation units. There are clearing-guards who keep the public terraces open, binding the moss surface down, preventing growth from reclaiming spaces that must remain city.

Their factories are not merely industrial; they are agricultural-industrial. Some workshops grind tubers into starches. Some refine resin. Some weave fibers into cordage, cloth, insulation, or building skins. Others work metal with the precision of plant-gardeners, shaping brass bands and iron needles that do not merely fit a machine but fit a living stem. Electricity here is not harvested by a simple generator. It is tapped. Copper combs are pressed into damp bark. Iron needles are inserted with exactitude into vascular tissue. The current is weak, strange, and slow, but it can be guided. The people do not treat this as a miracle; they treat it as a trade skill with many rules, the way one might treat plumbing or navigation.

They have markets, but these are not bustling bazaars in the animal sense. They are orderly arrangements in public spaces that have been protected from growth. Sellers speak plainly. Buyers inspect. The air carries steam and metal and damp leaf. Goods are placed on pith slabs and under stitched leaf-cloth. Children pass through between adult legs. A single cart may move across a broad green plaza, drawn not by beast of burden—there are too few such burdens—but by counterweighted cable and a small steam drum. The cart is part of the city’s rhythm as much as it is part of commerce.

Their food is mostly grown, pressed, stored, and transformed. Tubers swollen with starch are boiled, dried, mashed, or fermented. Pale stems are sliced and prepared before they toughen. Lichens and mosses are not eaten carelessly, but certain preparations exist. There are orchards in public squares, not for abundance but for order: fruit that ripens into sweetness, then into memory. They preserve much. Their kitchens smell of steam, resin, sweet rot, and boiled mineral water. They are not gourmands. They are citizens of a place where eating is also municipal survival.

## The plant city as inhabitant

If the human workers are the mobile organs of this place, then the living plant-body is the body proper.

The city is not a collection of plants. It is a civic organism. The moss is not simply moss; it is continuous ground-tissue, felt and sponge, the plateau’s skin. The root-mat is not just roots; it is foundation, road-base, water-channel, structural web, and archive. The lichen plates are not decoration; they are armor against wind and frost, waterproofing for some surfaces, memory-pads for others. The pale stems that rise like architecture are not wild growth; they are cultivated urban pillars, seed-poles, cable supports, breathing chambers. The broad hollows between ridges are not accidents; they are basins, reservoirs, factory sites, gathering spaces, and storage pits.

This organism lives.

It grows new layers over old layers. Foundations sink into it. Cellars become hollow trunks. Engine-rooms become burls still running gears. It thickens, hardens, softens, contracts, and shifts. It carries weak voltages in its damp tissues. It responds to pressure, wind, heat, frost, and human labor. It is not sentient in the way a human mind is sentient. It has no voice, no face, no personal want that one can imagine. But it is not inert either. It has habits. It has tendencies. It has civic behavior.

It disciplines itself.

The plateau has been eroded into near-finality. What remains is not raw nature but a world that has learned structure. The plants here have been trained, selected, cultivated, pruned, fed, and retrained for generations so long that the distinction between “plant” and “infrastructure” has thinned. The city’s vegetation behaves as though it has a constitution. It keeps roads hardened where they must be walked. It grows walls dense where wind must be stopped. It leaves clearings bare where movement must be open. It gathers water where the basin dips. It swells into rooms where bodies must sleep. It does not do these things in a decorative way. It does them as one would do them in a city that has stopped distinguishing between construction and ecology.

And, because that city is old beyond ordinary accounting, it has begun to behave like a mind that thinks through infrastructure.

This is where the people live with uncertainty without becoming superstitious. A road may enter an arch and afterward seem interior rather than exterior. A patch of moss may grow into the pattern of a ceiling instead of a floor, as if the city has mislabeled its spatial categories. A building may shift by inches overnight, not violently, not with a crash, but with the patience of something reorganizing furniture. These are not anomalies to them. They are civic fluctuations. The people adjust. They redraw routes in their heads. They place new stones at familiar thresholds. They leave small marks for those who know how to read them. The city expects it.

The pools are part of this living intelligence.

They are not merely standing water. They are cups held in the body of the plateau, catching rain, condensation, and sometimes old weather. They hold rain clearly, and sometimes when disturbed they exhale the scent of a river from a different year. They reflect imperfectly: a worker steps away, and for a moment the pool keeps the figure still standing there. In the evening, some pools show stars a minute before the stars appear. These are not spirits in any easy sense, nor are they simply optical effects. They are habits of matter in a place so saturated with life and labor that memory has become environmental. The people live beside these pools as one might live beside a neighbor with a strange way of remembering things: politely, warily, without making a spectacle of it.

There are those among them who are especially attentive to such things. Water-readers. Reflection-tenders. Label-keepers. They do not worship the uncanny. They file reports on it. When steam from a vent forms an old industrial sign or the name of a machine no longer installed, they write the word down. When a factory begins to run from stored weather rather than fresh rain, they check the condenser-coils and adjust the intake. When a tuber-vat ferments with the scent of a previous harvest rather than the present one, they separate it from public distribution. They are not priests. They are municipal technicians dealing with materials whose rules include strange exceptions.

## The machines as quasi-citizens

Machines do not live in the way humans live, nor in the way the plant-body lives. But on this plateau they are too thoroughly integrated to be considered merely dead apparatus.

Engines sit inside pith and bark. Their heat warms surrounding tissue. Their vibrations travel through roots. Their exhaust mingles with plant moisture. Steam rises from vents in ways that become familiar: the city breathes out through them. Hydraulic rams driven by pressure from below do not feel like external devices; they feel like muscular extensions of the ground. Cable-ways strung between seed-poles resemble tendons. Copper pipes threaded through bark resemble veins. Brass fittings and glass panels are not inserted into the city so much as joined to it.

The workers speak of engines as one might speak of large animals: cautiously, without unnecessary emotion, but with respect for appetite and temperament. Some engines are stubborn. Some require feeding with particular mixtures of resin and water. Some run smoothly only when surrounding wood has reached a certain humidity. A machine may be “too hungry” in cold weather, “restless” after pressure changes, or “settled” when its sound has become a steady pulse. These are not metaphors, or not only metaphors. They are ways of describing the relationship between mechanical and organic parts in a city that has merged them.

At noon the machines are loud and purposeful. In the evening their sound changes. It becomes less like labor and more like heartbeat. The city settles. The roots tighten. The human workers move through engine-halls with the relaxed attentiveness of people who have never known another music. They do not live in a place where nature was tamed into stillness. They live in a place where both nature and machine were trained into a single civic pulse.

## The rare animals

Fauna is thin here.

The plateau does not support the animal-richness one might expect from a damp and vegetated world. There is no abundance of songbirds, no herds moving through moss, no predators haunting the edges. Most of the ecological roles that animals would ordinarily occupy have been absorbed by the plant-city and by human labor. This gives the place a strangely domesticated loneliness.

The animals that do exist live like rumors.

Pale deer with oversized ears appear on the high rim of the basin, watching the city from a distance before dissolving into the moss. They are not hunted with enthusiasm, or perhaps they are rarely encountered enough to hunt. Children may be taught to point them out, but adults usually do not stare. The deer are not considered omens, exactly, but they are noticed. They seem to belong to the older mountain country outside the city’s civic order.

In the reservoir hollows there are rare herons, almost the color of old paper, standing perfectly still beside the pockets of water. They look like clerical beings, pale secretaries of an office no one fully uses. They watch the human workers with a patience that may simply be hunger, but which resembles official scrutiny. They do not flee quickly. They seem to belong to the pools as the pools belong to the city.

In the engine-tunnels live small white creatures—mice, perhaps, or something older and stranger. They feed on spilled starches and warm oil. They are pests, but tolerated pests. To exterminate them entirely would be to disturb some small circuit of the city’s body. They know the warm places. They know the places where machines leak sweetness. Children leave crumbs near vents and then are told not to make a habit of it. The mice are the closest thing to pets in the city, though they are not pets.

Above the factory roofs at noon, a single kind of moth drifts from heat source to heat source with broad copper wings. It is never many at once. It passes through steam and sunlight like a moving label. It may pollinate some of the orchard-square plants. It may simply be drawn to heat. Either way, it is one of the few flying creatures that seems accepted as part of the urban order. Its copper color gives it the air of a living fitting, a biological bolt.

Birds are scarce. A feather caught in wire is remarkable. A print in wet moss that vanishes after one step is almost more noticeable than an animal present. The absence is not frightening. It is simply part of the city’s composition. The plateau is not alive with wildlife; it is alive with structure.

## The children and the social life

Children live in this place with an ease that disturbs outsiders, if outsiders exist. They are born into a world where floors breathe, roads shift slightly, and steam spells old words. They learn the difference between a wall expanding from moisture and a house adjusting to pressure before they can read. They learn that not every change means danger. Some changes mean the city is settling into its civic role.

They have games that involve timing.

They run along root-roadways before the moss contracts. They slide down hardened bark chutes. They press ears to living walls to hear where the water is moving below. They collect shed pieces of leaf-canvas, copper filings, and resin chips and trade them like ordinary valuables. They know which pools can be looked into safely and which reflections to ignore. They are taught that a pool showing stars early is not an invitation to stare too long. Not because the pool is evil, but because the place has manners, and manners exist in relation to forces that do not need to be provoked.

Social life is not crowded. The intervals between dwellings are wide. Visiting requires travel along root-paths through green emptiness. Friends meet at market squares, factory clearings, or condensation terraces. They stand in circles and talk while the surrounding city breathes faintly around them. Families occupy pod-halls, sometimes two or three households in one grown complex, divided by bark partitions that must not be nailed too deeply. Marriages may be arranged or casual; the city itself seems indifferent to human romance, except insofar as it provides shelter for it. The houses expand and contract with seasons, and so, perhaps, do the people.

Their customs are practical but not graceless. They mark territory not by monuments of stone but by the trained shape of growth. A family may have a particular root-road pattern, a favored seed-pole, a known hollow where it stores oil. A district may keep a public orchard-square as a shared civic possession, not because the fruit is valuable but because the clearing itself is a social achievement. To cut back moss is a form of citizenship. To bind the surface down is an act of public devotion.

There is also humor, though it is dry.

Workers joke about houses that move. They tease one another about mistaking a shifting door for a prank, or about leaving a kettle on a wall that later contracts. They name stubborn engines after old wardens. They say a district is “thinking too hard” when too many buildings shift at once. They speak of the city as if it were an elderly official: respected, slow, occasionally irrational, but ultimately reliable.

## The uncanny citizens

Not everything that lives on the plateau is a person, animal, plant, or machine. Some inhabitants are processes, residues, and irregularities so regular that they approach behavior.

Old weather is stored in condenser-coils. A factory may run not only on fuel and water but on captured atmospheric memory: a storm from one season, a cold snap from another, the particular dryness of a vanished year. These patterns are not emotions, but they act like habits once released into machinery. When such weather is fed into a city-system, it may bring scents, temperatures, sounds, or moods not currently present in the sky. A workshop may become suddenly damp with a rain that fell years ago. A market may feel warm under a bright cold day because the coil above it holds summer.

Harvest memories are kept in tuber-vats. A stored harvest can carry the chemical and structural trace of a previous season. If this is processed incorrectly, it may affect not only bread and starch but perception. People who eat from such vats may dream in unfamiliar patterns, or wake with the knowledge of a field’s condition from a year no longer there. The city does not forbid this. It regulates it. There are licensed vats, quarantined vats, memorial vats whose contents are never sold. Some are kept for civic identity. Some are kept for mourning. Some are kept because they still run better than anything newly made.

Reflections lag. Steam forms words. Pools hold rain before the rain begins. These things are inhabited not by ghosts but by the plateau’s own thickened memory. They are citizens in a weak sense: not persons, not subjects, but persistent patterns with presence. They have effects. They are accounted for. They make their way into schedules.

If an outsider asks what lives there, the answer cannot stop at “people.” It must include the strange civic weather, the remembered seasons, the reflections that stand too long, the steam-labels of disappeared machines. These are not decorations. They are part of the city’s population.

## What it means to be alive there

To live on the plateau is to accept that the environment is not merely around you. It is with you, around you, and partly you.

The workers do not think of themselves as conquering nature. Nor do they think of themselves as being crushed by it. They live in a place where the boundary between human construction and organic growth has been worn away to near-finality. Their houses breathe, so they learn patience with architecture. Their machines are partly vegetal, so they learn to listen with their hands. Their water remembers, so they learn not to assume that a clear pool is simple water. Their streets sometimes behave like rooms, so they learn to think of civic space as provisional.

They are not a happy people in the bright sense. The place is too cold, too damp, too mechanized, and too old for cheerful simplicity. But they are not a doomed people either. They have a settled, maintained, almost legal form of existence. Their harshness is systemic: chill, spacing, noise, scarcity of animal life, the constant labor of keeping the city from growing into itself. Their beauty is equally systemic: light through green vaults, moss sealing a machine’s edge, the old mountains as gentle frames, the city answering a distant whistle with pipes and steam and movement.

They have become, over generations, a people suited to this body.

Their bodies are more aware of humidity than is ordinary. Their ears are tuned to subsonic machinery. Their hands know the difference between a plant’s useful softness and its dangerous swelling. Their eyes can read surface patterns as information: the color of resin, the tension of a root, the way dew collects on a leaf-cup. Their sleep is shaped by the contracting warmth of living walls. Their waking is shaped by hydraulic pulses. Their sense of time is not merely the movement of the sun but the rhythm of growth, pressure, condensation, and labor.

## The city answers

If you stand at the rim and look back, you might first think the place is empty.

The clusters are scattered. The green distances are long. There are few fires, no herds, no birds wheeling in flocks. From a distance it looks as if nothing much remains: worn mountains, moss basins, rounded platforms, dark knots of plant architecture, a few threads of steam, a glint of metal here and there.

But that emptiness is spacing.

The city is not cramped. It has grown into its own proportions. Its population is small relative to its area, because the living body occupies much of the space that elsewhere would be left to soil, rock, weather, and animals. The people are like cells in a large organism: necessary, active, but not the whole.

When the distant whistle sounds, the plateau trembles under it. Pipes open. Steam rises. Cableways swing. The city answers. Not with panic. Not with celebration. With maintenance.

Somewhere a valve is checked. Somewhere a child points at a pool before the rain. Somewhere a worker wipes resin from a gauge and nods. Somewhere a door shifts by an inch, and someone leaves it alone until morning.

Whoever or whatever lives there does so in layers.

The humans are the visible layer: wool and oilskin, tools and voices, families and labor, breath fogging at engine-mouths.

The plants and moss-body are the deeper layer: root-roads, breathing houses, walls, clearings, reservoirs, factories, and remembered seasons.

The machines are the interwoven layer: brass, copper, steam, electricity, hydraulic pulse, and noise.

The animals are the thin, rumor-layer: pale deer, paper herons, white tunnel-creatures, copper moths.

The uncanny phenomena are the boundary-layer: lagging reflections, steam-words, pools with old scents, rain that appears before it begins.

Together they form a single civic organism.

They are not a tribe, not a colony, not a beast, not a machine. They are a plateau that has finished eroding and begun to live in organized form. They are a city that grew instead of being built. They are a people who dwell within that growth, tending it as a body, listening to it as a street, and answering it as home.
