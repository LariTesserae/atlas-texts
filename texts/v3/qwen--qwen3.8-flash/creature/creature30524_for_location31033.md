Storms may spend only a few minutes over one basin and still alter an animal year. In the Thrumveld, life has learned to treat the smell of wet dust as the opening of a door. The inhabitants do not so much build themselves into the land as the land builds them into itself: low, threaded, patient, mostly quiet, and always listening through their feet.

The first citizens are not animals at all. They are the mat and everything the mat is made of. The thread-mosses, sun-lichens, creeping rosettes, and pale cushions are living populations with their own weather, seasons, wounds, and recoveries. A stone is never merely a stone. It is a roof, a reservoir, a nursery, a trading post, a grave for the wind. Fungal threads run beneath the visible skin in silver-black nets, stitching dead root to live root, dead stem to living stone, carrying minerals and moisture like slow blood. The solitary trees, if they are trees and not simply tall knots of fibrous growth, are less forest than punctuation. One stands on a rise for a mile, then the next stands alone over a hollow as if guarding a secret too small for anyone else to understand.

Above this living skin move the tiny multitudes.

At dawn, when the heat has not yet drunk the dew, the plateau hums with small bodies. Dust fleas climb the hollow stems and drink from the inner lip. Salt ants carry grains of mineral dust in their jaws, building pale trails across the mat like old veins. Capillary beetles have legs furred with fine setae that wick moisture from the air into their mouths before the sun can claim it. Lace-winged seed gnats emerge in brief clouds when rain is near, so that a column of water moving far to the east may first be known not by its darkness but by its shimmer: a thin, trembling smoke rising from the mat as though the ground were breathing out insects. Glass spiders, small and almost invisible, stretch silk across grass arches and stone lips to catch fog. When the fog withdraws, their webs leave a single bright bead at every junction, and those beads are drunk by beetles, lizards, and the young of larger animals that cannot yet reach deep moisture.

The threadworms are among the quiet builders. They spin across the mat, binding loose dust and fallen stems, creating tiny domes over burrows, bridges over cracks, and soft shelves beneath stones. They are not admired, and no one names them grandly, but the Thrumveld would fail without them. They make the surface continuous. They make it possible for larger things to travel.

Then come the herd creatures.

The wide plain has large grazers because abundance is terrible and slow. They are often called threadbacks, though that is a traveler’s name and not a proper taxon. The threadbacks are broad-bodied, low to the ground, and built like living mounds. Their shoulders rise in gentle humps. Their necks are not long, but their heads are flexible and wide-mouthed, with lips that seem half root, half tongue. Their feet are not hooves but four splayed toes, padded and ringed with coarse hair that spreads their weight across the mat. A threadback can step onto a dome of root-fiber and cushion-grass without breaking it. Its gait is rolling, deliberate, almost ceremonial. They move as if the ground were a floor that might crack if they stomped too loudly.

Their fur is sparse, wiry, and pale as dried stem, but along the back it thickens into a frayed ridge that blends with the surrounding mat when they lie down. Calves especially seem not to be animals but irregularities in the green-brown texture of the land. Females and young travel in bands called ropes, because in dry seasons the herds stretch into single files between basins, following hidden moisture and the faintest rumor of rain. The ropes can last for days, passing one by one over a low ridge like a dark thread being pulled through cloth. In the high season, after storms have soaked the shallow ground, the ropes loosen. The herds fan across a basin, their necks lowered, their lips working over the mats in a constant whisper of fiber.

Threadbacks feed by taking stems, leaves, and dead cushions, but they rarely cut roots. Their mouths have learned what human teeth would destroy. They bite above the node and leave the plant’s living skin intact. Their gut is packed with fungal colonies that break fiber into warmth and fat, and their dung, rich with lichen fragments, sprouts fast-growing seedlings within hours of rain.
