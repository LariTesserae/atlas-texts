What lives in the Sinter Lung cannot be named as a collection of creatures first. If you try to list them, you miss the way the pocket works: here life is not separated from the surface by any firm boundary, because the surface itself is alive in the broadest, strangest sense. The first inhabitants are not animals but skins. They are films. They are sheaths, threads, mats, rinds, and crusts that spread across wet glass and chalky mineral edges, occupying the spaces where heat and water argue quietly with one another. They do not grow upward toward sunlight. They grow into gradients. They are alive only where a particular narrow band of conditions occurs: not so hot that they vanish, not so cool that the chamber forgets them, not so wet that they drown in circulation, not so dry that their bodies turn to flakes.

These living films are the closest thing the Sinter Lung has to vegetation. Where the water is only warm, they form bright halos: white, pale pink, faintly yellow, sometimes the color of old cream, sometimes the color of wet chalk. Where it is hotter, the mats dull to gray-brown, orange-rust, or a blackened sulfurous stain. They do not smell like a forest floor; they smell like minerals under a fever—salt, sulfur, iron, wet stone, a faint medicinal sharpness in the steam. A human eye might mistake them for fungus at first because they branch, spread, and lace themselves into cracks. But if you were to look closer, the resemblance would fall apart. These are not hyphae seeking crumbs or rot. They are microbial communities, many of them made of filamentous bacteria or archaeal organisms that feed on chemistry rather than sunlight. They breathe dissolved gases, oxidize sulfur, consume hydrogen or iron, and excrete silica. Their bodies are not merely on the mineral surface; they help create it. The white threadlike growths in the fissures are bacterial sheaths encrusted with precipitated minerals. The orange mats are iron- and sulfur-feeding skins. The gray films in the cooler corners are biofilms so slow they seem geological, but they are still hungry, still copying themselves, still adjusting to the next pulse of heat.

This vegetation of chemistry is not lush. It is selective. It lives in margins. A ring forms at the edge of a brine pool where the water has thinned enough to be touched by cooler air. A patch grows along a channel where steam condenses at a steady rate. A black smear settles where gas seeps through a fracture and meets oxygen at the boundary between the chamber’s breath and its wetness. These patches are not clumped together the way plants might crowd around soil. They are spaced apart, almost deliberately, separated by clean obsidian, bare glass ledges, and open vapor. The Sinter Lung has a sparse ecology, and what life it supports is arranged by heat, not by soil.

Within those mineral skins there are smaller living things, so small that they are not noticed by the visitor’s first glance. There are nematode-like threads moving through the biofilm, water-bear shapes crawling slowly over droplets, protozoa drifting in the thin water that never fully stands and never fully dries. The mat is not just a surface. It is a crowded, microscopic landscape. The grazers that move across it are too small to see unless you know where to look, but they are present: pale, blind, patient, living their entire generations in a single inch of wet mineral. They do not need space. They need only the boundary where heat has not yet killed the water.

Then, among these almost invisible tenants, there are the animals that a person might actually see when crouched at the lip of the chamber: a few ghostly, eyeless creatures moving near the rims of the pools. They are the Sinter Lung’s rare fauna, and their rarity is part of their character. There are never many. There is no population in the ordinary sense, no herd, no school, no bustling colony. Perhaps a handful of individuals, scattered across the chamber like fragments of a broken promise. If you counted them twice, you might find one missing. If one were crushed by a falling shard, the chamber would not mourn, but the lineage would lose a portion of its future. They are not abundant because abundance has never been possible. They are remnants, or perhaps late arrivals, or perhaps the last few members of a small branch of life that learned to exist only in this hot, wet, closed pocket.

They look like the descendants of some brine-dwelling creature that wandered into a cooling fissure and never found a way out. Their bodies are pale to the point of translucence. They have no green, no brown, no protective coloration, because there is no background against which color matters. The light here is diffuse and overexposed, bright enough to erase the usefulness of pattern. Their shells are thin and glassy, with a faint opaline sheen when a droplet catches the steam-light. They are jointed, but not in the obvious way of an insect; the joints are delicate, almost too fine to belong to such a hostile place. They resemble cave-dwelling amphipods or isopods, yet more primitive, more uncertain, as if evolution tried them in this narrow room and is still deciding whether they will succeed.

They have no eyes. Not merely reduced eyes, not merely buried eyes, but the complete absence of any organ that would try to interpret this chamber as an image. Light here is not information about distance or shape; it is a condition of the air. It comes from the wet walls, the scalding surfaces, the vapor lenses, the faint glow of heated minerals. It does not make a picture. So the creatures do not look. They feel. Their heads are smooth, domed, unmarked by facets. At the front and along the sides, however, there are sensory hairs and heat-sensitive pits, each one a tiny antenna tuned to the chamber’s moods. They navigate by temperature, by humidity, by the chemical taste of steam, by the pressure changes that precede a vent’s pop. To them, the Sinter Lung is not a room of bright surfaces. It is a map of warm places, wet places, safe seams, lethal ledges, and food edges.

Their movements are quick, but the quickness is nervous, not playful. They cross the sinter rims in small bursts, pausing as if listening to the heat through the soles of their legs. They are always aware of the water film. Too little moisture and their shells begin to stiffen. Too much heat and the very air becomes a threat. They move between the wetness and the hot seams like creatures living inside a fever’s pause. When a pressure change rumbles through the rock and the chamber breathes harder, they scatter. One might retreat into a capillary channel too narrow for a fingertip. Another might flatten itself against a underside ledge, letting steam flow over its back as if testing whether it is still alive.

They do not hunt. There are no visible predators, no jaws with the purpose of capturing. Their ecology is grazing at the smallest possible scale. They sip from films of mineral water. They scrape microbial crusts with delicate mouth parts. They filter suspended precipitates. They consume the living skins of the chamber and in turn leave behind fine pellets that the mineral water carries into tiny crevices, where other microbes will dissolve them and begin again. Their bodies are therefore not only animals; they are carriers of chemistry. A dead grazer is not food for a larger creature, because there is none. It becomes substrate. Bacteria colonize it. Minerals begin to encrust it. If you were to find a pale corpse under a ledge, you might see white silica threads growing out from the joints, as if the chamber were slowly absorbing the body into its own skin. In the Sinter Lung, death is not a disappearance; it is a return to the wet mineral cycle.
