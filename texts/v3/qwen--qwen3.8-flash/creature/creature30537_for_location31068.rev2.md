What lives in the Terrace of the Upper Rain cannot be answered by counting heads, because heads are there only in part. Most of the life is without faces: skins that flow, filaments that bind, shells that settle, roots that thicken into walls, currents that act like breath through an enormous and patient body. If one insists on naming them as inhabitants, it is better to say that the Terrace is inhabited by relations. Everything there is a link between something else. The fish are a current of silvered feeding. The grazers are slow sculptors. The eels are threads of pruning. The shell-bed is a mortar moving on tiny feet. The great callers are hills of sound. The plants are the whole unfinished sentence that all the other living things have spent millennia completing.

There is no king there, no builder, no elder race, no hidden civilization with doors and tombs. There is no one who knows the Terrace is called the Terrace of the Upper Rain, though perhaps the name would be the least strange of all the things it would hear, if it had a hearing at all. The inhabitants live without knowing they live in a city, because to them the city is not a city. It is simply water, surface, pressure, edge, food, channel, chamber, and the endless falling above.

At the very top, immediately beneath the rain, there is first a life of skins and bladders. These are the surface organisms, the ones that live in the hammered ceiling of water. They are not plants in the same way as the column-stems below, though they are green or blue-green, faintly glowing, and made of the same slow mineral patience. They are flat, broad, translucent things, veined like wet paper, floating just under the surface film with tiny root-tendrils dangling into the dimness. Some resemble floating discs. Some resemble half-open hands. Some resemble nothing at all but a smudge of cold light that can be mistaken for a patch of phosphorescence until it folds when a current passes through it.

They drink the rain. That is their first gift to the Terrace. They are the living surface that receives the falling water not as a punishment but as a continuous offering. The rain carries dust, minerals, dissolved metals, organic grit, spores from distant and lower places carried upward by cloud, and particles of stone ground to nothingness elsewhere in the world. The surface skins filter and absorb some of this. They convert it into thin tissue. They build themselves into the underside of the ceiling like living moss on a drowned roof. When they grow too heavy, their bladders deflate slightly and they sink a few inches, then are lifted again by root-tendrils that catch onto the umbrellas of the column-stems.

These surface skins are not separate from the city. They are its ceiling garden. When they are grazed by passing silver fish, their wounded edges close with a mucus that glows faintly for a time. When they die, they do not drift away as ordinary leaves would. Their tissue softens into a fine haze, and their mineral fragments settle into the water column, feeding the frond-hairs below, thickening the felt of the root-floor, becoming part of a wall before they have ever touched the ground. The rain kills them by hammering too many of them at once into the same shallow pool, but the Terrace uses even this death as construction material.

Below the skins, the real architecture begins to thicken. This is where the column-stems live, and though the description might call them plants, it would be more truthful to call them colonial organisms. A single column is not a single plant in the ordinary sense. It is the fused remains of many generations. Its lower trunk is plant-bone, plant-stone, mineralized and living. Its outer bark is a crust of overlapping tissues. Its inner chambers are damp tunnels left behind by older shoots. Its crown is a translucent membrane or umbrella of fronds, some thin as fish-skin, some thick as the lid of a submerged pot. Each of these parts is inhabited.

The columns have a pulse. It is slow, almost imperceptible: a thickening and loosening of fluid pressure, a movement of sap and dissolved salts through channels bored or grown into the stem. If the water current pushes against one side of a young column, the column does not “decide” anything. It simply lays down denser tissue in the place where strain accumulates. If a hollow is blocked by debris, root-hairs grow into the blockage and dissolve, pull, or divert around it. If a wall is grazed too often by passing animals, the stem grows a smoother, harder surface, sometimes with a slight ridge that later becomes a handhold for shell-crawlers. If a chamber is opened inside the stem, it may be sealed after a season or two by new growth, unless some creature keeps it clear. In this way the columns are alive in a manner almost too gradual to watch. Their lives are not events but patterns.

There are also smaller living things that make the columns more than mere structure. Inside the hollow stems, there are blind worms, translucent as glass, that feed on biofilm and dead tissue. They line the inner walls with a glossy secretion that makes water move faster through the stem. In their presence, the chambers become resonance pipes. Their deaths become calcium deposits. Their young hatch as microscopic threads and drift through openings in the walls, sometimes carried from one column to another. No eye would know they are there unless it saw the faint shimmer inside an arch and mistook it for a trick of the glow.

The most visible inhabitants of the lower architecture are the silver clouds. These are fish, though they do not behave as individual fish might elsewhere. They are dense, pale, and numerous, with eyes large and white in the dim water. Their bodies are slender, flattened laterally, some of them shaped like leaves, some like needles, some like small axes. They move through the root-lanes in masses that change direction not because of a leader but because of a shared pressure sense, a lateral-line sensitivity so finely tuned that a school can read the motion of another school from far away.

These silver clouds are not only swimmers. They are the Terrace’s circulating blood. They carry life from chamber to chamber, lane to lane, wall to wall. They feed on suspended particles, on the surface skins when the water is close to the ceiling, on frond-hairs, on the microscopic life that clings to every edge. They graze the columns just enough to keep them from growing too wild. They scatter spores with their fins, sometimes intentionally, sometimes not. Their eggs are tiny, sticky, and pale, released in bursts near curtain-roots where currents are narrow and safe. The larvae hatch as transparent threads and live for weeks among root-hairs before descending into the open lanes.

Some of the fish are specialized beyond the general cloud. There are flat, dark-scaled cleaners that cling to the undersides of large arches and scrape the frond-hairs clean of mineral fuzz. There are long, almost eel-fish, with mouths like tubes, that hover in the open water and draw suspended motes into their throats. There are tiny fish that live entirely inside hollow stems, so rare that they are more often known by the faint glimmering in a chamber than by sight. They have lost their eyes and grown sensitive head-fronds. They feed only on the biofilm and on the bodies of small organisms trapped in the stem’s inner currents. They are as much a part of the stem’s inner life as sap.

The broad grazers are larger, slower, and more obviously architectural. They live between the columns like animals moving through the nave of a vast cathedral. Their bodies are wide, flattened, armored in flexible plates, with fins that ripple as though wet leaves are being turned over by an unseen hand. Their eyes are large but dull in color; they do not see sharply, but they feel water displacement with extraordinary delicacy. A change in current from a turning column, the passage of a predator far above, the vibration of a call from one of the hill-like wanderers—all of these are known to the grazers before any human eye could interpret them.

The grazers are the Terrace’s slow lawnmowers. They feed on low fronds, root-hair curtains, the soft edges of growing stems, and the felt-like mats near the floor. They do not eat everything. Their mouths are shaped to crop only where the tissue is young and mineral-rich, which is itself a form of construction. By eating the tender edges of stems, they prevent the columns from filling the open water with too much growth. By biting at regular intervals, they create rounded forms, channels, and ledges that later harden into architecture. Where they pause to rest, their bodies smooth the floor into shallow depressions. Where their fins brush a wall, the wall develops a polished curve. They are not builders, but no builder could have made the Terrace more efficiently.

Their young are born clinging to the undersides of the parents’ plates, riding for days or weeks before dropping into the root-felt. These juveniles are pale and almost transparent, shaped like tiny leaves with dark eyes. They do not immediately join the grazing herds. They live first in nursery pockets—shallow cavities formed by overlapping plates in the sides of stems. There, the water is calm, and the walls are covered with a soft, living pelt that shelters them. They feed on microscopic growths and on the waste of adult grazers. After they have hardened and learned the shape of the place, they fall into a slow herd life, moving through the columns in lanes that seem to have been made for them, though the lanes were actually made by their ancestors passing through the same place again and again.

There are also animals that live upon the grazers themselves. Small translucent crustaceans cling to the edges of their plates, filtering from the water as the grazer moves. Moss-like plants grow along their backs, not attached to the soil but to the body, feeding on the minerals carried by the grazer’s passage and by the water that flows over them. A mature grazer may therefore resemble a slow island. When it dies, it becomes more. Its armor sinks into a shallow basin or is carried into a chamber. Rootlets climb it. Mineral crusts settle on it. Within generations, it is no longer a carcass but a rounded mound, a bench, a buttress, a small hill in the architecture, overgrown with living fronds. Death does not interrupt the Terrace. Death is one of the materials it uses.

The eel-like creatures are less visible than the grazers but more constant in their shaping. They live along the walls, in the seams of roots, in the gaps between overlapping plates, in the narrow channels the water has worn. Their bodies are dark, slick, and flexible, with long fins running along their sides and a ridge of sensitive frills around their heads. They do not swim like open-water fish. They slip. They press. They bend around a column’s curve as though the curve has been waiting for them.

These eels are the Terrace’s tailors and plumbers. They crawl through openings too small for larger animals and keep them from silting. They eat dead tissue, small worms, and the eggs of fish that have fallen too near a wall. They are predators, but their predation is also maintenance. When one passes through a root-channel, it leaves behind a thin mucus that helps the channel retain its shape. When it digs into a soft seam, it disturbs mineral layers and stimulates nearby growth. Its burrow becomes a tube; its tube becomes a resonating chamber; its chamber becomes a place where larger currents hum. Some of these eels are solitary, moving with deliberate turns from one hollow to another. Others form narrow processions through wall-tunnels, one after another, in patterns that appear almost ritualized but are probably only the result of current and shelter.

Near the floor live the small shelled animals. They are not one kind. Some are mollusk-like, some crustacean-like, some so slow and flat that they might be mistaken for fragments of bone until they move their feathery legs. They crawl across the root-felt in processions, lines, and clusters. Their shells are thin, white, and often faintly ribbed. They feed by grazing the living mat, by filtering particles from the water that passes over them, by scraping mineral deposits from root surfaces. Their movement polishes the floor. Their waste binds it. Their shed shells fill it.

These small animals create the paved appearance of the root-floor without ever intending to pave anything. A line of shells accumulates in a current, and mineral growth cements them together. A cluster of shells dies, and their white remains become a patch of hard ground. A crawling swarm moves slowly around the base of a column, and their passage erodes a shallow trench. After centuries, that trench becomes a gutter; after millennia, it becomes a channel; after enough generations of shell and plant and current, it becomes a street. The white seams that look like deliberate masonry are only the corpses and dwellings of countless small lives layered with care by the indifference of time.

Some of the shelled animals also act as living mortar. When they die, their bodies are drawn into the crevices of root-tissue by mineral-rich water. They calcify around root-hairs and help seal the places where water would otherwise erode too quickly. In this way, they thicken arches. In this way, they create buttresses. No one has ever placed a block beside another block, and yet the blocks appear. No one has ever laid a foundation, and yet the foundations are everywhere, composed of old shells, old roots, old waste, and old water.

The rarest inhabitants are the large, slow, armored wanderers. They move through the broadest channels, bodies so massive that the water seems to rise and deepen around them. They are covered with fused plates, moss-like growths, and root-fiber tufts. They resemble fragments of the landscape that have learned to drift. Their heads are broad and blunt; their fins are wide and fan-like, trembling with pressure. Their eyes are dim, not because they are blind but because the light they see is mostly motion: the sway of a column, the passage of a school, the slow bend of an arch under the weight of centuries.

These wanderers are the Terrace’s singers. Their bodies contain hollow chambers, airless or nearly so, resonant cavities that vibrate with low calls. The sound does not begin in a throat but in a broad chest-like space where mineral plates and soft tissue meet. A call emerges first as pressure, a wave against the flank, then as a tone too deep for ordinary ears. It travels through the water for leagues. When it reaches a column, the column rings. When it reaches an arch, the arch answers with a fainter shimmer. When it reaches the open lanes, the whole basin seems to contract and breathe.

These calls are not messages in the way human voices carry messages. They are environmental tools. They move fine particles. They encourage certain larvae to settle in the direction of the sound. They synchronize the movements of grazers by making certain lanes more or less comfortable. They keep the architecture in tune, as one might keep a set of pipes clean by sending water through it. A wanderer’s call can disturb a cloud of fish, causing them to move from one sheltering wall to another, scattering food and spores as they go. It can wake dormant eggs in root-crevices. It can cause a soft layer of mineral sediment to ripple and settle into new lines. The wanderers do not understand this. They are simply alive in a way that shapes the world around them.

Their lives are slow. A young wanderer may spend decades grazing near a nursery chamber, growing its armor in the same pattern as the chamber walls. It may live for centuries in a single valley-like lane, moving from column to column with the seasons of pressure and food. Its body becomes a mobile garden. Filament moss grows on its plates. Small shell-animal communities settle in its wake. Fish swim through the loose fronds on its back. It is not a creature living in the Terrace so much as it is a piece of the Terrace temporarily awake and walking.

When a wanderer dies, it is not a violent event. Its body does not collapse into rot in any ordinary way. The tissue becomes softer, then harder. The plates separate slowly. Rootlets enter its form. Mineral growth seals the cavities. The place where it died becomes a mound or a chamber with rounded walls, a hollow that hums differently from the other hollows. Its shell may become a wall. Its jaw may become an arch. Its fin may fossilize into a buttress. The wanderer’s death is another act of construction.

There are also invisible inhabitants, perhaps the most important of all. In the walls, in the channels, in the root-felt, in the membranes, there are bacterial communities and filamentous organisms that resemble neither plant nor animal in the familiar sense. They are the quiet workers who make the Terrace look complete. They digest dead tissue. They bind mineral grains. They produce the faint blue-green phosphorescence that makes the dimness visible. They regulate the chemistry of the water so that the plants grow dense rather than soft, slow rather than sprawling. They prevent decay in the ordinary sense by converting death into another layer of structure.

Some of these unseen organisms live inside the translucent membranes of the column-umbrellas. There, they feed on light and mineral dust, and their glow is used by larger life as a signal, though no one has made the signal on purpose. Fish gather where the glow is brightest, because the bright membranes are the places where suspended food particles are densest. Grazers move toward patches that are too dark, because dark places are softer and easier to crop. Eels prefer seams where the glow is faint and broken, because those seams are warm with microbial activity and rich with tiny prey. The whole ecology is therefore guided by a light that is not illumination in any human sense, but a chemistry visible to bodies built for such darkness.

Inside the deeper hollows, there are chambers so enclosed that they are almost separate worlds. The water there may move very slowly, almost in secret. The walls are lined with soft living matter, a velvet of root-tissue, bacterial film, and tiny organisms that feed on it. Some of these organisms are blind, colorless, and delicate, with long filaments for sensing pressure. They have no need for the open lanes and have perhaps lived in those same chambers for generations so long that the chamber’s shape has changed around them. A crack has become a hall. A hole has become a cathedral. A small pocket has become a nursery, then a tomb, then a wall. Nothing in such a place is final, except the habit.

There are also the spaces between the animals, the spaces not made by bodies but by absence. These are inhabited too. The current itself has a kind of life, shaped by the built environment so thoroughly that it functions like an animal organ. In the open lanes, it moves in broad, slow rivers. At the edge of a column, it curls into spirals. In a root-tunnel, it hisses through a narrow throat. In a domed chamber, it pulses and settles in a rhythm that is almost breathing. These flows carry food, eggs, larvae, and mineral particles. They decide which plants will grow in one place and which will not. They tell the animals where shelter is. The current is not alive, but in the Terrace it behaves as if it has a memory.

If one tried to define the inhabitants, one might be tempted to say: the fish, the grazers, the eels, the shell-creatures, the wanderers, the plants. But that would still be too few names. There are cleaner-fish living in the gill-folds of the broad grazers. There are tiny parasites that ride the flanks of the silver clouds, feeding on mucus, kept in check by the eels. There are drifting organisms that resemble jellyfish without bells, floating in narrow curtains, feeding by touch. There are mineral-dwelling worms that bore into calcite seams and create holes that later become resonant chambers. There are seed-like creatures that float upward in bladders, attach to the underside of the ceiling, and spend their entire lives as fragments of the surface skin. There are organisms whose adult form is no larger than a grain of sand but whose death contributes to an arch.

Nothing in this place lives alone. That is the deepest law of the Terrace. A column without its fish is still a column, but it is not yet fully the kind of living architecture the place has become. A school without the grazers would overgrow the lanes. A grazer without its shell-crawlers would not be an island. An arch without the eels would close. A chamber without the microbes would be sterile, and there is nothing sterile here except perhaps the absence of any particular individual at any given moment. The life is distributed so thoroughly that it is difficult to decide where one organism ends and the next begins.

No one has ever carved a sign into the walls. No one has ever built a boat to cross the basin. No one has ever left a tool behind. There are no hearths, no thrones, no graves with markers, no roads with names. If a human being were to descend through the water, past the ceiling skins and into the dim architecture, there would be no reception, no warning, no curiosity. The inhabitants would not ask what it was. They would treat it as any other intrusion: current, surface, food, shelter, obstacle. If it remained long enough, its skin would be grazed by tiny organisms. If it died, its body would become a temporary island, then a mound, then a wall. Nothing would be lost. The Terrace does not tolerate waste, even if it cannot be said to intend tolerance.

But perhaps the most important inhabitants are not any of these named or countable things. They are the processes themselves: filtration, growth, erosion, mineralization, pressure, resonance, and settlement. These are the residents of the place as much as the fish or the plants. The Terrace is not merely filled with living things. It is filled by living actions. A root’s pressure is as much an inhabitant as the fish that shelter in it. The sound of a wanderer’s call is as much a resident as the creature that makes it. The white line where shells have died is as much a citizen as the shell-animal that once crawled along it.

What lives in the place, then, is a community without a center. There is no brain that knows the city. No heart that owns it. There is only a million small habits, repeated so long that they have become geography. The fish move because the lanes are made. The lanes are made because the fish move. The plants grow because the animals shape the water. The animals shape the water because the plants shape the animals. The calls of the large wanderers ring through the columns and the columns answer with their own slow tones. The floor is built of shells and root-plates. The shells are made by floor-dwellers. The floor-dwellers are fed by the water the floor shapes. The water is moved by the calls. The calls are made by bodies that are themselves grown from shells and plates. There is no first cause, no last worker, no master plan. There is only the pattern continuing.

And the pattern is not wild. It is not the disorder of a jungle or the chaos of an ocean. It is a wildness that has finished with wildness. It is a life so ancient and so integrated that it no longer struggles. The plants have hardened into patience. The animals have learned the shape of their own architecture without knowing they have learned it. The death of one generation becomes the floor of the next. The waste of one becomes the wall of another. The body of a grazer becomes a garden for moss. The moss becomes food for small creatures. The small creatures become shell. The shell becomes stair. The stair becomes chamber. The chamber becomes nursery. The nursery becomes wall. Nothing escapes. Nothing is allowed to remain merely dead.

At the highest point of the Terrace, where the rain continues its endless falling and the surface is a hammered ceiling of silver noise, there is no land to live upon. There is only the skin of organisms, the root-umbrellas beneath, the column-stems rising, the open lanes, the dark water, the slow grazers, the pale fish, the eels in their wall-tunnels, the shell-makers on the felt-floor, the rare wanderers moving through broad channels, and the countless unseen things that keep the whole thing alive. The place is full without being crowded. It is loud without being violent. It is built without being built by anyone.

If the inhabitants can be imagined all at once, they must be imagined not as a population but as a single slow act. The fish are the moving part. The eels are the threading part. The grazers are the cutting part. The shells are the binding part. The plants are the growing part. The wanderers are the tuning part. The microbes are the remembering part. The water is the carrying part. The rain is the beginning and end.

That is what lives in the Terrace of the Upper Rain: a city of mutual life, without a name that matters to it, without a ruler, without a ruin, without a final word. It lives as the forest lives, but more completely; it lives as a hive lives, but without a queen; it lives as a body lives, but without a mind; and it lives as a place lives, only if one understands that some places are not containers for life but life itself gathered into a shape so stable it can be mistaken for stone.
