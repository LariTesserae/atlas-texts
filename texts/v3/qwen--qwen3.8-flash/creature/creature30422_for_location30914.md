Not a beast, not a god—though outsiders, if any survived the roar long enough to speak of it, might mistake it for either.

It lives.

What lives there is a people so small and so fitted to the place that their presence and their absence are nearly the same thing. The mountain has no road to them. The clouds have no opinion of them. Even the crack in the basalt seems reluctant to admit that anything passes through it. There are no permanent fires, no full villages, no songs loud enough to carry beyond the next surge. But when the chamber is still—only in the false quiet between rains, only for the length of a held breath—there are signs of life moving with the storm: a hand pulling itself up onto a shelf, a bead turning its dull eye to the light, a face wiped by water so that the eyes open afterward like stones that have learned not to blink.

They are not numerous. That may be the first thing anyone who finds them will notice.

There may be five. There may be seven. There may be one, alone, doing something so quietly that the water cannot decide whether to erase it. The pocket cannot contain many, because the pocket does not contain anything for long. It is too wet for comfort, too loud for thought, too young for safety. A ledge that holds a shelter at the beginning of the hour may be gone before its smoke has dried on the wall. A shelter built in one life may be unmade in the next rain. So the people live in fractions: a few on the east ledge, one on a pumice slab, two working a crack where the mist thins, another sitting by the bone stake, listening to the rock with the heel of its palm pressed to the ground.

If they have a name for themselves, it is not a sound that survives in ordinary air.

It is closer to a breath held during the lull. The old ones can make it by tapping a chert flake against the bone stake twice, turning the head once toward the falling column, then letting the mist run over their teeth. Outsiders might call them the Short-Still, the Ledge-Born, the Drippers, the Rain-Makers. None of those names fit exactly. They do not make the rain. They make things beside it, between it, in the pauses where making is permitted.

They are human enough, but only in the way a bone is human: by shape, by structure, by the memory of warmth.

Their bodies have become narrow ledges themselves. They are lean, not fat; high-cold does not permit much fat, and the storm eats at them like a mouth that never closes. Their ribs show through damp skin. Their shoulders are broad and hard from hauling themselves up stone, from bracing against currents, from carrying water-heavy sinew or obsidian over slippery places. Their hands are cracked but strong, the fingers long, the nails thick and often blackened at the root with mineral dust. The palms have calluses grown so smooth and so layered that they can grip basalt without slipping, though every edge cuts them. Some have scars that shine like thin glass when a droplet-lens passes across them.

Their skin is pale where the rain is worst and darker where lichen crusts have settled. A pale, salt-furred whiteness forms along forearms and cheeks and the bridge of the nose, not from sun—the sun is too broken here—but from mineral water drying on skin in a thousand tiny layers. Some have patches of green-black where they have slept too long against shaded basalt. Some wear streaks of red ochre scraped from thin seams near the pocket’s upper wall, though they do not paint themselves for decoration. They paint for memory.

A red thumbprint on a wet stone will stay visible for minutes. That is enough.

Their hair is kept short, often by biting obsidian or chert held in the teeth while pulling it taut. Long hair would be caught by the rain curtains, tangled by droplets, weighted with water, and it could kill them during a surge. Those who are very old sometimes grow the hair back for the dead, letting it hang pale and wet like a thread in a seam. They do not wear caps except as emergency: small lichen mats, stretched hides, bits of antler or pumice pendants to keep the scalp dry when the air becomes too heavy to breathe.

Their faces are strange in daylight.

Because the pocket’s light is fractured by falling water, their eyes have adapted to glare rather than darkness. The pupils are large, not because the place is black, but because the brightness scatters. The whites around the irises have become stained with mineral dust, giving them a faint blue or ash-colored film. When they see clearly, they do not look at the light. They look through it, at the spaces where one falling thread does not touch another. This gives their gaze a strange, sideways quality. They seem to be reading gaps. They are.

They blink less than surface people. They can taste the difference in pressure before a droplet-stone the size of a fist bursts against a nearby ledge. If the air begins to feel thicker against the skin, they shift weight before any visible change occurs. Their heads tilt almost imperceptibly. Then a shelf disappears into a gray curtain, and only someone who had already moved is dry enough to keep working.

They are deaf in the ordinary sense.

No one speaks in this pocket and expects their words to reach another person. The roar would swallow a shout. It would swallow a scream if the scream lasted less than two syllables. So the Short-Still do not have voices the way others do. Their mouths open, yes; they breathe heavily; sometimes in pain or cold they make small animal sounds, but those are only for themselves. Communication happens through hand, body, breath, and vibration.

A child is not taught to say “mother” or “father.” It is taught to press its forehead to the stone wall until it feels a knock answer back. Later, it learns that a hand flat against a basalt shelf means stay low. That two quick taps of the fingers mean a surge is thinning. That a slow drag of the palm means the rock ahead is too porous to hold a stake. That a head turned toward the bone stake, followed by a single breath held through the nose, means the storm is listening and you should not be careless.

If two of them stand on opposite ledges, they can still talk. They do it by moving their hands in wide arcs and by throwing chert flakes, obsidian chips, or small pumice pieces into predictable patterns. A flake dropped on the left side is warning. On the right, invitation. Twice dropped, danger. Once caught between fingers and released into the mist, grief or apology. This is not casual gesture; it is a full grammar made from falling objects and air-pressure. Some of their stories begin when a chip is thrown upward into the column and does not return. Some end when it comes back altered by the storm, its edges softened, its sharpness taken away by water.

They live by rhythm, not by sun.

Above, there may be dawn or night, season or accident, but inside, time is made of cycles: the column rises; the mist thickens; the roar shifts pitch; the ledges are buried or spat out; the floor of gravel rearranges itself into new shapes. The old people say there are three times of day that matter, though the daylight shaft may not match them.

The first is the White Surge. During it, everything that can move is pinned. People cling to ledges or to themselves, because the only safe way to survive is to become as small as possible against rock. Tools are held in teeth or pressed flat under wrists. A misplaced obsidian flake will go into the current and be found a body-length lower, sometimes not at all. During the White Surge, the Short-Still are nearly invisible. Even if someone stood on a ledge across the pocket, the falling water would make them translucent, ghost-like, a dark shape wrapped in moving glass.

The second is the Thin Breath. This is when the column weakens enough that the ledges reappear, but the air still roars with the memory of weight. In the Thin Breath, work is possible only in bursts. A person may scrape moss from a ledge, set a bone pin, tie a sinew cord, or test whether a wall can be split. They must finish in the time between two large drops. If a lull is too generous, they become foolish; they stand up, open their mouths, look at the daylight shaft, let the mist run over them as if they were children again. That is dangerous. The storm punishes generosity. It returns.

The third is the False Lull. This is when the rain seems to slow, but the pocket itself hums below hearing, a pressure in the teeth and ribs. During the False Lull, the Short-Still do not work. They move to anchors: the two cairns, the bone stake, the deepest cracks behind ledges. They sit with their backs to stone and wait. Sometimes the chamber will shudder so violently that a shelf cracks into slivers. Sometimes a new bubble will burst in a wall and open a small alcove, instantly shining, instantly wet, instantly useful. The Short-Still do not celebrate new openings. They watch them the way one watches an animal approach. A new alcove may be a gift, but a gift from the storm is never free.

Their shelters are therefore humble not because they lack cleverness, but because cleverness here knows its limit.

A round shelter of basalt slabs and sinew is not meant to last. It is meant to survive one working season, maybe two, then to be taken. It is low, almost crouched, shaped like a cap pulled over a head. The walls are thick because they must stop small droplet-stones, not wind; wind cannot blow into the pocket. The roof is angled away from the column, not toward it, so that water is shed rather than gathered. A drying rack of bone pins holds nothing but moss for the simple reason that the storm keeps taking what is hung there. They have learned to hang things in sequences: first the heavy, then the light, then the almost empty. They make offerings of weight to the rain, then take back what remains dry enough to keep.

Their tools are primitive because there is no metal in the rock, no trade route through the cloud, and no fuel to smelt anything. They use what the storm leaves visible.

Obsidian flakes, chert chips, basalt mauls, bone awls, antler tines, shell fragments, sinew cords, pumice scrapers, lichen paste. They knap stone with sharp clicks that are swallowed instantly by the roar. They have learned to judge an edge by taste and touch. A tongue passed over a flake can tell them whether it is sharp enough to split pumice. A thumb dragged lightly over obsidian can tell them whether it will cut skin or cut cord. Their working sounds are not sounds to them. They are pressures in bone. The knapping of a point feels like a small bell inside the wrist. A failed strike feels like a stone dropped in the skull.

They have no fire.

This is one of the strangest facts about them, if strangeness requires facts. They cannot dry themselves by flame. They cannot warm the basalt by hearth. The water will take fire before it can be built, and the rock itself, young and glassy, cracks too easily if heated and cooled in such a place. So they use another kind of fire: the friction of survival. They make heat by rubbing basalt dust into their hands and breathing hard. They warm themselves by huddling in the narrowest cracks where the current does not pass cleanly. They share body heat in small groups, and because the roar makes them all nearly deaf, they have developed an extraordinary sensitivity to breath. A person who is dying or injured does not moan; they breathe too short or too long against the wall. A sleeping person is judged by the fog of their breath against a chert blade. A child learns to watch breath as much as hands.

The single bead of green malachite belongs to someone.

It is probably not a child’s. Children lose beads too quickly, and beads are dangerous in the pocket. A smooth object in a misty chamber can be caught by a droplet-lens and thrown like a pale seed across the room. But perhaps a child made it, because children have time during lulls that adults do not. Perhaps it is a token left by an adult who could not carry it through the next White Surge, and so set it on a ledge where the rain would know it. Green malachite is rare here, or rare enough to mean. It is the color of slow stone water, of copper stains in cracks, of something that has waited underground before being brought into light.

The bead is also a signal.

If a person touches the malachite and then places it back exactly where it was, they are saying: I was here. If they move it a finger’s width, they are saying: I saw danger. If they leave it among the chert chips on the circle floor, they are saying: the storm has begun to speak differently. It is not only jewelry. It is punctuation.

Their food is poor, and yet they are not starving because the pocket feeds them in a strange, mineral way.

They eat the storm’s leftovers.

When the rain column thins, small things appear: lichen crust lifted from porous stone by pressure, pale moss swollen with water, black flakes that have been shaken loose from ledges. They scrape and chew these, though most are bitter. In cracks where wet sand collects, they find white worms that have no eyes and taste like iron. These worms come from somewhere, or the pocket creates them in the way it creates new alcoves—without explanation. In deeper pumice skins, there are tiny beetles with carapaces like wet ash; they are edible if toasted against the warmth of a held stone. Rare birds that fly too near the crack may be stunned by the thunder and fall inward; they are cleaned on obsidian blades, their feathers stuck to the drying rack until the rack collapses.

But much of their nourishment is absorbed rather than eaten.

They lick mineral salts from ledges. They hold chert blades in their mouths until the edge leaves a thin metallic taste. They breathe mist into their lungs until it seems to make them heavy. They can survive days without real food if the water is thick enough, because the water carries the mountain into them. This is one reason they cannot leave easily. The world below their shoulder is too dry. Ordinary food from ordinary places makes their teeth ache and their stomachs turn. If one of them goes outside, even briefly, they become sick until they return and press their face to wet stone. The salt, the cold, the roar—these are their medicine. Without them, they become empty.

They drink by taste.

They have no cups because no cup can hold water for long. A cup placed on a ledge will be struck, tilted, swept away, or cracked by a droplet-stone. They drink by catching a lens of water in the air and letting it hit their tongue. They know how to find the lenses during a lull; a trained eye sees the hanging droplet as a small bright bubble. They bite it with their mouth open, and it bursts cold and mineral on the palate. They drink like that while working. This is why their mouths are often slightly open, their lips dark, their breath thin. They are drinking the room.

They reproduce slowly, and not all of them do.

There are few children because the pocket is not safe enough for many. Pregnancy is hidden by layers of lichen-cloth until the belly changes the way a woman moves. Birth happens only in the False Lull, in the deepest crack, behind the east cairn, because that is where the storm’s pressure is lowest. A child born there has never heard its own cry. The first thing it learns is vibration. It learns to move by being carried against a chest that is breathing hard. The mother or father will tap the stone once, twice, and the child will feel the knock through skin. This is the first language. A child who cannot learn this is often taken by the water.

The old ones do not call that cruelty.

They call it tuning.

When a child survives its first season, it is marked. Not with fire, because there is none. Not with metal, because there is none. A chert flake is pressed lightly to the back of the neck or the inner wrist while the child is held against wet basalt. A thin line is scratched, not deeply. Afterward, the line is rubbed with lichen paste until it becomes dark. This is not decoration. It says: the storm knows your shape now. It will not take you first. The child must learn to keep dry enough to be seen as separate from the rock.

Their dead are not buried, because there is nowhere to bury them.
