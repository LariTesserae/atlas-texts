The ground is not merely covered by plants; it is a living crust, a pale lattice of root-fine and mineral hair so dry and so thin that it seems like dust until pressure bends it into pattern. There is no soft soil in the Dry Choir, no dark crumb, no wet earth. The floor of the basin is a vegetal skin stretched over the stone, grown into the cracks, woven through the salt seams, bound to the undersides of the town by thousands of dry filaments that conduct not sap but charge, not water but tension. It is the color of old bone, chalk, rust, ash, and translucent horn. It does not rot. It does not bloom into green. It accumulates, stiffens, and remembers.

No one lives in the Dry Choir.

The Dry Choir lives.

If that sounds like a riddle, it is not meant to. It is only the most accurate way to say that in this place the boundary between creature and city has failed. A wall here is not an abandoned construction. It is a body. A street is not an empty channel. It is a passage for the movement of dry air, salt dust, static, pressure, and the slow chemistry of being. When wind passes through it, it is not simply blowing past an inert thing. It is moving through a living throat, a living corridor, a living instrument made of tissue that has learned to bear weight, to split into useful shapes, to harden where strain comes, and to stay open where the note must not stop.

There are, if one insists on naming them, many kinds of life here. They are all plantlike, but no plant as known to moist light would recognize them. They have no leaves, because there is no light to catch and no air wet enough to breathe through leaf-thin. They have no flowers, because there are no animals to attract and no need for bright advertisements. They have no bark as trees make bark, no fruit as vines would understand fruit, no roots that drink from streams. Instead, they have rinds, struts, tubes, plates, hollow piths, brittle waxes, crystalline ribs, and dry hairs that gather mineral gradients from the air itself. They do not grow upward because they are hungry for sun. They grow outward because pressure teaches them to.

The most obvious inhabitants are the tall hollow beings, the stalk-singers.

They rise from the ground-crust as pillars and towers, as broad reeds and narrow pipes, as clusters of split stems that bend toward one another and then fuse at points where their vibrations match. A single stalk-singer is not one creature in the simple way a creature on the surface world might be one. It is a colonial body, a federation of growing units: fibrous sleeves, mineral shells, dry membranes, seed-cups, sound-slits, and crystal joints. Some are as wide as a room. Some are thin as needles and rise into the dark like broken spines. Their interiors are mostly chambers, flutes, and channels. They are grown to be heard.

Their outer surfaces are hard enough to ring when struck by a sudden pressure change. Their interiors are lined with pale filaments that stiffen and relax according to the movement of air through them. They do not have senses in the animal manner. They have no nervous system as animals know it, no brain, no eye, no ear, no tongue, no hand to touch. But they perceive nonetheless. They perceive the difference between a calm draft and a violent one. They perceive the presence of another stalk because the first transmits a faint pressure through the ground-crust and the second answers with a change in tone. They perceive static in the air as a mineral body perceives salt: by rearrangement. They perceive sound as weight, as instruction, as food, as threat, as invitation, as memory.

When the wind enters the basin, the stalk-singers feed on its passage.

The air moves through their hollow necks and broad chambers, and in moving it carries with it microscopic particles of subliming salt, iron dust, dry silica, and powdered mineral residue. These particles lodge on the inner surfaces of the stalks. There, dry chemistry begins. The stalks do not swallow. They do not digest. They absorb. They take up the mineral gradients with the patience of stone, converting pressure and charge into growth, growth into sound, and sound into further growth. Their metabolism is slow, mineral, and audible. To listen to the Dry Choir is to listen to something eating, breathing, thinking, and building all at once, though none of those verbs quite fit.

There are the low dwellers, too: the wall-thickets and dust-lace organisms.

They spread sideways rather than upward. They are thickets of dry fiber, pale mats, crusts, and curled edges that bind loose stones together into streets and plazas. They grow around the bases of stalk-singers and along the rims of terraces. Where they encounter an opening between two stalks, they send out fibrous bridges. Where they encounter a wall that threatens to collapse, they stiffen into waxy plates. Where dust begins to gather, they weave it into floors. Where static builds, they split their outer skins into fine points that release it. Their bodies resemble the architecture so closely that the two are hard to distinguish. A street may begin as a groove in the crust, then become a channel, then become a road as the dust-lace grows along its edges and reinforces the banks. A doorway may begin as a crack in a thickening wall, then widen into an arch as the living fiber curls around it. The town is not built by them. The town is them, shaped by their growth.

There are the crystal organs.

These are plantlike only in their slowness and their capacity to accumulate structure, but they are not mineral in the dead sense. They begin as tiny mineral buds on the edges of rinds and stalks. They grow by adding facets, by conducting charge along their edges, by drawing mineral ions from the air through dry channels. A crystal joint may lock two walls together. A crystal lattice may line a hollow chamber and turn the air passing over it into a pure note. A crystal node may sit at the base of a stalk and record pressure changes across long cycles, like a dry heart.
