If one says “people” it is true, and if one says “moss” it is also true. The thing that lives in that chamber is not a single species but a society whose members include human families, cultivated algal films, trained fungal shelves, salt-bleeding beetles, and machines that have been kept so long they behave like domesticated animals. But there are still hands on the copper valves, shoulders climbing ladders at severe angles, mouths tasting the condensation, ears reading the hiss of the pipes.

They are human enough to be called people, though not human in the sense of surface-dwellers. Their origin is kept in pipe-knots and moss maps rather than in books. Some are born in the galleries. Some were carried into the crack as children from whatever world lay above the salt-streaked stones. Some came down seeking heat, or shelter, or a place where the old laws had lost their shape. Over generations, they became the chamber’s own: not merely inhabitants, but organs of it. They call themselves the Ashroot, a name that means less “who we are” than “what we keep”: ash, root, heat, water, and the narrow seams between them.

They do not live as people who occupy a cave. They live as people who have been partially absorbed into its metabolism. The settlement does not sit upon the chamber; it grows from it. Every surface is a negotiation between what can be built and what will colonize. A family does not own a room so much as a flow: the trickle above a basin, the thermal pulse under a floor, the green stain on a wall that keeps the air damp, the lamp shelf that must never be pruned too hard or the house will sweat or dry or collapse into moss.

The Ashroot are not pale in the old cave-folk manner. Heat and mineral air have given them a permanent dull flush, and their skin carries bronze, copper, ash-gray, and faint olive tones. Salt crystallizes at their hairlines, eyelashes, and knuckles. Hair is cropped close or braided tight with copper wire, root fiber, and strips of treated fungus, because loose hair snags on valves and carries spores into beds. They are lean but broad in the chest and hips, with large hands and long, sensitive fingers. Their feet are thickened by walking hot slag and wet stone; their palms are scarred by brass, glass, and condensation. Their eyes are wide and dark-adapted, often ringed by a faint greenish film from algal foods and smokeless heat. They do not leap. They do not shout. They move with the peculiar economy of creatures who know that a wrong weight on a vine-stair or a hand on the wrong pipe can undo a week’s humidity.

Because the chamber is a sealed dusk, they do not measure life by sunlight. Time is made of thermal breaths, condensation cycles, salt blooms, and the slow opening of flower-like fronds that are not flowers but fruiting bodies or heat traps. A season may be “when the upper crack exhales green.” A year may be “when the great basalt wall sweated for thirty rains.” Their calendars are not written on paper but kept in notches along pipes, in coils of vine, in layers of ash, and in the color of certain mosses at the edge of the light.

They live stacked against the walls like cells in a hive. A bedroom may be a hollow behind a condenser, cooled by its running skin. A nursery may be a shelf of woven root felt where the warm air is steady and the ceiling does not spit too hard. A workshop may be half the wall: a forge without flame, a press without smoke, a lathe driven by returning cool air. Floors are layered with compressed moss and dried leaf pulp, so that steps feel spongy and muffled. Walls are composite, “breathing” in the slowest sense: they tighten when humidity falls, relax when the chamber exhales. A beam may pulse once every half-hour when a thermal pocket shifts behind it, and the Ashroot learn to interpret such pulses the way other people learn the expression of a face.

Their clothes are grown as much as made. Root felt is softened, mineralized, and stitched with vine fibers. Moss leather is dried, powdered, and pressed into sheets that resist heat better than cloth. Fungal filaments are woven into linings that wick sweat away. They wear cloaks of lichen that store moisture and can be wrung out in a dry spell. Boots are lined with crushed salt to discourage beetles and prevent the feet from becoming too soft. Their garments are never waterproof; dryness is not the goal. Breathable, drainable, and resistant to heat are better virtues. Colors are muted: ash, jade, salt-white, iron-black, pale copper. Brightness belongs to the bioluminescent colonies and the heated coils, not to clothing.

Their food comes from the chamber itself. They do not have fields. They do not herd animals. They cultivate moisture.

Ferns are eaten young, boiled in copper until their bitterness turns sweet. Mosses are washed in mineral pools, dried, ground, and fermented into dense cakes that keep well and taste faintly of ash and green stone. Algal films are scraped from basins, formed into wafers, and roasted on hot slag plates. Shelf fungi grow on lintels and beams; some are eaten, some are used to start yeast mats in clay pots. Tubers develop in the deeper root pockets where condensation collects. Leaf pulp is pressed, dried, and reconstituted. Vines bleed a clear sap that hardens into beads; the beads can be dissolved in warm water to make a thin, sweetish broth, or used as sealant. Meat is rare. Blind grubs are harvested only in hard seasons. Salt-lice may be collected for mineral broth, but not from the clean basins; the chamber’s small creatures are too few to be wasted as ordinary food. Moths are mostly left to rest on pipes. When an amphibious thing is taken from a pool, it is because the pool was becoming too crowded, and even then the family says a quiet word to the water.

They drink warm water, often. Coldness is scarce and unstable. They collect condensation in copper basins, run it through charcoal, moss, and crushed shell, and store it in clay-lined niches sealed with sap resin. They taste water the way others taste wine: by mineral breath, by heat, by the faint green memory of the walls that gave it. A spilled cup is not merely wasteful; it is an insult to the chamber. A dried basin is not just dry; it is a small death.

Their industry is quiet but pervasive. They are not miners in the usual sense, because the rock is not being dug away so much as listened to, guided, and kept. The chamber’s heat is their first engine. They know how to make hot air rise and cool air fall, how to turn temperature into motion, how to stretch a thermal breath into a crank, a bellows, a pump. Machinery is bolted wherever a wall can bear it, hidden under moss where noise must be softened, insulated by living vine where heat must not escape.
