No one lives there in the way a question expects.

There are no people standing on the low walls, no herds moving between basins, no small creatures darting into cracks, no birds marking the sky with flight, no insects hovering over the black water. There is no hearth smoke, no footpath worn soft, no nest, no burrow, no midden, no body. There is no green thing leaning into the sun. The air is too clean, the surfaces too hard, the geometry too complete for ordinary life to have gained a foothold. If “whoever” means a named person, then the answer is simple: nobody remains.

But if “whatever lives there” means anything that occupies, responds, consumes, repairs, grows, or changes the place over time, then the plateau is full of inhabitants. They are not creatures. They are mineral processes that have taken on the patience of life. They are water, salt, crystal, air, light, and the absent memory of hands.

The first of these inhabitants is water.

Not water as a river, not water as a lake, not water as a spring with a mouth, but water as a distributed presence, a thin and traveling skin across the world. It arrives without a source. It condenses on broad black slabs in the cold hours. It beads along the lips of basins and the edges of fired clay. It seeps from porous stone and moves in hairline sheets that seem, in the early brightness, to rise out of the material itself. It flows through channels that were angled for it long before it found them, falls into shallow cups, fills dark columnar wells, touches the interiors of covered cisterns, returns to the air as vapor, freezes briefly, thaws again, and begins once more.

This water has no wildness in it. It is never allowed to spread through mud because there is no mud. It never pools among roots because there are no roots. It does not flood because every path has been prepared for it. Its life is one of circulation: condensation, movement, storage, evaporation, rerouting. It behaves less like a liquid in a landscape than like a blood moving through a body whose bones are made of fitted stone.

Around the water, the mineral cultures live.

They are the closest thing this place has to vegetation, though they are not plants, and to animals, though they are not animals. They grow as accretion. They seed as precipitation. They expand by repetition, layer by layer, drop by drop. On damp slabs there are pale branching mats of quartz-like filament, fine as frost but solid. From the mouths of channels creep fuzzy blooms that resemble calcium lace or the white skeletons of coral. Over surfaces kept wet long enough, crystalline patches spread slowly, bright and faintly iridescent, not melting with day because they are not ice, not truly, but mineral bodies built from the water’s own dissolved content.

In fast-moving water, these growths are thin and delicate, almost like threads of salt. They cling to the edges of channels and tremble in the current. In stagnant basins, they build rough ruffs, layered shelves, and crusts that thicken into low terraces. Where stone becomes too dry, they turn glassy and brittle, leaving behind shards that catch the light and fracture at the smallest touch. In deep, mirror-still water, they rise from the bottom as pale veins, as if reaching upward without desire, without nerves, without leaves, only by the logic of crystallization.

These mineral forms have niches. They have behaviors. They have what could almost be called populations.

The capillary filaments populate the hairline margins where a drop of water stays only a little longer than evaporation can remove it. The lace-growths colonize the outflow lips where moisture is secreted repeatedly. The white salt ruffs settle where basins are shallow and the sky is bright and the water is left alone long enough to give up its solids. The glassy crusts dominate the dry ledges, brittle and glittering. The deep-basin crystals grow slowly in stillness, their forms controlled not by light in any living way, but by pressure, temperature, mineral saturation, and the ancient angles of the vessel that holds them.

They reproduce by seeding and deposition. Tiny fragments break free and lodge in damp corners. Dust-thin crystals drift into channels. Evaporation leaves behind a film that, if another wetting follows, becomes a thread, then a mat, then a branch, then a pale lattice across the stone. They do not grow from soil. They do not feed on sun. They feed on contact: water touching mineral, air touching surface, cold touching heat, time touching stone.

They also die.

They die into shards. They crack when the temperature changes and make small crystalline reports that travel across the plateau. They collapse when their supporting moisture evaporates. They flake away when salt thickens beneath them or when frost pries at their bases. But death here is not removal. Death becomes raw material. A shattered mat feeds a new crust. A broken bridge of crystal becomes scattered seeds for another growth. The mineral ecology is a system of loss and reuse so slow that it can only be measured in seasons, centuries, or the quiet wear of one stone against another.

The architecture is the habitat, but the habitat is also the body.

The basins are organs. The channels are vessels. The domed cisterns are sealed interiors, dark and cool, holding water that has withdrawn from sight. The fluted walls, shelves, slabs, tiles, wedges, pins, and ceramic linings form a skeleton of made stone. The condensation films act as skin. The crystal mats act as fur, membrane, growth, and scar. The wind that slides through the built geometry is breath, not in a living sense, but in the sense that the whole place depends on air moving across surfaces.

This body has no heart, but it has gradients. It depends on differences: day and night, wet and dry, cold and bright, high and low, stillness and trickle. When the sun rises and the air cools to the temperature that lets moisture settle, the plateau begins to sweat. When the light strengthens, the upper surfaces warm and release some of what they have gathered. When wind passes, the evaporation rate changes. When frost comes, tiles lift, seams open, salt crusts crack, new paths are invited. The system responds, not with choice, but with the intelligence of physical law arranged by hands.

If one channel fails, water finds another seam. If a basin seals itself with mineral growth, flow reroutes. If a tile fractures, crystalline lace may creep across the gap and become a temporary bridge. If a surface becomes too dry, the growths upon it turn brittle and vanish into dust, leaving the stone bare until moisture returns. The plateau repairs itself with deposition, not with tissue. Its aging is fracture and accretion. Its growth is mineral. Its life is slow, distributed, and almost wholly dependent on the pattern left behind by the makers.

And the makers are absent, yet they are present as pattern.

No graves remain. No tools lie abandoned in dust, because there is no dust. No clothing rots in crevices. No bones bleach. No hearth ash is stored in the sealed rooms because there are no rooms, no doors, no markets, no dwellings. The people, if they were people, left no organic trace. Yet their presence is unmistakable. It is in the chisel marks. It is in the fitted joints. It is in the slight asymmetry of one block balanced beside another. It is in the patience of a long gutter aimed with impossible care across open stone. It is in the hand-width walkways, the cup-sized basins, the ceramic linings, the wedge-cut grooves, the places where someone once pressed thumb and knuckle against a slab and coaxed water’s path into being.

They may not have been human. Or they may have been human and changed beyond recognition. Or they may have been creatures whose bodies were always closer to stone than to flesh. The evidence does not permit certainty. What it does show is that the makers were not wanderers. They were builders who thought in flows. They understood condensation, capillary action, evaporation, thermal mass, the strength of balanced stone, the usefulness of broad cool surfaces, and the way mineral water could be taught to leave behind its solids as a kind of architecture. Their tools were sharp stone, rope or cordage, clay, fire, wedges, weights, basalt, obsidian, perhaps glass fiber or thin copper wire drawn by hand. Their precision came not from machines but from repetition, memory, and the slow accumulation of what worked.

If their bodies are gone, their labor remains. That labor is now a kind of inhabitant.

The ghost in the place is not supernatural. It is practical. It is the angle of a channel that must be watched at dawn to know where water will condense first. It is the thickness of a slab chosen because it holds cold. It is the texture of ceramic chosen because it will hold a film longer than polished basalt would. It is the spacing of basins, the height of walls, the direction of domed shadows, the placement of weights that make the whole structure lean into itself rather than apart. These choices are still present. They are still doing work. They still determine the fate of every drop.

In that sense, the vanished makers live as a habit of form.

The wind, too, has a kind of residence. It is not alive, but it behaves like a visiting presence. When it moves over the plateau, it does not rustle leaves. It slides. It finds the gaps between slabs and the narrow mouths of channels and makes a sound lower than a whistle, sustained like breath. The slotted walls, which were built for drainage or bracing, become accidental pipes. At certain angles and temperatures, the plateau hums faintly. Not music, not voice, but something like a warning, a sigh, a note left behind in stone after the weather has moved through it. This sound is rare enough that when it occurs, the place seems suddenly aware of itself.

Light also inhabits here, though it is not a living thing. It is too active to call inert. The brightness is high, cold, and direct. It strikes wet stone and returns in long thin reflections. It reveals toolmarks, hairline fractures, the newest growths, the smallest beads of condensation. It moves through the day as a changing force: at dawn it whitens the mineral mats; by midday it makes the whole surface tremble with glare; at dusk it leaves the deeper channels dark while still catching salt ruffs with a last pale shine. Night does not soften the geometry. It turns it into a map of cold lines, each edge defined by residual moisture and faint reflection. If clouds permit, the basins mirror stars, and the whole plateau becomes a scattered collection of tiny skies.

There is no warmth here in the way of a fire, a body, or sun-baked soil. But there is a warmth in the stone after bright hours, a stored heat in broad slabs, a slow release into the night that changes the rate of evaporation and condensation. Even this warmth is inhabited: it belongs to the system, moves through it, alters the water, feeds the crystallization, cracks the salts, wakes the ticking of cooling stone. The plateau is quiet because no creature breaks the silence, but it is not silent. It drips. It clicks. It fractures. It hums. It ticks. It breathes in the language of phase change.

If one tried to make a census of the living things there, the categories would not be species but surfaces.

The first census-takers would count the beading: tiny drops held at the corners of slabs, trembling before collapse.

The second would count the weeping films: broad sheets of condensation across the upper surfaces, silver under dawn.

The third would count the thread-growth: salt-like filaments in the fastest channels, pale and fragile.

The fourth would count the lace-colonies: calcium-like mats around basin mouths and channel edges, branching in slow, irregular networks.

The fifth would count the ruffs: rough white shelves in stagnant basins, thickened by long stillness.

The sixth would count the glassy crusts: brittle mineral skins on dry ledges, shining with internal fractures.

The seventh would count the spires: white mineral towers rising in some old basins, orienting not toward sun like leaves, but according to stress, temperature, moisture, and crystalline structure.

The eighth would count the bridges: thin crystal crossings over failed gaps, temporary until a new stone wedge or tile replaces them.

The ninth would count the seams: thin copper or dark mineral lines in the joints, too rare to call metal, too persistent to call mere color.

The tenth would count the mirrors: black water in deep columnar basins, holding sky without stirring, surrounded by pale veins of growth.

None of these things walk or feed or hunt. None of them call. None of them carry young. Yet each one occupies a place, changes its place, and is changed by it. Together they form a society of surfaces. Their politics is pressure. Their economy is water. Their religion, if one could imagine such a thing, would be brightness: the cool, exposing light that makes every edge visible and every failure impossible to hide.

The most unsettling thing about these inhabitants is not that they exist without blood, but that they make the absence of blood feel complete. There is no leftover. There is no fallen leaf to prove that something else was here first. There is no nest, no burrow, no carcass, no hoofprint, no root. The place is not wilderness abandoned. It is artificiality that has become nature so thoroughly that nature itself has been replaced. It has taken on the inevitability of geology while retaining the intention of architecture. Its stones are worn, but worn along human angles. Its crystals grow, but grow over surfaces that were laid out first. Its water is part of the landscape, but the landscape exists to arrange water.

So the thing that lives there is not a plant, animal, or person. It is a lithic system. It is an artifact-ecology. It is a plateau that behaves like a body because hands have made it into a body, and because water and crystal fill that body with slow, mineral activity.

It inhales moisture from the cool air. It circulates that moisture through stone veins and ceramic capillaries. It stores part of it in dark cisterns and shallow basins. It lets part return to the sky as vapor. It leaves part behind as salt, lace, filament, and glass. It repairs itself by deposition. It adapts by rerouting. It ages by fracture. It regenerates by new accretion. It does not reproduce in the ordinary way, because there is no egg, no spore, no seed in the ordinary sense. But it does propagate. It sends water to new seams. It carries mineral seeds into old gaps. It converts failure into structure. It turns the end of one channel into the beginning of another.

This is what lives there.

Not ghosts, exactly, though the hand-marks feel like ghosts. Not gods, though the scale feels like worship. Not cities, though the density feels like occupation. The place is a city of vessels, a mountain made into a lung, a stone machine still running with no operator, a mineral mind still thinking without a brain. It thinks in flows. It remembers in surfaces. It speaks in drip, tick, crack, and faint wind.

If asked again who lives there, after a long silence, after watching the light move across the basin rims, after hearing a single drop fall and knowing it has been waiting all night in the pores of a slab, the answer should not be “no one.” The better answer is: no one, and everything.

No one remains. No breath remains. No heartbeat remains. No animal remains. No plant remains. No child remains. No elder remains. No maker remains in body or voice.

But the water remains. The salt remains. The pale mineral growths remain. The channels remain. The basins remain. The chisel marks remain. The wind’s thin hum remains. The bright cold air remains. The plateau itself remains, not as ruin, not as quarry, not as city, but as an alternate nature, growing by precipitation, living by contact, thinking by angle, and holding, in every fitted edge, the afterlife of the hands that made it.
