On the Weeping Table, “who lives here” cannot be answered by pointing. There is no one standing in a beam of light, no face turned toward a horizon, no town made visible by smoke or lampposts. Instead, the plateau is crowded by pressure, breath, rhythm, heat, wetness, and long-standing sound. If you came with eyes, you would be useless. If you came with skin, ears, nose, and teeth sensitive to vibration, you would find that the place is not merely inhabited. It is a parliament of residents, most of them named only by the noises they make.

The first inhabitants are not creatures in any grand sense. They are films, skins, lattices, and slow creeping communities too small to count. Black slime molds move over wet stone like a thought spreading without words. Bacterial mats grow on warm ledges where steam condenses into shallow pools, tasting of iron and sulfur and something faintly sweet, like old bread left in rain. These films feed the smaller animals, bind minerals into shapes, and prepare stone for plant life. They are the quiet foundation of the Weeping Table, a living coating on the highland’s wet bones. They do not flee. They do not hunt. They simply spread, eat rust and salt, exhale mineral scent, and become the first thing any new stone will learn to hold.

Above that first skin of life rise the small swarm-people.

There are click-midges, tiny enough to be felt before they are known. Their wings do not merely beat; they interfere with the static hanging in the humid air. A swarm of them moving through a storm curtain can make the skin prickle, as though a field of very small bells were sounding under water. They feed on spores, dissolved minerals, and the oils on the undersides of cap-tree gills. They do not build nests in any solid sense. Their nest is the pattern they move in. Their eggs are carried in droplets, their larvae drift inside held-up rain, and their bodies return to the wet ground in soft deaths, becoming fertilizer for moss. When they are gone from a place, the place is not empty. It is waiting for a different rhythm.

There are silk-bodied crawlers, pale and limbless in the way that water is limbless. They move across peat and moss with a surface that seems always wet, leaving traces that are not slime exactly, but a fine conductive fiber. Their bodies are soft enough to pass through gaps no harder creature could enter. They spin threads between mushroom caps and thrum-vines, catching tiny droplets and storing charge. In their dens, the threads form bowl-shaped gardens where condensation gathers and fungal filaments grow on them. Some of these crawlers are eaten by larger things. Others are cultivated, unknowingly perhaps, by the plateau’s more deliberate residents, because their silk holds heat, binds soil, and catches the mineral spray that makes the upper marshes fertile. If disturbed, they do not run. They become thinner, quieter, more like a wet thread, and let the world forget they were there.

There are blind beetles with shells slicked by oil and lined with microscopic ridges. They burrow into the soft peat and leave behind tunnels that alter how water moves through the bog. Some are round and patient, some long and needle-nosed, some flattened so they can live beneath the skins of fallen fronds. They click with their legs when disturbed, and in large gatherings their clicking can sound like dry grain falling. They eat dead plant matter, fungal threads, and the fallen wings of larger animals. They clean the Weeping Table. Without them, the plateau would drown slowly in its own richness.

There are ore-worms that live in wet stone and eat rust. They do not eat metal with mouths in any simple sense; their bodies are lined with tiny acid filaments that dissolve mineral strata, and they excrete threads of magnetized waste that creak faintly when wind passes. They tunnel through abandoned tools, cable anchors, plates, pipes, and the buried bones of old machines. Where they go, cables lose their shine and become homes for moss. Where they dwell, peat grows strange and firm, threaded with the faint metallic taste of old industry being made biological again. Their presence is a slow negotiation between geology, machine, and flesh.

Then come the wide-bodied herds.

They have no good name, because names made of light do not fit them. The Tenders, if any still walk there, know them as a certain pressure in the soles of the feet. Travelers who might cross the plateau would know them as a soft thunder of peat, a smell of warm dung, a shifting of low breath against the skin. They are broad, close to the ground, built as if the plateau wanted living basins that could move. Their eyes are sealed under skin, smooth and useless, but their heads are vast instruments for hearing and balance. Their skulls flare outward like cupped hands. Along their brows and crests run combs of bone and keratin, each individual shaped slightly differently, as if the herd has spent millennia tuning itself to the Weeping Table’s endless acoustic weather.

These wide-bodies drink from thermal pools and stand for hours beneath dripping caps, letting water run along grooves in their skulls into open mouths. They eat fungi, reed mats, and the soft black peat that holds warmth and mineral life. They do not graze as sunlit animals would, biting grass by sight. They taste the ground with their lips and noses, finding the right texture, the right fermentation, the right heat. Calves are born into shallow warm hollows where the peat has been softened by older herd animals, and the first thing a calf learns is not a shape, but a rhythm. A mother’s hoofbeat. The particular sway of a herd column. The low resonant hum that travels through the chest wall of the wide-body beside it.

The herd is not a crowd. It is an instrument.

Each animal hears the others through touch, breath, and ground vibration. If a predator is near, one beast may stop mid-step. That stillness travels outward in waves, a soft collapse of movement through the herd. If a storm is building elsewhere, the air-pressure shift reaches them before the rain, and the herd begins to turn as though a single hand has guided it through the dark. They move across the plateau in slow migrations, following warmth, avoiding the deepest sinkholes, and gathering where steam vents make the peat livable. They are hunted, of course. But they are not helpless. Their combs resonate when they bellow, and a frightened herd can produce a layered, infrasonic roar that confuses smaller predators and presses water from the leaves of cap-trees. They are not beautiful to eyes that need light. They are beautiful the way a drum is beautiful: made for vibration, for weight, for the way sound moves through a body that has learned to live inside it.

The predators know them well.
