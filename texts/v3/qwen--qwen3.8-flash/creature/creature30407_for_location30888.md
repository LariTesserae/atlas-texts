What lives here does not come to you first as bodies. It comes as a glaze.

It begins in the sheen on the stone, in the faint oily brightness of a pool surface, in the pale dust that clings to the rim of a basin, in the thin dark seam between two terraces where water has no name yet and no channel except the one it keeps making. If you press your face close to the wet rock, you may see it: a skin of life, not quite algae, not quite mud, not quite slime, but a city’s oldest plaster. It is the living film that makes the landscape slippery, the biofilm that coats the world here like varnish on a shell. Beneath its gold-green surface, in the top millimeter of every wet place, there are creatures too small for the naked eye to catch: bacteria, cyanobacteria, diatoms, flagellates, spores, the tiny engines of chemistry that eat light and excrete stone.

These are the first citizens, and they have been here so long that their bodies and wastes have become the floor.

They do not build with hands. They build by leaving themselves behind. A mat of microbes traps a grain of dust; dust becomes sediment; sediment becomes a thin white crust; the crust hardens into a ledge; the ledge changes the way water runs, and the water, in turn, changes the way the microbes live. This is the city’s grammar. In the shallow pools, the mats are brightest near the light, where they breathe oxygen into the air and give the water its faint green-gold suspension. Lower down, where oxygen fades, other creatures take over, feeding on dead filaments and sulfides, making the sediment black and sour, working in the dark chemistry of decay. The pools are not simply filled with water; they are layered with worlds. There is a sunlit roof of algae, a middle street of suspended particles and tiny swimmers, and a basement floor of anoxic mud where bacteria trade their breath for the minerals locked in stone.

These microbes are not passive residents. They are weather-makers, mineral-forge makers, the slow masons of the terraces. They precipitate calcium carbonate into chalky fringes. They bind silica into fragile blades that rise like glass grass at the water’s edge. They trap salt in the rims of basins and hold it there until evaporation finishes the work. If there is a builder here, it is not a single creature but a community without edges: a congregation of cells that behaves, over centuries, as though it were planning the layout of streets.

Above them, and through them, run the threads.

Fungi live in this place as invisible nerves. They braid through peat, weave mineral grains together, colonize dead stems, and make the spongy black benches of peat into something like cloth. Where a plant has fallen, fungal filaments move in to digest it, to pull nutrients through the wet dark, to stitch broken bodies back into the city’s fabric. On the driest exposed stone, lichens take their turn. They are not moss, not plants, not animals, but partnerships: a fungus housing algae, a fungus housing cyanobacteria, a slow marriage of bodies that can sleep through drought and wake with dew. They spread in gray and orange and faint green maps across the terraces. They crack when the stone dries, then swell when the fog returns, living by a season measured not in months but in minutes of condensation. To walk across a lichen-covered shelf is to walk across the skin of an enormous, patient mind that is not a mind at all, only chemistry doing its work with astonishing regularity.

The mosses are the city’s damp furniture. They form low cushions in the wettest corners, holding water like dark wool. They grow in the lee of salt rims and tufa steps, where the wind has passed and the stone stays cool. Under magnification, moss leaves are crowded with tiny animals: rotifers turning their cilia like miniature whirlwinds, tardigrades moving in slow, eight-legged deliberation through a forest of droplets, nematodes writhing through the water films that cover every cell wall. This is one of the place’s most abundant worlds and one of its most hidden. A square centimeter of wet moss can contain a whole town: grazing animals, predators, detritivores, eggs waiting for damp, resting stages waiting for rain. When the surface dries, many of them simply stop, fold themselves into survival, become almost mineral. When dew returns, the world restarts.

The plants are fewer, and they are strange, as if the landscape has accepted only the most stubborn forms of green.

Sedges grow in tufts at the edges of channels, their roots darkening the water with tannin, their blades bending into the wind. They are not lush. They do not sway with the soft abundance of a meadow. They live as if they are negotiating with the ground, each clump a small colony in an argument about where moisture can be held. Their roots bind peat. Their leaves collect dust. When they die, their stems leave molds, negative spaces where the water will later deposit mineral walls, as if the dead plant had left behind a doorway.

The salt-tolerant rosettes cling to basin rims like gray-green hands pressed flat against the stone. Their leaves are thickened, waxy, designed to withhold water from a wind that wants to take it. Some excrete salt through special glands, leaving white specks on the leaf surface, small crystals that catch the light and become part of the landscape’s sparkle. They grow so low because the ground is not generous and the sky is too open. They do not make a canopy. They make punctuation: a green mark here, a pale rosette there, a short stroke of moss along the wetter seam, enough to let you know the place is alive without ever letting you forget it is stone.

There are peat benches too, and these are almost inhabitants in their own right. Peat is a body made of unfinished decay. Sedge roots, moss fragments, dead leaves, fungal threads, and mineral dust compress year after year into a black spongy shelf that holds water, resists burning, and slowly builds the level ground. It exhales when stepped on, releasing a cold breath of trapped air. It remembers the shape of what lived in it. It is not soil so much as a fossil archive still in formation: a library of pressed stems, root hairs, egg cases, shell fragments, spores, and tiny bones of animals long gone. In the peat, death is not disappearance. It is storage.

The true animals of this place are mostly small enough to be mistaken for particles, yet they are not passive. They are the movers, grazers, burrowers, predators, and survivors.

In the shallowest channels live springtails, no bigger than a grain of sand, leaping when disturbed, grazing on fungal threads and biofilm. In the damp dust under stone edges are mites, some feeding on decaying matter, others on smaller creatures, tiny armored hunters moving through the dark. In the pools and capillary threads, nematodes undulate through water films, some eating bacteria, some eating algae, some eating other nematodes, their bodies transparent enough that the gut contents show like seeds in fruit. Rotifers spin in the thin wetness at the edge of moss, filtering the water with crowns of moving cilia. Ciliates and amoebae travel through pore spaces no wider than a hair, engulfing bacteria and each other in a war so small it cannot be heard, though it is one of the city’s loudest activities in metabolic terms.

In the deeper, less disturbed basins, there may be ostracods, shelled organisms no larger than pinheads, moving through silt with delicate legs. There may be tiny snails, their shells worn thin by mineral water, grazing the algal skins off stone. There may be bivalve larvae in the sediment, not building reefs, not living in rivers, but surviving in the smallest wet margins where the water film is too permanent for air and too thin for fish. In temporary pools that form and vanish with the weather, crustaceans may leave their eggs in the dry salt dust, waiting years for water to return. When rain comes, they hatch in darkness and grow quickly, swimming in brine too concentrated for most animals, dying when the basin vanishes, their bodies settling as protein into the mud. Their deaths are brief but necessary: they feed midges, birds, the peat itself.

Insects are the most visible residents, though not abundant in the sense of swarms. Midges rise from pools at dusk in smoke-thin columns, mate in hovering clouds, die or drift away. Their larvae are often more permanent citizens than the adults: wormlike forms burrowing in silt and peat, feeding on detritus, constructing tiny cases from sand grains or plant fragments, breathing through gills or through water that has learned to hold oxygen in the cold. Water beetles may glide under the skin of a pool, their bodies dark, their legs paddling almost without ripple. They are not dramatic creatures here; they are movements in the water, interruptions of stillness. A larva might look like a thread of flesh, then become a pupa fixed in the mud, then become a thin-legged adult crawling onto a salt rim at night. Each stage is a different address within the same wet quarter.

There are also the legless things that seem to belong not to the air, nor quite to the water, but to the boundary between them: small annelids, flatworms, fly larvae, perhaps other creatures adapted to the interstices of peat and mineral film. They live in the places where a human eye would only see dampness. They travel through channels too small to call streams. They feed on the same bacterial and algal skin that builds the stone. They leave behind tubes of mucus and cast-off cuticles that become dust in the sheltered hollows. They are part of the reason the ground feels overfull even when nothing visible moves.

The larger animals are more like visitors than inhabitants, but the city makes residents of them nonetheless.

A wader may step through a shallow basin with slow, deliberate care, its legs dark, its pale body reflected in the thin water. It does not stride as it would in a river; it hesitates, because the ground is not solid in the way of land, nor liquid in the way of a pool. It is a membrane. The bird reads the tremor of peat, the mineral resistance of tufa, the hidden life under its feet. It pecks at midges, larvae, perhaps a small crustacean trapped in a vanishing basin. It leaves footprints that fill with water within minutes. If it dies here, its body becomes a gift: feathers decay, bone dissolves slowly, proteins feed microbes, minerals settle, and eventually the basin becomes a pale ring around its remains. Nothing is lost. The bird becomes architecture.

Bats come at dusk because the pools release midges. They cross the sky in short, black arcs, catching insects that themselves are almost not there, their lives brief enough to be called weather. The bats do not live in the stone; they live beyond it, or below it, or in caves unseen. But they visit, and their visits are seasonal and biological: their droppings can enrich sheltered ledges where lichens and mosses grow. Their dead, if they fall into a channel, may be consumed by larvae, bacteria, salt, frost, and eventually sedimented into the floor. A dead bat in this place would not remain a dead bat for long; it would become a record, a calcium fragment, a dark stain, a microclimate, a nursery for small life.

Hawks may circle far above, so small against the blue that one cannot be sure whether it is a bird, a stone falling, or simply a flaw in the sky. They hunt small rodents or insects if those exist nearby, but the open basin offers little for them to seize. Their presence is more a sign of the city’s edge than of its center. They belong to the larger weather system, not to the terraces. The same is true of most birds: they pass through, they drink, they may nest if a few reeds can be bent into shelter, but they do not make the city.

This is the place’s strange inversion: the most numerous inhabitants are not the ones you can see. The most lasting are not the ones with faces. The most architectural are not the ones who build walls in the way of hands and mortar. The true residents are the ones who make the walls by being walls.

Consider a dead stem of sedge. It stands in a channel like a pillar in a ruined hall. It has not been cut by anyone. It has simply grown, died, dried, and remained. Water runs over it. Salt crystallizes around it. Fungal threads enter its pores. A film of cyanobacteria coats its surface. Dust settles on its crown. Mineral blooms at its base. Over decades, the stem becomes a negative form: not the plant, but a hollow cast, then a solid mineral thread, then a fragile white blade that resembles silica but remembers a living stem. It has moved from organism to structure. It is still inhabited, but not in the way of the past. The inside now holds nematodes. The surface holds lichens. The tip collects water. It is a doorway, a column, a tool for evaporation, a seed trap for spores. Death has not emptied it; it has made it into civic space.

The egg cases and shells that pile up in sheltered corners are part of this same cycle. Empty cases are not rubbish here. They are architecture waiting to be colonized. They provide tiny rooms for mites, rotifers, dormant larvae, or microbial films. Their edges catch dust. Their surfaces become substrates for salt blooms. They contribute calcium, silica, chitin, protein, and shape to the ground. A broken shell can become a shelf. A shed larval skin can become a film in the peat. A feather can become a dark filament that slows water and encourages mineral deposition. The city’s streets are paved not with stones only, but with the remains of lives that have passed through.

What kind of society is this?

It is one without a leader, without a shared plan, without a sense of self. It is organized by gradients: moisture, salt, light, temperature, oxygen, mineral saturation, pH, surface tension, wind. Life gathers where water lingers. Salt crusts where evaporation wins. Algae brighten where light reaches. Fungi thread where decay begins. Plants root where peat can hold them. Animals appear where their food appears, and their food appears where the films and particles and eggs appear. The whole city runs as a single pattern because each part responds to the same conditions. There is no council, only physics. There is no intention, only repetition so constant it looks like design.

Their days are not days of activity in the human sense. Their mornings begin with condensation. Before the sun lifts the fog, the air gives itself to the stone. Droplets form on salt rims, lichen maps, peat edges, and dead stems. For many tiny organisms, this is the beginning of water. They wake. They move. They feed. They mate in the thin film. They graze the surface of moss. They climb through pore spaces as if following unseen roads. The city’s streets, which appear empty, are suddenly crowded with microscopic traffic. The sound is still almost nothing: the tick of a droplet, the hiss of salt crystals forming, the soft collapse of water into water.

By midday, the moisture has changed form. Some of it moves upward into the sky. Some remains as shine. The exposed salt rims may crack with heat and dryness. Lichens tighten. Tardigrades curl into sleeping bodies. The pools that looked still are busy with suspended life. In the shadow of a terrace, water may be cool enough to keep oxygen high; in the open, evaporation may concentrate salt until only the most tolerant microbes can survive. The city is not one habitat. It is a thousand microclimates separated by a hand’s breadth.

When wind comes, it ripples the films and lifts the midges. It bends sedges and pushes fog from one channel into another. For surface-dwelling insects, wind is a weather system that can kill. For spores and dust, wind is transportation. For plants with seeds or fragments, wind is the beginning of migration. For fungi, wind is a highway. The whole basin is full of arrivals: particles of old stems, grains of salt, specks of dried peat, egg cases carried from one pool to another by a bird’s foot, spores that can wait years in the dark until the right wetness comes.

At night, the city is not empty; it is differently populated. Many animals that avoid drying out at night emerge when the air is cool and the water film is unbroken. Larvae rise to the surface. Springtails leap through the dust. Beetles move with the caution of things that know the world above is thin and risky. If a frog or toad were present, it would be rare and silent, perhaps only a single dry croak in the distance, more rumor than resident. But if there are amphibians, they are marginal, living at the edges where water and moisture are enough, moving at night through channels and under peat shelves. Their eggs may be attached to submerged stems, their tadpoles feeding on algae and decay, their deaths becoming part of the pool’s dark bottom. In the cold high air, their life cycles would be slow, tied to the brief favorable seasons.

Frost brings its own inhabitants. Some organisms survive by producing sugars that act as antifreeze. Some retreat into pore spaces below the frozen crust. Some enter resting stages that can withstand desiccation and ice. The fine glaze on the rim of a basin is not merely decoration; it is a condition of life. It keeps moisture from vanishing entirely. It preserves the surface film. It makes the stone temporarily too smooth for some creatures and a sheltered bridge for others. When it cracks, it releases trapped droplets. When it melts, it feeds the channels. Frost is the city’s winter clock.

The seasons here are not marked by leaf-fall, because there are no leaves in any abundance. They are marked by depth of wetness, frequency of thaw, concentration of salt, and whether the fog forms more often than the dew. In the long cold months, the city may seem to stop. But it does not. It changes state. Microbial metabolism slows but continues under ice skins. Fungi persist in peat. Plant roots remain in the dark. Dormant stages wait. In the warm months, the same stone may be dry on its outer face and wet inside, alive in the middle layer where evaporation and capillary action meet. The city breathes between dryness and saturation. It has no permanent season except becoming.

If you ask what one of these inhabitants is like, you cannot answer with a face. You have to answer with a layer.

The microbe in the surface film is a speck that eats light and makes oxygen and excretes mineral. It is born in a droplet, perhaps lives a single afternoon, then becomes a crystal, a pore, a wall.

The fungus is a pale thread beneath peat, tasting stone and dead stems, digesting slowly, connecting what has no connection, pulling nutrients through dark channels that are not water but are almost water, almost air, almost body.

The lichen is a painted map that sleeps by day and wakes by night, by dew, by frost, by fog. It is two lives pretending to be one. It lives by withholding.

The moss is a dark cushion of wet needles and tiny forests, a reservoir and refuge. It shelters what cannot survive elsewhere. It dies into the stone and makes the stone wetter.

The sedge is a tuft of green negotiation, a body that traps dust, shades water, roots into peat, and eventually becomes a mold of itself. It is a building that forgets it once was alive.

The salt rosette is a gray hand on the rim of a basin, thick-lipped, waxy, excreting brine, making salt where there was none.

The springtail is a grain that jumps through water films, feeding on the city’s skin, surviving frost by entering sleep.

The rotifer is a spinning mouth in a droplet, filtering the world at the scale of the impossible, born quickly, living quickly, contributing to the abundance no one sees.

The water beetle is a dark slip under the surface tension, paddling between oxygen and stone, sometimes visible, then not.

The midge is a breath of the pool, an insect cloud at dusk, a larva in mud, an egg in dust, a brief life spent keeping the water alive with movement.

The wader is a guest with slow legs, a hunter who becomes nourishment.

The bat is a visitor at the boundary of air and stone.

None of these is the whole resident. Together they are not a creature, not a race, not a species, not a mind. But they are an ecology that has achieved an urban density. They are a place that lives by being inhabited from every side.

This is why the city feels crowded even when it looks empty. It is full of micro-worlds stacked on top of one another: films on stone, organisms in films, peat beneath, channels above, salt rims, spore layers, water veins, roots, dormant eggs, mineral crusts, dead shells, living threads. Every visible surface is also a habitat. Every empty space is a corridor for wind, water, or spores. Every pool is a town, a laboratory, a burial site, a bakery of minerals.

The inhabitants have no architecture because they are architecture. Their bodies are streets. Their waste is walls. Their rest is the frost. Their feeding is the growth of stone. Their reproduction is the sealing of basins with chalky crusts. Their memory is stratigraphy.

You might stand at the high ridge and see only terraces, salt, water, sparse grass, and silence. But look closer. The white shelf at the basin’s edge is not just mineral. It is the accumulated body of what has lived there. The dark seam between stones is not just moisture. It is a corridor of nematodes, bacteria, fungi, and tiny eggs. The thin green stroke of sedge is not just plant. It is a trap, a scaffold, a future doorway. The glimmering film on the water is not just reflection. It is a crowd.

If the city has a resident that could be called the city itself, it is this distributed community of water and life and mineral: not a ghost, not a spirit, not a creature with intent, but a pattern that persists because it makes its own conditions. It captures water, makes walls, creates shelter, concentrates salt, preserves dead stems, encourages moss, hosts microbes, feeds insects, lures birds, collects dust, survives frost, and repeats. It is not alive in the way a bird is alive, but it is not merely alive in the way a river is alive. It is a metabolism that has become a place.

And if one insists on a name for those who live here, perhaps the most honest name is not a noun but a verb: the living that secretes, the breathing that leaves stone, the growth that remembers by hardening. They are the wet ones. They are the thread-makers. They are the salt-keepers. They are the film-builders. They are the dead that become doors. They are the larvae in the mud. They are the spores in the dust. They are the tiny swimmers that do not break the surface. They are the moss that holds dew. They are the lichen that sleeps. They are the sedges leaning into the wind. They are the waders that pass. They are the bats that cross. They are the microbes whose numbers exceed the count of stars in any one visible patch of pool.

They do not know the city is a city. They do not know the place is high, wet, quiet, or strange. They live where moisture allows, where the surface film holds, where the salt does not kill, where the peat can be crossed, where the wind is not too constant, where the sun is bright enough and the night not too still. Their knowledge is chemical. Their memory is sediment. Their society is a lattice of gradients. Their immortality is the cycle.

In this way, the place is not abandoned. It is not empty. It is not a city without people. It is a city whose people are too numerous,
