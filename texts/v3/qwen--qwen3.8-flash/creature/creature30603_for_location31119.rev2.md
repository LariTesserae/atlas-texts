What lives in the Dry Hollow is not a collection of creatures occupying a basin. It is a dry congregation whose members are too tangled to be counted without listening. There are the root-mats that think in tension, the stalk-wrights that build in years, the shell-beings that crack and reassemble, the barking herd-lungs that give the valley a voice, and the small, low people near-people, perhaps—whose shelters lean against the living walls and whose lives are almost too temporary to notice, yet who are the only beings there that can name the sound.

They are inhabitants of a place that has no water, and because of that all their biology is shaped like mineral craft. Their bodies are not wet vessels but sealed constructions: pressed powder, resin-glazed plate, crystalline fiber, chalky skin, hollow bone, and the white threadwork of roots that can lift a slab of clay. They do not soften when alive. They do not rot when dead. They fracture, fold, grind, seal, and are returned to the living matrix by the endless patience of the root-mat. To live in the Underbloom is to be always under construction, even when you are sleeping.

The first inhabitants are not animals but the plants, if one can call them that.

The root-mat is the most basic resident, though it is difficult to know whether it is one being, many beings, or a place where being has become architectural. It is a pale web of threadlike roots so dense it holds the valley floor together. It has no central body, or perhaps it has thousands of tiny ones. It spreads through crushed gypsum, salt plate, and old husk, binding the valley’s bones with slow white stitches. When pressure falls upon it—a footstep, a grazing mouth, a collapsing pod—it tightens. Not instantly, but with a delayed, shivering intelligence, as if the mat is considering what shape to take around the disturbance. Holes made by burrowers do not remain open long. The root-hairs reach inward, carrying dust and fine shell fragments, and knit themselves into new seams. If a stalk falls, the mat closes over the place where it touched the ground, leaving only a faint ridge, a memory-line of collapse.

The braided stalks are the hills.

They grow not as individual stems but as bundles, twisting around one another, fusing at nodes, splitting their dry rinds, and then fusing again. Each stalk is hollow and packed with a fine powder that shifts within it like sand in an hourglass. When wind enters the valley from the throat above, the stalks sing, and their song is not melodic but structural. It tells other stalks where to brace, where to lean, where to bend into arches. Some braids have grown so thickly into one another that they become walls. Others arch over paths and meet overhead, forming vaulted chambers lined with dried flowering husks. These are not buildings made by animals; they are slow, living rooms. Occasionally a braid will snap. When it does, the sound is like a struck plank, and the powder within it bursts out in a small gray plume. The valley does not mourn the loss. Within hours, new shoots push through the wound, and the root-mat begins weaving the broken pieces into a fresh lattice.

The mineral barks are the more ancient-seeming constructors, though they too are unfinished.

Their outer layers are plates of white salt, ochre clay, and pale gypsum, fused by resin secretions until they look like armor laid by some dry blacksmith. Some have frond-like curtains that hang down from the upper terraces, each frond edged in crystalline teeth. They do not sway like leaves. They rasp against one another. Their surfaces are so hard that lichen-things crawl upon them in slow spirals, leaving trails of powder. At the base of many barks lie heaps of broken scales, like molted shells. The plants discard these scales the way other worlds shed skin, and the discarded pieces become the raw material for new growth. A young stalk may take salt-plate armor from its parent’s shed, fusing it along its outer fiber until it can stand firm enough to resist the wind.

The flowers of the Hollow are the loudest parts of the plant world.

They are not soft. They are not cup-shaped or trembling with dew. They are constructed like mechanisms: hammered plates, hinged husks, brittle petals edged with serrated salt, stamens of stiff fiber that hold dry pollen like powdered metal. When the sun reaches the proper angle through the opening above, a flower opens with a click. Sometimes it clicks shut again. Sometimes it remains open, facing the light, like a small white bone held up to the ordinary world. Their seeds are tiny shells, each one sealed with a bead of resin. They do not fall softly. They rattle when released, and when many burst together, the air fills with a sound like dry gravel poured over stone.

The insects, or insect-equivalents, are the valley’s first and most numerous voice.

The seed-crunchers are six-legged creatures no larger than a thumb, with broad flat backs and mandibles that work like tiny tongs. They swarm over the lower mats in moving sheets, their bodies clicking as they bite into dried seed-shells. Their armor is not grown solely by themselves; they gather discarded seed-hulls and resin fragments and carry them on their backs, so a swarm appears to be a carpet of moving pale flakes. When threatened, they do not flee so much as scatter into the root-mat, disappearing between threads and reappearing elsewhere as if they had been folded into the ground. Their bodies are dry and segmented, and when they die, their shells are taken by the mat within hours. A seed-cruncher’s corpse does not lie rotting in the sun. It becomes mineral.

The pale burrowers are thicker, longer, and stranger.

They look like flattened worms made of layered plates, or perhaps like unfinished ribs. They tunnel through root-mats, pushing up powder and leaving short, crooked holes that the plants close with irritating speed. Their bodies are pale because they live inside the fabric, but their edges are stained with mineral color—blue-gray from crushed basalt, yellow from resin, red-brown from iron dust. They have no eyes that a traveler would notice, or perhaps their eyes are too small and dry to be visible. Instead they navigate by the resistance of the threads around them. When the burrowers move through a terrace, the surface above them rises and falls in faint waves, as if something is smoothing a cloth from beneath. They feed on the inner powder of stalks and on the dead shell-plates packed into the root-mat. In this way they are gardeners, too. Their tunnels aerate the living floor, and their droppings—tiny pellets of ground mineral—become the raw grain from which new growth gathers itself.

The ashwing grazers are the ones that make the air look crowded.

They are small, fragile-seeming creatures with wings that resemble thin sheets of dried resin. Their bodies are coated in fine dust, so they seem like moving smudges rather than definite animals. When a flock lifts from a stalk-field, the light catches their wings and makes them glitter faintly, then dulls again, and the sky becomes a gray cloud that has not come from anywhere in particular. They feed on pollen and dry flower-powder, brushing the opened flowers with stiffened legs and carrying seeds in sacs along their flanks. Their wings are brittle. A gust of wind can snap one, and the creature falls, not dying, but folding into the mat, where its shell is quickly reclaimed. Their buzzing is not the sound of flight alone; it is a dry, papery wall, a constant whispering pressure at the edge of hearing.

The long-legged salt-walkers are among the most beautiful and most disruptive inhabitants.

They are thin, angular, and built as if made for balance on a shifting world. Their legs are longer than their bodies, ending in feet split into broad crystalline pads. They move across the salt flats with a slow, deliberate precision that makes their weight look almost accidental. Each footfall cracks a thin plate of salt, and the crack is small but audible, like a fingernail breaking against stone. They browse on arching vines and on the high fronds of mineral barks, stretching their necks in long, dry curves. Their bodies are not soft; they are covered in pale scales and resin-glazed plates, with hollow chambers along their sides that fill with air when they move. This gives them a slightly swollen appearance, like creatures made to resonate. When they are startled, they do not roar. They stamp. The stamp travels through the salt plates, through the root-mat, and into the stalks, alerting the whole terrace. Sometimes the long-legged creatures themselves become part of the construction: where they gather, their hooves or feet crack the salt in patterns that guide new crystalline growth, and the valley eventually builds ridges along the paths they have taken.

The barking hollow-beasts are the animals that make the valley feel like a single loud body.

They are larger than the salt-walkers, heavier than the stalk-thickets, and far more unsettling because they seem to belong to the landscape as much as they belong to themselves. Their bodies are dry, layered, and broad at the chest, like sheaves of braided stalks that have been taught to move. Their heads are not large, but they contain hollow chambers lined with crystal flakes. When they bark, the sound is not the wet bark of a dog or the trumpeting of a living animal. It is a hollow, percussive report, as if someone struck a dry log in a closed room. The bark carries through the root-mat. Other creatures feel it before they hear it: a tremor in the ground, a faint lift in the air, a quickening of the seed-crunchers’ mandibles.

The barking hollow-beasts travel in loose herds through the deeper terraces. They are not predators, but they shape the place through movement. A herd crossing a terrace can split the surface, cause a ridge to slump, and open a corridor where none existed. Their grazing is severe: they strip lower stalks, break arches, and leave behind a skeleton of white fiber. But the plants respond quickly. Within days, new shoots rise through the disturbed places, and the root-mat closes the gaps left by the herd. The barking animals do not destroy the Hollow. They edit it. They are one of the ways the valley decides what should stand, what should bend, and what should be folded back into powder.

The beetles are everywhere.

Some are flat as coins and move in spirals up the walls of the deeper hollows. Others are round, black, and hard as pebbles, and when a hand brushes them, they tuck themselves into themselves and become almost indistinguishable from basalt grit. Some carry resin lumps on their backs, as if burdened with little amber stones. When a hollow fills with beetles, the sound is like the rustle of dry cards. When they die, their shells become the walls of the next generation of burrowers. In a sense, the Hollow is always being rebuilt out of the bodies of its creatures. The dead are not left behind. They are folded into the pattern.

The moths are worse, in the most intimate way.

They rest in folds of bark, behind salt plates, and in the hollows of dried fruit-hulls. At first glance they look like parts of the plant. Their wings are papery, dusted with fine mineral grain, pale as bone or chalk, with edges like chipped flint. When a traveler moves too quickly, a whole wall of them explodes outward in a silent-looking burst, then becomes loud again as their wings snap and crackle against stalks. They are not beautiful in the usual soft sense. They are beautiful the way a dust storm is beautiful when it catches the sun: sudden, impersonal, and difficult to look at directly. Some pollinate the flowers, brushing pollen from one clicked-open mechanism to another. Some are trapped by the plants, drawn into resin folds and dried until they become part of the wall. Some are consumed slowly by the root-mat, their wings folded beneath new root-hairs, their bodies converted into powder that will feed a future stalk.

The lizards are the sharpest visible motion.

They are small, stiff, and made of scales like shards. Their bodies move with a dry snap, and they vanish into crevices no wider than a finger. They eat seed-crunchers, beetle larvae, and the pale burrowers that come near the surface. Their eyes are flat, mineral plates that catch the light without blinking. When a lizard is threatened, it may break off a tail-plate rather than a tail. The plate skitters across the ground and cracks into several shards, while the lizard itself is already gone. What remains looks like broken pottery. The root-mat receives it and begins its slow work.

There are also things beneath the root-mat that are too large to be seen directly.

Sometimes a section of ground rises in a long, slow curve, like a chest expanding in sleep. Sometimes a terrace shudders for no reason, and a cloud of dust lifts from the mat as if something below has exhaled. Sometimes a whole line of stalks bends in unison, not from wind but from pressure beneath. These are not necessarily monsters. They may be herds, or single vast bodies, or colonial creatures whose limbs are indistinguishable from root-systems. The valley does not explain them. The small people learn to avoid the places where such ground rises, but they do not name them as gods. Naming a thing in the Hollow is difficult; the ground changes shape too quickly, and the thing may be different by the time it is named.

Among all this, there are people or near-people.

They are not the center of the place, and yet their presence changes how the place feels to a visitor. Without them, the Hollow is only a roar. With them, the roar becomes a habitation. They are low-built and dense, with long arms, broad hands, and legs shaped for crouching. Their skin is matte, often cracked into fine patterns like dry clay, and it holds color in the same way the valley holds color: pale ochre, salt-white, ash-gray, copper-brown, with patches of red where iron dust has gathered in the creases. They wear no cloth that comes from soft fiber. Their garments are stitched from stretched resin-hardened hides, seed-hull plates, narrow strips of mineral bark, and woven stalks so fine they resemble cracked leather. Beads of copper, fragments of glassy crystal, and tiny carved shells are sewn into them, not for ornament alone but as counters, markers, and tools. Some wear small salt plates at the forehead, chin, or throat, as if reinforcing the places most vulnerable to wind and dust.

Their eyes are the most unsettling thing about them.

They are narrow, hard-surfaced, protected by inner membranes that look like thin sheets of mica or resin. They blink rarely, not because they are dry, but because dryness has made blinking a risk. Their eyes are often rimmed with chalk or ash, and their gaze seems less like seeing than like measuring a surface. They look at a stalk and know its thickness; at a salt ridge and know its weakness; at the ground and know whether it is still firm enough to step upon. They are not blind to the world’s strangeness. They are too intimate with it to be surprised.

They do not live upright as outsiders might imagine.

Their postures are compressed, balanced, and temporary. They squat against barks, crouch in doorways, lie along hollows, and climb by feeling the tensile strength of stalks before putting weight upon them. When they walk, they do not stride so much as test. Their feet are broad and calloused, hardened into plate-like pads, and their toes splay slightly to catch the shifting salt. A person stepping from one terrace to another may pause with one foot hovering, listening through the sole, waiting for the root-mat to answer with a stillness that can be trusted.

Their speech is a dry instrument.

A shout is not enough. A whisper is swallowed. They speak in a mixture of sharp sounds: clicks, rasps, hollow hums, and the grinding of teeth against mineral dust. Their language seems built to be heard over the constant rustle, not to compete with it but to fit through the spaces between sounds. When two people stand far apart on a terrace, one may strike a stalk in a short rhythm and the other answer with a foot-tap, a bark-mimic, or a resin-whistle shaped in the mouth. They also communicate by ground-pulse: a heel pressed to the mat, a hand slapped against a salt ridge, a stick tapped in a pattern too subtle for a stranger to distinguish from noise. Much of their meaning is not what is said but where it is placed: which arch carries the sound, which terrace reflects it, which hollow damps it into silence.

Their homes are few and apologetic.

A lean-to of woven stalks may sag between two living columns, its roof half-reclaimed by vines. A hut of packed root-thatch stands on a terrace, circular, low, with a doorway too small for a person to enter without bending. Inside, the walls are lined with shells, dried seed-hulls, and flat resin plates. There are no great rooms. No one in the Hollow trusts permanence. Structures are expected to shift. If a root-arch bends into a doorway, the people do not fight it. They close the doorway and open another. If a platform sinks, they raise it with fresh salt slabs. If a wind carries off a roof panel, the root-mat may begin growing toward the place where it fell, as if trying to incorporate it into the floor. The houses are not separate from the valley. They are knots in it.

Their shelters are built against noise as much as heat.

They choose hollows where the roar becomes layered and manageable, but never truly absent. Their sleeping places are often enclosed by thick stalks or piled with dried beetles and broken shell-plates, not for warmth—though the valley is warm—but to soften the tremor of movement around them. Children are born into this sound. They are not startled by it. They learn early that silence is not safety; silence may only be the instant before a ridge collapses, a pod bursts, or a swarm lifts. The first game is often not visual but tactile: a child presses a palm to the mat and waits to feel where the ground is answering.

They eat the valley without appearing to eat it.

Their food is ground, sealed, and reheated without the confidence of open flame. They gather seed-hulls and grind them into powder on stone plates. They press resin into cakes, fold powdered beetles and roasted stalks into them, and bake them between clay slabs warmed by the living heat beneath the floor. They eat fruit-hulls when they contain a dry, starchy powder, not flesh. They eat hollow stalk-bread scraped from the inside of braids, mineral salts in small measured pieces, beetle larvae when a burrow is fresh enough to yield them, and dried moth wings pounded into flour. Their bodies do not require wetness in the way outsiders might expect. Their mouths produce no dampness that can be noticed. Their chewing is loud, a dry crackling, and their faces seem always faintly powdered from the inside.

They do not hoard.

This is not because they are virtuous, but because storage is difficult. The root-mat will reach into caches. Wind will scatter them. Animals will open them. The living floor will grow over them. Food left too long becomes part of the substrate, and a pot left uncovered becomes a home for burrowers. Their caches are therefore clever and temporary: seed-hulls pressed into resin, pots buried shallow and marked with cairns, nets hung in places where a pod burst will not scatter them, dried beetles stored inside sealed shells. Even then, the place takes back what is not moved. A traveler might find a cache of seed-cakes, but by the time they kneel to touch it, the mat is already closing around it.

Fire is suspected.

The Hollow is so dry, so packed with brittle material, that open flame is not cooking but an act of reckless ambition. Their fire pits are shallow, walled with clay and salt, often unused or used only for brief warmth. Most work that requires heat is done with sun-warmed stones, resin focus, or the body-heat of the ground. If they make fire, they keep it small, sealed, and watched. A spark in the Underbloom is not a spark. It is an invitation. The valley’s dry sound would answer it, and the wind through the throat above would carry the danger into every braid.

Their tools are extensions of the land’s own hardness.

They make digging sticks with flint flakes lashed to stalks. They grind seed with stone hammers and curved plates. They cut resin and bark with bone awls. They fasten with sinew, dried stalk-cord, and the occasional softened copper bead shaped by patient hand-work. Their nets are not hung to catch fish but to collect dust, seeds, and low-flying moth clouds. Their platforms are built from salt slabs and cross-hatched stalks, low and broad, designed to flex when the ground beneath changes. Their containers are not bottles but sealed shells, woven tubes, and pots made of pressed clay, sometimes cracked with fine lines but mended with resin. They make no roads. A road would only be an argument with the mat, and the mat usually wins.

Their social life is scattered and mobile.

They do not build towns, though a valley can seem town-sized from the rim. They move with the seasons of growth and collapse. A band may remain for a time in a stable terrace where barks have formed windbreaks and the root-mat is deep enough to hold weight. Then the terrace tilts, or the stalks thicken, or a herd of barking beasts breaks the path, or the salt flats bloom into new crystalline blisters. The band shifts. They do not mourn a place. They leave behind a cairn of stones, a scratched mark on a bark, a bead embedded in a pod, and a line of packed stalks that points the next people toward a safe hollow.

Their children are tough and quiet.

A child in the Hollow learns to move before speaking clearly. The first lesson is to step lightly. The second is to listen to the feet of the animals around them. The third is to know when to freeze. A child may spend hours watching a swarm of seed-crunchers pass over a mat, or pressing fingers to a pod until it bursts in their hand with a cloud of dust. They are not gentle. They are careful. Carelessness in the Dry Hollow is not punished by a scold; it is punished by a collapse. The valley does not care if one is young.

Their elders are often the most still.

They can stand in one place long enough for dust to settle on their shoulders, listening to tremors with their feet. They know the difference between the vibration of a burrower, the pressure of a salt-walker, the slow swell of a deep body below the mat, and the dry shudder of an arch about to split. When they speak, they often do not raise their voices. They tap the ground with a stick, or strike a stalk in a rhythm, and the message travels through the architecture of the living valley. An elder’s knowledge is not a library. It is a map of cracks, and it is constantly being erased.

They are not rulers.

They cannot tame the Hollow, because the Hollow is already too busy being made. They cannot wall it off, because the stalks grow walls better and faster than hands can. They cannot drain it, because there is nothing to drain. They cannot store it, because it will not be stored. They can only participate in it: choosing the right time to cross a terrace, setting their huts where the mat is thickest, grinding the right seed, listening for the barking beasts, avoiding the hollows where the ground rises like a lung, and accepting the small, strange intimacy of living in a world that is always taking back.

Their rituals are shaped by this.

When someone dies in the Hollow, the body is not hidden underground, because underground is the domain of the burrowers and the shifting mat. It is placed on a shallow platform or laid in a hollow where the root-hairs can reach. The body dries. The people may seal the joints with resin, cover the eyes with thin salt plates, and press a bead or a fragment of a stalk-bone against the chest. They do not bury the dead in pits. The pit might collapse. The dead might be lost in the movement of the ground. Instead, they allow the dead to become visible for a time, then folded, then absorbed. After a season, the person is a ridge of new mat, a line of pale fibers, a place where the valley holds the shape of a body without needing a grave.

Their grief is not soft.

It is not weeping, not tears, not the gentle language of wetness. It is a rhythm struck against a dry stalk, a small cairn built with deliberate hands, a bead set into a wall, a name spoken once into the roar and then allowed to disappear. If a child dies, the root-mat may close over the place where they lay more quickly than it closes over a burrow. The people notice. They do not call it miracle. They call it the valley accepting.

Their hope is not grand.

They do not dream of a city that will stand. They do not imagine a temple that will last. They hope for a safe terrace, a season without collapse, a path across the salt flats that holds firm long enough for the band to cross, a pod-field thick enough to feed them, and a hollow where the noise becomes a kind of quiet. Their highest art is not monument but navigation: a carved line on a bark, a sequence of low cairns, a song that can be heard through the wind, a map made of notches that will change before it can be drawn completely.

A traveler passing through the Hollow might see one person first as a smudge, then as a hut-smoke shape, then as a small seated figure grinding powder beside a root ridge. The traveler might hear a click of tools, a dry cough, a foot stamping against a salt plate to test it. If the traveler moves too quickly, the person will not flee. They will flatten themselves against the ground, press both palms to the mat, and listen. If the traveler moves with care, the person may raise a hand and strike a stalk in a short rhythm. The sound will be swallowed, but the gesture will be understood: You are too loud. Be still. Be dry. Let the valley know what you are.

What lives there, then, is not a single species, nor an ecosystem in the ordinary sense. It is a dry society of bodies, shells, stalks, and sounds, all working in concert because separation is impossible. The root-mat is skin and floor and memory. The stalk-wrights are hills and roofs and arches. The seed-crunchers are the teeth that grind the world into future growth. The burrowers are the veins that carry powder through the living body. The ashwing grazers are dust made flight. The salt-walkers are balance made motion. The barking hollow-beasts are voice made mass. The beetles, moths, and lizards are fragments that move and multiply and become part of the noise. And the near-people are the briefest, strangest element: a temporary attention that knows the valley is not hostile, only too complete.

They live as if water were a rumor, as if silence were a myth, as if final form were a weakness. They fracture instead of wilting. They click instead of calling. They fold instead of rotting. They build their homes not against the world but with it, and they die not apart from the world but into it. The Dry Hollow contains no single creature that can be pointed to and said, “This is the inhabitant.” It contains a thousand overlapping lives, all of them unfinished, all of them loud, all of them dry, all of them answering the light from above by refusing to settle into darkness or rest.
