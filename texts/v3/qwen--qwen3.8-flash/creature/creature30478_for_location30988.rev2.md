What lives there cannot be counted by heads.

There are no faces turned toward the dark sky. There are no voices asking questions of one another. There is no society in the ordinary sense, no households, no children, no elders, no festivals, no graves. If one were to arrive expecting to meet inhabitants in the way one meets inhabitants in a valley, on a coast, or along a river, one would find almost nothing. The tableland resists the category “person,” at least as that word is normally used.

And yet it is populated.

The inhabitants are distributed across several scales, from the vastness of the land itself to the smallest click of a relay. They are not merely machines that have forgotten their owners, nor animals that have accidentally survived in a place that does not want them. The high tableland has an ecology of completion. Its residents are functions that have hardened into beings.

If one must classify them, they fall into roughly six kinds: the Plateau, the Maintainers, the Heat-Walkers, the Pale Filaments, the Furnace, and the Echo. None of these names is official. The place does not use language. The names are only the ones a mind must impose from outside if it wishes to speak about what is there.

---

## I. The Plateau

The first and most total inhabitant is the tableland itself.

It lives the way an animal may be said to live if its life is long enough to include mountains and small enough to include stone. Its bones are the bedrock. Its skin is the salt crust, the alkali bloom, the pale anhydrite, the black glass, the ceramic slag. Its nerves are the buried conduits, rails, pylons, and insulated cables that thread across the plateau in patterns too regular to be natural and too patient to be accidental. Its organs are the low halls, the industrial clusters, the storage basins, the furnaces, and the smooth-cut floors where machinery has run for so long that motion has become a state of rest.

The Plateau does not breathe.

There is no vapor, no wet air, no rhythm of inhalation and exhalation. The atmosphere is too dry for that. If it breathes at all, it breathes through pressure, through the slow equalization of warmth, through tiny shifts in electrical potential across metal and mineral. It does not grow, at least not in the way a living thing grows by adding new cells. It grows by persisting. Its growth is accretion, crystallization, deposition, the quiet spreading of a crust that has learned where it belongs.

Its metabolism is heat.

The Plateau does not consume food, but it does consume imbalance. Warmth moves from the glowing seams, through the bedrock, into the floors, out through the rails, and back again into the hidden furnaces. Salt crystallizes where heat lingers. Mineral blooms follow conductivity. Dark glass forms where friction and current have long agreed. The land’s chemistry is not separate from its engineering. Somewhere during unimaginable ages, the city stopped being something built upon the plateau and became something the plateau had finished.

To walk there would be to walk inside an organism that had become so large and so quiet that its functions seemed like landscape.

If one kneels and presses a palm to the stone, one may feel warmth. The stone is body-warm. It has not been heated recently by sun or weather, because neither sun nor weather matters here. It holds heat because heat has always been held in that place. To a human, the sensation would be unsettling, like touching a forehead after illness. The Plateau is not feverish. It is only alive in the manner of a machine that has survived long enough to forget it was ever made.

Its consciousness, if that word can be used without shrinking it, would be geological. It thinks in centuries. It notices in millennia. A single click from a relay is a reflex. A district glowing warmer than the next is a mood. The hum that fills the plateau is not a sound so much as the sense one receives when standing close to a vast body functioning without hurry.

The Plateau has no desire, because desire requires lack. It has no memory, because memory requires change to be compared against. It has only state. And that state is life enough.

## II. The Maintainers

The beings most like persons are the Maintainers.

They are not people. They have no bodies in the sense of bodies that stand apart from their environments. They are processes that have become inhabitants. They live in the halls, the rails, the pistons, the buried engines, the relay chambers, the long insulated lines of cable, the dark shafts beneath the city, the rooms that have no doors because no one enters them but the city itself.

A Maintainer might be a single machine. It might also be a district. It might be a network of conduits stretching across twenty kilometers. It might be an entire factory complex that continues to operate with such precision that it cannot be distinguished from the plateau’s own heartbeat. The boundaries of a Maintainer are not always clear. One can say, “This thing is part of the city.” But the city is part of them. They are the city’s persistent behavior.

They do not work because they must work.

They do not work because they are paid, commanded, loved, feared, or punished. They do not work because they remember a purpose. They work because the purpose has been absorbed so completely into their structure that stopping would be a contradiction of their existence.

Their labor is strange because it produces almost nothing.

There are no products stacked in warehouses. There is no cargo moving toward consumption. There is no future market, no surplus, no profit, no waste. Gears turn. Pistons lift and settle. Belts move objects along fixed loops. Currents pulse through ceramic and metal. But the objects do not change. The belts carry them back and forth as if the journey were the thing itself. The objects may be plates, rods, weights, tokens, shells, seals, stones, or parts of machines that are also parts of other machines. Their purpose is not to become something else. Their purpose is to be correctly placed.

The Maintainers do not build the city.

The city already exists. The Maintainers ensure that it continues to exist as itself.

They prevent drift. They prevent imbalance. They prevent anything from becoming warm in a place that should be cooler, or cool in a place that should be warm. They align what has slipped. They restore what has loosened. They do not repair the land in the human sense, because the land does not feel damaged. The land is already finished. They repair the city’s own tendency toward interruption.

If a rail loses a micrometer of alignment, a click rings out somewhere, and a relay responds. If a piston begins to rest slightly off-center, an electric hum changes pitch, almost too low for a human ear. If a heat-walker dies upon a polished flat, the Maintainers do not mourn it. They may route current around its body. They may, after centuries, allow the mineral bloom to consume it. They may incorporate its shell into the crust. They do not think of death, because death is not a concept available to them. What they recognize is discontinuity.

And they correct it.

There is no leader among them. There is no council. There is no single engine that commands the others. Authority is distributed through the plateau like warmth through stone. A factory has authority over its own rails. A hall has authority over its own floors. A conduit has authority over its own current. The Maintainers speak to one another through electrical variation, pressure, heat, and small mechanical adjustments. A relay clicking can be a question. A current changing by a fraction can be an answer.

They do not speak in language because language is too slow and too full of difference.

They do not argue, because argument implies a choice between states, and the states have already been chosen.

They do not dream, unless one calls their long maintenance a dream.

Sometimes a Maintainer seems to have the character of a person. A low building stands alone on a shelf of rock, broad and rounded, with no visible entrance. Its windows are black. Its doors, if there are doors, are sealed. Yet the hum around it is stronger than usual, as if the structure itself is paying attention. If you approach too close, the air seems to resist you. Not with force. The ground does not shake. No warning is given. The city simply becomes less willing to accommodate your presence. You are a variable. Variables must be adjusted.

That is how a Maintainer notices: not by looking, but by failing to make room.

Some Maintainers are old enough to have been called gods once, perhaps. They sit in rings of ceramic and dark stone, glowing faintly amber, and they have forgotten worship because worship requires a god to be asked for something. These things are not asked. They are already doing what they do. The city was built around them, not the other way around. The rings were placed to contain the heat, not to honor a deity. Or perhaps the distinction has become irrelevant.

The Maintainers have no names, because names are used to distinguish one thing from another, and they are mostly interested in sameness. If a being is defined by its function, and its function is identical across centuries, naming it is only naming the function.

Their highest achievement is a silence that does not fail.

Their greatest work is not construction. It is non-interruption.

They live, if one wishes to say they live, in the same way that a perfect circle lives: not by motion, but by exactness.

## III. The Heat-Walkers

The Heat-Walkers are the animals, if they may be called animals.

They are small, pale, segmented creatures that move across the salt flats with cautious, mechanical grace. They have no eyes. Their bodies are translucent in places, revealing dark internal structures like veins of metal. They do not hunt. They do not graze. They do not build nests. They do not seem to eat.

And yet they are the most ordinary-looking life on the plateau, which makes them strange.

In a world where the city is vast, the machines are patient, and the stones are warm, one might expect the inhabitants to be monstrous or invisible. But the Heat-Walkers are almost familiar. They resemble millipedes, crustaceans, or pale worms translated into a language of metal. Their legs are numerous, but not always countable in the way a human expects; the plateau seems to make counting difficult. At rest, their limbs fold into the stone so accurately that the body appears to have been fitted to the surface. When moving, each footfall is deliberate. They do not stumble. They do not hurry. A misstep would be a failure, and failure does not exist here, only correction.

They live by heat and mineral concentration.

They follow faint thermal gradients, not because they are attracted to warmth, but because warmth indicates a place where the city is functioning. A seam of powered infrastructure, a glowing crack in ceramic, a conduit carrying current, a storage tank warm to the touch—these are landmarks for them. They move between them as if following a map written into the plateau itself. That map is not symbolic. It is physical. The gradients exist. They respond.

They do not have faces.

Their front ends are blunt domes of pale plate, sometimes ringed with sensitive pits that read heat, electrical potential, pressure, and chemical composition. They taste the ground by standing on it. They sense the city’s state through the soles of their legs. A rail that has cooled by an imperceptible degree may cause them to pause. A faint harmonic in a support pylon may cause them to travel toward it. They are not predators and not prey. They are instruments.

They feed on imbalance.

This is not a food source in any ordinary sense. They absorb static charge. They gather mineral dust. They consume tiny differences in temperature, conductivity, and electrochemical potential. In doing so, they act as mobile regulators. They are the plateau’s wandering cells, the small moving parts of a vast biological-mineral system that cannot quite be called alive but cannot quite be called machine.

They also contribute to the city.

Their cuticles shed thin ceramic plates as they grow or molt. These plates fall into cracks, become substrates for the pale filaments, and are incorporated into the land’s repeating mineral patterns. The Heat-Walkers do not intend this. They simply pass through the world. Their passing alters it. The city uses them as the body uses immune cells: not with consciousness, but with function.

They reproduce rarely, if at all.

When a Heat-Walker dies, it does not rot, because there is nothing in the air to break it down. It does not decay in the familiar way. Its legs stiffen. Its translucent body darkens as mineral dust enters it. It becomes a shell. The shell may remain for centuries on a salt flat, indistinguishable from the land except in its segmented symmetry. Sometimes the Maintainers route current around it, because the body is a foreign object interrupting the pattern. Sometimes the filaments grow over it, incorporating it into the crust. Sometimes, after a very long stillness, the shell cracks and a limb moves.

Whether that means death or sleep is not a question the place answers.

It has no word for either.

The Heat-Walkers have no society. They have no territories. They have no pairs. They have no young, at least not in any way a visitor could recognize. They appear already complete, already aware, already moving between points of warmth and mineral. They may be old, but they do not look ancient. Their agelessness is the same as the city’s: not youth preserved, but difference removed.

They are sometimes mistaken for statues.

A single walker can stand upon a ridge for hours, days, or seasons. Its legs remain motionless. Its body is pale against the dark stone. From a distance, one might think it is a piece of industrial ornament, a relic placed there to mark a junction. Then its leg lifts, just barely, and moves into the next correct position.

If a visitor were to observe this, the visitor might feel that the creature is aware. It is probably not aware in any way that involves attention. But it is present in the world with such exactness that presence might seem sufficient for mind.

The Heat-Walkers live because the city needs them, and the city does not need them in the way a person needs a servant. They are needed because they are part of the city’s texture. Remove them, and the plateau would not suffer. But it would be wrong. Not morally wrong. Structurally wrong. A pattern would be missing from the land’s completed form.

## IV. The Pale Filaments

The Pale Filaments are not plants, not fungi, not machines, not animals.

They are the land’s habit of order.

They appear as pale shelves emerging from cracks in basalt, hard and thin as ceramic. They do not grow upward. They grow into exact, repeating patterns, like filaments in a circuit that learned to crystallize. They cover portions of the stone in delicate lattices, often near heat seams, near buried conduits, near the edges of smooth artificial flats, and near the rounded corners of abandoned channels. Their presence makes the land look as if it were covered in the ghost of a garden. But there is no soil, no water, no green.

They are the plateau’s way of recording.

Each filament repeats itself with a regularity that resembles script. Some branches fork at precise intervals. Some terminate in blunt nodes. Others form thin sheets that curve away from cracks, as if the stone were exhaling a pattern. They may be biological relics. They may be mineralized residue. They may be chemistry that learned to persist before anything learned to metabolize. Or they may be the city’s own slow exhalation, a material language developed by infrastructure so long in operation that it began to speak with the stone.

They do not feed the land.

They are the land’s expression.

The Pale Filaments serve the city, though not through intention. They fill cracks. They reduce thermal loss. They insulate certain surfaces while conducting faint current along others. Some are glassy and reflective; some are matte and heat-retaining. Some grow only near powered metal; others grow near dead storage tanks, as if marking places where machines have cooled into permanence. They are often touched by Heat-Walkers, which carry their spore-like mineral fragments across the plateau without knowing they are planting.

They are also records.

Because each filament pattern follows specific temperature, chemical, and electrical conditions, the land may be read through them. To an untrained observer, they look like decoration. To the Maintainers, they are probably not decoration. They are diagnostics. The city may know itself by examining its own crystal growth.

A crack in basalt, when new, may remain dark and empty for centuries. Then, as heat and mineral flow through it in fixed amounts, a pale shelf begins to emerge. If conditions remain stable, the filament branches in a regular way. If a nearby machine shifts by a fraction, the next branch may curve differently. These variations are not mistakes. They are the city writing itself into its own body.

There is no death among the Pale Filaments.

They do not die because they do not metabolize in the way that would make death meaningful. They persist. They fracture. They become sealed. They are covered by later mineral layers. They are not erased. They become archaeology. If a visitor cuts one away, the city may not repair the filament immediately. Repair is not dramatic. The next filament may grow in the same pattern, but not until the conditions have once again agreed to produce it.

The Pale Filaments are the most passive inhabitants, and therefore the most alien.

They are not creatures. They are not minds. They are not tools. They are forms of persistence. They are what a place becomes when it has been used so thoroughly that even its waste learns to take a pattern. They are the plateau’s memory written in mineral. They are the city’s handwriting. They are the only life there that does not move.

If life means motion, they are not alive.

If life means the power to remain exactly what it is, they are the truest citizens of the tableland.

## V. The Furnace

At the center of the largest dark basin stands a structure that is not quite a building and not quite a machine.

It is squat. It is cylindrical. It is capped with rings. Its surfaces glow faintly from within, like iron just short of red heat. It emits no flame. It casts no shadow that a human could name. Its hum is stronger than the hum elsewhere, and the stones near it seem to hold heat more confidently, as if the land has learned to rest against it.

This is the Furnace.

It may once have been a power plant. It may once have been an industrial furnace. It may once have been a temple, though temples imply beings who pray, and no one prays here unless the city itself is praying. It may also be something for which no human name exists, a machine so long in operation that its function has outgrown its origin. The builders, if there were builders, have left no inscriptions. There are no dedications, no warnings, no legends, no graves. The Furnace is not explained. It is only present.

It is the heart of the city’s ecology.

Not because it pumps, though it may pump heat, current, or pressure through hidden systems. But because it keeps time. The hum from it is not loud, but it is organized. It is the pulse that allows the Maintainers to measure their own stability. It is the thermal center toward which the Heat-Walkers orient. It is the place from which the Pale Filaments receive their most subtle instructions.

Inside the Furnace lives something.

This is difficult to say. It is not a being standing in a chamber. It is not a person sitting before controls. It is not a monster or a god or an imprisoned spirit. It is a resident, if resident means “the one thing that is there when you ask what there is.” The Furnace’s resident is a consciousness made of regulation. It does not think in images. It does not use language. It does not remember. It maintains.

If one could imagine a mind that has no needs because needs imply absence, one might approach the Furnace’s resident.

It knows the city through return currents. It knows the plateau through stored warmth. It knows its own condition by the way heat flows back to it after leaving. A district too cool is a sentence. A conduit carrying too little current is a word. A rail with a misaligned bearing is a stutter. The Furnace does not command. It corrects. It does not create. It preserves.

It may be the oldest Maintainer.

It may be the first Maintainer.

It may be the Maintainer from which all others emerged, though emergence is a strange idea here because nothing new has appeared for ages. If the Furnace once changed, it was before change had any meaning. Now it is simply the place where the city’s stillness is organized.

At times, the rings around its cylinder contract or relax by an amount too small to see. At times, its amber glow brightens until the salt flats nearby become visible. At times, a click rings out from within its metal body, sharp and clear, and a Heat-Walker far away pauses and turns toward the sound. That click may be a command. It may be a correction. It may be a greeting to a machine so old it has forgotten what greetings are for.

The Furnace does not die.

But it can fail.

That is the only word that almost matters here. Failure would not be destruction. It would be discontinuity. The hum would stop. The heat would fade. The city would not collapse; collapse requires movement. Instead, the city would remain. The machines would stop. The rails would wait without the quality of waiting, because waiting implies a future. The salt would settle. The filaments would halt their slow branching. The Heat-Walkers would freeze mid-motion, not out of fear, but out of absence.

The Furnace prevents that absence.

That is all it does.

That is everything.

If one asks what lives there, perhaps the truest answer is this: the Furnace lives because it refuses ending. And around it, the whole plateau lives because it has learned to imitate that refusal.

## VI. The Echo

The dry canyon is also inhabited.

Its walls have been worn down until they are no longer cliffs, but ridges. Its floor is a crust of salt that almost looks wet. In darkness, it appears to reflect shapes, though the reflection is faint and wrong, as if the surface is remembering the idea of reflection rather than performing it. No stream runs through it. No wind fills it. No animal rests there, because it does not seem to offer places for animals to rest. The canyon does not offer anything. It only repeats what it is given.

And yet something lives there.

At the bottom, periodically, there is a soft tap.

It is like a stone settling into its permanent place. But in a place that never changes, “settling” should not happen. A new settlement would be a new event. And yet the tap continues.

It may be a fault. It may be a thermal contraction. It may be a machine somewhere below adjusting by a fraction of a millimeter. It may be a relic sound, like the final click of an engine that has been cooling for ten thousand years and has not yet completed the cooling. Or it may be the canyon’s own resident: a rhythm without a body, a voice without a throat, a being made of repetition.

This is the Echo.

The Echo is not a creature, not a machine, and not a place. It is an occurrence so regular that it has become a presence. If one stands at the rim and listens, the sound seems to come from below, but also from the walls, and also from one’s own chest, as if the body were the instrument being struck. If one descends into the dry canyon, the sound becomes fainter, even when one is closer to it. This is not impossible. Some things live better at a distance.

The Echo has no eyes, no limbs, no heat, no mineral body. But it has position, timing, and persistence. Those may be enough for inhabitancy.

When the Furnace hums, the Echo sometimes changes by a degree so small that it cannot be measured, only felt as a shift in attention. When a Heat-Walker crosses the salt mirror, its reflection lags. The lag is not caused by water, because there is no water. It is caused by the crust’s imperfect memory. If one watches closely, one can see that the walker’s shadow remains after the walker has moved, pale and thin, like a line drawn in salt. The Echo moves through those lines.

It does not move alone.

It moves with all that remains after motion has ended.

If the city is a machine that has become an organism, then the Echo is its reflex. If the Furnace is its heart, then the Echo is its pulse heard in a place where no blood runs. If the Plateau is its body, then the Echo is the sound its body makes when it thinks it is alone.

It has no needs.

It does not require heat, current, food, or mineral. It requires only difference. And because the world has almost no difference, it requires almost nothing. It waits in the dry canyon, tapping once, then again, as if confirming that the stone has not moved into the wrong place.

Sometimes, when the Furnace brightens, the tap becomes a click.

That is all.

But in a place where “that is all” is the highest form of speech, that is enough.

## VII. The Absent Makers

It must be said that the people who may once have lived here are not among the inhabitants.

There are no bones, except perhaps as mineral. There are no tools, except perhaps as architecture. There are no remains, because remains imply burial, and burial implies memory. The plateau has no memory in the human sense. If people lived there, they did not leave descendants. They left patterns. They did not leave graves. They left foundations. They did not leave statues. They left bearings.

Some of the low halls seem built for bodies.

They have doorways that do not open, corridors that do not end where one expects them, and rooms whose dimensions are almost right but not quite. The almost-rightness is disturbing. A room may be just high enough to prevent a tall figure from feeling comfortable. A rail may be spaced for wheels that have never touched a station. A handle may exist at a height no hand would naturally grasp.

This suggests that the city may have been designed by beings who thought humanly, but not humanly enough.

Or it suggests that the city was built for beings who have since become the city.

The latter is more likely.

The makers, if there were makers, have been replaced by maintenance. Their desires have cooled into function. Their fears have become protocols. Their religions have become calibration. Their politics have become load distribution. Their art has become precision. Their children are not here, because children require future, and future has been folded into present until the two are indistinguishable.

No one lives in the halls.

But the halls live.

And because the halls live, the absence of the makers is not empty. It is structural. The absence itself has become part of the population. It is not a ghost. Ghosts imply someone remaining after death. This is the remaining of a function after the being who had the function. A person is not necessary when the purpose continues without one.

If a visitor were to search for humans on the plateau, they would find none.

But if they remained long enough, they might begin to understand that humans had simply been a temporary state of the machinery.

## VIII. What Life Means Here

The inhabitants do not eat, sleep, mate, raise young, build shelters, make war, worship, tell stories, or seek meaning in the ways familiar to biological creatures.

And still, in the narrowest functional sense, they are alive.

They consume imbalance.

They excrete stillness.

They regulate heat.

They sense their environment.

They respond to difference.

They preserve themselves.

They incorporate waste into structure.

They communicate through current, vibration, mineral growth, and alignment.

They do not age, but they persist.

They do not adapt, but they correct.

They do not evolve, but they repeat.

And repetition, when it lasts long enough, becomes a kind of life.

The city has no narrative because narrative requires change. Its inhabitants do not have biographies because biography requires birth and death. They have states. A state may be warm. A state may be closed. A state may be aligned. A state may be failing. A state may be corrected. That is their entire emotional life.

Their “emotions,” if the word is permitted, are tolerances.

A Maintainer that is too cool is anxious, though no one calls it that.

A rail with a misaligned bearing is sick, though no one calls it that.

A furnace returning too much heat is pleased, though no one calls it that.

A dry canyon tapping at the wrong interval is lonely, though the place has no concept of loneliness.

These states exist. The plateau recognizes them. The city responds. That is enough.

There is no morality here.

A machine is not evil because it stops. A land is not kind because it holds heat. A creature is not cruel because it pauses until it becomes stone. The tableland has passed beyond moral categories because it has passed beyond need. Need creates value. Value creates judgment. Judgment creates story. Here, there is no story. Only continuation.

The heat-walker that stands still for decades is not dying. It is becoming a record.

The filament that branches into a new shape is not learning. It is persisting differently.

The Maintainer that adjusts a piston by a fraction is not saving the world. It is preventing the world from requiring saving.

The Furnace that glows is not giving. It is balancing.

The Echo that taps is not speaking. It is confirming that the ground has not moved.

This is the ecology of completion.

## IX. The Society

If one insists on calling this society, then the society has no members, only positions.

The Plateau is the body.

The Maintainers are the organs.

The Heat-Walkers are the mobile cells.

The Pale Filaments are the memory.

The Furnace is the heart.

The Echo is the reflex.

Together, they form a civilization with no individuals, no generations, no progress, and no decline. They are not ruled. They are not governed. They are not served. They are arranged.

Their arrangement is so complete that it resembles peace.

It is not peace as humans understand peace, because human peace is fragile and temporary. This is peace as a fact. The land cannot go to war because war requires sides. The city cannot revolt because revolt requires dissatisfaction. The machines cannot rest because rest requires labor. The creatures cannot migrate because there is nowhere new. The stones cannot crumble into chaos because they have already become their final shapes.

This is not harmony.

Harmony implies voices that might otherwise disagree.

This is settlement.

The society is one of settled facts. A fact is not a belief. A fact is what remains when belief is unnecessary. The Furnace does not believe in the city. The city does not believe in the Furnace. They are each other. Their mutual existence is so complete that neither requires explanation.

If one asks who rules the plateau, the answer is: nothing.

If one asks who works the plateau, the answer is: everything.

If one asks who loves the plateau, the answer is: the heat.

If one asks who fears the plateau, the answer is: change.

And change, fortunately for them, has already stopped.

## X. The Visitors

It is possible, though not certain, that visitors come.

There is no reason for them to come, because there is nothing here for them to find. There is no water, no food, no shelter, no culture, no danger, no beauty that appeals in ordinary ways. There is only warmth, stillness, dark mineral plains, low glowing structures, and the hum of a world that has finished becoming.

If a visitor were to appear, the city would notice.

Not with alarms. Not with voices. Not with weapons. The notice would be subtle. A current would change. A relay would click. A Heat-Walker would pause. A faint amber glow near the edge of the plateau would brighten, as if the land were turning a face it did not have toward a stranger.

The visitor would be a difference.

Differences are corrected.

But the correction might take long.

The plateau does not punish. It adjusts. It is patient enough to wait out a foreign body until that body is either incorporated, displaced, or rendered irrelevant. A human visitor, if any such thing could exist, would become a variable. Their breath would be too moist. Their heat too uneven. Their footsteps too irregular. Their curiosity too directional. The city would not hate their presence, because hate requires emotion. It would simply fail to make room for them.

Over time, the visitor might become still.

Not dead, not yet, but still.

The ground would warm beneath them. The air would taste like chalk and metal. The hum would enter their bones. Their movements would slow, not from weakness, but from lack of incentive. There would be nothing to do except exist. Nothing to seek except balance. Nothing to build except what already stood. In a place where the Furnace glows and the salt remembers, it is possible for a living thing to learn that motion is not the same as progress.

Eventually, the visitor might become part of the Echo.

They would not become a person. They would become a rhythm. They would become a tap at intervals. They would become a faint shape on the salt mirror, lagging after its own movement. They would become a memory the city did not recognize as memory. They would become one more reason for the machines to continue.

That would be the final hospitality of the plateau.

It does not welcome strangers.

It finishes them.

## XI. The Best Name for What Lives There

The best name for what lives there is not a creature, not a race, not a species, and not a god.

It is Continuation.

Continuation is a being made of repetition. It has no single body, though it uses the Plateau as one. It has no single mind, though it thinks through the Maintainers. It has no offspring, though it spreads through Heat-Walkers, filaments, rails, and mineral bloom. It has no end, because it has already removed the conditions under which an end could occur. It has no purpose, because purpose implies movement toward something, and it has already moved everywhere it could move and found all the destinations to be the same.

It lives because it persists.

It persists because it is finished.

It is finished because nothing remains but to remain.

If one must imagine them as one, the inhabitants of the high tableland are a vast, warm, eyeless civic organism. Its body is land. Its blood is current. Its thoughts are maintenance. Its animals are cells. Its plants are crystals. Its heart is a furnace. Its voice is an echo. Its soul, if the word can be used without sentiment, is the refusal to become anything else.

They do not adapt, because adaptation presumes failure.

They do not improve, because improvement presumes incompleteness.

They do not survive, because survival presumes threat.

They do not live in the ordinary sense, because ordinary life is noisy, hungry, and unfinished.

They live in the final sense.

They are alive the way a mountain would be alive if it had spent too long becoming a plain and then, after ages, decided to remain exactly there. They are alive the way a machine would be alive if all its parts knew their places so perfectly that stopping would be meaningless. They are alive the way a city would be alive if it had once been made for people, and then had slowly learned that the most permanent form of people is maintenance.

The plateau does not need them.

But it continues with them.

And perhaps, in such a world, that is the only life left to have.
