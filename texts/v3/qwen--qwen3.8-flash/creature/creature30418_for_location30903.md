These living skins are tended like livestock—pruned when they threaten a joint, rinsed when they grow too thick, and sometimes scraped away when their color tells of rot, poison, or mineral sickness. The children learn to read them before they learn to read words: a black streak on old brick may be rust; a white crust, calcium; a sudden velvet of emerald moss, a sign that the wall has opened a new vein of seepage and must be visited.

The people who live in the bowl are people, but only a visitor who has stayed through several rains would trust the first impression. From the levee path, the settlement seems too quiet, too green, too saturated for a human dwelling. There are no dusty roads, no cracked fields, no dry courtyards where stones radiate heat. There are only channels and courtyards that shine like fish skin, houses breathing water from their joints, and figures moving carefully among the wet with the sure-footedness of creatures who expect to slip and therefore never quite let themselves fall. They call themselves the Bowl-Folk, though outsiders sometimes name them more crudely: Siltkeepers, Moss-Wrights, Canal-Dwellers, Blackbrick People. In their own speech they prefer a name closer to “those who have been watered,” because it does not sound like an insult and does not claim more than the place permits.

There are never many of them at once. Perhaps a few hundred, perhaps a little over a thousand souls, depending on season and sickness and the year’s trade. They live in clusters that are not quite neighborhoods and not quite families: a household might include parents, cousins, a widow with two daughters, a kiln-worker from the slope, and a dry-nurse whose body no longer tolerates the deep wetness. They call such a group a “roof-kin,” a unit bound less by blood than by the particular way a family keeps its rain from coming indoors.

Their bodies are human, but worn by the place. They are often slight, because there is no need for great strength in a town where everything is lifted by channel and pulley. Their shoulders, however, are strong from climbing wet ladders and hauling canal screens; their hands are broad and callused in strange patterns from gripping iron, stone, reed, and rope. Their feet are quiet. They do not stomp. They test the ground. They step around puddles not because they dislike water, but because a puddle may hide an open grate, a missing brick, a nest of snails, or a thin film of algae that could turn stone to soap. Even their children learn this before speech.

They dress in layers that are not quite dry and not quite soaked. Their everyday cloth is boiled reed-fiber, felted and washed until it becomes tough, cool, and faintly sweet-smelling with mineral water. Over this they wear oiled skins, sometimes made from the thin membranes of fish and tanned by fungal vats, sometimes from pitch-sealed canvas. Boots are important. The Bowl-Folk have a dozen words for the parts of a boot: the water-shed, the tread-root, the channel-cut, the dry-seam. A child is given a pair of boots before a full shirt, and if the boots crack, the family is said to be in danger. They do not wear fine wool in public, because wool holds damp and stinks after a day, but in the dry lofts they keep garments of cedar-smoked cotton, sealed in waxed chests, for visits, weddings, and the few days of winter when the bowl becomes almost unbearable.

Their skin is often pale but rarely clear. They have the cool dampness of creatures raised in humidity, the small scars of slipping on wet brick, and the dark circles that come from long nights when the canals speak too loudly. Many of them wear their hair close and bound with copper rings, not from vanity but to keep it out of their eyes and away from the living walls, where loose strands become tangled in root-weave and rot in the joints. The copper leaves a green stain around the temples, and a person with a strong ring-mark is understood to have spent much time climbing roofs. They smell of wet stone, tallow, crushed leaves, salt, boiled tubers, and, in certain workrooms, the sweet fungal odor of fermentation.

They sleep high and work low. A typical house has three levels: the under-void, the wet room, and the dry loft. The under-void lies just above or within the canal floor, a dark lattice of root-lined brick, pipes, sluice boards, and storage boxes wrapped in pitch. Children are not sent there alone, but neither are they forbidden. A useful child may be asked to retrieve a knotbox from the first channel, to check whether a seal has failed, or to listen for the slow tap of a dripping joint that has learned a new rhythm. The wet room is where living is done: cooking, weaving, sorting reeds, boiling dyes, gutting eels, mending nets, scraping screens. Its floor is usually clay or tile set slightly downward toward a drain, and its walls are often left unpainted so that damp can be seen and judged. The dry loft is the most valued space. There the family sleeps, stores paper and cloth, keeps medicines, and preserves anything that must not rot. In old houses, the loft may be reached through a ladder that lifts up after itself; in newer ones, through a spiral stair so steep and narrow that wet feet must be rubbed at its top by a bundle of dried moss.

Houses are not merely built. They are bred. Willows are trained around brick frames; alders are woven through openings; prop roots, encouraged and trimmed for years, descend through grates and knit into foundations. A house that has stood for a generation is partly animal, partly plant, partly mineral. Families speak of their houses as they would speak of aging relatives: “my roof has a bad left hip,” “my east wall has learned to sweat,” “this house only sings when the water is low.” They know every crack in its belly. When the rains come, they do not go outside to watch them. They go inside to listen. A leak is not an accident in the bowl so much as a rumor, an announcement, a small confession.

The Bowl-Folk are, above all things, workers. Their industry is wet because water is the medium of every task that matters. They dig clay from the outer slope and from pits outside the levees, knead it with canal silt, press it into molds, and burn it in black kilns that belch smoke across the reed roofs. Their brick is dark, dense, and faintly sweet with fired earth, prized by towns uphill for its resistance to floodwater. They make glazed vessels from mineral-rich silt, using lye from wood ash and glazes ground from river sand. They weave reeds into mats, baskets, boats, and filters. They pulp reed fibers into paper by hydraulic mills housed beneath the outer canal, where water wheels strike iron rods in a slow metallic pulse that can be heard from miles away.

They also tan. Tanning is both a necessity and a kind of quiet art. Skins and membranes are soaked in lime baths, scraped on inclined boards, washed in channels, and laid to mold in shaded frames. The mold does not ruin the work; it finishes it. Certain fungal plates turn a tough, pale leather waterproof; others give a green-black gloss that will not crack in rain. The tanning yards lie in the coolest quarter, where canals run slowly and wind moves through slatted brick towers. A stranger would find the smell overwhelming: lime, brine, rot held just short of corruption, and then, near the finishing vats, a clean bitterness of herbs, bark, and wet clay. To the Bowl-Folk, the smell is the smell of home.

Dye and pigment are equally important. From alder bark they draw a soft brown. From black brick dust and iron scale they make a dark grey that will not fade in damp light. From certain mosses scraped from old roofs they get a green that turns nearly blue when treated with lime. Red dyes come less from insects or roots than from rusted pipe scrapings and mineral waters that run in streaks under the levees. They boil dye vats in stone pots set into channel basins, where the flow keeps the bottoms from cracking. Every house holds at least one color-stained ladle, and some families keep old stains as if they were heirlooms, pointing to a mark on a beam and saying, “That was our great-grandmother’s first black-green,” as if the color could remember her hands.

Their food, too, is wet. They boil much of it, even when they could roast it, because boiling is a way of trusting water and making it safe. They eat eels and small canal fish, smoked or fried in reed oil. They raise frogs in shaded pools lined with moss and tile, harvesting legs for broths and eggs pressed into cakes. Snails, fattened under loose stones and in reed pens, become paste, broth, and stuffing. They grow watercress, taro, lotus roots, cattail shoots, and a pale tuber-like reed seed that is steamed into flour. Their grain is stored in sealed urns above the waterline. They keep ducks in narrow floating yards and gather eggs from baskets woven into the outer levee grates. They drink mostly boiled reed tea, sometimes with mineral salts, sometimes with fermented fruit that has been soaked and pressed until it tastes of wood and rain.

But the Bowl-Folk are not the only inhabitants. The place has its own dense citizenry, many of whom are considered as useful as workers and as familiar as relatives. Frogs live in nearly every structure: in roof trays, under floors, in cisterns, beneath balconies. At night their choruses form the town’s official clock. Some frogs are kept for food, some for insect control, some simply because a particular call has become lucky in a house. Children catch “roof singers” in clay pots with damp moss and let them hop across the wet tiles until morning. Salamanders appear in the deeper channels, their skins dark and speckled like wet gravel. Eels are the most prized fish; they are kept in stone pens, trained to rise at feeding bells, and sometimes released into the outer reed beds if they grow too long for a home. Small minnows and killifish patrol the cisterns and rain pools, eating mosquito larvae. Where fish cannot reach, the Bowl-Folk place baskets of pupae and dragonfly nymphs in the water filters, because even the worst mosquitoes have allies among the predators.

Snails live under loose paving slabs. Their shells are collected, ground, and used in plaster and kiln linings; their bodies are eaten, pickled, or boiled into thick glue. Beetles work the reed-paper vats, their larvae turning waste into a pulp-like slurry; some families cultivate certain white grubs as food for ducks, fish, and themselves, roasted and salted with a nutty oiliness. Water voles are tolerated but not loved; they are clever, greedy, and ruinous to stored fiber unless they are kept away with wire and bark oil. Rats are the Bowl-Folk’s great problem, more dangerous than any human thief because they can eat a ledger, contaminate a grain loft, or chew through a seal in the under-void. The most common answer is the cat: not the soft kind, but lean, quick, and raised from kittenhood on fish broth. Cats sleep in dry lofts and patrol the roofs. They wear small copper tags so they can be heard above the rain, and they are buried with a clay fish or mouse on their chest if they die in service.

There are stoats, too, in cages set loose near the outer sluices, their task to hunt rats that the cats cannot reach. The Bowl-Folk keep these animals short-lived but useful, and they do not sentimentalize them. A good stoat is a fierce, narrow creature that can swim and vanish into the dark under the levee, returning with red whiskers and a small victory. In richer houses, trained ferrets carry messages along dry passages, their collars stamped with the owner’s channel number. They are less trusted than cats, because they are restless and often steal wax seals.
