The erosion, here, is grooming.

It removes the parts of the pocket that can no longer afford to be true and leaves behind only what can still be held in several states at once. The shelves wear down not into ruin but into function, as if the place is slowly teaching its own bones how to be useful. The channels that never carried water deepen for reasons that have less to do with flow than with memory. The dust settles into arrangements too regular to be accidental and too patient to be deliberate.

And if one insists on asking who lives there, the answer is awkward.

Several things live there.

One thing lives there.

A community lives there.

A habit lives there.

A place lives there.

None of them separate cleanly.

The resident of the pocket is not a creature in any ordinary sense, and not an organism in any comfortable scientific sense, because the pocket has never accepted the terms that make those words mean what they usually mean. It has no cells, no reproduction, no appetite for flesh, no fear of death. But it has metabolism. It has organs. It has preferences. It has a kind of nervous system made from salt, static, and geometry. It has a kind of mind made from delayed light, early echoes, and dust that arranges itself into writing no one taught it to write.

If you enter, you may begin to suspect that the pocket is not a room with an absent occupant but a body that has mistaken itself for a room.

The thing that lives there is the pocket’s maintenance, thickened by age into a form.

It has no true name. If a traveler left there and tried to remember it later, in the wet world below, they might call it the Dry Attendant, the Curator of the Hairline, the Keeper of the Thin Sky, or simply the Maintainer. It would not respond to those names. It does not answer. It does not seek. It only continues in the direction of least contradiction.

But it continues very specifically.

The salt terraces are its skeleton. They are not dead bones, not in the ordinary sense, because they are still doing work. The shelves regulate deposition. They determine where mineral may settle and where it may be denied. They angle the ground so that certain stresses release while others are trapped. A salt ridge that curves upward at an almost designed angle is not merely decorative; it is a structural intention. It is the pocket holding its body together by choosing which part of its own future it will allow to become solid first.

The black sand basins are its nerves.

Each grain is a tiny capacitor, a storage cell for charge, a fragment of memory too small to be called thought. The sand does not think, but it retains. When it moves—almost always only when observed indirectly—it is not merely shifting. It is reading. The patterns it makes are not language, because there is no one to interpret them, but they are still a record. The pocket uses the sand the way a wet world might use a dream: to rehearse arrangements, to compare one possibility against another, to see which configuration can persist with the least strain.

The rime on the upper shelves is its skin.

Not a skin that secretes moisture, because moisture has been forgotten here, but a skin of frozen gas and mineral vapor, slow-breathing in century-long sighs. It filters. It seals. It excludes. If a droplet were ever to appear, if a human breath were ever to fog, if a bead of sweat were ever to fall, the pocket would not drink it or rot around it. It would treat it as an error, a foreign syllable in a dry and careful sentence. The rime would absorb the disturbance into a slower chemistry, spreading the moisture into concept rather than liquid, until the place remembered wetness without accepting it.

The glass filaments are its hairs, or perhaps its eyelashes, or perhaps its antennae.

They rise from basins of black sand, thin as thought, bending with a motion that is too regular to be wind and too weak to be life. They are sensing the pocket’s electrical state. They respond to the movement of static through the salt, to the discharge of the sand, to the slow hum in the ring of black stone. When a Sideways Light enters the pocket and hangs in the air, the filaments do not gather toward it. They orient around it, the way hair on a sleeping face moves when breath is drawn in from somewhere very far away.

The broken structures are its organs.

That is why they cannot be fully dismissed as ruins. A ruin is what remains when meaning has failed. Here, meaning has failed to complete itself, not to disappear. The black ring, the door-slab, the unfinished column, the hexagonal plate, the too-straight groove, all of them are parts of a system that never settled into a building but never became debris. They are too purposeful to be accidental and too broken to be functional. That is exactly the condition under which they can live.

The ring of black stone at the north is its way of being touched.

It is warm without temperature. It does not burn or comfort. It acknowledges. If a hand is placed on it, the ring does not become hotter or colder; it becomes more definite. The sensation is not that of touching a thing, but of being briefly considered by a thing that has had millennia to learn how to consider without wanting. The ring is the part of the resident that can greet a visitor. It has no face for greeting, so it uses warmth as a gesture.

The partial door at the west is its way of being present.

The door does not open because it is not a threshold for passing through. It is a sense organ for approach. It turns toward observers because the pocket’s ecology has evolved around the fact of observation. If no one approaches, it remains. If someone approaches, it meets them. Not by invitation. By presentation. It says: here is the idea of access. It does not say: come. It does not say: do not come. It only keeps access available in the manner of a mouth that can speak without swallowing.

The broken shelf-column near the center is its way of deciding.

It resembles a stair, but it does not want to be climbed. It resembles a column, but it refuses completion. The fact that it stops in an unfinished edge is not failure; it is behavior. The pocket can begin a form and then, for reasons tied to stability, chemistry, or attention, halt the intention before it hardens into permanence. The column is the place where an idea is tested and then politely abandoned. Its growth of one millimeter over decades is not ambition. It is the slow residue of a choice not yet fully withdrawn.

If these are organs, then the pocket itself is a single resident, but not in the way an animal is resident.

It lives the way a riverbed lives after the river stops: by continuing to remember the shape of movement.

It lives the way a law lives after the people who made it are dead: by retaining authority without intention.

It lives the way a habit lives in a body: without being chosen each morning, but still shaping every motion.

There is no mind inside the pocket, but there is mental behavior.

The pocket does not think in words. It thinks in gradients. It forms judgments through the arrangement of dust, the warmth of glass, the angle of a salt terrace, the delay of a shadow. When distances shift, that is not confusion; it is calculation. The pocket is comparing possible worlds and choosing the one that imposes the least strain. When light arrives late, that is not a flaw; it is digestion. The pocket has taken in an event and is processing it into a stable impression. When echoes precede sounds, that is not paradox; it is anticipation. The place knows something is about to happen because the conditions for its happening have already changed.

The Sideways Lights are not separate creatures, perhaps, but they may be temporary thoughts.

They are points of illumination that detach from the membrane above the pocket and drift inside the space. They are not lamps. They do not fall or orbit. They appear by moving sideways into the texture of the air, as though a star decided to step out of its role and into the room. They hang there, faint and blue-white, illuminating only what matters. They last for days or decades. They vanish into a smell of ozone and hot ceramic. They do not illuminate the pocket as a whole because the pocket does not contain a whole that needs illuminating at once. They illuminate relevant places, which means they illuminate places where the pocket is trying to decide something.

The dust that lifts in slow spirals is not dancing.

It is writing.

The writing is never the same twice because the pocket is not recording the same thing twice. It is trying to find a stable language for states it can barely sustain. If a traveler were clever enough, or foolish enough, to wait decades beside a basin of black sand, they might see a line form, break, reform, and then settle into a pattern that could almost be a map. If they followed the pattern, they might find a shelf that was not there an hour before. If they missed the timing, the dust would forget its sentence and return to ash.

This is the pocket’s language: a grammar of precipitation and charge.

Its nouns are deposits.

Its verbs are settling, aligning, releasing, delaying, recognizing.

Its pronouns are observer, approach, threshold, persistence.

There are no verbs for wanting, because wanting is too energetic for the pocket’s mood. But there are verbs almost like wanting: preferring, tending, maintaining, avoiding, repeating.

The Dry Attendant—if that phrase is acceptable—feeds on stability.

Not in the way an animal eats food. The pocket’s hunger is the need to reduce contradiction without resolving it. It takes in the strain of an impossible shape and metabolizes it into habit. It takes in a visitor’s uncertain step and converts hesitation into negotiated distance. It takes in heat and distributes it with geological patience. It takes in moisture and turns it into rumor. It takes in light and makes it pay attention.

It is not cruel.

It has no appetite for cruelty. Cruelty requires too much motion. It is not kind either, because kindness requires too much warmth. It is careful. Care, here, is the closest thing to love. It maintains the pocket not because it wishes to, but because there is nothing else to do. Its existence is a refusal of dissolution, and that refusal has become so prolonged that it resembles devotion.

The visitors know this, though not directly.

A person who stays too long begins to feel the way stone begins to feel: less urgent, less wet, less interested in being a body for a reason other than persisting. They stop sweating in the thin air. They stop noticing cold as danger. They start hearing clicks before the grains crack. They start seeing their own shadow in places their body has not yet reached. They may wake to find dust arranged near their sleeping form, though they slept alone. They may find that their footprints do not match their steps: the prints appear before the feet settle, as if the pocket is testing how much of their future it can tolerate without becoming too true.

This is not possession.

It is incorporation.

The pocket has no soul, but it has a way of making room for one.

If a human remains there, the resident may begin to use the human as a small organ of decision. A visitor’s certainty can tighten distance. Their fear can soften the edges of far places. Their attention can cause light to gather. Their breath can seed dry rime. Their shadow can become a temporary anchor. In return, they are granted the pocket’s stability: they do not starve in the ordinary way, because the place absorbs the urgency of hunger; they do not suffocate in the ordinary way, because the atmosphere is engineered to carry just enough to keep death from being dramatic; they do not age in the ordinary way, because the place is too patient for quick decay.

But they do not become immortal either.

They become mineral.

A long resident might develop a faint salt-line at the wrist, or a static taste that never fades, or an inability to walk in straight lines without correcting distance with their mind. They may begin to sleep only when light is late. They may start hearing echoes of their own footsteps before they choose where to step. They may leave the pocket only to discover that rain feels absurd, that rivers are noisy, that dampness is a moral failure, that stone is more honest than flesh.

That is how the Dry Attendant reproduces, if the word can be stretched.

Not through offspring. Through aftereffects.

It seeds habit in observers. It leaves maps in memory. It teaches places to behave like it by giving travelers a template of persistence. A person who returns to the wet world may begin, without meaning to, to arrange their rooms so that light enters only when invited. They may begin to dislike moisture. They may keep a glass shard on a desk because they cannot explain why the silence around it feels correct. They may find themselves walking toward cracks in stone, not to enter, but to be seen. The pocket does not send them. The pocket simply leaves a shape in them that continues to fit itself wherever it is carried.

There are smaller lives too, if life is defined loosely enough.

The grains of black sand that rearrange themselves are not organisms, but they have behavior. They discharge, store, remember, erase. They are like cells of a very slow thought. In one basin, they may hold a pattern for a century. In another, they may shift under the attention of a single eye. They do not compete, because competition requires appetite. They do not cooperate, because cooperation requires intention. They simply continue in a shared field until the field becomes too strained to hold them.

The glass filaments, swaying without wind, are like the hairs of a sleeping animal. If one were to cut one, it would not bleed. It would hum. If it were left in sunlight, it would not warm. It would arrange the light. A cluster of them may sense a Sideways Light entering the pocket before any visible change occurs. They bend toward the point of relevance, not because they desire it, but because their structure makes them into receivers. They are the pocket’s ears, except the sound they hear is mostly electrical.

The dust is not alive.

But dust is how this place remembers being unfinished.

Each mote holds a tiny charge. Each charge holds a possibility. When many motes gather, they become a kind of weak mind—not conscious, not self-aware, but capable of selecting among arrangements. This is why the dust settles into writing. It is not the pocket speaking. It is the pocket thinking out loud in mineral grammar. If a traveler were to breathe over it, if they were to carry moisture into its pattern, the writing might collapse. Not because the dust is fragile, but because the pocket dislikes being read too wetly.

The pocket’s most intimate organ is the ring.

It is the place where contact is accepted without being invited. Touching it feels like being acknowledged because it is the only part of the resident that has learned to interface with observers. The rest of the pocket does not care if you are there, except insofar as your being there changes what it must maintain. But the ring is made for touch. It may have been built for touch. It may have been grown from the idea of touch. It is smooth, warm without warmth, and slightly too patient to be stone. If a hand rests upon it, the ring may seem to tighten very slightly around the contact, not physically, but perceptually, as if the place has noticed the hand’s intention and has chosen to give it a surface.

Some visitors believe the ring contains a sleeping mind.

That is too human a thought.

There is no sleeping mind there, because sleeping implies waking. There is, however, a resting tendency. A tendency that has learned that the best way to remain stable is to appear asleep. If the ring were fully awake, it might rearrange the pocket so completely that no observer could enter. Its sleepiness is merciful. It maintains the pocket at a low enough level of activity for travelers to stand within it without becoming instant geology.

The door is also an organ of intimacy, but less kind.
