What lives in the hanging sea cannot be named first by animals. It begins as a skin.

At the upper edge, where the standing wall of rain meets the sky, there is a living rind coating the surface of the water and the basalt around it. It is thin as breath, strong as wet glass, and so interwoven with mineral that it seems more a condition of the place than a body. The veil-mats. They are not moss, not lichen, not algae, not anything green. They are colonies of microscopic organisms embedded in films of salt, iron oxide, sulfur, and silica, and they feed on the mountain’s chemistry the way other creatures feed on sunlight. They metabolize the stone. They eat the faint electric potential where quartz rubs against basalt, where warm deep water meets cold upper water, where spray dries and re-wets on angular shelves. They build a living membrane over the fissure. In places they are yellow as old teeth, in others blue-green like the inside of a storm, and sometimes nearly invisible except by the way the water hesitates on them.

These mats are not simply on the walls. They help the walls exist as surfaces. Without them, the water might slide down the basalt in ordinary sheets, but with them it clings, spreads, and is held in a skin-tight film. They produce microscopic fibers that bind salt crystals together, forming a kind of living wallpaper across the stone. When the fissure is disturbed, the mat contracts in slow pulses, like a creature flinching at a loud sound. When pressure changes, they exhale tiny beads of gas that rise through the upper layer of water and collect beneath the rain wall as a silver fringe. When a cloud passes overhead and the light dims, the mat darkens, its mineral pigments shifting in response to the loss of brightness. It does not photosynthesize, but it reads light the way a moth reads heat.

Beneath this skin, and within it, live the small drifting bodies of the surface layer. Pepper-glass animals. They are no larger than grains of dust, and their shells are so thin they seem like fragments of blown amber. They are armored against pressure, salt, and the abrasive current, but they move as though weightless, suspended in the upper water as if the entire sea had been gently stirred. They filter-feed on broken mat-filaments, suspended mineral motes, and the dissolved salts that settle down from the rim. In calm layers they hang in golden clouds. When disturbed, they do not dart away; they turn in slow spirals, a movement so graceful and silent that it looks like thought made visible. When a wave of pressure passes up through the fissure, the clouds of pepper-glass tighten for an instant, then loosen again, releasing a faint sparkle that hangs in the water like scattered mica.

Near them, but moving more slowly, are the bubble-grazers. These are the translucent spheres trailing filaments. They are barely alive by ordinary standards, or at least they are alive in a way that seems to have not yet decided whether it wants to be animal. Some are no wider than a pea; others are larger, swollen with filtered matter, and when they drift past a shaft of light they resemble floating pearls filled with smoke. They take in suspended particles, mineral dust, dead cells, and the tiny shed fragments of other creatures. In exchange, they release a thin vapor into the water. The bubbles rise slowly and pop against the rain wall with a soft crackle, as though the entire upper boundary of the fissure were faintly frying. In places where steam vents exhale cool mist into the water, clusters of bubble-grazers gather, feeding on the mineral cloud and growing into pale knobby mats around the vent rims. They are not rooted, but they can remain in one place for long stretches of time, trailing their filaments like jellyfish made of glass and salt.

The walls of the fissure are where the first visible animals live.

They are crabs, or crab-like things, but not as a sailor would recognize a crab. Their bodies are pale blue and translucent, so transparent that the dark viscera within them can be seen moving, curling, and settling when the animal shifts its weight. Their legs are long, brittle-looking, and segmented like rods of quartz. At first glance they seem too fragile to move through a world of hard basalt and glass. They are not. Each leg joint contains a small internal tendon of mineralized tissue, flexible when wet, rigid when dry. When the fissure’s ambient water becomes cold or agitated, the creatures tighten their stance and press themselves flat against the stone. When the current warms and slackens, they rise and begin to graze.

They move in sheets across the walls, often hundreds together, their long legs making a dry scraping sound that carries through water as a faint metallic whisper. They feed by rasping the living mineral films from the basalt. Their mouths are small and sideways, lined with fine crystalline teeth that are replaced every few days. They do not chew in the way a mammal chews; they scrape, filter, and reabsorb. The film they eat is full of salt, iron, and microbial matter, and so the animals are themselves partly mineral. Their shells are not shell in the ordinary sense, but living crusts built from what the water gives them. When they shed these crusts, the old exoskeletons drift down as glittering flakes, sometimes collecting in crevices like fallen snow. The shed remains are then scavenged by smaller organisms or ground slowly into powder by the floor’s grinding currents.

The quartz-legged grazers are the most industrious inhabitants of the walls. They do not dig, but they wear. Their mouths remove mineral films at a pace the mats can tolerate, so the surface never grows over too thickly. Their legs leave faint scratches that become channels for future biofilm growth. Their bodies collect dust, their molts become detritus, their dead become substrate. They are the first true animals in the vertical world, and they seem to live not for themselves but as maintenance workers of the stone. When a wall is too rich in mineral growth, they crowd there, scraping the surface until it glints. When a wall is bare, they move away, leaving it to be colonized again by the slow chemically active mats. They are, in their way, gardeners of an edible rock.

Older grazers can be recognized by the way their carapaces carry encrustations: tiny salt chimneys, rust-colored nodes, filamentous threads, and the minute skeletons of other creatures that have attached themselves to the shells during the animal’s slow crawling. Some are so overgrown that they seem to be part of the basalt itself, walking pieces of stone until their legs unfold and their bodies begin to move. Others grow malformed, carrying extra legs or fused plates from incomplete molts, and they are often rejected by the herd when they become too heavy to graze properly. These outcasts drift lower, where the currents slow and predators can pick them off with ease.

If the grazers are the working population of the walls, the filament-networks are the place’s nervous system.

They rise from the glass shelves as pale, branching structures that look at first like frost, then like fungal mycelium, then like lightning frozen mid-strike. They are not rooted in soil because there is no soil. They are anchored in mineral surfaces and fed by cold seawater percolating through the basalt. Some are fine as spider silk. Others are thicker, hollow in the center, and filled with a liquid so dense it refracts light like oil. They pulse. Not with blood, perhaps, but with something adjacent to it: a slow movement of charged fluid, a current that changes with pressure, temperature, and the chemistry of the surrounding water. When the current passes, the filaments emit a thin tone, a sound so high and clear it seems to come from inside the listener’s skull rather than from the wall itself.

These filaments are colonial. A single visible thread may be composed of many linked organisms, each one metabolizing mineral particles and excreting a crystalline sheath. Together they form a branching lattice that can vibrate, flex, and conduct weak electrical impulses. They are the strangest living architecture in the fissure. They do not build in the way a termite builds; they grow into the available space as a response to charge, flow, and particle density. Where the electric potential is high, they become denser. Where the water is more acidic, they curl inward. Where a basalt column has recently shifted, they may grow rapidly along the new fracture line, sealing the exposed stone with a pale rind of mineral tissue.

When the place is quiet, the filaments are still. When the pressure changes, they wake.

A wave of movement through the standing rain wall may cause the upper filaments to flicker with blue-white sparks. The light is not bright, but it is enough to make the walls seem briefly like a machine panel. In those flashes, one can see how the threads branch into one another, forming a continuous network from rim to floor. It is tempting to call this a mind, but a mind implies intention. There is no intention here. There is reaction, coordination, and something that feels like memory only because the filaments preserve the history of previous currents in their layered growth. Older sections are darker, denser, more mineralized. Newer tips are translucent and pale. When the fissure experiences a pressure event, the whole network may resonate for minutes afterward, not because it is thinking, but because the stone remembers through its living parts.

These networks feed the water as much as they feed themselves. They collect microscopic particles, trap motes, and release clouds of fine matter back into the current when they vibrate. They are both filters and generators. They create habitat. Small animals shelter inside the gaps between filaments. Larval forms attach to their tips and grow slowly in the suspended chemistry. Predators hover near them, not because they want to eat the filaments themselves, but because the filaments gather prey into visible clouds. In this way the filaments are forests without trees, factories without machines, nerves without a brain.

Below the wall-grazers and the filament-forests, in the midwater, live the eels.

They are slow, deliberate creatures with bodies like segmented rods of mineral. They do not swim with the smooth undulation of fish. Their movement is more like crawling through liquid. Each plate of their armor is grown separately, then joined to the next by flexible cords of tissue, so that when they curl around a basalt column or press into a crack, their bodies articulate with the patience of hinges. They range in size from slender strands no longer than a hand to thick coils as wide as a man’s wrist. The largest are often hidden, living so deeply within fissures that only part of their bodies emerges into the visible water, like roots growing in reverse.

They hunt by sensing voltage.

The hanging sea is full of weak electrical potentials. Where mineral films contact moving salt water, where quartz meets basalt, where temperature gradients push currents through narrow channels, there are fields of charge. The eels sense these fields with specialized organs along the sides of their heads and beneath their segmented plates. They do not chase prey. They hover near fields of filaments, near the edges of mat-covered walls, near the thermal layers where water changes density, and then they emit faint crackles. The crackle is not a sound first; it is a disturbance. Small organisms respond to it by being drawn closer. Motes shift. Young grazers lose their attachment. Pepper-glass clouds tighten and drift inward, caught in the electrical current like filings around a magnet. Then the eel moves.

Its mouth is not built for tearing. It is built for crushing. Rows of crystalline grinding plates open in a sideways cleft, lined with pale shards and backed by mineralized ridges. When an eel captures an armored animal, it does not swallow it whole. It draws the prey into the grinding chamber, reduces it to glittering fragments, filters the edible matter, and expels the rest back into the current. The discarded fragments are one of the reasons the deeper water seems filled with suspended stardust. Every predator in this place is also a mill, a grinder, a recycler. Nothing is allowed to remain whole for long.

Some eels live almost like plants, though they are not plants. They grow attached to walls or ledges, using their tails to anchor themselves in cracks while their bodies extend into the current. They are no longer mobile enough to chase even the slowest prey. Instead, they have become living traps, their filaments trailing, their heads oriented toward the main flow. They grow so mineralized that after many years they seem to be part of the basalt. Small animals crawl over them, mistaking them for shelves. When one is caught, the whole apparent shelf shudders, the plates flex, and the trap closes with a sound like a handful of glass beads dropped into a bucket.

There are larger predators, too, but they are not giants. The fissure is too crowded, too close-walled, too pressure-defined for large bodies to move easily. The top predators here are medium-sized, jointed, pale, and silent. They have elongated thoraxes and many articulated legs, but they do not walk on the floor so much as flow across it, pressing themselves into the spaces between basalt columns.
