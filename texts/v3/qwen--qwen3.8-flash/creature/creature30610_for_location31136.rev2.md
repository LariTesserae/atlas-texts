If you ask who lives there, you first have to give up the idea that inhabitants must be easy to count.

The pocket is not a crowd, and it is not a wilderness of obvious residents. It is a place whose creatures have learned to live spread out, almost politely, across a small world that has become too large for them and too crowded for any single life. There is no herd that stamps through the basin, no flock that darkens the sky, no solitary predator that announces itself by size. Instead, the place is filled by many kinds of living things, each one positioned as if by an invisible map: one beetle crossing a heated slab, one lizard pressed into a stone shadow, one pair of birds calling from different edges of a bowl, one long-legged animal drinking from a pool that may not exist an hour later.

What lives there is a distributed ecology. The residents are not merely plants, insects, birds, and mammals. The most complete description would include the roots, the moss, the dust, the mineral fibers, the water films, the worn metal-ceramic fragments, the thermal currents, and the old architecture that has become indistinguishable from geology. In a sense, the pocket itself is the largest living thing there: a place-shaped organism with stone bones, moss skin, and a metabolism of condensation, erosion, heat, and sound.

The first residents a visitor would notice are the plants, because they are the most patient and the most organized.

The ground is covered in pale grasses with translucent edges, not thick enough to look like a meadow and not sparse enough to look like failure. They grow in soft bands along the low terraces, sometimes in single strands, sometimes in little clusters that seem to mark invisible lines under the soil. Their roots are pale and wiry, branching in geometric intervals, ending in small swollen nodes that smell sweet when torn. If you dig one up, the earth does not crumble. It stays woven, as if the soil has been sewn together with mineral thread.

The mosses are more obvious because of what they do with light and water. They form cushions around stone hollows, fringes around shaded ledges, and bright green threads near the smallest depressions where moisture is likely to collect. They darken when wet and glow faintly when dried, giving the impression that the basin remembers its own weather. A patch of moss may seem to appear in a place overnight, but really it was always there, dormant, waiting for a few minutes of dampness, a thread of condensation, or the shadow of a cloud passing overhead.

The cup-shaped leaves are among the strangest plant-residents. Small shrubs grow alone on low shelves, each one broad enough to cover a fallen stone but never touching its neighbor. Their leaves are not only green; some have faint metallic veins, like copper threads under skin. The leaves turn during the day, not simply toward the sun, but toward the cooler pockets of air where dew will form. In the morning, they hold small cups of water. In the afternoon, they curl slightly inward to protect the moisture in their centers. When beetles cross them, the leaves tremble and release a few drops onto the stem network below.

There are ferns too, but not in thickets. A single frond may sit at the edge of a stone channel, unrolling after a change in humidity. When the air is too dry, it remains a tight spiral; when fog arrives, it opens like a small hand. These ferns are not decorative. They shelter tiny insects. They catch drifting seeds. They shade the soil enough to prevent it from turning to powder. In a place that seems so open, such small shelters matter.

The trees are the most conspicuous of the plant residents, but they are not a forest. They stand alone or in twos and threes, their trunks smooth and twisted, their roots visible as knuckles above the soil. Each one seems to know exactly how far it can grow without interfering with the next. Their leaves have a dull sheen, like polished bone, and their branches spread horizontally rather than climbing straight toward the sky. Some are rooted along faint lines in the ground, lines that may follow buried channels or the edges of old constructed plates. Their presence is almost navigational: certain birds nest only beneath them, certain insects gather on their bark, and small animals shade beside their roots during the hottest part of the day.

Below the plants, the smaller residents make the place feel occupied from all sides.

The insects are the loudest inhabitants, but they do not swarm. They are everywhere and nowhere at once. Tiny glass-backed beetles cross the moss with slow purpose, their domed shells holding droplets of dew like accidental bowls of silver. When they climb over warm stone, the droplets slide along microscopic ridges and sometimes run upward, pulled into tiny grooves before vanishing. Their bodies catch the light in small sparks, and their movement seems as if it has a schedule: feeding, watering, crossing from one wet patch to another.

Other beetles have pitted shells that mimic the surface of the leaves. When rain comes, these beetles sit very still, their bodies collecting droplets in patterns that make them look like pieces of living stone. Some of them carry spores from one moss patch to another. Some carry seeds from the grasses to the ceramic rims. Their bodies are small messengers between places that are otherwise too far apart for chance alone.

The crickets and humming insects are the ones that make the emptiness loud. Their calls rise in layered pulses, each species occupying a different register. Some make high, needle-bright notes that seem to come from the air itself. Others rumble through the ground with a vibration felt before heard. When they begin, the pocket does not fill with one chorus but with many, each one separated by pitch, time, and location. If you sit still, you may think you can map the basin entirely from sound: one voice near the north ledge, one near the damp stone bowl, one coming from beneath a worn slab, one from the mouth of a small hole.

In the ephemeral pools, larvae live brief lives. After rain fills a shallow hollow, the water becomes suddenly alive with tiny silver flashes. These are not fish in the ordinary sense; they are more like swimming threads of motion, thin and pale, disappearing when the water closes again. They grow fast, feed fast, and leave when the pool becomes too small. By afternoon, the water may be gone, but in the dark mineral ring left behind, eggs or pupae remain in the damp crevices of the rock. The pool has been used and discarded, but not wasted.

The reptiles are the residents most perfectly adapted to the pocket’s warmth.

They might be called leafbacks, not because they are beautiful, but because they look like the stone has grown a skin. A small lizard may stand on a heated slab for a long time without moving. Its flanks are pale and fringed with thin, leaf-like flaps that shift color with the light. From a distance, it is lichen, a patch of shadow, a broken scale of stone. If you watch long enough, you see it blink, then turn its head very slowly, measuring the air.

These lizards drink from dew, not from standing water. In the morning, they lie along the edges of wet leaves and let the droplets run across their bodies. They also use the built fragments as thermal instruments. One slab may hold heat after dark; another may remain cool because of a shadow or a hidden channel. The lizards move from fragment to fragment in narrow routes that seem almost planned. Their skin picks up mineral traces from the stone, and their droppings feed small colonies of lichen. They are not important because they are large or fierce. They are important because they are everywhere in a quiet way, and they help move water through the body of the place.

The birds are the most dramatic, though their drama is subtle.

They have broad, slightly translucent wings that glow against the ordinary daylight, not with color, but with structure. Their feathers show faint geometric patterns when the light catches them at the right angle, like tiny lattices of bone and glass. They land on the stone-metal rims, not because those edges are pretty, but because they are good for calling and good for listening. The birds are not flocking creatures. They prefer pairs or singles, spacing themselves across the basin. Yet their voices make the place feel crowded.

A single bird may produce a bright metallic note that carries farther than should be possible in such an open bowl. The sound rebounds from terrace to terrace, from fragment to fragment, and seems to come from three directions at once. The birds choose their perches with acoustic attention. A hollow in a worn wall amplifies a low note. The edge of a ceramic ring gives a high call clarity. A curved slab throws a whistle outward. The pocket is full of places where a voice becomes larger than itself, and the birds know them.

They nest in the built remains. The structures are not human shelters anymore; they have become stone-caves, dry bowls, and shadowed shelves. A pair may lay eggs in the mouth of a pipe-like fragment, not because the pipes are safe in any obvious sense, but because the geometry keeps rain from falling directly inward and keeps certain predators from reaching the inner rim. Their droppings are mineral-rich, and wherever they settle on ceramic slabs, pale lichens bloom within days. The birds are gardeners by accident. They do not tend the lichens, but they feed them.

The long-legged mammals are the residents that make the basin seem almost noble.

They are soft-coated, pale as ash or warm bone, with narrow legs and long necks. When a group of six or seven crosses the far side of the bowl, their hooves make no dust. This is because their feet are broad, lightly furred, and flexible at the tip, as if designed not to disturb the ground. They do not move like herds in open plains. They move like lines drawn slowly across stone. They lower their heads to drink from temporary pools, then thin into the distance as if they have only just appeared.

They are social only in brief moments. They know each other through scent and through the routes their feet have made. They leave behind faint grooves where water may collect, small depressions where moss may hold moisture, and trails of seeds caught in their coats. One animal may visit three different patches of grass in a day, carrying pollen or spores in its fur without knowing it.

Larger mammals live there too, but they are solitary or paired, moving in wide circuits through the basin. Their bodies are heavy enough to leave scent marks that persist for days: warm fur, damp grass, mineral breath. They do not gather in the open unless water and cool air allow it. They know the pocket as a series of shelters, not a single home. If you stand in the middle of the basin at midday, you may not see them. Later, near a worn arch or a shaded stone shelf, you will understand that they are always near, watching from the terrain’s memory.

The burrowers are perhaps the most essential residents.

They are small, round-bodied animals with soft fur and powerful claws, more mole-like than rodent-like, though they may belong to neither category. At dusk, they emerge from holes that seem too regular to be accidental. The holes are often circular at first glance, but their edges are lined with fine fibers, as if the burrower wove the soil into a rim before entering. Some holes sit at the base of a moss patch. Others surround a slight depression in the stone. Others are placed along invisible lines where water must move through the substrate.

These burrowers create the pocket’s micro-wells. A single hole, no wider than a hand, can collect condensation and keep it damp longer than the bare stone would. Around that hole, lichens and mosses begin to gather. Insect larvae may later fall into the moisture. Seeds may lodge in the soft edge. The burrower itself eats mineral-rich soil, insects, and perhaps the sweet nodes at the ends of root networks. Its body carries spores in its fur. Its movement mixes dust, salt, and living fibers, maintaining the textile of the ground.

If you looked too closely at the soil beneath the green, you would see that it is not ordinary dirt. It is layered and threaded, packed with pale filaments finer than hair. Some of these are roots. Some may be fungal. Some may be mineral. Some seem to belong to no category you have a word for. They are woven into the built remnants, through ceramic pores and metal grooves, forming a network that is both ecological and artificial. This network is not just what lives in the place. It is the place’s way of holding itself together.

The built fragments themselves become residents, not because they are alive in the human sense, but because they behave like organs.

A curved slab of stone-metal may be half-sunk in the ground, but it holds heat and directs wind. A ring of black ceramic fused to a hillside may collect dew and channel it into a root line. A short wall with no corners may be worn by feet and breath, yet still alter the sound of a bird call. A series of low steps leading nowhere may slow runoff, trap soil, and shade the moss that grows beside it. These objects do not glow or hum, but they have a presence so ancient and functional that removing one in the mind’s eye feels like damaging the basin.

Some surfaces glitter with an oil-like film under sunlight. Others absorb light so deeply that they appear to be made of compacted shadow. Water beads on them in slow, strange droplets that sometimes crawl upward along microscopic grooves. The patterns on them, when magnified, are too regular to be erosion. They look like channels, lattices, hinges, sensors, or something older than human categories. They are worn beyond usefulness and yet they are still useful.

This is the great strangeness of the pocket: the technology has not been abandoned. It has been absorbed. The life of the place has grown around it, through it, and perhaps because of it. The animals drink at its edges. The roots follow its lines. The insects nest in its pits. The birds amplify their songs against its curves. The moss brightens where its surfaces retain the right moisture. The stone shelves release heat because the built materials were made to release heat. The land has not defeated the machine. The machine has become a quiet law of the land.

So if you ask who lives there, one answer is: many residents, none of them rulers.

Another answer is: a distributed being.

The pocket’s true inhabitant is the system itself. It has no face, no voice in any human sense, no intention you could point to. But it has habits. It has preferences. It has a way of moving water, a way of storing warmth, a way of choosing where moss appears, a way of deciding which hollow should amplify a bird and which hole should catch dew. If you observe long enough, you begin to feel that the basin is not merely inhabited. It is acting.

It acts at dawn, when the stone releases stored heat and fog crawls just above the ground. It acts when the leaves open to collect moisture. It acts when the insects begin their layered song. It acts when the lizards climb onto warm slabs. It acts when the long-legged animals arrive at a pool that appeared after rain and will vanish by midday. It acts when the burrowers loosen the soil and the wind moves dust across the terraces in visible veils.

This being does not panic. That is part of its strangeness. Even the rain does not seem like an event but like a recurring instruction. The wind does not disturb; it adjusts. The animals do not flee from the emptiness; they move through it as if it were full of hidden roads. The trees do not compete for space because the space has already been divided by water, heat, and structure. The ecology is lush, but it does not pile itself in one corner. It breathes across the whole pocket.

There is no evidence of people living there now. There are no fires, no voices, no recent footprints, no signs of maintenance. If a builder species once shaped this place, it has become absence, geology, and function. Its works remain not as monuments but as bones. The living inhabitants have inherited the bones and taught the world to move through them.

The place does not feel haunted. It feels used.

If you stayed long enough, you would stop trying to find the most impressive animal. You would notice instead how each creature seems to be doing a small job. The beetles carry dew. The birds spread mineral deposits. The burrowers maintain the holes. The lizards regulate their bodies against the slabs and in turn fertilize the lichen. The plants hold the soil and mark the water routes. The roots read the buried lattice. The wind reads the fragments. The stones remember the rain.

If you removed one species, not violently, but simply as a thought experiment, the basin would change. Remove the burrowers, and the condensation holes would lose their woven rims. Remove the birds, and the ceramic slabs would grow dull because the mineral dustings would slow. Remove the beetles, and certain moss patches would become dry islands. Remove the leafback lizards, and the warm stones would lose one of their living scales. Remove one fragment, and the moisture might move differently, the wind might sharpen, and the insects might scatter from their old route.

This is what lives there: not a collection, but a set of mutual dependencies so old that it has become landscape.

The creatures are abundant without being visible in crowds. They are loud without seeming urgent. They are scattered without seeming lost. They do not own the basin. They are the basin’s ways of continuing.

The pocket itself is the final resident, and perhaps the oldest. It lives in its erosion. It lives in its water that appears, hesitates, and leaves. It lives in its sound that seems larger than the small space around it. It lives in its warmth that rises from no obvious source. It lives in the way the leaves turn to moisture instead of light, the way the stones hold heat after sunset, the way the moss brightens within hours, the way the animals move in patterns that seem more architectural than instinctive.

If you walked through it for an hour, you would see very little and hear almost everything. You would learn that life here is not centered. It is distributed. You would notice a ridge of mineral crust where water passed, a circle of moss around a shallow scar, a dark ring where a pool closed, a line of insects moving around a half-buried arch as if following an invisible current. You would feel that the emptiness is not empty at all. It is merely waiting for you to learn how to notice its residents.

And if you stood in the middle of the basin and called out, your voice would not simply echo. Some of it would be caught and redirected by the old stones, the worn rings, the hollows, the micro-grooves. It would return softened, changed, partly lost. That would be the clearest sign of what lives there.

The pocket does not have one inhabitant.

It is inhabited by a process.

A process that has taken shape, grown moss, learned to sing, and refused to collapse.
