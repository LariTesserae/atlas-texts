If the question is **who** lives in this basin, the first honest answer is: *no one*. There are no people, no herd animals, no pack, no colony in the usual dense, place-making sense. There is no owner, no shepherd, no spirit sitting at the center. The basin has no capital. It has no village. It has no obvious resident.

But if the question is **what** lives there, the answer changes completely. The answer becomes so many things that they can no longer be seen as individuals. It becomes a slow, distributed occupation of warm stone and wet ground by creatures so small, so scattered, and so well adapted to waiting that their presence is usually invisible until you know where to look.

The true citizens of this place are the ones who live as films, skins, and stains.

Before anything green can claim a stone, before insects can rise or fish can hold station in a warm channel, there are microbes. They live in the thin water that stands on silica shelves, in the orange bloom spreading across black basalt, in the white-yellow glaze around steam vents, in the black silt where warm water has seeped through cracks from below. Some of them eat iron. Some eat sulfur. Some build silica into skins. Some fix carbon out of thin air and mineral water, making the first crude living substance of the land. They do not look like life because they are so much like color: a faint rust on wet rock, a pale yellow edge around a vent, a blue-green smear in a warm pool, a matte white crust where water has cooled. They work slowly. They build in millimeters. They are the basin’s first architects, and they are still building.

The mats are not just living paint. They are ecosystems. A thin sheet of microbial film may contain bacteria, cyanobacteria, algae, diatoms, rotifers, and the eggs of insects too small to see with the naked eye. If you press your finger into a seep-margin mat, you are not touching mud only; you are touching a neighborhood. Water trembles in the capillaries. Microscopic grazers move through it. The surface may taste faintly metallic. When the wind passes, the mat holds a breath of warmth from the water beneath it. When frost comes, it contracts and cracks, but does not vanish. It remains attached to the stone, waiting for the next thin water, the next warm pulse.

Above these microbial skins come the green things, but they too are cautious. There are no forests here, no bushes, no thickets, no fields of tall grass. The vegetation looks like it is trying, not succeeding in the ordinary sense. It is low and patchy, gathered around heat and moisture in tight little experiments.

Lichens are among the first to take the rock. They are not decoration. They are pioneers, spreading across basalt and pumice with slow patience, eating wind-borne dust, holding moisture, dissolving stone into something that can become soil. A lichen crust on a warm ledge may be green-black, yellow, cream, or rust-colored. If you look closely, it is crowded. Tiny arthropods move among its lobes. Water gathers in its grooves. It shelters mites and springtails, and its spores and fragments drift across the basin when wind lifts them.

Moss is richer life, but still humble. Around the edges of seeps and channels, moss makes small cushions and dark green mats, hugging the wettest places. It looks like velvet until touched, then it becomes wet, dense, and alive with movement. Within a square yard of moss there are whole invisible communities: springtails that burst through the surface like grains of white pepper, mites no larger than dust, rotifers swimming in the films of water between leaves, eggs of insects attached to the stems. Moss holds water after storms, feeds larvae, traps wind-borne organic matter, and shelters animals that could not survive the open flats.

Cushion plants are more ambitious, but still modest. They spread sideways, not upward, forming dense mats or low domes over stones, often near warm runoff. Their leaves may be small, leathery, pale green or tinged with red or bronze. Their flowers, if they bloom at all, are tiny, often hidden among the leaves, pollinated by cold-adapted insects that rise briefly from the silt or the moss. They conserve heat, reduce wind damage, and create microclimates where other life can exist. Near a cushion plant, the air is a degree or two warmer. Under it, moisture lingers.

Sedge and rush appear only in the wettest hollows, where the water is shallow but always present. They stand in thin, dark green blades, sometimes almost black where mineral-rich water stains them. They are not lush. They do not form marshes in the ordinary sense, with tall stems and open water. They form sparse fringe communities at the edges of ponds and thermal runnels, their roots barely threading the saturated mud. In the wind they rustle softly, but only if you are close enough to hear it.

The insects are one of the basin’s strongest hidden populations. They do not arrive as clouds everywhere, only where warmth and water make conditions possible. Midges rise and fall above the thermal edges in dense, shimmering swarms when the water is warm and still. Their hum is so low that it can be felt as much as heard. At the surface of warm channels, eggs are attached to silica shelves or mineral ridges, and larvae drift in the water, feeding on microbial films and algae. Some are pale, some translucent, some with dark heads or reddish bodies carrying oxygen in cold, mineral-rich water. Others burrow into the black silt, making tiny galleries that ventilate the ground.

If you stand near a shallow pond at the right moment, the surface may break with emergences: small flies, midges, and other insects rising from the water in bursts. They do not appear at random. They respond to steam movement, light, temperature, pressure, and the timing of condensation. A warm pulse from a vent can trigger a bloom of insects. A mist falling from the sky can darken the ground, then a brief clearing can send thousands of tiny wings into the air. They are the basin’s sudden confetti of life, then gone, then returning.

Beetles live where there is decay. They move through moss, silt, and dead organic matter, feeding on fungi, larvae, and plant fragments. Some are small, dark, and quick in the water films; others live underground in pumice and clay, hunting or scavenging. In the dry benches, where wind scours the stone, tiny arthropods still live in sheltered cracks, waiting for moisture from vapor. They are not abundant where you can see them, but the ground is threaded with their presence.

In the water itself, there are animals more visible than microbes and less visible than fish: crustaceans, worms, and larvae. Some move through warm runnels on slender legs, grazing microbial mats. Others burrow through saturated silt, taking oxygen from water that trickles through mineral grains. Shallow ponds may contain snails or freshwater limpets, small and hard to notice, clinging to rocks where algae grows. In the spring-fed channels, there may be pale, translucent worms that live in the silt and become active when warmer water flows over them. Life here is not dramatic, but it is complete: there are grazers, predators, scavengers, and builders, even if most of them are smaller than a fingernail.

Then there are the fish.

They live in the warmest channels and the shallow spring-fed flats, where mineral water keeps the bottom from freezing completely. They are small, pale, and quick, adapted to a strange environment of cold surface air and warm water below. Their bodies may be almost too bright against the dark silt and black clay: silver, faintly pinkish, or translucent at the belly, with eyes that look large in dim light. They are not schooling so much as station-keeping: they hover behind silica shelves, in the mouths of runnels, or in the slight warm plumes where geothermal water enters a pond. When the steam shifts, they shift with it. When cold tightens the surface, they sink toward warmer layers. When the water chemistry changes, they read it through lateral lines and behavior rather than sight.

Their reproduction is quiet and hidden. Eggs may be attached to smooth mineral surfaces, sheltered in shallow runnels, or deposited in warm silt where oxygen is enough but predators are fewer. The larvae drift into margins where microbial mats provide food. Some remain in thermal channels year-round. Others are washed into meltwater pools that may dry, freeze, or vanish. The fish are not abundant in the way a lake is abundant. They are abundant in the way a thread is continuous: one small life here, another there, each connected to the same system of heat and water.

Birds live in the basin mostly as arrivals, departures, and occasional presence. There are shorebirds along the meltwater margins, pale-legged and watchful, moving in careful steps through the black silt. They probe for insects, worms, and larvae. They are not loud. Their calls are thin and far, carrying across steam and stone in short bursts. They may nest on flat stones, gravel shelves, or sheltered hollows, sometimes near warm seeps where the ground thaws earlier or stays damp longer. Their eggs are cryptic: brown, green, or speckled, blending with mineral crusts, wet basalt, and pale pumice.

Above the vent fields, raptors ride the thermals. They are not always visible because they circle high in thin air, where the heat from below rises and the steam drifts sideways. Sometimes they appear as black crosses against the dim sky, turning slowly. They hunt small mammals, birds, and carrion, but they do not need dense populations to survive. They are enough. Their presence is a sign of the whole system: heat rises, life gathers, and predators read the margins where weakness or death becomes opportunity.

Scavengers patrol the cliffs and flats. There are birds like ravens or crows, dark against pale stone, curious about movement, carcasses, and anything the weather leaves behind. They may circle a vent field for a long time, watching steam, watching other birds, watching nothing. They land on basalt shelves that may still be warm. They may ruffle feathers in mist, shake water from their wings, and stand motionless while static crackles faintly around them. They take whatever the basin produces: eggs, fish killed by sudden cold, insects caught in ice skins, carrion left by predators, or the remains of animals lost in windstorms. They are not mourners; they are reminders that death is also part of the circulation.

In the open ground, there are small hare-like mammals. They live in burrows and hollows beneath pumice, clay, and frost-heaved stone, especially near geothermal warmth. Their bodies are compact, their ears short, their fur pale or dark depending on the terrain. They graze moss, sedge, cushion plants, and whatever tender growth finds space near the heat. They are cautious, often invisible until they freeze in the middle of a flat, or until they suddenly hop over a crack in the ground. Their droppings near vents tell of overnight shelter: an animal that stayed close to warmth when the wind turned.

Burrowers are important here. Some mammals dig into wind-polished benches, using cracks between rock faces and pumice to make dens. Others, perhaps like pika-like grazers or marmot-like burrowers, store food in sheltered places, though in such a young, sparse landscape that behavior may be limited. The ground itself is unstable: pumice crumbles, clay cracks, steam warms the underside of rocks. Burrowing life benefits from that instability. A warm seam in the earth can shelter a nest through a cold season. A collapsed vent channel can become a den. A shelf of silica can provide a roof.

There may be a small mustelid-like hunter in the water margins: a sleek, short-tailed predator that follows fish and insects through warm runnels, using steam as cover and mineral shelves as ambush points. It is not large, but it is precise. It listens for movement in silt, dips into shallow channels, and catches the small aquatic animals that feed on microbial mats. It would leave little trace except tracks, a few disturbed eggs, or a body half-carried away.

Farther out, herds move across the open flats so thinly that they look like drifting dust. They are not dense, not loud, not clearly visible against the empty horizon. They may be caribou-like, antelope-like, or some other cold-plateau ungulate that reads steam from a distance and knows where warmth gathers. They lick mineral deposits, drink from shallow pools, and rest on benches where wind is less severe. Their presence changes the vegetation: they graze, they trample, they spread seeds and spores through mud, and they leave paths that are not paths but temporary compressions of moss and silt. When the weather shifts, they shift. When the steam drifts, they read it as weather and danger and possible water.

Predators live at the margins because the basin supports them just enough. There may be fox-like hunters, lynx-like stalkers, or wolf-like animals passing through in distant bands. They do not need large territories because the system is rich in small, scattered life: hare, birds, fish, insects, eggs, carrion, and the remains of those killed by cold or mist. They are patient. They do not chase across open ground unless they must. They wait near vent fields, meltwater edges, and mineral shelves where prey must come to drink or warm itself. They hunt at dawn and dusk, when the light is dim and the steam hides movement. Their tracks may disappear into cracks or dissolve in frost. A predator’s path is not a road; it is a hypothesis, tested and erased.

In this place, carrion is a whole event. A hare may lie on a wind-polished bench, half-covered in silt, and for days become a city. First insects arrive: flies, beetles, and tiny parasites. Then birds, then a scavenger with a long neck, then perhaps a predator returning to finish what it cannot resist. Microbes work through the tissues. Water moves through the remains. Steam condenses onto fur and bone. Frost may close the scene for a night, then thaw in a warm pulse and resume the work. The carcass becomes mineral, then dust, then food chain, then soil, then moss. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is honored. It simply enters circulation.

The strange abundance of the basin is not visible as abundance. It is distributed across time. There may be no fish seen in one moment and fish in every channel in another. There may be no birds for hours and then a shorebird appears from behind steam as though it has always been there. There may be no insects until a warm breath from a vent changes the air, and then a small cloud rises. The basin is empty and full in the same way that a clock face is empty while its machinery works behind it.

There is no social life in the human sense, no courtship of the land by one species for itself. Life here is a web of small permissions. A fish lives because microbial mats produce oxygen and insects. Insects live because warm water holds their larvae. Birds live because fish and insects move at the surface. Hares live because moss, sedge, and cushion plants exist in pockets of warmth. Predators live because prey is scattered but constant. The whole system is a permission slip signed by chemistry.

The basin also has a kind of awareness, but not a mind. It is the awareness of surfaces responding to conditions. A microbe moves toward light or chemical gradient. An algal band grows toward warmth. A moss patch holds water and shelters springtails. A fish changes position when a shadow passes over a silica shelf. A hare freezes when wind shifts steam into the open. A bird lands where condensation makes ground warm enough to search. Nothing decides, yet everything adjusts.

There is no one watching this, but it is not unobserved. The residents observe each other as animals observe weather: by pressure, movement, color, sound, and the sudden absence of something that was there a moment ago. A fish may know a bird by the shadow across the water, not by shape. A hare may know a predator by the stillness of midges, not by their absence. A bird may know a vent by warmth rising under its feet, not by steam alone. Knowledge here is not symbolic. It is sensory, immediate, and embedded in the body.

The electric quality of the basin touches life too. In thin air, mineral dust, steam, and cold, small charges gather. Insects may lift off charged stone with a tremor in their wings. A bird standing on wet basalt may twitch as static passes through its feathers. A midge in a misting column may flicker with fine light. This is not dramatic, but it makes life here feel like part of the same low-voltage field. Animals are not separate from the landscape’s charge; they are small conductors moving through it. Their bodies complete circuits between stone, water, air, and heat.

The animals here are not beautiful in the way people expect beauty. They are functional, cryptic, and often plain in dim light. But their beauty is in their timing. A fish holding its place behind a mineral shelf as steam passes is beautiful because it knows where warmth lives. A hare with frost in its fur is beautiful because it has found a pocket of air under a bench. A shorebird with a long bill probing black silt is beautiful because it has read the chemical smell of water beneath the ground. Life in this basin is not ornamental. It is exact.

There is no grand drama in the daily lives of these beings. There is feeding, hiding, breeding, waiting, and dying. But the smallness of each act is what makes the ecology so intense. A single patch of moss can determine whether a generation of springtails survives. A warm channel can decide whether fish eggs hatch. A sudden condensation pulse can trigger a midge bloom. A cold snap can break an ice skin and reveal unseen movement beneath. The stakes are low in size but absolute in outcome. If three inches of water become two inches, something dies. If a vent shifts half a meter, a meadow can be born.

The basin’s inhabitants are, in effect, citizens of anomalies. They do not live in the whole place. They live at edges: where steam meets cold, where mineral water meets air, where black silt meets warm runoff, where wind-polished stone meets moss, where a temporary channel meets a temporary pond. They are edge-livers. They read the boundary, not the center.

Because the center of most habitats here is unstable, life clings to margins. A warm shelf becomes a ledge of existence. A shallow pool becomes a nursery. A moss patch becomes a city. A burrow under warm pumice becomes a country. The scale of survival is tiny. But the cumulative effect is large: the land itself is slowly assembled by the activity of its residents. Microbes build mats. Lichens break stone. Moss holds moisture. Roots stabilize silt. Fish move nutrients. Insects aerate water. Birds carry eggs and spores. Mammals dig, scatter, and fertilize. The basin’s life is not only living in the landscape. It is making the landscape.

If you want a portrait of a resident, you must choose a scale.

At the smallest scale, a resident is a bacterium in a film of water, breathing metal, turning sulfur, leaving a rust-colored stain. It does not see the world. It feels chemistry.

At a slightly larger scale, a resident is a rotifer in a water film, or a springtail in moss, or a chironomid larva in silt, grazing, hiding, waiting to become a wing.

At the scale of plants, a resident is a cushion of lichen on warm basalt, or a moss mat around a seep, or a single sedge blade standing in black water.

At the scale of animals, a resident is a pale fish holding station behind a silica shelf, a midge rising from steam, a shorebird walking through the edge of a pond, or a hare-like grazer resting in a burrow warmed by a crack in the earth.

At the largest scale, a resident is the entire pattern of these beings, moving through the basin in different seasons, temperatures, and times of day. It is the whole distributed population that makes the emptiness alive without filling it visibly.

The basin is not lonely to them. Loneliness is a human judgment based on the absence of a familiar center. Here, the center is everywhere except one place. The center is the nearest warm seam. The center is the next puddle. The center is the edge of the steam. The center is the moment before frost or the moment after thaw.

There is no one who tends this place, yet it is tended. Microbes tend the chemistry. Plants tend the moisture. Fish tend the channels. Birds tend the margins. Mammals tend the ground. Scavengers tend the dead. Wind tends the surface. Heat tends the depths. The land tends itself through a thousand small bodies doing what their bodies are.

At night, the basin is not silent. It is quiet in a way that feels full. Steam sighs through vents. Water drips from mineral ledges. Ice snaps on shallow pools. Insects move in the warm margins. A bird shifts on a ledge. A hare’s nose appears briefly from a burrow and withdraws. Fish turn in the dark channels, waiting for dawn or for the change of a current. The whole population is active, but not visible. If you could see at the scale of movement, not size, the basin would glow with motion: tiny legs in moss, larvae in water, microbial mats spreading, fish reading pressure, birds listening, mammals breathing in the cold.

At dawn, the basin is a sequence of small openings. Frost breaks. Steam thickens. Insects lift. Fish rise to warmth. Birds call. A hare steps out of shelter. A raptor circles above the vent field. The ice skins on ponds crack where water moves beneath. The land becomes wet again from condensation. The residents do not wake as one. They wake as many separate experiments in survival, each responding to a slightly different gradient of heat, water, light, or chemistry.

There is no creature here that dominates the basin. Even if there were a predator large enough to shape the land, it would still be small compared to the steam vents, the frost, the mineral currents, and the time it takes a lichen to eat stone. No animal is the hero of this place. The land is the event, and the creatures are its ongoing processes.

If you ask what lives there, the most complete answer is not a list of species but a description of relationships. Life lives here in thin films, warm channels, moss cities, mineral shelves, seasonal pulses, hidden eggs, distant herds, patient predators, and microbial labor. It lives in the space between water and stone, heat and cold, chemistry and time.

It lives without knowing itself.

It lives without building.

It lives by the same rule that built the basin itself: heat rises, water moves, minerals settle, cold presses back, and life finds a way to gather wherever those movements touch.
