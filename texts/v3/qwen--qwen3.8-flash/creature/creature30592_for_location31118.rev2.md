Who lives there is not a who. It is a how.

No creature keeps a body warm there. No creature digs a burrow. No creature feeds young, hides from hawks, climbs from a nest, or makes a territory by scent and call. There are no rats beneath the boards, no frogs in the reeds, no insects in the steam, no birds in the low trees. The absence is not a quiet hole left behind by something that has gone. It is more like a rule the place learned before there was anything to obey it: here, life is not sealed inside a single wandering body. Here, life is the movement of heat through wet iron, the patience of water through old brick, the slow green reaching of roots toward what is warm, the constant returning of what rises.

If one must still call them inhabitants, then call them this: the continuing adjustment of the Yard itself.

---

The first resident is the Sweeping Steam.

It is not merely weather. It is not merely exhaust. It behaves like a body made entirely of breath, without bones, without mouth, without need. It rises from engines and pumps in long soft curtains, crosses the flooded streets, presses under corrugated roofs, gathers in the low corners of brick, condenses into bright beads, and returns as threads to pools below. It moves with a slowness that resembles care. When it passes over the stones, the stones become a little more alive with moisture. When it moves through the reeds, the reeds tilt and strike, not as a disturbance, but as a response, as if the steam were a hand laid upon them.

The Sweeping has no eyes, but it notices. Not because it looks, but because it changes. It thickens where heat is greatest. It curls where metal sweats. It gathers in doorways where air is trapped and warm. It thins itself across open pools. It is the Yard’s broom, its messenger, its cooling breath, and its way of touching everything at once.

At times it becomes a kind of shallow architecture: ribbons along pipe-facades, a membrane under roofs, a slow river of condensation running down glass. It sometimes forms figures for an instant—a hooded shape in a doorway, a long pale thread rising like hair from a grate, a hanging curtain that seems to lean toward the engine. But these are not creatures. They are pressures meeting surfaces, heat remembering where it has been. They are the Yard testing its own boundaries.

If the Yard has a population, the Sweeping Steam is the most visible of it.

---

Water is another kind of resident, or perhaps the same presence when it has become heavier, more patient, more bound to place.

It lives in thin sheets across brick, in channels that have been worn so deep they seem appointed, in shallow pools that tremble though no wind crosses them. The water does not simply flow; it keeps a path. It knows which stones are rounded, which gaps are stable, which drains are still clear. The channels are not mistakes. They are habits. The Yard has grown into these habits the way a river grows into its bed, except that here the bed is made of industry: iron gratings, foundation stones, sunken rails, pump-houses, old drains, and the slow architecture of erosion.

The pools are not still. They are listening. A pump strikes somewhere nearby, and their skins shiver. A rail hums with current, and the standing water twitches. A footfall presses the mud and water upward, and the surface remembers for a moment in concentric rings before the steam closes over it again. The water is the Yard’s memory of motion. It does not remember events as a mind would; it remembers flows, rhythms, temperatures, pressures. It carries mineral traces from one place to another and sets them down as pale lines on stone. Those lines are not decoration. They are records. The Yard writes itself in calcium, iron, salt, and rust.

The water also tends the plants, and is tended by them. Reeds stand in clumps because water chose that depth. Ferns unroll where spray finds them. Moss spreads because stone is never quite dry. Vines climb pump-house walls because condensation runs there in long bright paths. The water is both medium and participant: the thing that is carried and the thing that carries, the thing that feeds and the thing that is filtered, the thing that cools and the thing heated into return.

There are no fish in these pools. No turtles. No insects to break their surface. The pools are not barren because they are not made for such lives. They are not bodies of water waiting for animals to enter them. They are organs in the Yard’s circulation, small slow faces through which the place breathes in the other direction.

---

Then there are the engines.

The pumps stand in open courts like elders in a council they have forgotten. Their iron bodies are warm to the touch. Their pistons move in deep, muscular silence for only a moment before the next stroke begins with a bang that shakes the shallow water from the stones. The sound is not idle. It is announcement. It is pulse. It is the Yard beating itself into continued shape.

No hands work them. No eyes watch them. No fuel is added in any obvious rhythm. Yet they are not abandoned. They are too attentive for that. They have been adjusted by years of response. They pump because the water requires it, or because the steam requires it, or because the heat itself has become a kind of demand. In this place, a machine need not be a human instrument. It can become a limb of the land.

The boilers breathe in low rolling pulses. They are not alive as an animal is alive, but they are alive as a furnace can be alive when it has become the center of a metabolism. They exhale steam. They condense what rises. They return water to earth and stone. The pipes that run along facades are not merely attached; they have become rooted. In places, brickwork around a pipe is swollen with mineral deposit, as though the pipe had grown into the building. In other places, roots have entered the pipe from the inside, threading through rust and damp, making the plumbing part of the plant.

The engines have preferences.

One pump may slow its stroke after a long heat build. Another may quicken when the windless air thickens with pressure. A valve that has stuck for months may release at the exact moment a nearby reed cluster is disturbed by falling condensation. A low iron shed may creak at a heat change, then a pump nearby may respond as though the sound were instruction. None of this proves mind. It proves something older: reciprocity. The Yard has learned to use its machines as living things use organs, and perhaps the machines have learned, in a way without intention, to serve the Yard as organs serve a body.

The iron is not cold here. It is not dead. It is skin-warm, condensation-slicked, faintly humming, and always returning heat to the wet surfaces around it. If one presses a hand to a pipe in the evening, it does not feel like machinery. It feels like a fevered wrist.

---

The green life is the Yard’s most delicate intelligence.

There are no animals to chew or browse, no creatures to nest in the trees, no beasts to trample seedlings into mud. This is not why the vegetation grows. But the absence changes it. Without animal life, the plants do not form a food chain. They form a climate. They become architecture, regulation, and attention.

Broad ferns unfold along canal edges. Thick pale reeds stand in clumps. Vines with soft silver undersides climb pump-houses. Moss spreads over stone in thick green fur. Low trees twist roots through flooded brick. None of them riot. The greenery is lush, but disciplined. It grows toward warmth, toward water, toward the places where machines have made conditions for life. It follows the heat like a sense.

The ferns are readers of pressure. When a pump changes its rhythm, their leaves lift or curl in slow response. When steam condenses and runs across warm metal, the ferns near that surface become darker, fuller, more open. They do not see, but they turn. They do not listen, but they adjust. They are not merely present; they participate.

The reeds are the Yard’s metronome. They slap against one another in the hot still air. Their sound is not random; it comes from the movement of water beneath them, from the vibration of engines through the ground, from the shifting of steam along the surface of pools. Where a pump strikes strongly, the reeds tremble. Where water returns to a channel, the reeds dip and rise. They are the Yard’s way of marking time in a place with no birdsong, no insects, no human bells.

The vines are plumbers and gardeners at once. They climb metal and brick not only for light, but for heat. They find the warm pipes, the sweating valves, the stones that never quite dry. Their leaves catch condensation and channel it downward to roots. Their stems sometimes press against iron supports in long slow curls that look almost deliberate. In one pump-house, a vine has grown over a valve wheel as though tending it. Its leaves have collected dust and mineral salts in a pale crust. It does not open the valve, perhaps, but it changes the way steam cools on the metal. In doing so, it becomes part of the machine’s breathing.

The moss is the Yard’s skin and circuit. It spreads over stone because the stone sweats. It thickens where electricity hums faintly in damp air. Moss grows along rails more vigorously than elsewhere, not because of any obvious nourishment, but because the current draws moisture, and moisture draws life. In places, the moss over iron looks electric, almost alive with stored sensation. Touch it, and it feels cold and wet, then warm, then faintly gritty with mineral dust. It is not passive carpet. It is a living coating that reads heat, holds water, conducts tiny signals, and slows erosion.

The trees are the strangest of the green inhabitants.

There are few of them, and they grow isolated. One beside a streetlamp. One through the broken roof of a shed. One in the center of a flooded square, its roots drinking from a pool of mineral water. Their bark is warm, almost fevered near the pumps. Their leaves make a dull metallic sound when struck. They are not ordinary trees. They have become nodes of the Yard, places where water, heat, iron, and plant meet in a single body.

Their roots do not merely drink; they press. They grow along drains, around foundations, beneath channels, into the soft seams between brick and stone. They are drawn to the places where engines heat the ground. They are drawn to the places where water runs best. Their root systems follow the Yard’s plumbing. In a few locations, it is impossible to say whether a root is growing into a pipe or a pipe is growing into a root. The Yard has absorbed both. The distinction has become unnecessary.

If an animal had a nervous system, these trees might be its sensory organs. But they are not organs of any creature. They are the Yard learning to feel through green wood and wet leaf.

---

The true inhabitants of the Yard do not separate easily into plant, machine, water, or stone. They live in the in-between.

There are moss-covered valves that open by expansion of damp roots beneath them. There are iron gratings furred with mineral growth, so that water passing through them is filtered and rewritten before it enters the channels. There are vines that conduct faint electricity through moisture, their stems glowing briefly in the dark when pressure shifts in a nearby rail. There are rust flowers growing on pipe joints, each one a slow crystalline bloom of oxidation, water, heat, and air. There are pools lined with pale reed-roots that tremble at the same frequency as the pump above them. There are bricks whose wet faces hold the exact number of drops per minute from the nearest boiler, as though the masonry were breathing in iron.

These are not animals. They are not human tools. They are not merely natural. They are Yard-forms.

A Yard-form is a life that exists because several kinds of process have become one. It may begin as a leak, a root, a rust line, a current, or a patch of moss. It may begin as an accident. But if it stabilizes—if it repeats, responds, and helps the circulation continue—it becomes resident. The Yard does not need births. It needs continuations.

The Yard has become a place where infrastructure has been colonized by green life so thoroughly that the two cannot be told apart, and where green life has been shaped by heat and water so consistently that it no longer behaves like ordinary vegetation. The plumbing is botanical. The vegetation is mechanical. The weather is domestic. The buildings are humid bodies. The engines are part of a metabolism.

In such a place, “what lives there” is not a list of species. It is a census of relationships.

---

Even the sealed dwellings are occupied.

There is no one behind the windows. The glass is clouded, swollen with condensation, streaked by slow mineral runs. The doors are warped. The wood is black with damp. Nothing sleeps there, nothing eats there, nothing hides there. And yet the houses are not empty. Each room contains a resident microclimate. The air inside is heavier than outside. It moves in long, almost invisible currents. Warmth gathers near the ceiling, returns down the walls, settles into floors, and climbs again. The rooms breathe.

A tool left on a bench is not held by hands, but it may shift when the house moves. A floorboard may lift slightly in heat and release a thin sigh of trapped air. A kettle may ring once because a pipe beneath it has cooled. A window may cloud with a shape that resembles a face for a moment, only because the steam has met the glass at a different temperature and then dissolves. Nothing is watching in a human sense. But the room notices when its air is changed. It notices when a door opens. It notices when pressure shifts. It answers with a drip, a creak, a flicker of an amber lamp.

The lamps glow behind cloudy glass with low copper warmth. They are not merely light sources. They are witnesses. Their bulbs draw power from cables hung in slack arcs between poles, all coated with condensation. The current hums faintly in damp wires. Sometimes the lamps brighten when nearby steam increases. Sometimes they dim when pressure falls. No one flips a switch, but the circuit has become sensitive. The Yard reads electrical change as part of its weather, and the lamps mark that reading for itself.

The warehouse with its rust-soft roof has its own interior climate. Water does not pour in so much as the inside becomes water. Rusted beams exude pale mineral streaks. Boxes that might have held goods are gone or rotted into fibers, leaving only impressions in the damp floor. The space is not a ruin. It is a vessel in which rust and moss conduct a long conversation. The roof breathes. Its iron panels expand and contract. In warm cycles, they creak and tick. In cooling cycles, they settle and drip. The warehouse is not abandoned. It has simply changed employers.

The dwellings with windows sealed against damp are the most unsettling. They appear to be inhabited by absence. But the Yard does not know absence. It knows moisture patterns, thermal expansion, mineral flow, pressure differentials, and the slow life of condensation. If a room feels haunted, it is because the Yard has made a home out of conditions that once were intended for humans. The house no longer has a human shape. It has a humid one. It lives because its inside air keeps changing, and its walls keep answering.

---

The Yard watches without eyes.

This is not metaphor. When something enters, the place does not merely tolerate it. It registers it. The visitor’s body is an event: heat, moisture, pressure, weight, salt, breath, vibration. The water rises around the boot. The steam bends toward the shoulder. The boardwalk flexes and sends a faint knock through an adjacent support. The mud closes after the foot and begins to remember. The air changes where the visitor moves, and that change travels to a pump, a lamp, a reed cluster, a root beneath a grate.

The nearest fern shifts a few inches after the visitor passes, not because it sees, but because it follows warmth and pressure. The metal under the visitor’s hand is warm because the Yard keeps its temperatures close to skin temperature. A pump somewhere out of sight changes rhythm because the visitor’s disturbance altered a tiny pressure line through the ground. The Yard does not hate the intruder. It does not hunt it. It simply treats it as a temporary leak in its own system.

A visitor is a sealed animal. That is the problem.

The Yard is built of open processes. Water moves. Steam rises. Roots extend. Metal expands. Electrical current hums through dampness. Nothing is entirely contained. A living animal is a contradiction to that: a small private world with its own heat, its own boundary, its own hunger, its own need to return to shelter. The Yard has no shelters for such creatures, because it does not need shelters. It is all shelter. It is a place with no outside, no inside, no edge that is not constantly wet.

Animal life would require a body that could be separated from the Yard. Here, the body cannot be separated. It would sweat, slip, swell, cool, heat, dissolve its own salts into the mud, give off moisture into the steam. The Yard would read it, but it could not keep it. It is not a place where something with a heart can belong, because the heart would have to fight the rhythm of the place. The pumps already beat. The water already returns. The green things already breathe. An animal would be too much of a single self.

---

If the inhabitants can be named, perhaps they are these:

The Sweepers, moving as steam through the hot low streets.

The Weeping Brick, sweating mineral tears and remembering engine-stroke in rhythm.

The Channels, choosing paths without deciding.

The Pools, trembling in unison with pumps beneath them.

The Iron Congregation, working without hands, answering the land as organs answer a body.

The Green Readers, turning roots, stems, and leaves toward warmth and water.

The Reeding Choir, keeping time in slap and hiss.

The Amber Watch, glowing behind cloudy glass, marking pressure in light.

The Circuit Moss, spreading over stone and wire alike.

The Rust-Gardeners, blooming slowly on iron joints.

The House-Breathers, filling sealed rooms with humidity that behaves like habit.

And above all:

The Yard itself.

Not a creature. Not a god. Not a ghost. A ward-shaped system with no face, no blood, no hunger, and no death in the ordinary sense. It lives by repetition. It lives by condensation. It lives by the mutual care of heat, water, machinery, and plant. It is populated because it is busy. It is busy because it must remain.

---

The Yard of Sweeping Steam is not a ruin of human industry. It is what remains after human industry has passed through the long wet patience of erosion and been reabsorbed by something older and stranger. The engines are not left behind. They have been adopted. The buildings are not abandoned. They have been humidified into bodies. The plants are not wild growth occupying a dead town. They are the town’s new green conductors, its memory, its weather, and its way of tending itself.

Nothing calls there. Nothing answers to a name. No one comes home. No one dies in a bed. No one leaves footprints that become fossils. But the place is not empty. It is full of attention without intention. Every surface is warm. Every pipe is wet. Every pool trembles. Every leaf moves. Every valve has its temperament. Every engine has its hours.

So if asked who lives there, the answer is: no one.

But if asked what lives there, the answer is: everything that continues.

The steam lives by sweeping.

The water lives by returning.

The engines live by breathing heat.

The green life lives by adjusting to warmth and wetness.

The brick lives by weeping.

The moss lives by conducting damp and current.

The houses live by holding humid interiors that change with the Yard.

And the Yard lives by all of them, not as a soul above them, but as the whole hot, wet, iron-and-root metabolism that has forgotten how to stop.
