The basin is an open city of water, stone, fungus, and standing noise. It stretches past the immediate horizon in multiple layers: a high rim of rounded cliffs, a middle ring of terraces and salt flats, a broad floor of marsh, root-latticed avenues, and shallow pools so still they look like holes punched through the world. Farther out, separate districts continue over ridges and down into gullies, as if the settlement has grown across a small region without ever being founded. Its scale is regional, valley-sized, town-sized, yet none of it is inhabited by people.

It is inhabited by what the noise has become.

At this altitude the air is not thin but saturated, hot as a mouthful of steam. The humidity hangs in it like suspended cloth. Sound does not leave. A stone cracking somewhere above the rim, the wet slap of a root pulling itself through mud, the long groan of shifting salt, the hiss of a pool disturbed by wind—all of it enters the basin and remains. The roar is not one roar, but every roar ever made within or carried toward this place, layered, thickened, kept alive by the constant returning and re-returning of itself. The city stands in that noise the way a body stands in blood. It is not a silence broken by inhabitants. It is an inhabitance made of broken silence.

The citizens have no fixed shapes.

Some are low and broad, like tables of wet fungus, moving slowly across the terraces by means of thousands of threadlike feet. When they stop, their edges curl up slightly, and if one were close enough to hear them, they would murmur in fragments: a cow’s bell, a child’s shout, the creak of a door, a word from a language no one now living could explain. Other citizens are tall and porous, shaped as if the cliffs had remembered how to stand upright. They do not walk so much as lean forward and permit the ground to carry them. Their bodies are made of mineral crust, root, salt, and old echoes; if struck, they ring in more than one voice at a time. Still others are mere gatherings of mist and spores, hovering in the root-latticed avenues at precisely the height where the heat makes breathing uncomfortable. They look less like animals than like weather that has developed opinions.

There are no houses, because the architecture is alive.

The avenues are not paths cut through the marsh, but fungal roads grown by the city for its own circulation. They are pale, spongy, threaded with black veins, and they exhale a clean smell of iron and old rain. At intervals, larger knots of this fungus rise into the shape of rooms: vaulted, damp, dimly luminous with yellow-white light trapped in their walls. Nothing has been built. It has accumulated. What looks like a plaza is a dried salt terrace worn smooth by generations of moving bodies. What looks like a well is a mouth in the stone that remembers voices falling into it. What looks like a statue is a held cry grown into basalt.

The pools are the city’s most delicate inhabitants, or perhaps its memory.

A pool at first appears lifeless: black in the center, silver at the rim, so calm it seems impossible that anything moves in it. But if one watches long, ripples begin without source. Then something steps from the surface as if lifting a lid. The pool-folk are thin creatures, made of water and reflected light, with faces that are never quite settled. They can imitate what stands above them, but imitate it with delay. They may appear as a traveler’s silhouette an hour after that traveler has gone. They gather at the shallows to drink rain, which is their only true news, and when the heat thickens they slip back beneath the surface, leaving only a brief tremor in the stillness.

The salt flats are tended by herds of slow mineral grazers.

They resemble wide, many-legged stones. Their backs are crusted with pale crystals; their mouths are not mouths but sucking depressions that feed on brine and sound residue. They move in great circles, and those circles, repeated over years, become terraces, roads, enclosures. The salt-herds do not belong to a single district. They migrate between terraces, marshes, and cliff bases according to the pressure of held noise. When a sound becomes too old, too heavy, too sour with repetition, the herds gather to graze it flat, leaving smooth salt plates behind. These plates then collect new echoes, and the cycle begins again.

The cliff districts belong to something older.

Along the rounded high walls, the rock itself is threaded with fungal veins and resonant cavities. At the smallest change in pressure, the cliffs answer. Whole faces of stone may vibrate at once, releasing trapped voices: not words, exactly, but shapes of sound, the memory of a windstorm, a river that used to run there, a bird now gone from every other place. The cliff-inhabitants are not separate from these voices. They are the voices given posture. They rise and retreat, lean into the basins like listening elders, and are sometimes so still that one must look for a long time before recognizing the breathing in their surfaces. They are ancient beyond repair, yet they are also constantly renewed, because when the rains come down from above, the fungus blooms along the old fissures and the stones appear as if just washed, just born, just now remembering how to be there.

The city does not require food in the ordinary sense.

It feeds on persistence. Every sound that enters the basin is a meal; every repetition is a birth. But not all sounds can become citizens. A single cry that fades too quickly is absorbed into the salt or lost in the marsh. To live, a noise must have enough weight to be returned, enough difference to be recognized, enough warmth to gather moisture. Human voices often qualify for this reason: they are strange, layered, self-propagating, capable of repeating themselves even when no longer intended. A name shouted once may become a pool. A quarried wall of song may become a cliff. A scream that cannot find its way out of a mountain may become one of the tall porous beings, forever turning the same phrase into weather.

This is why the city is said to hold a roar.

The roar is not a battle sound. It is not even one roar. It is all that ever tried to be heard here and could not stop being heard. It is the sum of accumulated urgencies, preserved by humidity and fungus and mineral. The city is therefore less a settlement than a condition: a place where sound has been forced to become body. Its citizens are not born of wombs or eggs, but of interruption. They begin as an echo that failed to fade, then gathered dust, then gathered spores, then found a mouth, and finally found themselves obliged to continue existing.

They move by call and response.

A fungal road will brighten when it is asked for passage. A salt terrace will shift beneath a grazing herd if the correct pressure is sung into it. A pool-folk will surface only when addressed by someone whose own voice has already been heard by the basin. Even the cliff-elders are slow not because they lack motion, but because their bodies are composed of long intervals; they respond as mountains respond to rain, with patience, and sometimes too late to be visible. The city has no traffic but it has rhythm. It has no marketplaces but it has exchange. Echoes are traded, pruned, returned, amplified, and sometimes released in brief bursts of silence so intense that nearby pools crack and new mineral growth begins at once.

There are roles among them, though they have no names that translate cleanly.

Some are gatherers. They crawl through marshes and root avenues, collecting stray vibrations too thin to sustain themselves. They deposit these into the fungal walls, where they may grow into larger forms over years. Some are keepers. They stand at the edges of the salt flats and absorb dissonance, preventing the held roar from collapsing into noise. Some are mirrors. The pool-folk and the still-silvered creatures of the high terraces preserve shapes and sounds from the outer world, though imperfectly; their memories are always slightly wet, slightly warped. Some are mourners, though no one here would call it that; they circle districts where an old roar is dying and repeat its final frequencies until a new citizen takes that shape and carries it forward.

The city also has its predators, if that word can be trusted.

There are swarm-things, clouds of spore-dust that enter a voice and divide it into competing echoes. They feed on certainty. If a being is too certain, too coherent, the swarm may surround it until it becomes plural. There are sinkholes that are not sinkholes but mouths in the root network, hungry for sustained sound. There are the still-pool reflections that can copy an entity so accurately that, after a time, it becomes difficult to tell which is the citizen and which the image. These dangers do not make the city wild. They make it civic. Balance is maintained not by laws but by feedback: every act of consumption provokes an answering echo, every silence invites a replacement sound.

The light is part of their life.

It is strong, steady, not strange: yellow-white, like a lamp behind glass. It does not seem to come from a sun. It comes from everywhere above the basin and seems to keep the moisture at the edge of boiling. To the city’s inhabitants this light is not scenery but metabolism. It holds the spores suspended. It warms the salt without drying it. It allows the old echoes in the stone to remain readable. If the light ever changed—brightened, darkened, shifted toward cold blue—the roar would alter its texture. Some citizens would fall quiet. Others would become strange. The cliff-elders might begin to answer in voices that did not belong to them. The fungal avenues might bloom in spirals instead of streets. The pools might become mirrors that reflect what is not there.

They have no religion, or perhaps the entire city is one.

Every morning, if morning is a word that applies to this heatless, bright, perpetual hour, the held roar shifts. The terraces change direction. The marsh breathes outward. The salt flats release a faint vapor of old noise that smells, absurdly, of rain on iron. The citizens do not worship this. They attend to it. They place themselves in relation to it. They tune. The whole basin becomes a vast instrument being slowly adjusted by its inhabitants. It is not beautiful in any ordinary sense. It is beautiful as pressure is beautiful, as weather is beautiful, as memory is beautiful when it has been left alone too long and has begun to grow.

The city is ancient beyond repair because so many things within it are broken voices.

A roar is not one animal. It is many mouths failing to synchronize. A name is not one person. It is a sound stretched across years, worn smooth, cracked, repaired with salt, repaired with fungus, repaired with rain. The city is ancient because it contains every failure of silence. It is new because the constant wetness keeps growing fresh life over the brokenness, and the brokenness keeps collecting fresh sound. Nothing has a beginning in the sense a human town does. It appears as if the roar has always been there, and perhaps it has, in some other valley, on some other rim, waiting to be trapped by this particular heat.

If people were to enter, they would not be eaten.

At first they would find it strange that the city has no obvious shelters, yet everywhere there are hollows the size of bodies. The salt flats would seem too wide for travel, yet after an hour of walking their legs might begin to answer footsteps that are not theirs. The pools would show them reflections that stand too still. The root avenues might brighten ahead, inviting them onward with paths that did not exist a moment before. If they spoke, the basin would repeat them, but not as an echo repeats. It would begin to improve upon them, carrying their words to places where other listeners are always leaning forward, always almost hearing. If they stayed too long, one of three things would happen.

Either their voices would loosen from them and become citizens.

Or the city would learn them so thoroughly that their footsteps began to look like old roads.

Or they would finally understand the held roar well enough that their own minds became one more layer in it, no longer belonging to a person but to the sound itself.

Most travelers leave before that. They climb back down to where mountains stand far below and call the city a legend. But not all do. Some simply walk farther, into the districts beyond the ridges, where the terraces fold into gullies and the air is so full of preserved noise that silence is only another word for exhaustion. There, among the mineral grazers and fungal plazas and cliff-faces that murmur in extinct tongues, a human voice would be nothing special. It would be one more arrival. One more held note. One more citizen in the long civic process of not stopping.

In that way, the City of the Held Roar is inhabited.

It is inhabited by the consequences of sound.

By echo-bodies and salt-memory. By root-roads that ask to be walked. By pool-folk who copy shapes and wait for them to become real. By cliff-elders whose bones are old weather. By swarm-things that divide voices and mineral herds that grind noise into silence. By fungal chambers that breathe and terraces that shift when addressed. By names grown into stone. By shouts preserved in wet air. By the endless, layered hum of everything that entered and did not leave.

The city is not empty.

It is full of what a shout becomes when no one is there to stop it from going on.
