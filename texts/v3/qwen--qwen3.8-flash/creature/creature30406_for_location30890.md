What lives in the Dry City of the Thin Heights is not a population in any ordinary sense. There is no crowd, no herd, no murmuration, no colony. Life here has too little water, too little warmth, too much time, and too much silence for those kinds of abundance. The plateau does not teem. It waits. And what waits here is mostly brittle, translucent, slow, and strange, made of the same materials as the streets and walls it passes through, so that at first the animal and the architecture seem to be the same substance, separated only by a pause in the ticking.

If one asks for the inhabitants, the first answer is deceptively small: there are the walkers, the tall reflective grazers, the fog-drifters, the buried predators, and the growths that are neither plants nor fungi. The second answer is more accurate: the city itself is a kind of host, a slow metabolism of vapor, salt, glass, sound, and fracture, and the creatures are not merely living in it. They are expressions of it.

## The Small Walkers

The most common animals are the six-legged walkers, though even their commonness is relative. They are pale, almost colorless, and about as thick as a finger’s width. Their shells are not smooth like insect carapaces but faintly prismatic, as though fine glass had been blown into a living outline and then polished by breath. They move with legs so broadly placed and lightly padded that they seem to float over the salt fields rather than touch them.

These walkers do not see in the usual way. There is no pair of eyes on their heads, no cluster of ocelli, no suggestion of sight as a central sense. Instead, their bodies are distributed listening. The pads of their feet read pressure differences across the surface of a salt plate. The fine filaments along their legs detect the faintest change in temperature, the way a thermal edge forms when the sun shifts behind cloud. The underside of their shells, when pressed near the ground, can taste static: the minute charge left by dust moving over glass, by salt lifting along a crack, by a crystal stalk beginning to grow.

They feed on microscopic blooms, the tiny crystalline structures that form where fog has passed and left mineral film behind. They do not chew in the ordinary sense. Their mouths are small folds lined with crystalline teeth that do not crush but scrape and draw upward, like dry dust being pulled from stone. As they feed, they leave behind faint trails of powdered shell and salt, which over long time become almost like chalk drawings on the plateau. A traveler may see these trails only when the low sun strikes them at the correct angle: a series of broken curves leading into a plaza, across a glass shard, and into a crack too narrow to enter.

They stop often. This is one of their most uncanny habits. A walker will cross a pale field with steady, silent steps, then freeze with no visible warning. Its legs remain where they were. Its shell does not shift. It appears to have turned itself into a fragment of the ground. This is not death. It is the creature “listening” with its whole body, reading pressure from the air and from the salt plate beneath it. After a pause that may last seconds or minutes, it resumes, as if it had only been interrupted by a thought too small for the animal to continue immediately.

When startled, they do not flee in any obvious direction. They flatten. Their shells spread against the salt until the pale translucency and the pale surface become continuous. If the light is dim enough, and if one has not been watching the exact place where the creature stood, it is gone. Not because it has moved far, but because it has refused to separate itself from the ground.

Their lives are tied to crystallization. After a night when the dry frost forms in fine needles, the walkers are almost absent, as though the plateau were empty. When the needles release into fogs and crawl into hollows, leaving mineral films behind, the walkers emerge from fissures under basalt terraces, moving with quiet certainty toward the freshest surfaces. They do not gather in groups. But if a fog has just passed across a broad salt field, leaving a thin, glittering film, then dozens, sometimes hundreds, may appear over hours, spaced evenly apart as if arranged by an unseen rule. Their spacing is not territorial in the way mammalian predators space themselves. It is more like the spacing between crystals on a cooling surface.

They reproduce by shedding tiny egg-crystals into warm cracks, particularly where the sun has spent long hours on black basalt. These eggs are dry, pale, almost invisible. They do not hatch in moisture. They hatch when the next thermal pulse moves through the stone and causes the crack to open slightly, exposing them to mineral vapor. The young are not smaller copies of the adult. At first they resemble nothing but a cluster of prisms, a grain of salt with six faint lines of growth. They climb barite stalks, remain motionless for seasons, and only when their shells have hardened enough to reflect the dim light do they become recognizable as walkers.

They are also, in a subtle way, gardeners. Their grazing exposes fresh mineral surfaces. Their trails leave behind fragments that become nucleation points for new crystal blooms. The walkers do not plant anything. They do not know they are creating. But the growths spread where they feed, and the feeding spreads where the growths form, so that the walkers and the pale films of salt and glass share a slow reciprocity. The city is not cultivated. It is merely maintained by appetite.

## The Glasswalkers

Larger and much rarer are the glasswalkers. These are not animals in a familiar sense. They stand on two long hind legs, balanced with an ease that seems to suggest their skeletons are partly mineral and partly something else. Their forelimbs are slender, often held close to the body or extended toward crystal stalks with a patience that makes them look like branches of the same material. Their skin is not fur, not feather, not wet hide. It is smooth and reflective, layered like thin sheets of silicate, catching the bruised daylight in broken flashes.

From a distance, a glasswalker may look like an empty coat rack covered in dull metal, or like a sculpture abandoned by a sculptor who had lost interest in faces. They have no obvious head, though there is a raised area at the top of the body where their sense organs are concentrated. This is not a skull. It is a folded crown of transparent tissue and crystalline plates, angled to receive changes in light, pressure, and static charge.

Glasswalkers stand among the basalt columns and salt terraces, motionless for long hours. At first one might assume they are dead, or that they are simply part of the architecture. But their stillness is active. They are waiting for the plateau to speak. Their bodies are sensitive to resonance, and they prefer the humming places: terraces where the wind passes through columns in low repeated tones, glass plains where thermal stress keeps the shards ticking, courtyards where dust drifts against standing salt and produces faint vibrations in the ground. A glasswalker will remain on one terrace through a full dim day and most of a night, not from hunger, but because the tone has not changed. When the hum shifts, the animal moves.

They feed on charged mineral growths. In particular, they press their narrow folds against the black branching structures, the ash-coral-like bodies that conduct tiny electrical charges. When they feed, a faint shimmer lifts from the coral into the glasswalker’s mouth-folds, like heat leaving a stone in reverse. No moisture is drawn. No bite is taken. The glasswalker appears to drink electricity and mineral essence at once, pulling a pale gleam from the growth until the branch dulls and becomes brittle. They may also draw charge from barite stalks and sulfur blooms, though less efficiently. What they are truly taking is uncertain. It may be chemical gradient, it may be electrostatic potential, it may be something that has no name because there are no observers left to name it.

Their behavior is slow but not simple. A glasswalker does not hurry, and yet it can cross a plaza in a stride that seems to make the distance less than expected. Its steps are placed with strange care, each foot finding a patch of pumice, a ledge of salt, a stable line in broken glass. It avoids thin plates, cracks with sharp edges, and areas where the ground is silent. This last avoidance is especially important. Where the plateau does not hum, where the stone is mute and the fogs settle without vibration, glasswalkers are absent. It is as though they need the city’s voice to feel solid enough to exist.

They are uncommonly solitary, but not entirely alone. Occasionally two may stand at opposite ends of a courtyard, facing each other without moving. The light changes as the sun shifts behind cloud, and their bodies throw dim reflections onto the salt. Sometimes those reflections meet, forming a brief bridge of pale silver across the ground. This may be communication. It may be courtship. It may be nothing more than weather striking two surfaces that have grown into the same habit. The glasswalkers do not call. They do not need to. They make themselves visible to one another through light.

When a hum passes through a long colonnade, glasswalkers often align. Not all at once, and not in any dramatic display. One turns slightly. Another changes the angle of its shoulders. A third lowers itself half a step toward the ground. Over hours, if one watches from a high terrace, the glasswalkers appear to become part of the colonnade itself, their bodies matching the spacing of the pillars, their silhouettes resolving into the architecture. It is not mimicry. It is a shared rhythm. The city shapes the animal’s stillness as much as the animal chooses it.
