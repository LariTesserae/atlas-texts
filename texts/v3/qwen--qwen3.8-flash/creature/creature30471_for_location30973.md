To ask who lives there, you must first stop looking at the horizon.

The horizon is not where they are. It is only where the scale of the world becomes too large for ordinary life to occupy. What lives in this dry, made plain does not announce itself with forests, herds, or cities. It appears as process first: as patience, as heat, as dust that knows where it belongs. If you stand still long enough, you begin to notice that the silence is not empty. It is composed of many small arrivals and departures.

At the smallest scale, the inhabitants are not animals at all, and perhaps not even organisms in any familiar sense. They are lattice-cells and salt nodes, microscopic struts, oxide filaments, crystalline spores, and charged dust grains that behave as if they have intention because they have organization. In every cubic meter of the made substrate there is a slow, dense parliament of structures: tiny cubes within cubes, curled shards, dormant filaments, and warm metallic cells that open and close according to thermal migration. Some of these presences are clearly biological; some are engineered remnants so ancient they have become ecology; some cannot be classified, because the categories of living and built have worn against one another for so many cycles that they no longer divide cleanly.

These microscopic inhabitants do not seek water, because water has never been a requirement here. They regulate themselves through mineral balance and electrostatic charge. They “drink” by taking up trace compounds from dust. They “breathe” through thermal exchange: heat entering, heat leaving, heat changing the angle of crystal faces, changing what can grow where. When the star’s light moves sideways across the salt flats, the smallest cells open their lattice mouths and begin their slow metabolism. When the heat tide deepens, they seal. When the magnetic weather drifts through the upper regolith, they rearrange, not consciously, but with the inevitability of filings obeying a buried magnet.

A stretch of ground that appears barren is therefore never truly uninhabited. The land itself is populated at the scale of matter. A traveler would have to lie flat on the fused ceramic surface to feel it: a faint pressure moving beneath the cheek, a whispering tick in the ears, the sense that the plain is not merely solid, but arranged.

Above that hidden population are the mineral plants.

They do not look like plants because they do not compete for dampness. Their forms are not leaves and stems but panels, rods, and dry branches of fused fiber. They stand in small scattered gardens along the flanks of ridges, in shallow basins where dust collects, in creases where the dense lattice below thins enough to permit vertical growth. Their surfaces are translucent, layered, angled like the faces of cut stones. They catch light not all at once, but by degrees, folding it through inner channels, storing its charge in refractory structures. Some resemble pale bones; others resemble folded glass flowers with no scent, because scent requires moisture to carry. When they bloom, it is not with soft petals but with thin crystalline sheets that click faintly as heat makes them expand and contract. Their flowers are dry instruments for refracting sunlight and attracting airborne filaments.

These plant-like beings are not individual in the way Earth plants are individual. Each “plant” may be a colony, a lattice-rooted community of cells, filaments, and mineralized filaments sharing a single architecture. A stand of dry glass flowers may be one organism, or ten thousand organisms negotiating the same heat tide. They do not rot. When they die, they lose their charge, dry further, and fragment into grains that return to the made substrate. The ground receives them the way a loom receives thread.

The larger fauna are rarer still, and their rarity is part of their beauty.

There are no clouds of insects, no flocks wheeling through the light, no soft bodies to rot in the heat. Instead, animals appear as isolated presences, each one a small miracle of dry persistence. Some resemble beetles, but not in the tender, fragile way of Earth beetles. These are hand-sized ceramic creatures with shells made of layered scales, each scale a fused plate that reflects light in narrow bands so they seem half-visible against salt flats. Their backs are rounded as kiln stones, their legs low and broad, their feet padded with mineral ridges that distribute their weight without cracking the brittle surface. They move slowly because fast movement wastes heat and draws attention in a world of too much light. They feed not on living tissue alone, but on micro-ecologies: salt cells, dormant spores, lichen, engineered dust, and the thin mineral films that settle into basins after heat tides pass.

Others are grazers with broad heads and wide mouths, pressing to the ground as if kissing the plain. They are not gentle creatures by sentiment, but by necessity. Their jaws do not tear flesh; they grind and filter. Their mouths are lined with electrostatic combs that separate usable mineral grains from inert dust. They eat the world layer by layer, leaving no stain, only a shallow impression that will be annealed smooth again by the slow thermal flow. Their eyes are not wet. They are faceted, reflective, or sealed behind thin panels of refractive crystal, built to handle brightness without tears, without drying membranes, without any need for softness.

Above them drift the film-winged light-keepers.

They are difficult to see except when they cross a bright slope. Their wings are not feathered or membranous in any animal sense, but sheets of transparent refractive film stretched over fine ceramic ribs. They float through the amber air like fragments of sky that have decided to become creatures. They feed on suspended mineral dust and light itself, channeling both through thin internal chambers. They do not sing, but when they turn their wings at the correct angle, they scatter the light into small prismatic sparks, and a traveler might mistake them for floating shards of broken glass until one of them moves.

Predation, too, is quiet.

The glass-shelled stalker is perhaps the closest this world has to a monster, but it does not roar, pounce in the air, or hunt in packs. It lies beneath salt ridges with its body compressed flat, its limbs spread so its weight is carried across many microscopic support points. It waits for heat tides to move prey near its hiding place. It strikes with surprising speed, its grinding beak clicking once or twice against carapace. It eats by breaking mineral structure into smaller structure, rearranging what it can, discarding what cannot be used. Its kills are not dramatic. The prey animal may collapse in a faint shower of ceramic shards and dry dust. The predator withdraws. The plain continues. Even violence here has to conserve energy, avoid vibration, respect the silence.

If one insists on persons, however, on beings who might call themselves “I,” then there are the Annealed.

No other name fits them as well, because they have been made and remade so many times that they seem more finished than born. They are not numerous. They do not crowd the landscape. They appear rarely, standing in folds of terrain where their pale bodies blend with salt, tile, and worn metal. From a distance, they may be mistaken for a low statue, a standing slab of mineral, or a pillar that has grown a head. When they move, the movement is careful, deliberate, almost ceremonial. Their bodies are dry inside in the way a clockwork mechanism is dry inside: sealed chambers, layered surfaces, clear warm fluids that move without moisture, and internal structures that store and shed heat according to need.

They are humanoid only in the loosest sense. They have heads, shoulders, arms, and legs, but these parts are not soft. They are built for brightness, weight distribution, and thermal regulation. Their skin is layered like pale ceramic, scaled or tiled at the backs of their necks and along the outer edges of their limbs. Their faces have no eyes as familiar mammals know them. Instead, they have faceted panels or smooth crystal surfaces that gather and separate light, giving them the ability to see heat migration, magnetic weather, and subtle variations in mineral charge. Their mouths are narrow slits or grinding plates, suited not for wet speech but for dry sound: clicks, soft churring, the tapping of mineral against mineral.

They breathe, but not to exchange water-vapor. Their respiration is electrochemical and thermal, drawing in fine dust and filtered air, separating trace compounds, and expelling compacted waste that falls from their bodies as powder or tiny pellets. Their circulatory medium is not blood like red wet fluid, but a clear, warm, dense substance more like oil or silicate suspension. It carries mineral salts, dissolved charges, and structural nutrients through nested channels. Their bodies do not sweat. To cool themselves, they expose broad panels of reflective skin to the light; to warm themselves, they curl inward around internal heat reservoirs.

The Annealed do not drink, because there is nothing to drink.

This is perhaps the first thing a visitor must understand. There is no thirst here, not as thirst is known to wet creatures. Their need for balance is mineral, not liquid. They seek the right heat, the right dust, the right crystalline charge. They feed from salt gardens, absorb trace elements from ceramic lichens, and take in light through living surfaces grown over their clothing. Their garments, if they can be called garments, are not woven from fiber alone. They are cultivated: sheets of refractory polymer, translucent panels, layered dust-cloth, and living crystalline collars that grow outward from the wearer’s skin when tended. A single Annealed may carry a small garden on the back of its neck: a rosette of dry flowers that opens only at certain heat phases, serving as both ornament, sensor, and nutrient store.

They live in the interstices of the made world.

They do not build cities, because the world itself is already a city worn into landscape. They inhabit niches: shallow chambers beneath salt ridges, corridors of fused mineral glass, hollow tiles where old circuitry has become wall, basins with circular depressions that may once have been reactors or may simply be places where the substrate thinned enough to shelter them. Their homes are found, negotiated, and slowly shaped by patience. They do not raise towers. They polish. They clear. They widen creases with heat-directed tools and static brushes. They repair fractures in the surface by encouraging crystalline growth, letting the land heal itself into livable form. A dwelling is less a construction than an agreement between body and substrate.

Inside these dwellings, the light is folded and redirected. Panels of thin crystal carry sunlight into nested rooms without heating them unevenly. The walls may appear natural, but close inspection reveals that their patterns repeat at regular intervals, like grammar. A traveler might touch a wall and feel it give faintly, not because it is weak, but because it is a membrane over a deeper made structure. The Annealed accept this as ordinary. They do not ask why the ground feels alive, because it is.

They communicate without voices as soft-bodied creatures use them.

Their speech is a combination of ground-tapping, plate vibration, heat pulses, and faint magnetic modulation. When they touch the surface with long mineral fingertips, they can send small pressure waves through the substrate. Two Annealed standing many meters apart may appear still while speaking in low tones that seem to rise from the earth itself. Their clicks are dry, precise, musical in a severe way: the sound of glass teeth, of ceramic feet, of salt crystals blooming in narrow rows. Their hums are not emotional moans, but stabilizing frequencies, sounds meant to resonate with bodies already tuned to vibration.

They do not shout, because this world punishes excess.

A loud sound is unnecessary. A panicked run is wasteful. An explosion of conflict threatens the brittle, ancient surface. Their social life is therefore restrained, but not unfeeling. They have attention, loyalty, curiosity, and memory. They form small kindreds, not because they need crowds, but because the land is vast and life is rare. A kindred may consist of two adults and one young lattice, or three adults sharing a ridge-side basin, or a single traveler accompanied by a cultivated film-garden growing from a ceramic pouch at the hip. They recognize one another by the patterns etched into their outer scales, by the thermal signatures they carry, and by the particular way they shift dust when they move.

Their young are rare and slow.

A new Annealed may begin as a crystalline seedling: a lattice-globule no larger than a fist, placed in a warm basin where the substrate is rich in dormant filaments. It is fed by light-charged dust, mineral solution carried in dry channels, and the patient attention of its kindred. It grows inward before it grows outward. Its first structures are support lattices, then heat chambers, then sensory plates, then limbs. It takes years to become visible as a body, longer to become a person. Because there is no rot, there is no nursery smell of decay, no wetness of birth, no softness of infancy. The young being emerges as though the ground is slowly agreeing to stand up.

Their lives are long, not because they are immortal, but because they are durable.

They do not age into frail wet tissue. They age into surface wear. Their outer scales become smoother. Their internal heat chambers accumulate mineral patterns. Their faces may appear more carved than fleshed. They grow more resonant with the ground, more difficult to distinguish from the landscape, until only their deliberate motion reveals that they are present. This is not frightening to them. It is completion.

When an Annealed dies, there is no funeral of water or soil. There is an annealing.

The body is placed in a shallow basin, often one already prepared by generations of use. The heat tide finishes the drying. The mineral structures are separated by electrostatic combs, cracked gently by salt bloom, and sorted into usable components. The bones become lattice rods. The scales become surface tiles. The clear circulatory residue is drawn into channels and transformed into dust. What remains is not a corpse but a distribution. The person returns to the made ground, not as memory only, but as material again. The kindred then etch the name into a flat tile or press it into the pattern of a wall, using a stylus that clicks like a small tooth. The land keeps what was.

They are not the rulers of this world, even if they have the most complex culture. They are the latest inhabitants of an old machine that has become body. The world does not exist for them, and they do not control it. They move through it like cells moving through a vast organ. Some of their rituals are maintenance. Some are worship without gods. Some are engineering so ancient that it has become custom. They know how to encourage salt blooms along ridges, how to harvest spores from film-winged creatures, how to read the repetition of horizon patterns, how to avoid the deep places where the substrate gives too much and may not support weight. They know which dust will settle in false footprints and which dust is truly empty. They know when the magnetic weather is shifting because the metal plates beneath the surface begin to hum a note that seems to come from inside the skull.

Their science, if one can call it that, is not separate from their ecology.

A child of the Annealed learns first to touch the ground and distinguish dormant lattice from active crystal. Then learns to read heat shadows. Then learns which dry flowers signal stable dust and which signal migration fronts. Then learns how to speak to a grazer without alarming it, how to approach a beetle-carapace without cracking its shell, how to stand near a stalker-ridge and not mistake its stillness for absence. Knowledge here is not domination. It is coordination. To live is to find the thin places in a dense world and become useful to the patterns already moving there.

Encounters with them feel immense because they are infrequent.

A traveler across the made plain may walk for long spans without seeing an Annealed, and may not even understand that they were seen. The land can hide in plain sight. An Annealed may stand motionless for hours beside a salt ridge, its body matched to the pale mineral skin of the world, its faceted panels reflecting the amber light so perfectly that it looks like a shard of the landscape. Then it moves. The traveler startles not because of sudden speed, but because of sudden intention. The Annealed does not flee. It regards. It assesses the stranger’s weight distribution, heat signature, dust load, and mineral balance. If the traveler is dry enough, slow enough, non-vibrating, the encounter may pass without conflict. The Annealed may lift one hand and tap the ground once, not as greeting, but as a request for permission or a warning that the surface ahead is too dense with dormant lattice to step on carelessly.

They do not offer comfort in familiar terms. There is no cup of water to share, no fire to huddle beside, no soft bed of moss or cloth. Hospitality among the Annealed consists of being led to a stable basin, being shown where the substrate will bear weight, being given a small polished tile that records heat flow, or being allowed to rest in the shadow of a refractory plant that bends light into cooler wavelengths. They do not touch the traveler unless they need to read their thermal state. But when they do, their hands are warm, dry, faintly rough, like fine stone held in sunlight.

There are also the solitary ones.

Some Annealed leave their kindred during long heat phases and wander the salt flats, not because they are lost, but because they are reading the world. These wanderers may carry living dust cultures in pouches on their backs. They may cultivate small gardens of dry flowers along their shoulders
