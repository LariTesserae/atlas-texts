The bowl has a population, but no capital.

No single creature stands in its shadow as if it owns the place. No beast crouches at the lip, no sentinel cries at the rim, no bright-eyed monarch rules from a throne of stone. The inhabitants are too small, too patient, too layered for that. If one asks who lives there, the answer must be given in stages, like the lichen itself: first a skin, then a structure, then a breath, then, near the deepest stone, a human.

The first residents are not plants, nor animals, nor even things most people would think to look for. They are the quiet organisms that have colonized the surface of the rock and the pores beneath it. In the vertical channels of the sheer wall, where the wind has polished the stone to a dull sheen, there are endolithic communities: cyanobacteria, fungi, pale films of life that live inside the mineral flesh of the mountain itself. Where the surface has flaked away, they appear as faint green or yellow traces, like the memory of algae beneath a skin of stone. They do not require streams or pools. They drink from the barest condensation, from frost that briefly becomes water, from snow that melts into a dampness no larger than a fingerprint. They photosynthesize in thin light. They wait. They are the oldest inhabitants, and perhaps the truest.

Above and among them spread the lichens.
