If you approach the place asking **who** lives there, the first answer is a negative one:

**No one lives there in the way an animal lives somewhere.**

No person has ever slept beneath its arches. No beast has ever made a den in its root hollows. No bird has nested in its canopy, no insect has chewed through its bark, no lizard has warmed itself on a leaf-flat beside a channel. There is no paw-print, no feather, no fur, no excrement, no nest, no burrow, no herd trail, no carcass picked clean, no predator’s shadow in the mid-canopy, no chatter of creatures passing through. If life means **creature**, then the Green City is empty.

But that emptiness is deceptive.

The valley is not empty. It is not abandoned. It is not a dead city with vacant streets and silent houses. It is not a forest without people, not a town without inhabitants. It is a place where the ordinary boundary between **living being** and **living place** has dissolved. The architecture itself is alive, but the life is vegetal, collective, processual, architectural. What lives there is not a who, but a **how**: a city made of growth, pressure, wetness, light, cracking, unfurling, settling, collapsing, and rebuilding.

So the longer answer is this:

**The Green City is inhabited by itself.**

Not as a single organism in a simple sense, and not as a society of separate plant-people. It is inhabited by a dense, valley-scale civic body of plant matter. Its citizens are stems, roots, bracts, moss shelves, fused trunks, hollow reeds, vine-bridges, leaf-roofs, root-arches, seed-pods, dead corridors, living walls, water channels, and the fast green tissues that turn toward light with audible strain. It has a population, but the population is not made of animals. It is made of **vegetal relations**.

A tower there is not merely one tree. It may be several trunks wound around one another, their bark grown together into a single column, their surfaces colonized by moss curtains, epiphytic rosettes, fern-like fronds, pale young shoots, and hanging tendrils. To count its residents you would first have to decide where one body ends and another begins, and that decision would change with every season, every rain, every fast burst of growth. A bridge is not a structure placed between two trees; it is a relation that has become architecture. It begins as lean, contact, pressure, adhesion, and fusion. It may last as a bridge until one of its supports grows too thick, tilts, cracks, or overgrows its own path. Then the bridge becomes a buttress, a corridor, a wall, a ruin, a seedbed.

The ground is not a floor beneath these beings. The ground is one of their bodies. The streets are not roads cleared by feet. They are gaps left temporarily by the withdrawal of vines, roots, fallen stems, or fast colonizing moss. The plazas are not parks. They are pauses in growth, wounds made green again, openings where something has died back or been overthrown by its neighbors. The avenues are tunnels formed when long stems bend, meet, and fuse overhead, making a passage below that is more like the inside of a living rib cage than a built corridor. The bridges, arches, balconies, vaults, and underpasses are all **bodies that have become civic forms**.

In that sense, the inhabitants of the Green City are not separate from the city.

There are, of course, plants. If you wanted the plainest answer, you could say: **plants live there**. But that would be too narrow, because the plants are not scattered through the valley like objects placed in a space. They are the valley’s substance. The mosses are skins. The roots are supports and pipes. The stems are walls, columns, and conduits. The leaves are roofs, reservoirs, sails, funnels, and instruments of sound. The dead wood is foundation, vault, and future. To say “plants live there” is like saying “bricks live in a city.” It is true in one way, but it misses the more important fact: in this place, the bricks are alive, and the living thing that calls itself a city is the collective activity of those bricks.

The life there has no face, no eyes, no mouth, no limbs, no migration, no hunt, no mate, no nest. Yet it behaves as though it were populated. Not because it has minds, but because it has **presence**. Its presence comes from constant motion. Leaves turn. Stems elongate. Vines search and lock. Roots crack and lift. Moss mats spread across surfaces like slow green waves. Branches groan and collapse. Hollow pods burst. Seeds shower into crevices. Water beads on bracts and falls. Air moves through thousands of leaf-slits, stem-tubes, and fibrous edges, making a continuous city-roar. The place is deafening not because it is full of voices, but because it is full of **living motion so total that silence becomes impossible**.

If one were to make a strange field guide to its residents, it might look less like a guide to species than a guide to functions and forms.

The first residents are the **vertical builders**.

They are the fused trunks, the pale new columns, the reed-like spires, the twisting stems that grow until they lean against one another and begin a shared life. They are the tallest citizens of the valley, the ones who live closest to ordinary sky. Their existence is a kind of climbing ambition. They do not seek one another out of will, but they answer to light. Where light falls, they lean. Where wind pushes, they thicken. Where a gap opens above, they accelerate. They grow with a pressure that can be heard: fibers splitting, wood creaking, buds cracking open, young shoots forcing themselves through older leaves. They are towers, but they are not towers placed in a city. They are towers that **become** towers.

Below them are the **horizontal citizens**, the bracts, plates, shelves, leaf-roofs, and hanging curtains. They do not build upward in the same way. They make the city’s floors, roofs, rain-gatherers, and shaded galleries. A broad leaf may act as a roof over a small hollow, catching drizzle and releasing it later as dripping threads. A shelf of growth may become a terrace, a water basin, a nursery for smaller shoots, a moss-covered platform from which newer stems begin their own ascent. These are the parts that make the city feel domestic even though there are no homes. They are the rooms without dwellers.

Then there are the **root-lattice people**, though they are not people. They are the buttress-roots, the interlacing underground fans, the pale wet fibers that grip young stone and hold the slopes together. They are the city’s foundations, but foundations that still grow. They push into cracks with audible crunching. They lift fragments of rock and settle them into their own mass. They make doorways by arching over hollows and diving back into the damp ground. A plaza ringed by low buttresses may look like a public space with benches around its edge. But those benches are ribs of living plant matter, and the plaza itself is a temporary agreement between many root systems that have not yet closed the gap between them.

And there are the **bridge-vines**, the most civic of all the residents. A vine that latches across a gap becomes an infrastructure. It connects. It reroutes. It joins one district to another. It may thicken until the gap is no longer open, no longer a street, no longer a plaza, but a roof. The vine does not intend to build. It only grows, presses, curls, and holds. Yet the result is a bridge. If a storm breaks a stem, if another vine seizes the remaining path, if a trunk shifts, the bridge is remade. The city’s passages are not permanent, but they are real. They exist because so many living forms continually meet, strain, and grow together.

The city also has its **dead inhabitants**, or what might be called ancestors.

There are no graves, no corpses as animal remains, no bones separate from the city. The dead are still residents. A fallen trunk becomes a roof. A rotted hollow becomes a vault. A cracked branch becomes a trellis. A compressed layer of dead leaves becomes a floor. A hollowed stem becomes a pipe. The city consumes its own death and rebuilds with it. The boundary between living and dead is not a line but a sequence. One form’s ending is another’s beginning. Decay is not absence; decay is a phase of construction. The demolished become materials for new districts. The dead remain inside the city’s architecture, softened, greened, compressed, remembered as wood and channel and hollow.

So what lives there is not a single “creature,” but a **pattern of lives** so intertwined that the pattern itself becomes the city.

It would be wrong to say the city has no individual organisms. It does. There are plants. There are stems, roots, leaves, buds, seeds. But those organisms are not separate from the civic form in the way houses and inhabitants are separate. A single trunk is not just a trunk. It is a neighborhood. It supports mosses, ferns, epiphytes, young shoots, dripping water, trapped seeds, broken stems, and layers of its own old growth. Another plant grows through it. A vine becomes part of its structure. A dead section becomes a vault. A living section thickens around it. If you tried to define one organism, you would find that its borders keep moving. The organism leaks into the architecture. The architecture leaks back into the organism.

This is why the place feels populated.

It has the density of a crowd, but no crowd of beings. It has the grammar of a society, but no society. It has urban rhythm: opening, closing, rebuilding, rerouting, falling, rising, flooding, drying, cracking, blooming. Those rhythms are not performed by anyone. They are not scheduled. They are not commanded. They emerge from light, water, pressure, gravity, and the explosive tendency of plants to fill every gap. And yet the emergence is so complete, so dense, so relentless, that it mimics civilization.

If you listen to what lives there, you hear it.

The sound is the clearest evidence that the place is not empty. The wind does not pass through dead branches and make a simple rustle. It plays the city like an instrument. Every surface makes a different noise. Loose leaves flap. Stretched fibers thrum. Hollow stems whistle. Rain drums against broad bracts and leaf-roofs. Water taps and gurgles as it moves through channels. New stems split with a sound like small bones. Vines tighten with dry cracks. Whole walls of growth surge forward with a noise like cloth tearing or distant applause. The acoustic environment is so full that it has weight. You can feel the place as a **volume of living motion**, not a forest, not a town, but a botanical storm with urban structure.

The smell also tells you what lives there.

There is no animal musk, no warm breath, no dung, no blood. The air tastes of green juice, damp fiber, resin, crushed stems, sun-warmed bark, and the faint sweet fermentation of plant matter dying and becoming new growth. It is the smell of cut vegetation multiplied across a valley. It is fresh, wet, thick, vegetal, almost overwhelming. The city does not smell like a home. It smells like a living substance being constantly broken down and reconstructed.

The city’s inhabitants do not need to move from place to place because they are places.

A stem is a building. A root is a bridge. A leaf is a roof. A bract is a basin. A moss mat is a wall and a water-holder and a surface for new growth. A hollow trunk is a chamber. A vine is a road. A dead branch is a foundation. The distinction between inhabitant and habitat is not merely blurred; it is abolished.

This is not a place where creatures make tools to live. It is a place where living growth makes itself into the tools, the dwellings, the streets, the infrastructure. It has no technology, but it has function everywhere. Every part serves, though nothing has been invented. Every passage leads, though no one has surveyed. Every arch supports, though no one has measured. The city has utility without purpose. It has organization without order. It has planning without planner.

If one asks again, more carefully:

**Who lives there?**

No one.

**What lives there?**

Everything vegetal that has become city.

But that answer is still too neat. The truest answer is stranger.

What lives there is not “the city” as a noun, and not “the plants” as a list of organisms. What lives there is the **green civic process**: the endless making and unmaking of structure by growth. It is the valley’s way of becoming. It is light converted into shape. It is water converted into pressure. It is death converted into foundation. It is wind converted into sound. It is plant converted into town.

The Green City has no citizens because its citizens are its architecture.

It has no houses because its houses are its bodies.

It has no inhabitants because the place itself is the inhabitant.

It is a place where life is not something that happens inside structures, but something that **is** the structure. The streets are not roads walked by feet. They are gaps between living walls. The towers are not buildings occupied by beings. They are thickened stems grown into vertical forms. The plazas are not empty spaces between houses. They are openings in the body of vegetation, temporary pauses where growth has withdrawn, died, failed, or been replaced. The bridges are not artifacts. They are relationships between stems that have become load-bearing.

If an observer could stand among them—though there is no observer in the description, no creature allowed there—the first impression would be of crowding. Every surface is occupied by another surface. Every trunk has shelves of growth. Every branch has hanging curtains of vines. Every gap has filaments of moss. Every hollow has moisture. Every fallen part is being used. Nothing is allowed to remain empty. The city behaves as though it has a civic law: **fill the space, connect the form, store the water, catch the light, repair the breach, remake the path**.

Yet those laws are not laws. They are just what happens when warmth, dampness, light, and growth pressure meet at sufficient scale. The valley does not obey a master plan. It has no blueprint. It has no zoning, no survey, no ownership, no maintenance crew, no council. But because plant life there is so fast, so dense, so organized in layers, and so sensitive to openings, it produces effects that look like civic organization. A gap becomes a street. A fallen trunk becomes a bridge. A root arch becomes a doorway. A bract becomes a cistern. A hollow stem becomes a pipe. A dying district becomes a plaza. A new shoot becomes a tower. A vine becomes a road. The city is not planned. It is **grown into the appearance of planning**.

This is the strange intimacy of the place: its inhabitants cannot be separated from their environment.

There is no “here is a tree, and here is its habitat.” There is no “here is a root, and here is the ground it grows in.” The ground is root. The wall is bark. The roof is leaf. The pipe is stem. The bridge is vine. The floor is compressed growth. The aquifer is condensation held in living tissue. The clock is cycles of wetness and drying. The breath is wind and transpiration. The city is not inhabited by living things in the ordinary sense. It is a living thing made of too many living things to count, and too entangled with itself to be a mere collection.

So perhaps the most accurate way to describe what lives there is to say:

**A botanical presence.**

Not a being. Not a society. Not a creature. A presence.

It has the pressure of a crowd, but no bodies that move by muscle. It has the noise of a market, but no commerce. It has the rhythm of a town, but no sleep, no waking, no workday. It has the scale of a region, but no government. It has the feeling of occupation, but no occupants. It has all the signs of a city except one: the absence of anyone living outside its own structure.

The Green City is inhabited by the **fact of vegetation becoming architecture**.

Its residents are not separate entities with homes. Their homes are themselves. Their bodies are districts. Their deaths are streets. Their growth is construction. Their motion is sound. Their memory is wood. Their breath is condensation. Their sight, if one dares use the word, is the slow turning of leaves toward light, not because they see, but because they respond. Their hands, if one dares use the word, are tendrils that latch onto supports, not because they reach, but because growth curves around what it touches.

And at the edge of all this description, the final answer becomes paradoxical but true:

**Nothing lives there in the animal sense. Everything lives there in the vegetal sense. The city itself lives there.**

Not as a person. Not as an animal. Not as a machine. Not as a consciousness. Not as a soul. But as a place that has made life into form so completely that form and life cannot be told apart.

If you want to name what lives in the Green City That Makes Itself, call it many things, because no single name is stable enough:

A city without citizens.

A forest without animals.

A town made of tissue.

A parliament of stems.

A civic body of leaves.

A population of processes.

An ecology with municipal grammar.

A valley that has turned into living architecture.

A botanical civilization without civilization.

A crowd of forms that do not walk.

A metropolis grown, not built.

A place where growth is masonry, water is infrastructure, decay is reconstruction, and the city is its own inhabitant.

That is what lives there.

Not someone. Not something with a face or a name. Not a creature moving through the passages. But a **living municipal abundance**, green and loud and wet and ceaselessly rebuilding itself. It lives by unfurling. It lives by cracking. It lives by holding water. It lives by casting shadow. It lives by collapsing and becoming a bridge. It lives by dying and becoming a plaza. It lives by remembering moisture in the grooves of its wood. It lives by leaning toward light. It lives by filling every empty space until emptiness becomes only a pause in growth.

The Green City does not have inhabitants as a house has people. It is inhabited by the **continuity of plant life itself**, so organized, so dense, so urban, and so loud that the valley seems to be full of beings when it is actually full of becoming.
