What lives there is not a species. It is a chorus.

If you go looking for inhabitants in the ordinary sense — for creatures that emerge from doorways, gather in markets, sleep in rooms, leave footprints in dust, or answer a voice from across the street — you will find almost nothing. A fly walks across a condensation pool and is gone. A beetle moves through moss like a dark stone with legs. A vole flickers in a crack and disappears. At dusk, a fox may enter one square, pause between two low walls, and turn its head as though listening for someone who has been called and never came. A moth rests inside a niche with its wings closed, pale as a folded leaf. But none of these creatures is the city. None of them has built it. None of them, for very long, remains. They are visitors through a living place, transient as weather.

The real inhabitants are slower, stranger, and far more numerous. They are the things that made the streets and the things that maintain them. They are the things that answer the drip with growth, the wind with porosity, the cold with contraction, and the morning with a green, damp, mineral breathing. The city is inhabited by its own architecture, because its architecture is alive.

Begin with the walls.

They are not walls in the way a human understands a wall, which is to say they are not deliberate barriers raised to separate one interior from another. They are accretions. They are the residue of many tiny lives layered over old stone. Lichens cover them, and lichens are not one organism but a partnership: a fungal body housing photosynthetic algae or cyanobacteria, the fungus building the skeleton, the alga feeding it. Together they make the hard, flaky, granular crusts that color the pale rock in jade, black-green, ash, and pale rust. Some lichens are smooth where the wind scours them. Some are cracked into small tiles. Some swell outward into rounded shelves. Some grow as thin veins, threading through the surface like ink lines. These lichens are the city’s masons. They secrete acids, dissolve traces of mineral, trap particles from fog and dust, and slowly bind the rock surface into plates and ridges. Over centuries, that crust thickens, curls, cracks, repairs itself, and becomes a wall.

Moss is the city’s softer citizen. In the damp places, mosses gather into deep cushions of green velvet, but that green velvet is not passive. Each shoot is a small plant with rhizoids gripping stone, leaves that soak up moisture directly, and internal tissues that expand when wet and contract when dry. In the morning, when the air is thick with condensation, the moss is heavy and open, each tiny stem standing up to catch the light and the dripping water. When wind and sun dry it, the same cushions shrink inward, turning brittle, pale-edged, almost dormant. They seem to close like fists. At the base of the larger walls, where water runs in small channels, mosses form thick mats that hold moisture for days after the rain has stopped. Between the mineral tiles, low cushion-plants and ferns take advantage of the same wet niches, so that the “pavement” of the city is never truly bare. It is a patchwork of tiny gardens, each one filling the crack that water or wind has allowed.

Fungi live there too. They appear at first as pale teeth around the bases of the larger accretions, or as flat, wet discs catching condensation in the shadow of the walls. Some are too small to notice until they release spores with faint popping clicks. Others are broad and fleshy, glistening in the cool light. But the more important fungal presence is underground and invisible: a network of hyphae, pale as fine thread, running beneath and through the crust, binding particles, feeding on decay, linking patches of life to one another. Where a lichen dies, where moss thins, where mineral crust cracks, fungal activity helps the system heal. The city does not have plumbing, but it has fungal pathways. It does not have nails or mortar, but it has living glue.

Algae and cyanobacteria make up still another layer of citizens. These are the film-makers, the darkeners, the green-black stains that gather wherever water lingers long enough. In shallow depressions and on damp shelves, they build thin skins that trap dust and precipitate minerals. They are the reason the tiles have a sheen when wet, the reason certain hollows look polished while others look rough. They are also the reason the basin appears to have gutters. A trace of moisture, repeated over years, encourages cyanobacteria and mineral-depositing organisms to form ridges that direct water more effectively than if the water ran over bare stone. The gutters are not carved. They are grown. The city’s drainage is not engineering; it is the accumulated preference of living surfaces.

Minerals themselves may not be alive, but in this place they behave as participants. The surrounding land is ancient and worn, releasing calcium, silica, iron, salts, and other dissolved materials into rain and fog. Water carries these minerals through the basin. Evaporation, cooling, and biological activity cause them to precipitate into thin plates and crusts. Some plates form where the wind is gentle and water pools. Others form where moisture passes quickly, leaving mineral traces along its route. Over time, these mineral skins fuse with lichen crusts and moss mats, creating surfaces that look like fired clay, baked enamel, or pale bone. The walls of the city are therefore partly animal, partly vegetable, partly fungal, partly microbial, and partly mineral. To say that the city is “made” of moss is too simple. It is a living sediment. It is a biofilm that learned, by accident and time, to look like streets.

The most accurate answer is this: what lives there is a community of organisms that has become so extensive it has taken on the appearance of a settlement. A single moss does not make a plaza. A single lichen does not make a wall. But over centuries, in the exact conditions of this basin, these small creatures grow in response to the same gradients: light, water, wind, stone shape, mineral flow, freezing, thawing, and decay. Their growth is not planned, but it is ordered. The basin’s hydrology selects which forms succeed. The wind selects which surfaces become porous. The stone selects where roots can grip. The cold selects which cycles of wetting and drying can occur. The living city is therefore an expression of the nonliving basin. It is the basin growing itself into urban forms.

There is a name that almost fits but should be used carefully: a biological crust. In many high, dry, cold places, soil is held together by lichens, mosses, cyanobacteria, and fungi. Such crusts stabilize ground, trap moisture, and preserve mineral structure. Here, the crust has gone far beyond a thin skin. The conditions are favorable enough, stable enough, ancient enough, for the crust to build upward. It thickens. It forms ledges. It forms arches. It forms columns. It forms platforms. It forms streets. In effect, the biological crust has become a three-dimensional organism. It is like coral in mineral construction, except that the reef here is made of cold water, fog, lichen, moss, and time instead of tropical animals building limestone.

If you walk through the basin, you are walking through the body of that organism. The spaces between the walls are not gaps in its life; they are part of its form, the way the openings between branches are part of the tree. The arches are not monuments; they are the shape that growth has taken where moisture, wind, and mineral have met. The plazas are not empty lots; they are places where the crust has spread flat, where spores settle, where water slows, and where the surface has become a thin shield against further erosion. The “streets” are the preferred routes of dampness. The “thresholds” are the places where one microclimate changes into another. The “steps” are ridges formed by repeated water flow and drying. The “benches” are thicker accretions where a slight hollow filled in. The “towers” are places where lichen and mineral built upward faster than around them. None of these forms is metaphorical. They are literal living structures, grown by nonhuman processes that happen to resemble human ones.

Their behavior gives the city a strange kind of personality.

At first it appears as a collection of passive organisms enduring the weather. But look more closely and the city responds with astonishing consistency. When rain comes, it catches the water, stores it, and releases it gradually. When wind passes, it bends, sheds, and channels it. When frost forms, it cracks and heals. When a drip begins in a place where none had run before, the crust changes its habits: new ridges appear, new hollows deepen, new shelves thicken. The city does not have nerves, but it has chemistry. It does not have memory in the sense of recollection, but it has memory in the sense of structure: the way a riverbed remembers the river, the way a cave remembers water. A wall that once carried runoff continues, for a long while, to be wetter, greener, and more porous than the stone beside it. A corner exposed to scouring wind grows harder and flatter than the one shielded from it. A channel where condensation drips develops a lip that modifies the sound of the drip. This is not intention, but it is continuity. The inhabitants are not choosing streets, but they are responding to them.

The city also has a sense of time that is difficult for humans to inhabit.

A single season of growth may seem like a blink to a person, but for the organisms it is a life. The mosses that appear green in one afternoon may take many years to cover a tile. A fungal shelf may persist through dozens of winters, shrinking and swelling, dying at its edges and regenerating from its heart. A lichen plate may grow so slowly that its movement is invisible except in the accumulation of centuries. The city therefore experiences change not as event but as weather. It does not celebrate births or mourn deaths. It repairs. It fills gaps. It re-cracks. It softens. It mineralizes. It becomes. To stand in it feels like standing inside a long metabolic sentence that has been speaking itself for millennia.

This slowness is why it looks empty. Human eyes are quick and look for quick movements. They scan for faces, gestures, tools, fire, doorways that open and close. The city offers almost none of these things. It offers spores released into air. It offers hyphae spreading beneath a crust. It offers moss absorbing dew. It offers lichen expanding by a millimeter a year. It offers mineral precipitating out of fog. It offers tiny animals walking through the green hair of a cushion plant, crossing distances that, to them, are enormous. In the scale of their bodies, one patch of moss can be a forest, one dripping crack a canyon, one wet tile a plain, one lichen shelf a cliff face. A beetle navigating between moss stems moves through a jungle. A midge stepping across a condensation pool is crossing a lake. A springtail grazing among dead filaments in a damp crevice may spend its entire life within a space smaller than a fingernail. These small beings do not make the city, but they live inside its texture, and the city is rich with niches for them: shaded pools, moss cushions, fungal shelves, mineral ridges, and cracks that retain water after the larger world has dried.

Larger fauna are rarer. The high cold and the thin food make the basin inhospitable to sustained animal life. The birds pass overhead but do not nest. The fox appears only briefly, perhaps checking whether the stone and green have produced prey. The vole hides in a crack and vanishes. There are no herds, no packs, no settled colonies. And yet even these rare visitors alter the emotional reading of the place. When the fox stands in a courtyard, the city suddenly seems watched. When the vole appears, the streets seem to have inhabitants who can hide. When a moth rests inside a niche, the architecture appears to be in use. But the presence of these animals does not prove habitation. It proves only that the empty city can hold small lives for short durations, the way a ruined temple can hold a bird for the night.

The most persistent animal presence may be microscopic or nearly so. Inside the moss and damp crusts, there are mites, springtails, nematodes, rotifers, tardigrades, and bacteria that do not appear as a fauna of grand sightings but form part of the city’s metabolism. They graze, digest, move through water films, and contribute to the breakdown of organic matter. They are not architects, but they help maintain the substrate. They are part of the community that makes the living city possible. In that sense, the inhabitants include both the visible citizens of moss and lichen and the invisible citizens of microbes and small animals.

Still, the true resident is the collective.

If one insists on imagining a creature, then the creature is the basin itself. It has a body of stone and accretion. It has organs of moisture, channels, shelves, and crusts. It breathes by fog and frost. It speaks by wind and cracking ice. It grows by mineral and organism. It ages by erosion and repair. It remembers by structure. It is not sentient. It does not plan. It does not want. It does not dream in the way a person dreams. But it has a kind of coherence so total that it becomes reasonable to speak of it as a being. A human city, viewed from enough distance, also looks like a single organism with streets and plazas. Here the comparison is reversed: the organism has learned to look like a city, or perhaps the city was never separate from the organism. The town and its inhabitants are the same living mass, only expressed at different scales.

This is the strangest thing about the place: it does not merely contain life; it is life.

The green life of moss, lichen, fungus, and algae is not decoration applied to an empty stone frame. The stone itself has been incorporated into the living system. The walls are alive where lichens cover them. The roofs are alive where crusts thicken. The streets are alive because they are channels of mineral water and spores. The plazas are alive because they hold moisture, dust, and biological film. Even the bare-looking tiles are alive in the sense that they are the residue of living processes. Remove the moss, scrape the lichen, wash away the fungal threads, and the basin would no longer be the city. It would be only a bowl of old rock. The city is the community. The city is the metabolism.

What lives there, then, is a slow civic ecology.

It is an ecology that does not flourish with warmth and abundance but with scarcity, patience, cold, moisture, and mineral repetition. It is lush not because it is generous, but because it has had too much time to fill every surface. It is quiet not because it is dead, but because its activities are small and slow. It is empty not because nothing lives there, but because life here does not look like life when looked at too quickly. A person expecting human signs will see absence. A person willing to look at scale will see a teeming city.

The city’s daily rhythm can be read as its living day.

In early light, before wind rises, the basin is most visibly alive. Condensation weighs on every surface. The moss is expanded and dark, the lichens soft, the pools glassy. The city is wet and listening. Sound is mostly dripping, a soft percussion from ledge to ledge. The air holds the smell of mineral dust and cold water. At this hour, the inhabitants are saturated, open to light, and chemically active. The algae and mosses begin photosynthesis as soon as the sun touches the stone. The fungi catch and hold the moisture. The crust softens slightly where it can.

By midday, the wind arrives. The moss begins to dry. The fungal heads tense and release spores in faint puffs. The lichen crusts become more brittle. Air moves through holes formed by years of uneven growth, and the city’s voice changes from dripping to humming, whistling, rattling, and creaking. This is when the empty city is most loud. The sounds come from everywhere: from the walls, the ground, the thin edges of mineral plates, the tiny cups of lichen, the hollow arches, and the moss itself. The city is not calling to anyone. It is being itself in weather. But to a human ear, it sounds like habitation, because habitation also makes noise through bodies moving through space.

In afternoon, when shadows lengthen and heat fades, the city begins to contract. Frost forms. Ice cracks small surfaces. The green becomes less bright but not less alive. At night, with the rim dark against the stars, the basin may be still except for the soft ticking of freezing water and the whisper of dry lichen. The city is not sleeping. It is in another phase of the same continuous metabolism: storage, resistance, hardening, spore waiting, mineral settling.

If one wanted to describe its inhabitants in human terms, they would be a parliament of cryptogams. Mosses would be the green wardens of dampness. Lichens would be the architects of stone surfaces. Fungi would be the repairers and connectors. Algae and cyanobacteria would be the film-makers and glue-makers. Mineral precipitates would be the silent laborers that the living organisms recruit. Tiny insects, moths, beetles, and flies would be the temporary citizens. The fox would be a visitor at the border. The vole would be a rumor in a crack. The birds would be gods moving over the rim.

But those human roles are only analogies, useful because the city itself is analogic. It has the forms of settlement without the settlement’s cause. It has the appearance of habitation without a habitant’s intention. It is not a town with missing people. It is a town made by nonpeople. The inhabitants do not occupy the city because they built it. The city is their building, their body, their archive, and their method of living.

There is another way to say it, less literal but perhaps truer.

What lives there is a long relationship between water and surface.

The basin is old. Its stone is soft. The atmosphere is cold and thin. The land no longer offers drama. There are no glaciers here, no torrents, no forests, no human roads, no animals with voices that echo across hills. Everything has been reduced to slow cycles: condensation, freezing, thawing, evaporation, dust, frost cracking, spore drifting, mineral accretion. Into this quiet, repetitive system comes life that is equally patient. Moss, lichen, fungi, and algae do not compete for dominance in the way a forest canopy does. They succeed by filling small niches, surviving harshness, holding water, and waiting. Over time, their collective waiting becomes form. The water’s paths become streets. The wind’s scouring becomes walls. The mineral’s deposition becomes tiles. The organisms’ death and regrowth become ruins, but ruins that keep building themselves.

So if you ask what lives in the city, the answer is not that a hidden creature has built it, or that a ghostly race left it behind. The answer is stranger: the city is alive because life is not limited to animals. The city is empty of people because people are not required for a settlement to emerge. The city is populated by processes that have become places. The streets are living membranes. The plazas are breathing surfaces. The arches are mineral bodies grown by damp time.

And if you stand in the largest plaza at midday, when the wind has shifted through the porous walls and the whole basin begins to sound like an audience that has not yet decided to speak, you can almost understand it. You do not hear a mind. You do not see a face. But the place behaves as though it knows you are there, not because it thinks, but because everything you do — your shadow, your warmth, the carbon dioxide you exhale, the tiny disturbances of your boots on the frost — changes the city’s immediate environment. The moss beneath you compresses. A tile cracks. Spores release near your foot. The wind changes around your shoulders. For a few seconds, the basin alters its behavior because of your presence.

In that sense, you too become one of its passing inhabitants.

But the permanent residents remain what they always were: the green and gray community, the crust and cushion, the thread and film, the slow builders that turn water paths into streets and mineral dust into walls. They do not need your approval, your awe, your explanation, or your story. They only continue. They drink the fog. They hold the rain. They crack under frost. They mend in damp. They spread across the old stone. They make the small world look, to your eye, like a city, and in doing so they prove that a city can be a biological habit rather than a human invention.

What lives there is therefore not a population of individuals but a collective being: a town of moss, lichen, algae, fungi, mineral, water, and wind, with rare animals moving through it as if through an empty room they did not own. It is the basin’s own metabolism, grown so large and layered that it has taken on the grammar of settlement. The city is not haunted. The city is not abandoned. The city is occupied by the very thing that makes it a city.
