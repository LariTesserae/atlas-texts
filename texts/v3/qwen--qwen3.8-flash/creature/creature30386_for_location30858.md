Nothing there has a permanent address, because the ground is not a floor. It is a body. The inhabitants of the Green Foundry Plateau are therefore not merely creatures living upon it, though they are also that. They are processes embedded in a living tissue that has already begun to behave as if stone, water, weather, and animal life were only slow organs of a single planetary anatomy.

If you ask the plateau what lives there, it does not answer with a list. It answers with a rhythm: the click of frost in fiber, the hammer-thud of hidden pumps, the whisper of fog dragged uphill, the soft green pulse of light in veins of moss. To say who or what lives there is to describe many nested beings, each one too strange to be mistaken for an ordinary creature, each one also too tightly woven into the whole to be mistaken for an individual.

The first thing that lives there is the mat.

Not the trees, though there are trees if you stretch the word until it nearly breaks. Not the ponds, though the ponds think with the roots. Not even the animals, though they carry the mat’s spores in their bellies and leave its fibers tangled in their coats. The first and largest living thing is the skin of the plateau itself: a fused, creeping, secretory membrane of horizontal wood, moss, lichen, sedge-fibers, mineral crusts, fungal threads, and pale translucent stalks that move in water like submerged hands. This skin is not a carpet laid over the land. It is the land’s physiology made visible.

The mat breathes.

In cold places, when the air is wet enough to be almost liquid, the peat domes draw inward. Their pores tighten. The water beneath the roots pulls back by fractions of an inch. Then the heat trapped inside their dark bodies lifts, exhaling a green vapor through tiny mouths of plant-tissue. The breath smells of iron, of old sap, of melted snow, and of something faintly sweet that is not sugar but the chemical ghost of sweetness, a molecule the plateau learned to imitate long before anything had tongues capable of tasting it. When the exhalation is strong enough, nearby stones become coated in a fine white efflorescence, like salt, like bone-dust, like frost that has remembered being a sea.

The mat drinks.

Water does not simply lie on the surface. It is drawn through root-filaments, capillary tubes, mineral films, and the hollow cores of horizontal tree-ribs. Some of these ribs are dead on the outside and alive inside, their bark split into pale slats that hold moisture without ever seeming wet. Water moves through them at night when the air condenses, and by morning the branches are swollen with it, heavy enough to bend the ground beneath. Other channels are almost invisible: seams of gel between leaf-base and stone, channels under gravel bars, veins of sap-black fluid running below the green skin. To stand on the mat is to stand on something wet, alive, and listening.

The mat sees without eyes.

It has no single mind, or if it has a mind it is distributed so broadly that “mind” becomes a poor word. It senses pressure: the weight of a grazing beast, the footfall of an unseen bird, the slow settling of a stone that has decided after millennia to roll. It senses chemical change: the acidity of a predator’s breath, the salt released from a wound, the mineral bloom of a pond about to turn over. It senses sound through vibration in the fiber-web: the hum of insect wings, the hammering of hidden pistons, the delayed crack of ice forming inside a stem. Where the mat grows thin over porous rock, its cells elongate into pale antennae and tremble when certain frequencies pass. These tremors are not nerves in the animal sense, but they perform the same work. They tell the ground where to close, where to open, where to store water, where to release it.

The mat remembers.

Not as a brain remembers. As stone remembers heat. As frost remembers the shape of the crack it fell into. Where a herd once stood for a thousand slow years, the ground holds a faint oval depression even after the animals are gone. Where a bird flew through a tree that should have blocked it, the wood grows a translucent scar of resin around the memory of the passage. Where the reality of the plateau frayed—where a reflection belonged to dry ground, where a sound came before its source, where a hollow had not yet fully formed—the mat often thickens, as if covering a mistake with tissue. These scars do not heal into normality. They become architecture. The plateau builds itself out of its errors.

This is why the Green Foundry Plateau cannot be described as wilderness. A wilderness has the pretense of being outside time, outside purpose. Here everything is time-made-flesh. The land has been worn down so completely that even its ruins are productive. The mountains are gone, but their collapse left an industry: roots as pumps, peat as condensers, basins as reactors, lichen as refinery, wind as ventilation, water as solvent. The mat is not merely alive. It is operational.

Beneath the mat, in tunnels that are part root, part intestine, part abandoned memory, live the thread-crawlers.

They are the most numerous inhabitants of the plateau, and perhaps the most essential. They are pale, segmented things, some no larger than a grain of sand, some as long as a finger, some longer, folded into themselves in ways that make their anatomy difficult to follow. They have too many eyes or too few; in the lower depths, eyes are rare and usually useless. They have instead chemical pits, heat-sensitive bristles, and mouths that move sideways, grinding dead fiber, living fiber, mineral sediment, fungal mats, the shed skins of larvae, the white frost that forms inside stems, and sometimes the broken edges of reality where the plant-mat has split.

The thread-crawlers are the digestion of the land.

They move through tunnels lined with root-hairs, pushing ahead them a slow plug of processed matter that becomes white fiber, mineral paste, breathable air, or heat. Some species generate warmth as they feed. Their bodies are pale because there is no light in these passages, but they are not cold. They are warm, faintly swollen, and smell of wet yeast and iron. Where a herd of grazers presses into a peat dome, the thread-crawlers below redistribute the weight by shifting the moisture around them. Where a stone weeps mineral water, they seal the seep with a resinous film that later becomes food. Where the mat tears open and a pale root-lattice is exposed, they flood the edges with slow green fluid and begin to weave it closed.

They are never fully absent from observation, even when no one sees them. Their work creates the sounds that make the plateau seem like a factory: the popping of sap chambers, the soft grinding of chitinous mouths, the whisper of air escaping tunnels that open and close with pressure changes. Sometimes, when the mat is very thin, you can see their movement as a ripple under a broad sheet of green skin, a ripple with no wind, moving from inside the ground as if something below were breathing out.

On top of the mat move the frost-kine.

These are the herds of thin-limbed, cold-adapted grazers mentioned by the landscape itself, but they are not ordinary livestock. Their bodies are stretched and narrow, as though drawn out by long generations of standing in wind. Their legs are jointed higher and lower than one expects, giving them a slightly folded, springy posture. Their hooves are broad and layered, not with the usual horn alone but with a flexible outer rime of pale, almost glassy tissue that spreads pressure so carefully that the animal can stand in shallow water without breaking the surface. To see one from a distance, it appears suspended. To stand near it, you realize it is not floating but distributing its weight across the mat as if the mat were a membrane stretched over a drum.

Their coats are pale, frayed, and green-streaked, because the mat has incorporated their shed hairs into its fibers. This is not mere camouflage. It is exchange. As the frost-kine walk, they deposit tiny fragments of hoof-tissue, skin-dust, bile residue, and spore-laden breath. The mat absorbs them. In return, the mat produces edible fibers, mineral-rich mosses, and low buds that grow only in the shadows of standing animals. A frost-kine that has grazed for generations does not eat the landscape. It participates in a slow mutualism in which its body becomes a nutrient source and its presence becomes a weather station for the plants around it.

They are herd creatures, but their herds are not tight knots of bodies. They are scattered across the plateau as if the land has distributed them according to some secret census. One animal may stand beside a black pond for hours. Another may lie along the base of a horizontal tree, its neck curved around the wood until the two appear fused. A third may walk in a straight line that seems meaningless from above but follows a chemical gradient in the mat, a path where the water beneath is slightly warmer, slightly more acidic, slightly more charged with dissolved minerals. The herds are a lattice, not a crowd. When one animal moves, another far away may shift its head at the same moment, as if the pressure of walking has traveled through the ground.

Their eyes are wide, dark, and reflective, set low on narrow heads. They see poorly in bright light—there is little—but well in the dim green glow of the plateau at night. Their pupils dilate into rings. Their ears are large and can rotate backward, catching the delayed cracks and hums that drift over distance. They can hear the mat’s slow contraction. They can hear water moving under skin. They can hear, sometimes, sounds from places that are not yet visible. When a valley ahead has not settled into its own shape, a frost-kine may stop and tilt its head, as if listening for the land to finish becoming itself.

They eat by cropping, not tearing. Their mouths are low, wide, and lined with fibrous tissue that filters the green mat into digestible strands. They do not rip large sections from the ground; that would be too much like violence, and the plateau is not violent, even when it is lethal. Instead they bite, pull, chew, and spit. What they spit is not waste but a paste of saliva, mineral fragments, and selected spores. In this way, the frost-kine plant the ground as they graze. Their dung is equally important: dark, wet, and often containing pale beetle-like insects that live only inside the dung of a grazing herd and then fall into the mat, where they begin their next stage as thread-crawlers.

They reproduce rarely.

Their births are not dramatic. A calf or young—whatever word fits for such a lineage—emerges already thin-legged and alert, lying in a hollow that the mat has made around it. It does not stand immediately. It waits until the chemical tide of the herd matches the chemical tide of the ground. The mother does not call. She stands near the hollow and adjusts her weight until the mat lifts or lowers by a fraction of an inch. If the young is correct for that moment, it follows. If it is not, the herd does not reject it. It simply stops carrying it, and the young is absorbed into the larger body of the mat, becoming fiber, becoming nutrient, becoming memory. This sounds cruel only if one insists on separating the young from the land. Here, absorption is not punishment. It is continuation.

When a frost-kine dies, its body does not remain for long. The mat grows over it in pale waves. The bones become hollows filled with fungal threads. The skin becomes a mesh for lichen. Sometimes a herd will stand around a place where one has died, but there is no corpse, only a slight green mound breathing slowly. Sometimes their shadows fall incorrectly around the mound. Sometimes the mound itself is not there until after the herd has stopped looking at it.

Over the water live the ringwalkers.

They are long-legged insects, though “insect” is a word borrowed from a warmer, more solid world. Their bodies are thin and segmented, often transparent at the edges, with a faint blue-white sheen like cold glass. Their legs are so fine that they seem to touch the water without breaking it, but the surface tension is not the only thing they rely on. The plateau’s water is chemically active, threaded with suspended plant matter, mineral films, and microscopic fibers. The ringwalkers move across a surface that is partly liquid and partly living. Their feet leave tiny rings because their weight distorts the water’s skin and releases gases trapped beneath it. The rings multiply into geometric patterns because the surface is not uniform: it contains currents, spore-fields, thermal pockets, and memory-lattices left by earlier insects.

A swarm of ringwalkers does not simply fly. It computes.

When thousands gather above a black basin, their wings create a low whine that changes pitch according to the density of the air. The rings beneath them form intersecting lines, arcs, and star-points. To a careless eye, this is decoration. To a careful eye, it is navigation. The ringwalkers are using the water surface as a map of pressure and chemistry. They are reading the hidden structure of the pond. They are telling one another where the surface will hold, where it will open, where a thread-crawler tunnel lies beneath, where the reflection of a cloud belongs to a sky not yet arrived.

They feed on biofilm: the slippery green and brown layers that grow on still water, on damp stones, on the undersides of floating leaves. They also feed on the dead bodies of other insects, on shed frost-kine hair, on droplets that form in the air itself, on mineral dust released by eroding ridges, and on spores that grow in the dim light.
