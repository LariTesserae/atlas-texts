To ask what lives in the Upper Anhydrite Table is to ask too small a question at first, and too large a question afterward. There are no faces looking back from doorways, no families arranging themselves around warmth, no children running along the slag roads, no domestic fire in the ordinary sense. The houses stand empty, but they are not abandoned. The machines turn, but they are not being operated by hands. The streets are paved and marked, but no footfall is known to have altered them for ages. The city breathes only because heat rises and settles again, and even that breathing is dry.

And yet the Stillworks is full.

It is full of inhabitants that would be missed by a visitor who arrived expecting a population. There are residents here, but they do not call themselves residents. They do not gather, migrate, nest, court, feed, or flee in the ways that would make them obvious. Their lives are spread out so slowly that to observe one complete day in the life of something dwelling here may be to see almost nothing. A single wall may have been occupied for centuries by a pattern that only becomes visible when the pattern is looked at across five hundred years. A road may contain the record of an entire civilization’s habit in the worn edge of its fused surface. A shaft may be home to a rhythm that repeats itself with the patience of an organ.

The largest inhabitant of the Stillworks is not a creature at all, but the place itself.

The plateau is not simply a stage upon which other beings have arranged themselves. The anhydrite table, the black-brown basalt, the ash-white mineral shelves, the sealed salt, the dark iron roads, the glass-threaded veins, the warm stone—these are not inert. They have been organized so thoroughly by the city and by the slow chemistry of the table that the land behaves like an organism whose body is mostly geology. The plateaus are its skin. The shafts are its pores. The gear trains and flywheels are its organs. The mineral threads are its nerves. The lithovores moving through cracks are its wandering cells. The ash-shivers drifting in shafts and plazas are the visible part of its breath, if breath can mean the motion of heat, static, and suspended crystal without moisture.

This is not a living city in the way a green forest lives, nor in the way a river lives, nor in the way a human town lives when smoke rises from chimneys and voices carry through streets. The Stillworks lives the way a sealed furnace lives after the workers have gone: still hot, still structured, still answering to habits that no one remembers. It is not animated by desire. It is animated by recurrence.

Beneath the apparent barrenness, there is the first and most pervasive population: the thread networks.

The pale, branching filaments that move through pores of anhydrite and along seams of cooled slag are not quite crystal and not quite fungus, but if one must call them something, one might call them the mineral nerves of the table. They spread through walls without weakening them. They run beneath roadways without lifting them. They penetrate vaulted underpasses and old cisterns that never held water, following the old paths of drainage that water would once have taken if there had ever been water. They occupy the inside of stone as a thought occupies the inside of memory: invisibly, persistently, and with a structure that is not random.

These threads feed on chemical tension. They draw sustenance from the subtle differences between one mineral surface and another, from heat stored in iron and released from basalt, from static charge along cables and pipes, from the slow recrystallization of salt, from the tiny stresses that accumulate in a wall that has been holding itself together for thousands of years. They do not photosynthesize, because there is no bright sun and no green plant to require it. They do not rot, because there is no moisture to support the ordinary decay of organic bodies. They do not bloom, because they have no flowers, no fruits, no seeds in the biological sense. They persist. They spread. They adjust. They remember the shapes that have passed through them.

If one presses an ear to a salt wall, or lies flat on a polished slab of basalt in a sealed plaza, the faint dry rustle that can be heard is not wind moving through the structure. It is the sound of the threads. Their movement is not a rustle exactly, but a microscopic reordering, a shifting of crystalline strain, a release of tension from one node to the next. It is a sound like sand moving inside a sealed vessel, like the earth speaking in its sleep.

These thread networks do not have individuals in any way that can be counted. A line that appears to begin at the base of a chimney may continue beneath a road, enter a house, run through a sealed boiler chamber, emerge in a terrace, and vanish into a shaft. Or it may be part of a wider system that has no true beginning or end. The city’s buildings are not separated from the land by a firm boundary, because the threads pass through both. A house built of slag blocks may contain the same mineral network that underlies a mile of plain. A wall may be inhabited by the same pattern as a distant ridge. To ask which is the inhabitant and which the inhabited surface is to misunderstand the place entirely.

The thread-networks are not conscious, if consciousness is taken to mean the human ability to reflect upon itself. They do not feel fear or longing or curiosity. But they do respond. If heat rises too rapidly in a shaft, the threads near the shaft wall adjust their pattern, drawing off tension. If a gear train changes its rhythm, even by a fractional degree, threads running through the mounting stone may thicken at stress points. If a lithovore passes along a seam, the network may leave behind a faint mineral scar that records the creature’s route, not as a memory of sight, but as a physical habit. The threads are the Stillworks’ slow form of recognition. They do not know the city, but the city knows itself through them.

The machines of the Stillworks, too, are not merely machinery.

They are not inhabited by people, but they have become inhabitants in their own right. The distinction is subtle but important. In a younger industrial city, a flywheel turns because a workforce needs it to turn, or because fuel drives it, or because some external purpose requires its motion. In the Stillworks, a flywheel turns because the flywheel’s turning is part of the city’s equilibrium. It has no purpose beyond continuing itself. It is not producing goods. It is not transporting goods. It is not preparing for a future that has any intention of arriving. It turns because turning is its settled state, and because its turning helps the flywheel remain a flywheel.

There is something animal about this.

The great pistons extend from walls into sealed cylinders with a rhythm that is almost breath-like, though there is no air entering or leaving their chambers. The gear trains mesh without shriek or spark, their motion so precise that it does not seem to strain metal. Belts run over pulleys taut and silent, carrying nothing named, yet still carrying something. Boilers stand in rows, warm without fire. The heat that enters them leaves them without diminishing either their warmth or their form. The city has become so old that its technology has lost the appearance of use. It now has the appearance of metabolism.

One could say the machines are organs, except that “organ” implies a body. In the Stillworks, the machines are part of a body too large and too strange to be called a body in any biological way. They are the city’s slow organs of maintenance. They preserve pressure, temperature, mineral stability, and the arrangement of dust. They do not repair themselves, because nothing has broken in the ordinary sense. They do not wear out, because wear has found its final rate. They do not stop, because stopping would mean change, and change in the Stillworks is not forbidden but unnecessary.

The flywheel chamber, the boiler vault, the elevated walk of riveted iron, the capped borehole, the long row of ceramic houses, the plaza beneath which pipes lie motionless but not dead—these are not ruins of an industry. They are the settled organs of a non-biological organism. If a human stood in one of the shafts and listened, they would hear not the voice of a person, but the voice of a condition.

And the condition is warm.

Heat here is not a guest. It is a resident. It rises from below because the Stillworks was built to encourage that rising, but not so much to produce energy as to prevent change. The warmth is distributed by stone, by metal, by ceramic tile, by salt, by dust. It does not create weather. It does not melt ice, because there is no ice. It does not dry what is already dry. It maintains the temperature at which the table’s mineral patterns remain possible. Heat, in such a place, is not merely a physical quantity. It is an inhabitant with habits, a citizen without a face.

The smaller fauna of the Stillworks moves at intervals that seem geological to any creature used to faster lives.

The lithovores are the closest things in the place to animals. They emerge from deep cracks and shaft-rooms where the warmth pools and the darkness is nearly absolute. They are not large. A lithovore might fit within the span of two human hands, if such hands existed to measure it, but its slowness makes it seem larger than its body. It resembles a creature assembled from the patience of stone: thin plates of calcium-like shell over flexible black tissue, each plate joined at microscopic hinges that permit movement without the appearance of hurry. Its legs end in points that do not break rock. Instead, they press against seams as though the rock is being politely asked to open.

They are not blind, because they have no eyes in the familiar sense. They perceive through pressure, through temperature, through chemical tension, through vibration transmitted through mineral. They read the world the way a root might read water if water were a rumor rather than a substance. A lithovore does not see a wall; it senses the wall’s strain, the direction of the thread network beneath it, the warmth moving through it, the slight differences in hardness along a seam. A road is not a visual object to a lithovore; it is a long, slow pressure-pattern under its feet.

These creatures graze on mineral threads.

They move from vein to vein, rubbing their plates against the pale filaments that grow inside slag and anhydrite. The contact releases chemical tension, and the lithovore absorbs that release through its shell-plates. This is not eating in the way an animal eats leaves or flesh. There is no chewing, no swallowing, no digestion of organic matter into dung. Instead, the process is more like a tuning of bodies. The lithovore draws sustenance from a chemical imbalance, and the thread network is altered by the drawing, not simply damaged. The two have been associated for so long that neither can be called predator or prey. They are partners in a closed economy of mineral patience.

Their reproduction—if it can be called reproduction—is a slow event. A lithovore does not mate in a season. It does not lay eggs in a nest. It sheds plates at intervals measured in years or decades, leaving behind fragments that can accumulate mineral thread and become seeds of future structure. Some fragments die. Some are buried. Some are carried into cracks by ash-shivers or dust movement. Some settle against a warm wall and begin to develop a shell-plate of their own, slowly, over what a human lifetime would call centuries.
