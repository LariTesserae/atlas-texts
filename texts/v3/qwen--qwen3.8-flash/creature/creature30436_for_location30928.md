There are people here.

They are human—if the word still fits them without stretching—or at least the descendants of humans who came to the plateau so long ago that the stone has rewritten them in the way it rewrites water, slowly, by touch, by warmth, by the constant insistence of moisture. They are not the pale, blind things one might expect of high rock and wet dark. The sun is too near for that. They are salt-stained and lamp-marked, bright-skinned where the glare has kissed them, dark-ringed where smoke and rust have settled into creases. Their hands are the first thing to show where they live. Palms glazed with callus. Fingertips often cracked and healed many times, sealed by mineral films. Nails stained with red-brown iron, blue-green copper bloom, or the chalky white of shell dust. Knuckles swollen from steam and cold metal. They have learned to grip wet stone without slipping, to judge heat by the color of condensation, to hear the difference between a full pipe, an empty pipe, and a pipe beginning to fail.

They are broad through the chest and not especially tall, as if many generations spent drawing thin high air through wet lungs had narrowed their bodies toward efficiency. Their shoulders are strong from carrying casks, shell plates, filter mats, and tools along stairs cut into living rock. Their faces have a look common to the city: not fear, not fatigue, not arrogance, but attention. They notice surfaces. A visitor looks at a wall and sees stone. They look at the same wall and see whether it is wetter today, whether the biofilm is pale or blooming, whether a seam is drinking water from behind it, whether the skin there will be good to scrape in two days. They think in surfaces because surfaces are where the city earns its life.

The people do not call themselves “the nation” or “the country” in any proud, cartographic sense. In their own speech they name themselves by what they tend: seam-folk, channel-keepers, roof-skinners, shell-markers, pump-tenders, mist-workers. In the outer towns, those who come up from below the cloud layer call them shelf people, wet-fingered, stone-eaters, cloud-dwellers, or the city above the ordinary.
