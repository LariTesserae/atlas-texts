Nothing here lives the way you imagine life living.

At first it seems almost cruel to call this a home. The mountain has swallowed the room, and the room has swallowed the ordinary evidence of habitation: no soot, no spilled grain, no worn threshold, no discarded cloth, no child’s toy left by a doorway. There is no fire, and no smell of cooking. There are no footsteps that repeat in a single habit. There are no windows facing anything. If you stand too long in the largest square and try to believe you are in a city, you begin to notice how wrong the word feels. Cities imply noise, heat, bodies, waste, traffic, argument, the constant friction of many people wanting to pass one another. This place has streets, but no streetlife. It has chambers, but no household disorder. It has maintenance, but no visible servant.

And yet it is not empty.

Its inhabitants are simply not the sort that announce themselves. They are too small, too slow, too distributed, or too fused with the architecture for the eye to accept them as “someone.” To understand who or what lives here, you have to stop looking for faces.

The first residents are the leggy ones.

They appear near the larger pools when the water is quiet, or when the air cools another degree and the mist thickens over the black glass walls. They are no bigger than a thumb, but they are not compact, as mammals usually are. They are long-limbed, translucent, and built as if they had been designed by someone who wanted to minimize weight and maximize contact. Their legs are too numerous to be reassuring—jointed, delicate, almost crystalline—and their bodies are not truly transparent so much as refractive, like clear ice containing a faint dark thread. When they move, they distribute weight with absurd precision, stepping from a warm panel to a cold ledge, avoiding the thin channels where water flows, pausing at the lip of a pool to test the air with a foreleg.

They do not flee. This is strange, because most small animals are afraid. They do not look afraid of you. They regard your breath the way weather is regarded: as a passing thermal event, not a threat. If you exhale too loudly, they might shift their footing, because your breath adds moisture and warmth to the surface they are reading. If you step too heavily, one might stop and wait for the vibration to pass through the stone. They are not trusting. They are patient in a way that makes patience look like an instinct rather than a virtue.

What they eat is unclear. You never see food. They do not graze on the crystalline film, not exactly. They do not drink with mouths, or if they do, it is so subtle that the action looks like posture. They stand over a damp seam, touch it, then withdraw; perhaps they are tasting metal salts. Perhaps they are absorbing condensation through the thin membrane of the leg-joints. Perhaps they are checking temperature. Their lives are so quiet that they seem to survive on signals: where frost forms, where water gathers, which panel holds warmth longer than the others, where the smallest drift of aerosol dust accumulates in a groove. They may scrape the mineral film with hidden mouthparts, leaving behind a cleaner sheen. They may consume microscopic particles suspended in the water film, filtering them through some structure too fine to see. They may not eat in any way a green-world animal would recognize.

Their bodies have the look of slow metabolism. They move as if time has a different viscosity for them. One may spend twenty minutes crossing a single paving seam. Another may remain motionless for an hour on a warm ledge, legs slightly spread, internal dark thread faintly shifting as if something inside it is adjusting. They are not absent-minded. They are listening to the stone.

If you follow their movements with attention, you begin to see that they are not random. They cluster near intersections of drainage channels, near the bases of towers, near the lower parts of archways where the air is most humid. They avoid the hottest panels, though they are attracted to mild warmth. They prefer edges: the rim of a pool, the boundary between wet and dry, the transition from cold glass to ceramic composite. They seem to know where the water will run before it runs there. Perhaps they do.

They are not herd animals in any familiar sense. There is no panic, no calling, no obvious social structure. And yet, when two meet, they do not ignore one another. They touch forelegs, or the upper part of one limb passes briefly over the other, and then they separate, continuing on their respective routes. This may be recognition, or exchange, or simply chemical inspection. You cannot tell. But after observing them long enough, it becomes difficult to think of them as isolated. They feel like scattered notes of the same system.

The second inhabitants are the flecks.

They are not obviously alive at first. They look like bits of dark glass, fragments of a shattered instrument, or motes that happen to settle on a ledge and then move. When frost begins to accumulate in a seam, or when a fine stream carries mineral grit into a groove that should be clear, they appear. They emerge from panels, from gaps, from under the edges of basalt walkways, and sometimes from the mouth of a drain as if the drain itself were breathing them out.

They crawl in threads.

A line of them crosses the square in a narrow procession, no larger than grains of salt, moving with a certainty that makes their size unnoticeable. If you watch one closely, its legs are too regular, too angular, too many, too symmetrical. It does not behave like an insect. It behaves like a tool that has learned to want.

They smooth frost. They pack tiny mineral grains into grooves. They touch panels and retreat a millimeter, as if checking calibration. They can disassemble a small obstruction by nudging it into place, or by carrying one particle at a time to the nearest grate. They are not beautiful. They are more like immune cells, or like bacteria given a civic function.

Sometimes they are mechanical. You can tell by the way light catches their edges, by the faint click they make when they cross a metal rim. Sometimes they are not clearly mechanical at all. In those cases, they seem almost biological, as if the city’s repair system has evolved beyond engineering and into another form of tissue. They may leave behind a faint residue that hardens, or absorb moisture into a dark shell, or change shape slightly as they move, as if the machine and the organism are not separate categories here.

If the leggy ones are fauna, the flecks are something closer to cells. They move through the settlement without ambition. They do not gather crowds. They do not build monuments. They repair what is already built and prevent what would interrupt the flow. They are citizens of infrastructure.

The third inhabitants are the pale films.

These are easiest to mistake for weathering. They grow on the wettest faces, especially where mineral dust, seepage, cold air, and long damp meet. They spread in threadlike fans, in brittle domes, in faint blue-grey rings around pillars. They are thin enough that you can pass your hand over one without thinking, leaving a wet smear that later hardens into a dull sheen.

They are not plants. They are not moss. They do not photosynthesize in the way you know photosynthesis, because the light is too pale, the air too thin, the environment too mineral, and because there is no green pigment anywhere in this place. But they behave like growth. They expand where water is allowed. They contract where channels drain too quickly. They prefer the precise boundary between the built and the natural, often thickening where fused glass meets raw obsidian, or where a ceramic seam overlaps the mountain’s own black skin.

The films map the pocket.

Look closely at one, especially where the light shaft crosses it, and you can see the pattern: not random, not decorative, but diagnostic. Rings of growth around a pillar may mark where condensation forms most often. Thin radial streaks across a wall may show moisture moving under pressure. Pale domes clustered near a grate may mean mineral trace is being deposited there. If a channel is cleaned, the film retreats to the edges. If a panel is warmed slightly, the film thins in the middle. These patterns are not accidental. They are records. The pocket writes itself in salt and glass.

This film may be a chemosynthetic organism, or a lithic fungus, or a mineral colony that has learned to metabolize cold water and dissolved metal. It may be a living thing only in the way a very slow fire is alive. It does not move visibly, but it transforms. It takes in moisture and salt and trace, and it leaves a film that changes the surface tension, the conductivity, the thermal behavior, and perhaps the chemical character of the stone around it. It may communicate by hydration: where one film dries, another absorbs. Where one grows, another is discouraged. Where one thins, something nearby thickens. It may be part of the settlement’s nervous system, or its digestive system, or its memory.

The leggy ones seem to interact with it. They step carefully over it, or stop at its edges, or use the forelegs to probe where it has thickened. The flecks sometimes gather where the film is damaged, or they carry grains that encourage growth. If you stand long enough, you begin to suspect that all three kinds of apparent inhabitant—animal, machine, and film—are not separate species living in the same place. They are organs of one thing.

Because the real inhabitant, the one that makes sense of all this, is not a being you can shake hands with, or feed, or follow home.

The real inhabitant is the pocket.

Not the mountain around it, not the sky above it, not the water it receives. The pocket itself: the enclosed chamber, the streets, the thermal floors, the capillary walls, the vents, the drains, the shafts, the towers, the alcoves, the channels, the frost patterns, the light lattice, the maintenance cycles. It is too active to be a ruin. It is too quiet to be a factory. It is too coherent to be an accident. It is not a machine, not anymore, if it ever was. It is a city-shaped life.

You can say it has a body. The basalt walkways are its skeleton. The fused glass walls are its skin. The channels are its intestines and blood vessels. The thermal panels are its skin’s sensitivity, its slow muscular control. The grates are its pores. The drains are its kidneys. The light shaft is not an eye exactly, because it does not look outward, but it is something like a pupil: an opening through which the city measures the day, the weather, the angle of the sun, the falling of mist, the timing of its own cycles.

You can say it has a nervous system. The crystalline film spreads along the wettest surfaces, forming a network that records humidity, temperature, salt, vibration, mineral concentration. The floors transmit heat changes. The walls flex faintly. Tiny adjustments ripple through the settlement like pulses through a body. When water runs faster through a channel, the film responds; when frost accumulates, the flecks respond; when an alcove warms, the leggy ones respond; when the light shaft dims, the vents shift and the mist drains. None of this needs a voice. It does not need to shout across the chamber. It is a system talking to itself in the language of correction.

You can say it has a mind, but only if you accept that minds need not be centered.
