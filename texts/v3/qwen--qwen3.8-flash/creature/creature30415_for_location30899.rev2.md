## The Dwellers of the Drowned Terrace

No one single creature lives there. If you ask what inhabits the Drowned Terrace, the answer begins as a refusal: not ghosts, not a lone watcher beneath the water, not even a species. The Terrace is populated the way a reef, a forest floor, a mountain stream, and a ruined city are populated at once—by a distributed parliament of small lives, slow lives, hungry lives, grazing lives, swimming lives, clinging lives, filtering lives, singing lives, and by the water itself, which is both their medium and one of their forms.

The old builders may be gone, but their absence has not left an empty space. Their stones, beams, foundations, hearths, lanes, and courtyards have become niches. Every angle now has use. Every low place gathers life. Every gap admits a root, a fish, a larva, a current, a shadow. The town no longer shelters people, but it shelters everything else with great thoroughness.

The smallest citizens arrive first, and they are so numerous that they are easily mistaken for color.

On every submerged surface—on the rough flank of a wall, the underside of a lintel, the pitted cheek of a stone adze-marked block, the dark face of old timber—there lives a film of microorganisms. Bacteria and archaea work at the scale of dust. Diatoms catch light with their glassy bodies and settle into golden veils. Cyanobacteria spread in slicks and threads, breathing slowly through the cold water. Rotifers turn like tiny gears in their microscopic gardens. Amoebae and ciliates creep across the mineral skin of the terrace, hunting particles, tasting chemistry, converting decay into biomass. These beings do not live in houses; they live on houses. They make the first thin skin over every object, the gloss that turns a dead stone into a living place. They are the town’s painters, its varnishers, its first citizens, its constant digestion.

Above them, but still among the smallest residents, move the crustacean fleas and seed-shrimp: tiny ostracods in pear-shaped shells, daphnia rolling past in their translucent helmets, copepods jerking through the light as if struck by invisible hands. They graze the film, filter the water, fall to fish, and rise again. Their generations pass over a season as quickly as a day passes in a human life. They are not the town’s permanent citizens in any deep sense, but they are always present in its current, as thoughts are always present in a dreaming mind.

Then there are the algae.

The filamentous algae are the terrace’s hair, its moss-green lace, its slow fire. They grow in strands from stone and timber, trembling in the current, forming veils that can be walked through by minnows or broken by a fish’s tail. Some are fine and silken, spreading over walls in gray-green shawls. Some are bushier, growing in soft tangles around the corners of doorframes. Some settle on the floor as low mats, turning the gravel into fields. They are the terrace’s light-eaters: they take high, cold sunlight and turn it into living thread. In their cells the water is oxygenated; in their filaments larvae hide; on their surfaces snails graze and tiny animals crawl as if across a wet green country.

The algae do not behave like animals, but they have their own politics. They compete for surfaces. They colonize the brightest lintels, the most exposed roof stones, the clearest channels of water. They yield to heavier grazers where snails and nymphs keep them short. They flourish in the slower corners, in courtyards where the current is patient. When wind drives the whole terrace into motion, the algae sway like a crowd responding to a common voice. They are the most obvious green citizens of the submerged town, and they make every artifact part of the living world.

The mosses are slower, and so they feel more permanent.

They grow at the edges where water thins into mist, where stone lips meet air, where roof timbers have softened into spongy bones. They also form on the upper surfaces of the submerged town in the cooler, brighter shallows, where currents keep them clean but not harsh. Mosses on hearthstones, mosses on threshold blocks, mosses along fallen beams. They do not merely cover the human-built town; they finish it. A wall without moss is still only a wall; a wall with moss becomes a slope, a cushion, a nursery. Tiny water bears—tardigrades—walk through their cushions like travelers through a forest of glassy trunks. Springtails and mites, where they can find them, climb the moss as if it were a tree. The moss holds moisture, softens edges, traps fine particles, shelters eggs. It is the terrace’s velvet skin, its gentle engineer, its keeper of small damp spaces.

The rooted plants are the terrace’s true architects.

Pondweed rises from the floor in long, swaying ribbons, each strand a corridor and a curtain. In courtyards it forms meadows where the current slows and light bends through the water. In lanes it grows in patches, marking former walls with living lines. Its leaves are soft and translucent, edged by nibbled holes from grazing invertebrates, its stems holding little bubbles of gas that rise when broken.

Rushes and reeds are the pillars and beams of the new city. They grow where walls have failed, where doorways are open to sediment, where old foundations have cracked enough to admit root. Their rhizomes move through gaps like patient explorers. They do not destroy the human construction so much as continue it. A reed pushing through a stone joint is a buttress, a fence, a doorway, a channel. Its stems stiffen the floor with shadows. Its leaves cut the water into green verticals. When wind presses the terrace, the reeds bow in one direction, and the whole street becomes a moving hedge.

Broad-leaved plants lie near the margins where light gathers in sheets. Their leaves float or rise, forming islands of shadow. Beneath them the water cools further, and tiny animals cluster. The leaf edges become coastlines for water beetles, nurseries for larvae, roofs for those who cannot bear the open surface. Where a house once stood, a leaf now hovers, making the air-water boundary into a floor for insects and a ceiling for fish.

The sedge blades fan from split walls. Each blade waves separately, so that a broken house appears to breathe. Their roots knit the rubble together. They do not mourn the builders. They use the builders.

The invertebrates are the town’s workers, tailors, masons, and scavengers.

Freshwater snails graze the moss-covered walls, leaving slow silver tracks behind them, like handwriting made of clean stone. They are archivists and gardeners, trimming algae, polishing surfaces, opening paths through the green film. Their shells scatter in the courtyards like coins no longer valuable, except that every shell becomes a future home or a small shelter for an insect, a fish fry, or a nest of eggs.

Caddis larvae are the most human-looking of the nonhuman citizens, if human-looking can mean industrious, small, and self-contained. They wear cases built from the town itself: grains of quartz, fragments of shell, tiny chips of peat, grains of gravel, bits of leaf, and sometimes a sliver of old tile from the drowned settlement. They drag these cases along stones, filter the current, hide from predators, and build the next generation of themselves from the rubble around them. Their cases move across the street floor like little towns in miniature. They are the terrace’s tailors, sewing shelter from the remains of the terrace.

Mayfly nymphs live in the gravel lanes. They burrow, cling, feed, and wait for wings. Their bodies are flat or spiny, adapted to hold fast when meltwater quickens. They are the town’s quiet engineers of the bottom, turning coarse substrate into channels and air. When they emerge, the streets briefly rise in shimmering columns, and the old town exhales wings into the mountain air.

Dragonfly nymphs crouch in the shadows of reeds and beneath fallen beams. They are predators, ambush citizens, motionless as stones until something careless passes. Their lower faces unfold into spears. They are not gentle residents, but neither are they monsters; they are part of the terrace’s balancing appetite. After their long underwater childhood, they climb stalks or stones, split open, and leave the town behind in a glassy shell.

Water beetles patrol the underside of the surface film, carrying their own tiny air. They scuttle along roots and the ceilings of former windows as though searching for rooms in a building they never entered. Their legs move with mechanical purpose, their bodies catching flashes of light. They are commuters of the interface, living half below and half above, belonging to both the water and the air.

Leeches stretch from stalk to stalk, patient and strange. They are not the town’s villains but its quiet observers, testing fish, snails, and larvae with chemical senses we barely share. They remind the Terrace that appetite is one of the laws by which water becomes a world.

Worms, planarians, nematodes, oligochaetes, freshwater mussels where conditions permit, sponges in dark porous wood, bryozoans forming lace on stems, hydroids waving their tiny stingers—all live in the gaps. The town is never a single habitat; it is a thousand micro-habitats arranged by old human lines and renewed by current.

The fish are the Terrace’s most visible swimmers, but they are not its only vertebrate citizens.

Small pale fish dart through doorways and along gutters. They have learned to use the old architecture as a natural feature, as if stone were always meant to stand at intervals, as if human walls were merely a peculiar species of rock. They enter rooms without hesitation. They turn under fallen lintels. They rise and drop through courtyards where once people might have stood, and to them these open spaces are feeding grounds, meeting places, hiding places, hunting lanes, and breeding floors.

Minnows flash in clouds where water meets reeds. They move as one organism, changing direction in milliseconds, their bodies like scattered coins falling through light. They avoid shadows that mean predators and gather where current stirs food toward their mouths.

Sculpins rest along the bottom of stone-lined channels, their mottled bodies nearly indistinguishable from the silt and pebbles. They are the town’s old guards, sitting at corners, beneath beams, near pipe mouths, rising with sudden precision to take a larva or retreat under a stone with the same abruptness.

Darker fish hover above the silt, their fins working quickly against the slow current. They are hunters and waiters, reading pressure, taste, and movement with an attention more refined than any instrument.

In the deeper, cooler center of the terrace, larger fish patrol with patient, muscular slowness. Trout, char, or something shaped by the cold—names matter less here than behavior. They use the drowned streets as a country. A roof beam is a hunting blind. A hollow wall is shelter. A gravel lane is spawning ground. A courtyard is a pool. They spawn in the gravel once laid by human feet, and their eggs breathe through the spaces between stones.

Fish make the old town legible again. If you watch them, you can trace the forgotten streets by their movement. Where they dart in lines, the lane is clear. Where they scatter, current bends. Where they settle, food collects. Where they avoid, danger has been learned.

At the edges of the water live the singers.

Frogs come from the shallows in spring, their loud wet chorus filling the basin with a wet, insistent music. They do not belong entirely to either side. They are boundary citizens, creatures who remember the transition between water and land. Their tadpoles graze among pondweed and the lower courses of walls, darkening shallow courtyards with living points. Their eggs form strings and clumps on reed stems. They are the town’s seasonal musicians, the ones who make the water feel alive to listeners standing on the rim.

Newts move slowly among roots and submerged stone faces, their skin velvet, their tails undulating with a grace that seems almost deliberate. They hide beneath roof timbers, climb through moss, and lay their eggs folded into plant leaves. They are amphibious librarians of the Terrace, carrying the memory of both water and land in their bodies.

Birds come in daily conversation with the drowned settlement.

Dippers and warblers dart between stone and plant along the cliffs, calling, hunting, watching, and plunging into the margins for larvae. They are ambassadors between air and water. A dipper’s dive is a short argument with the liquid world. It disappears into the Terrace, returns to the rim, and carries out a meal. Warblers flit through reeds and tussock, their thin voices softening as their reflections touch the surface. Their shadow crosses the water as a brief darkening, and below, fish and beetles react without knowing why.

Insects are the Terrace’s aerial citizens, born below but leaving to the air.

Midges spin in columns above the water. Mosquitoes, if they are there, are more than pest; they are the hum of the surface, the tiny engines of food chains. Caddisflies and mayflies emerge in evenings when the light turns thin and green. Their bodies rise from drowned rooms and streets into the world of wind. The Terrace exhales them as a forest exhales spores.

The most difficult inhabitant to name is the Terrace itself.

If one considers the water, the silt, the roots, the fish, the algae, the current, the sound, the light, and the old bones of the town, the place begins to behave like a single being with many members. It has metabolism: it digests leaves, absorbs minerals, breathes oxygen, releases carbon, filters meltwater, stores nutrients in plant tissue and sediment. It has sensation: fish taste pressure and movement; snails leave chemical trails; larvae detect shadows; plants grow toward light and away from force; roots sense gaps; water senses wind. It has memory, but not a mind’s memory. Its memory is in the arrangement of stones, the patterns of root scars, the sorted silts, the old channels, the positions of colonies, the inherited shapes of its streets. It has reproduction, but not by one species only. It reproduces through spores, eggs, larvae, rhizomes, fish fry, tadpoles, insects rising, seedlings, and seasonal currents that carry organisms from one corner to another.

This is the strange civic being of the Drowned Terrace: a hybrid organism, half human intention and half patient biology. It is not haunted by the town that was. It has become a new kind of town. Its citizens are moss and fish, root and larva, algae and current, shell and shadow. Its streets are highways for minnows, its courtyards feeding grounds, its walls root-masses, its roofs moss gardens, its hearthstones colonies of soft life. Its public squares are places where current and light gather. Its sewers are channels for water and chemical speech. Its foundations are shelters for crayfish and eggs. Its broken timbers are nurseries for insects. Its collapsed roofs are bridges.

There are times of day when this seems obvious.

At morning, when the sun enters at a low angle and lays bright bars through doorways, the terrace wakes into motion. Algae brighten in the light. Small fish slip out of shadow into illumination. Snails begin their slow grazing. Caddis cases shift along the floor. A dragonfly nymph holds still beneath a beam, waiting for the day to offer something.

At noon, when light reaches deep, the open water becomes a moving ceiling. Beetles cross the underside of it. Dippers cast shadows. The whole green town is lit as a stage, every citizen visible in their chosen niche. The current bends around walls and carries the taste of upstream melt through the rooms. The plants sway in slow argument.

At evening, the terrace cools. Fish hover in the dim streets. The frogs’ chorus thickens. Insects rise from the surface in brief bright trails. The water’s noise softens, but does not cease. The town turns gently. The citizens do not sleep in unison; they sleep in shifts. The microscopic film works on. A crayfish emerges from a gap. A tadpole clings to a leaf. A minnow drifts close to a stone and becomes shadow.

In winter, ice forms along the margins, making the terrace a closed room under a pale skin. Beneath it, life continues without drama. Fish slow but do not cease. Larvae shelter in gravel. Eggs wait in moss and stone crevices. Snails cling through the chill. The current still moves through the old lanes, though its voice is muffled. The ice is a ceiling that holds the weather, while the water below keeps the town alive. The inhabitants are not absent; they are waiting in different forms.

In spring, the melt arrives. The Terrace listens to the mountain. The water rises and falls, carrying taste and temperature and sediment. Reeds deepen in color. Algae bloom in pale threads. Frogs begin to sing. Fish shift into the lanes and courtyards. The old streets fill with new bodies. The drowned settlement becomes a season’s nursery.

The Terrace is not a place without predators, without death, without waste, without hunger. It is not peaceful in the sentimental sense. It is balanced. Dragonflies eat larvae. Fish eat nymphs. Snails graze algae to survival limits. Frogs eat midges. Current removes what is too loose. Stone resists what is too soft. Root reinforces what can be reinforced. The weak timbers collapse, the stronger walls become anchors, the porous surfaces are colonized, the open spaces are filled by plants. The water judges everything.

And so the inhabitants are also shaped by what is not human.

A fish’s life in the Drowned Terrace is not a metaphor for a human life. It is not a ghost in a house. It is an actual swim in actual cold, through actual light, into actual gravel. A snail is not a symbol; it is a body grazing a wall of algae, leaving a path of clean stone. A moss is not a sentiment; it is a slow plant making a stone into a home. The inhabitants live because the human forms persist, but their lives are entirely their own.

If the town were asked who lives there, it would not answer with names. It would answer with motion: the sway of pondweed in a current, the dart of fish through a doorway, the slow line of a snail across a mossy lintel, the case-drag of a caddis along a submerged street, the sudden flash of minnows in a courtyard, the ripple caused by a diving dipper, the wet breath of frogs in spring, the light falling through an empty room.

The Drowned Terrace is inhabited by a city of organisms who never made the city, yet who use its remains with perfect civic obedience. Its residents are invisible films and visible schools. They are rooted plants and swimming eggs. They are shells, stalks, fins, wings, claws, gills, tendrils, shadows. They are the small daily citizens and the slow long-term citizens. They are the water that carries them, the silt they leave behind, the light that feeds them, the cold that orders their lives.

The true resident of the Drowned Terrace, if one must name it, is the Terrace itself: a living settlement of roots, film, shell, fin, spore, current, stone, and shadow. It does not dream the town of the builders. It has converted that town into an ecology. It is not empty because people are gone. It is full.
