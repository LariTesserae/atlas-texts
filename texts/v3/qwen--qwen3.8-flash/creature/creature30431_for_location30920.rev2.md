If you ask who lives in that hollow, the first answer is too small. It is not simply the people, and it is not simply the animals, and it is not simply the mosses and fungi and frogs and reeds. The inhabitants of the bowl are a crowded coalition of beings, each occupying a different layer of the same wet, green, breathing place. The town is human in its organization, but the ground is not. The air is not. The stone is not. Everything is shared with somebody else, and everyone lives by knowing that.

The people are small, quick, and accustomed to damp. They are not the kind of people who stride across open land or stand apart in bright emptiness. Their bodies are made for climbing roots, balancing on narrow reed mats, hauling water in clay jars through fog, and reaching across balconies to hand a stone adze to a neighbor before looking down at what lies below. They have tough palms and callused soles, fingers stained with charcoal or clay or the green juice of pounded bark, hair kept back with woven cords or twisted fiber caps, skin often shining faintly with rendered fat or smoke-treated oil so the mist will bead and roll rather than soak straight in. They smell of wet stone, wood smoke, crushed herbs, salt from their own bodies, and the faint green sweetness of moss that grows between their floorboards.

They are pale where the sun rarely reaches and darker where the light strikes the rim, and their faces carry the particular alertness of people who live in a place that can change its mind in a single storm. They listen more than they look. They can tell from the way water moves through a hidden crack whether a cistern is near full or nearly empty. They can hear the difference between a safe dripping and an ominous rush. They know which mossy shelf will hold a foot and which will slide. They can say, without seeing the sky, whether cloud has touched the high trees, because the whole bowl answers with a different kind of sound.

These people live in platforms, lofts, root-braced huts, stone shelves, reed walkways, and dwellings trained into growing branches. A household is not a free-standing object but a layer within a layer. One family may occupy a split-wood platform tied between two curved trunks, while the family above has a smaller loft roofed with overlapping bark sheets, and the family below uses the same root system as a wall. Drying racks, sleeping mats, cooking baskets, storage holes, and lashing points all belong to the vertical architecture of the town. People move through it constantly: stepping from one narrow walkway to another, passing over a wet cleft, ducking beneath a hanging rope, climbing a notched log where generations have worn smooth handholds. They have learned to live without disturbing the structural patience of the place.

Their tools are made from what is at hand. Stone edges, bone points, wooden wedges, charcoal for marking, sharp flakes scraped thin, reed cords spun and rolled on thighs, split fibers soaked and beaten until pliant, clay pressed and fired in small pits or beneath slow smokes. There are no bright metal blades, no nails, no rigid frames that insist the world should hold still. The people join things with tension and friction, tying wood to wood, binding root to root, using knots that can loosen under strain and be retied when needed. They understand that a structure in the wet bowl is not finished so much as maintained. A lashing must be checked. A channel must be scraped. A roof of broad leaves must be renewed before the next cloud comes.

They are a town of small specialists. Some keep the water. They read the seepage from the eastern stone, watch the channels, clear leaves from the shallow grooves, guide overflow away from sleeping platforms, and know which pools will tremble with tadpoles and which will hold fish or fishlike things that dart when the light changes. Others keep the dry places. They know where smoke can rise without smothering the surrounding leaves, where fire pots can be sheltered from mist, how to store fiber in sealed bundles, how to lay out fish or tubers on racks so the wind can take the wet before it turns to rot. Still others keep the green structure: trained branches, trimmed rosettes, cultivated moss shelves, shaded fungus beds, carefully left root mats that hold the slope. They are not farmers in the open-field sense. They are tenders of a vertical, damp garden-city, constantly pruning, guiding, lifting, moving, and replacing pieces of their own habitat.

Children are raised in the sound of the place. They learn first to be quiet enough to hear what the bowl is saying. They play by dangling water from a vine, filling hollow stones, catching damselflies in cups of leaf, watching frogs swell and sink in the channels. They pass objects hand to hand between balconies, shout names across root shelves, crawl under platforms where small mammals have left trails, and return with wet feet and serious eyes. Their games are rehearsals for work: knot-tying, stone-balancing, water-finding, branch-listening, frog-counting, channel-clearing. By the time they are old enough to help, they already know the social geography of the town better than any map could show: where the water is deepest, which families hang their drying racks in the morning light, which platform will sway if too many people climb at once, which path is unsafe after a storm, which moss-covered stone is used as a meeting point without needing to be announced.

The people do not seem to think of themselves as separate from the rest of the life there. They are one of the noisy, busy, water-using classes of organisms. Frogs have more voices. Insects fill the lower air. Birds occupy the heights. Fungi occupy what is falling apart. Moss occupies nearly everything else. The humans occupy the built niches, but even those are alive, colonized, and shared. A sleeping platform becomes home to tiny insects. A wooden peg draws lichen over time. A reed mat collects dust, spores, beetle husks, bird seed, and the dried footprints of unseen animals. No thing belongs entirely to one being. Ownership is provisional. Use is negotiated.

Their language must sound like the place: close, rhythmic, full of clicks against wood and short calls across wet air. There are words for the way fog clings to vines, for the difference between slow dripping and sudden sheet-flow, for the particular softness of a root shelf after rain, for the sound of a frog whose throat is full of water, for the feel of bark cloth dried near smoke. Their greetings may be practical: *Your hands are dry.* *The channel is moving.* *The rim fog has touched the trees.* Their jokes probably have to do with leaks, lashing failures, and the people who claim the wrong drying place. Their stories, if they have them, are not likely to be about gods above so much as about patience below: how a family found the stone that weeps, how a platform saved itself by leaning, how the town learned not to cut a particular tree, how the first cistern was made after a season of thirst, how someone once failed to listen to the frogs and walked into a wet slope that slid.

The animals are as real as the people and in many cases more numerous. The frogs are not decoration. They are a chorus that defines the hollow. At dusk they become the town’s second population, filling the air with wet pulses, rising and falling like breathing. Some live in the stone channels, throats working with each drop. Some hide under leaves, invisible until disturbed. Some breed in the same little pools that children net for food, and some are left alone because the people know their calls warn of rain or of a full water table. The people live inside the frogs’ world as much as the frogs live inside the town. A terrace may be avoided because it has become a nursery pool. A path may be widened slightly because a species of frog always sits along it in the evening. A child may learn not to splash near a certain stone because something is sleeping there.

Insects are constant. Damselflies flash over shallow pools like bright stitches in air. Mosquitoes and midges make dusk a haze of tiny motion. Some people probably carry small reed fans or smoke to drive them off while working near water. Some may keep their hair wrapped so the midges do not nest in it. Snails, slugs, beetles, worms, springtails, and countless unglamorous crawlers inhabit the wet litter that forms the ground. They are part of the town’s economy of decay: breaking down leaves, feeding birds, fouling water if not managed, turning dead matter into the spongy mat that supports new growth. The people know them. They may eat some, ignore others, avoid others because of itch or irritation, and learn their habits the way anyone in a wet forest eventually does.

Birds live at every height. Small darting birds pick through the reed shelves and moss pockets, chasing insects, carrying seeds, dropping shells on walkways. Larger soft-voiced birds move among the upper branches, their weight creaking the trained architecture. Some nest near human platforms; some are kept away. The people know which birds wake before the wind, which roost on dry ledges, which will suddenly explode from cover into noisy flocks when a storm gathers. Birds are also resources: eggs from safe reaches, small game when it is needed, feathers for wrapping and marking. But their presence is more often useful than simply food. They are watchers and indicators. If a certain bird stops calling near the eastern rim, it may mean the air has changed. If a flock works the reeds hard, something has disturbed the insects.

Bats live behind bark. They are easy to miss, but their presence is known in the fine dust around hidden hollows, in the soft rustle of dark crevices, in the way some roof beams are left undisturbed because something roosts there in the warmest part of the day. They are welcome, even if rarely seen. They keep the midges down. They belong to the night layers of the bowl, when the town becomes louder with small life. Children know where not to poke with sticks. Adults leave certain dry spaces alone. The settlement is practical enough to understand that bats are worth more alive in the walls than chased away for curiosity.

Small mammals and lizards occupy the spaces humans cannot use: under root mats, between stone and decay, in the dark wet corners where platforms do not reach. Some are pests. Some are companions by proximity rather than training. A lizard may sun briefly on a stone shelf after the mist lifts; a small mammal may run along a hidden root line and pause with its nose working in the air. The people probably do not name them all. They know them by their sounds, their droppings, their paths through litter, and the way they make a night seem less empty.

If there are domesticated animals, they are small, quiet, and adapted to damp. Perhaps birds kept in baskets for eggs or alarms. Perhaps fish in stone-lined pools where they grow slowly in cold water. Perhaps snails or grubs in shaded boxes, raised with leaves and damp litter. Perhaps a few low, sure-footed animals used for carrying short loads along walkways, but not herds grazing open fields. The place does not make room for abundance on a broad scale; it makes room for abundance in folds, pockets, trays, holes, and channels. Even domestic life has to be vertical and hidden, because every square inch is already occupied.

Underfoot, the ground itself is a community. It is not dirt but a built thing: leaf litter pressed into root mat, moss growing over fallen wood, fungi threading decay, sediment catching in reed traps, minerals staining stone where water has wept for years. A footstep there disturbs no empty earth but joins a slow war and peace of decomposition and growth. A person standing on a root shelf is resting on generations of fallen leaves, old platforms, buried seeds, and the bodies of small animals returning to the wet. The town’s foundations are ecological as much as human. A wall may be a trained shrub. A stair may be a stabilized root. A cistern may depend on clay laid into a natural hollow. A path may be kept open only because it is used, and when it is neglected, green reclaims it within a season.

The people’s food is ordinary and seasonal. They soak tubers, dry fish, pound seeds, roast small game if found, eat leaf shoots, collect fungi, crack eggs from hidden nests, catch frogs and tadpoles from certain pools, and gather fruits or nuts that ripen in the upper branches where sun can reach. Because the bowl is not a fertile plain but a wet, dense, high hollow, nothing is casually abundant in a single kind. There is no feast of one crop. There is a constant assembly of small yields: this handful of leaves, this fat snail, this basket of damp seed, this fish flash from a pool, this edible mushroom cap lifted from rotting bark. Meals are likely simple, warm, salty with stone or smoke, and eaten quickly between work. Cooking happens where smoke can rise. Eating happens where hands can dry. Nothing is taken for granted. Even a full belly is a success of maintenance.

Their water life is central. Water does not come from a single river, so it has to be coaxed, caught, and respected. Fog is combed from vines and trees. Stone is led to drip into basins. Channels are rounded and cleaned. Cisterns are shaded to keep water cool and clean. Pools are watched for animals as well as usefulness. Some water is for drinking, some for washing, some for soaking fiber, some for cooling clay, some for fish, some for ritual, though if there is ritual it probably has a practical edge: pour the first catch from a net into the moss, tie a cord around the channel where overflow nearly took a path, leave a hollow for birds to drink. These are not magical acts so much as careful social gestures toward a place that must be kept alive.

The people also live with the constant presence of mild danger. A platform can slip if its braces loosen. A path can wash away if a root dies. A heavy rain can turn a trickle into a blade. A tree that seems solid may be hollow behind the moss. A lashing may hold for years and then fail in one night. They are not afraid of the place because they have never lived without it, but they remain attentive. Their fear is the fear of misreading. Their security comes from knowing the bowl’s habits better than their own ambitions. They do not impose grand plans. They adjust. They repair. They move a sleeping mat from a terrace that has become too wet. They add a log to a slope. They open a spillway before the storm reaches its peak. They trim a branch because it has begun to lean across a water channel. Their safety is a practice, not a monument.

At midday in light rain, the town is full of motion. People climb and descend in wet clothes. Fish scales flash in shallow pools. Smoke curls from fire pots protected from drips. Children pass objects hand to hand between balconies. Adults shout to one another in short, practical voices while lashing a new board into place. Damselflies dart over cisterns. Frogs pulse from every green shade. A bird lifts from the upper branches, shaking a rain curtain loose from its feathers. Water moves through channels, over mossy stones, into clay jars, across walkways, down hidden gutters. The entire town looks like a wet machine made of living materials, and it hums.

At night, the human presence softens. Fires dim. Voices lower. Platforms creak with settling. Fog closes around the bowls. The frogs take over the air. Bats pass through dark gaps in the bark. Small mammals cross wet stone without fear because they know the town by its hidden routes. The pools become mirrors for cloud, trembling when something disturbs them. People sleep under layered bark-cloth and reed mats, with their tools near their hands and their cisterns watched in memory. The town does not stop at night; it changes owners. Humans share it with the nocturnal chorus, and they know enough to listen but not always to wake.

In storm, the place becomes loud enough to test everyone. Rain strikes broad leaves like many small hands beating at once. Wind moans through the rim and presses fog down into the trees. Channels fill, spill, rerun, and sometimes overflow. People move quickly in oil-skinned fiber, checking lashings, clearing blockages, moving goods up from low platforms, closing lids over stored dry fiber, pulling ropes to shift drying racks, listening to the stone for signs of shifting roots or sliding slope. They do not panic unless they are foolish or too young. The storm is part of the town’s calendar, a period of repair and negotiation. By morning, the settlement may have changed shape: a walkway lifted, a channel widened, a balcony braced, a tree branch trained back into place, a pool cleared of fallen bark, a new stain of moss beginning to grow over an old wound.

These are the inhabitants of the bowl: human, frog, fish, fly, fungus, root, mist, stone, child, elder, lashing-knot, channel-cutter, smoke-tender, root-guard, pool-watcher, bird, bat, leaf-mat, moss-wall, and all the unseen lives that press through damp air and wet ground. They are not a single species with a name. They are a dense community of forms, each dependent on the others, each occupying a niche in a place that leaves no empty corners. The people are the most organized, the most vocal, the most tool-making, and the most aware of themselves as a town. But they do not rule there. They live in it. They are one layer of the bowl’s crowded life, shaped by cloud, water, stone, and the endless soft pressure of green growth.
