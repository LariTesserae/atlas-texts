What lives in the Kettle Fen is not a society in the usual sense, and not even a wilderness in the usual sense. It is a wet, low, crowded commons of creatures, plants, people, machines, and microbial films, all arguing with the same storm and calling that argument life.

If you begin by looking, you will not know where one inhabitant ends and another begins. A hummock of moss may contain the burrow of a vole, the root network of engineered sedge, the eggs of midges, a patch of thermally active bacteria, and a self-healing film of synthetic peat-stabilizer all pressed into a volume the size of a stool. A stone ledge may wear a skin of iron-oxidizing slime, a crust of liverwort, a nest cavity of a shorebird, and a faint metallic coating that was laid there by a drone or a human hand or both. A reed stem may be hollowed by larvae, reinforced by mineral fibers, tapped by a maintenance probe, and used by a wren as a resonant instrument. The Fen is dense because it is young, cold, dark, and wet, and everything that survives in it has to be useful in more than one way.

## The living skin

The first inhabitants are so small that they seem more like chemistry than creatures.

Across basalt shelves, in gravel beds, and along the thermal seeps, there are microbial mats that feed on mineral water, peat acid, dissolved iron, hydrogen, methane, and faint chemical plumes rising from the young volcanic plumbing below. In some places these colonies form bright rust threads, orange-brown films, or dark gray crusts that smell sharply of metal. On the shaded faces of basalt, they grow in slow concentric bands, expanding as moisture moves and shrinking when frost bites. They are the foundation of much of the Fen’s biological wealth: they turn mineral water into organic matter, and organic matter into food for larger mouths.
