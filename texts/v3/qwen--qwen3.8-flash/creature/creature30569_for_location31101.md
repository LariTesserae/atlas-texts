No one owns the Chittering Terraces. The place is inhabited by a commonwealth of bodies so numerous, so interlocked, that the distinction between “creature” and “ground” has become unreliable. If one asks who lives there, the answer begins with the smallest film of life and ends, eventually, with the terrace itself: a slow, breathing, chittering assemblage made of organisms, excrement, rot, silk, spores, water, wing-beat, and mineral crust.

It is not a forest with animals, nor a cave full of bats, nor a nest of insects. It is a living architecture of deposition. Every living thing there is both resident and builder. Every death is a contribution. Every drooling of saliva, every falling of guano, every shed larval skin, every molted wing, every spore, every feather, every broken egg membrane becomes part of the floor someone else stands on.

The inhabitants can be understood as layers.

## The Films: The First Residents

Before moss, before mite, before bird or bat, there is the film.

The walls of the terraces are sheathed in biological varnishes: pale bacterial mats, cyanobacterial glazes, chalky calcium films, green-black algal slicks, fungal skins, and microscopic threads no wider than a hair. These are the first inhabitants of any fresh stone surface, and in the Chittering Terraces there is almost no fresh stone surface left. Even when a ledge cracks and a new face appears, the films come immediately, drawn by moisture and mineral.

They are not glamorous life. They smell of wet chalk, old milk, rust, warm dust, and faintly of eggs. They are the life that makes the place feel sealed rather than bare. A person could scrape their nail across a wall and bring up a smear of living pigment: microscopic workers that have been eating, exhaling, and calcifying for generations.

Some of these films are mineralizing. They pull calcium and silicate from condensation and slowly lay down a thin crust over whatever they cover. This crust is the mortar of the terraces. It turns guano into something nearly stone. It turns feather dust into a wall. It turns rotting moss into a substrate that can hold weight. Other films are nitrogenous: they feed on guano, dead larvae, and exhalations from thousands of small bodies, and in return they make the surface fertile for mosses and fungi.

Beneath the visible green-black skin, there are threadlike fungi, bacterial colonies, dormant spores, and nematode-like bodies so small they live almost entirely inside the wet films on walls. These invisible inhabitants are what make the air taste heavy. They are what give the dark its organic density. If you put your face close to a recessed wall, you are not merely looking at the surface of the terraces. You are looking into a city of microfauna.

## Springtails, Mites, and Chalk-Mites: The Dust Dwellers

The next order of life is the population of the dust itself.

The Chittering Terraces are full of mites. They are not only parasites; they are grazers, recyclers, engineers, and prey. Many are tiny arachnids with rounded, glossy, or powdery bodies, moving through guano crusts, shed larval cases, feather dust, and fungal fuzz as though those materials were a landscape rather than refuse. Some have translucent legs and pearl-white bodies; others are stained dark by the mineral films they consume. Some carry spores on their backs like pollen. Some eat algae from the wet walls. Others chew at the edges of dried egg membranes or harvest the dead skins of insects.

The most characteristic of these is the chalk-mite. It lives in the calcified layers that coat nesting ledges and ceiling crusts. Its mouthparts are adapted for scraping microbial films and consuming the dead bodies of smaller organisms. Its frass becomes part of the calcium phosphate-rich dust that later accumulates into solid layers. Chalk-mites move in slow tides across surfaces. A patch of wall that looked dead an hour before may, after a rise in humidity, appear briefly to ripple with a fine movement of dust.

Springtails, or Collembola-like creatures, live in the dampest microhabitats: the underside of moss cushions, the rims of condensation bowls, the mouths of tiny pores in mineral crusts, and the moist layers where guano is still fresh enough to be tacky. They are the population that blooms after the warm-damp phase returns. When the air is slightly cooler and the films on the wall carry a full film of water, springtails rise like a pale dust and fall back again, thousands moving through the blackness between moss and stone. They eat fungi, algae, decay, and microbial skin. They are so numerous that they can be mistaken for a texture until one steps close enough to disturb them.

Nematodes and flatworm-like film creatures live in water films no thicker than a breath on glass. They are not large enough to be noticed individually, but collectively they turn the moisture on a wall into a living broth. They feed bacteria, circulate nutrients, and are themselves eaten by mites, larvae, and predatory microbugs. They are the reason condensation does not merely exist here; it is metabolized.

## Isopods, Cockroaches, and Floor Crawlers

The lower floors of the terraces, where guano, dead insects, shed cases, moss litter, and mineral crumbs have compacted into soft black-brown ground, are the province of crawling life.

Isopods live there in enormous numbers. They are pale or grey-green, some speckled with chalky frass, others darkened by tannins from moss and rotting plant fiber. They roll over in the damp, eat decay, and recycle it. They are not merely scavengers; they are part of the ground. Their fecal pellets contribute to the soil-like crust. Their molted exoskeletons become part of the wall. Their bodies, when they die, are folded into future layers of floor. Some species have flattened carapaces so that they can live beneath thin sheets of mineral crust, in gaps where larger creatures cannot reach.

Pale, soft-bodied cockroaches live in the deepest crevices and beneath nest pulp. They are not the large, glossy outdoor cockroaches of lowland life. In the terraces, they are smaller, duller, sometimes wing-reduced, with antennae that seem too long for the body. They eat almost anything organic that has begun to rot: feather dust, dried egg membrane, fungal growths, dead moss, animal waste, discarded larval cases. They are sensitive to disturbance. A vibration through the floor can send them scattering into cracks too narrow to see. Their droppings accumulate in small heaps and later become part of the built wall.

Beetles and beetle larvae dominate many of the lower layers. Darkling-like beetles feed on dead moss, dried fungus, and compacted organic crust. Their larvae are often thick-bodied, pale, or yellowish, with hardened heads and short legs, boring through rotting nest material. They eat the boundary between decay and construction. Where a ceiling of guano has become too hard, larvae soften it. Where moss has overgrown a wall, beetle larvae consume the base and create new pockets. Their tunnels become homes for mites, springtails, predatory bugs, and future eggs.

There are also small, quick, dull-colored predatory bugs: flattened bodies, sharp mouthparts, legs that seem too thin for the weight they support. They stalk through dust and fungal fuzz, catching springtails, young mites, and the larvae of flies. They are silent in a way that makes them more frightening than their size suggests. They move with a pause-and-dart habit, reading the terrain through vibration and touch rather than sight.

## Case-Borers and Lime-Workers: The Small Architects

A great deal of life here is larval life.

The walls of the terraces are full of cases, tubes, pellets, and folded shells built by creatures that spend most of their existence enclosed. Some are fly larvae. Some are beetle grubs. Some are moth caterpillars. Some are caddis-like builders that gather feather dust, guano crumbs, mineral flakes, and silk into compact tubes. These cases are not scattered randomly. They fuse into the substrate over time, becoming part of the architecture.

The most notable are the lime-layers. These are larvae that consume calcium-rich crust and excrete a soft, white, mineral-rich paste that they use to line their cases and seal the entrances. When many larvae live close together, their secretions can merge into a pale wall. If a ledge becomes too soft, too unstable, too wet, the lime-layers appear. They eat the decayed organic matter, process it, and cement it into something firmer. Their abandoned cases become bricks. Their fecal layers become mortar. Their deaths become part of the wall.

Some moth-like larvae build cases from chewed plant fiber and feather dust, then cover themselves with a crust of dried secretions. When they are disturbed, they retract and seal the case entrance. In large populations, they turn soft patches of nest material into ridged, pebble-like surfaces. Their pupae hang in rows along shaded crevices, and their adult forms are often pale, drab, and difficult to notice because they emerge directly into the texture of the wall.

There are also snail-like creatures, though they are not large. They are thin-shelled, translucent, or chalk-white gastropods living in the moistest ledges. They graze algae and microbial films with tiny radulas. Their shells accumulate calcium carbonate. Their slime strengthens surfaces. Their opercula, when shed or broken, become fragments in the wall. They move slowly through water films on the underside of overhangs, and if a person touches a wet ledge, they may feel the soft trail of a slime snail as well as the grit of mineral dust.

## Spiders: The Weavers and Wall-Makers

Spiders in the Chittering Terraces are not merely hanging from branches or corners. They are part of the structural system.

Between terraces, across gaps, from ledge to pillar, from nest ceiling to damp wall, they build curtains. Some are ordinary in appearance: small, dark, quick-bodied hunters. Others are pale, leggy, or flattened, adapted to live in crevices and behind crusts. The most characteristic are the plasterers: spiders that mix silk with salivary secretions, guano dust, mineral crumbs, feather fragments, and bits of dead insects.
