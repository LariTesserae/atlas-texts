What lives on the Anvil of Umm is not one people, not one animal, not one landscape. It is a layered being. The plateau itself is the first inhabitant; the ground is not merely beneath life but is itself a living skin. The humans who move there are not its masters, nor even its true tenants. They are guests who learned too well that ownership does not work on a place so wide and so patient. The animals, insects, birds, predators, and the breathing mat of moss and root and algal film all belong to one another more completely than any of them belongs to a fixed home.

The most honest way to describe them is to start low, at the surface of the world.

The ground of the Anvil is a living mat. It has the appearance of soil, but it is more like a slow green animal spread in a thin, vast layer over ancient stone. Where a traveler presses a boot into it, the mat dips and slowly recovers. Where a campfire has been, the blackened ring fills first with pale fuzz, then with darker moss, then with tiny rosettes of succulent leaves, and after enough seasons no one can say what grew first: the wound or the healing. This ground does not want to be broken. It wants to be crossed. People learn that early. They do not cut the plateau open the way lowlanders might cut fields. They step around it, sleep on it, press shelters into it, scrape food from it, and always with a certain care, as though they were moving across the belly of a resting beast.

The mat itself has niches and moods. In the shallow basins where moisture lingers, it thickens into spongy hills, rounded as loaves, soft enough for children to lie in without sinking. On the dry slopes it becomes a thin lichen-skin, green-gray to blue-black, rough with salt dust. In the wind-protected hollows it grows in dense felted cushions that hold water for hours after rain. In the long terraces, where the sun falls hardest, it is brittle, pale, alive but quiet, like an animal conserving breath. Under all of it runs the root-felt, a pale web that binds the living floor together and keeps the plateau from unraveling into dust.

If one must name the largest resident, then, it is the ground. The Anvil is not a place with life on it so much as a place that is life, with creatures moving across its surface like thoughts across a mind.

---

The people who live there are scattered and difficult to see.

They do not build towns. They do not raise stone houses. They do not plant straight rows or dig canals. They do not fence the plateau, because the plateau is too wide to fence, and any fence would become a strange growth within a season, half-swallowed by creeping mats. To outsiders, their presence might be barely visible: a bent ring of stalks, a windbreak of woven root-felt, a flattened circle of cushion-moss where bodies have slept, a shell set in a hollow to catch dew, a small pile of water-bulbs left by a rain-track, a fire ring blackened and then softened into moss.

Yet they are there, and they are shaped by the place.

They are lean in the way of animals that must travel without wasting effort, broad-chested from the thin air, sun-dark on the face and forearms, dust-pale on the soles of their feet. Their skin often carries a fine greenish or ochre stain from plant-licking, moss-poultices, and the dust of the plateau. They do not wear much clothing when the weather is warm; when it is not, they wrap themselves in layered reed-fiber and root-cloth, sometimes lined with shed grazers’ fur or with the dried inner skins of cushion-plants. Their footgear is soft and wide, made to spread weight rather than cut into the mat: padded wraps of hide, woven grass sandals, thick leaf-boots with roots chewed into flexible soles. They are very good at walking without breaking the ground.

Their hair is often tied back with sinew or split stem-fiber, not for vanity, but because the wind here is constant and loose hair catches on every leaf-collar and stalk. Their hands are rough at the palm but strange in their sensitivity; they can feel moisture through a thin layer of plant skin, can tell by touch whether a basin has held water recently, whether a mat is alive and elastic or dried to brittle lichen. Their eyes are trained to the horizon, to read slight changes in color across miles of open surface: the green-dark shadow of a water-hollow, the paler shine of salt, the gray movement of a distant flock, the faint line of a herd crossing terraces.

They are quiet, but not silent. That is an important difference. On the Anvil, complete silence can be as dangerous as noise. Silence may mean a predator has entered the area and the other creatures have stopped listening. So the people speak often, but in short phrases, clicks, and low hums carried along the wind when they can, dropped into a softer murmur when they cannot. Their speech sounds strange to outsiders because it has many breath-words, words made without moving the lips fully, words that ride in the exhalation of wind. They also use small tools for making sound: hollowed bone pieces, reed flutes, shell rattles, split sticks tapped together. These are not for play. A click pattern may mean *flock has risen*. A soft hum may mean *wind is changing toward the wet hollows*. A sudden silence may mean *do not move*.

They do not name themselves in stone. They do not mark territories with carved signs. Names among them are closer to weather: temporary, repeated, sometimes changed. A child might be given a name after the sound rain makes when it first strikes a dry rim, or after the way a plant-tower creaks before wind changes, or after a flock that passed overhead during birth. But if the name becomes associated with death, drought, or danger, it may fade. People do not carve names into basalt. They let names wear as the land wears.

Their social units are small and fluid. A family may travel together through a season; a cluster of families may gather at a good basin when rain is promised; then they scatter again, taking different paths before the mat gives out. Too many people in one place for too long would tear the living skin, dry out the dew-traps, break the root-felt, scare the herds. So they keep camps modest, usually no more than a dozen or twenty people unless the water has returned and the plant-towers are full. In those times, several bands may meet. Such meetings are not markets or festivals, not in the lowland sense. They are quiet occupations: exchanging fire-tinders, checking the health of windbreaks, mending tools, sharing water-bulbs, telling one another which basins have dried, which herds are moving east, which predator-bands have grown bold.

Children learn first to listen, then to walk, then to eat without waste. A child’s earliest task is not hunting or gathering but knowing sounds: the click of insects that means nothing is near, the altered hum that means a shadow has moved through the reeds, the sudden stillness that means a predator has crossed a terrace. By age four they can tell whether a distant flock is feeding, startled, or preparing to move. By age seven they know which hollows hold water even when dry, because they have memorized not the water itself but the behavior of fog and the shade of plants.

Their shelters are not meant to last. A shelter on the Anvil is a temporary collaboration with the living ground. People bend young stalks into domes or half-cylinders, weave them with root-felt, thatch them with dry reed and cushion-moss. Sometimes the mat grows over the edges within days, turning the structure into a mound. Sometimes the roof is a green roof in every sense, a living roof with tiny plants rooting into it. By the time the occupants leave, it may be impossible to tell where the frame ends and the mound begins. Some abandoned windbreaks become berms of pale moss. Some drying racks become trellises for trailing tendrils. Some hearth-rings become bright rings of algal growth, then soft cushions, then plain ground that remembers only heat.

To live here is to know that every structure must be given back. People do not hoard buildings. They do not inherit walls. They inherit paths, water-lessons, sounds, and stories. A family may return to the same basin every wet season, but the basin will not be the same, and the family will not be the same. The Anvil is too patient to be owned, but not too ancient to be known.

Their tools are simple and beautiful in their adequacy. They use flakes of sharp stone split by hand, not quarried into grand mines. They use bone awls, shell scrapers, wooden wedges, reed needles, sinew cords, and polished shells carried from somewhere far below or beside the plateau, where rivers and sea-memory exist. They do not forge metal here. There is little reason to, and the Anvil resists anything that seems meant to outlast the mat. Metal would be too loud, too cold, too permanent. Instead, they shape what can be reshaped, bound what can rot away, and make things meant to break gently into the living ground.

Fire is important but used with reverence. Fires are small, built in depressions where the mat has been dampened or where bare warm stone has been carefully exposed and then covered with a thin layer of living root-felt. A fire pit may be ringed with water-stones to keep it from spreading. When the fire goes out, it is smothered with wet moss and ash, and the site is scattered with seed-paste and dew-water to help the mat recover. People say, in a phrase too low for outsiders to translate well, *fire sleeps, ground breathes*. That is not merely poetry. It is instruction.

Their food is as temporary and varied as the land. In wet seasons they eat tender shoots, water-rosettes, soft stems, algae scraped from damp collars, seeds, bulbs, and tubers that store moisture. In dry seasons they eat more sparingly: parched seed-cakes, dried grazers’ meat, insects gathered in clouds, eggs taken from ground-nesting birds when the flocks move, and the dense flesh of cushion-plants that hold water in their bodies. They carry food in woven pods, in scraped leaf-cups, in hollow stalks sealed with resin, and in pouches lined with soft inner bark. They do not store in great granaries. Storage invites permanence, and permanence here invites ruin. Instead, they store in knowledge: this basin dries first, this plant-tower holds dew longest, this wind brings fog, this salt ring marks a former pool.

They are few. This is essential. The Anvil cannot support thick settlements. There is no way to sustain a town upon a living skin unless the town becomes as careful as the mat itself. Even the most prosperous gatherings feel temporary, like clouds on a plain. People stand at the edge of a basin in the morning, looking at one another across distances that make a village impossible to imagine. They know that the herds pass overhead, the flocks darken the sky, the insects roar in the wind, and that all of it belongs to a life that cannot be fenced. So they remain few, and their fewness is a kind of reverence.

There is no single leader. Authority is local, situational, seasonal. The best reader of wind may lead a camp toward a fog-hollow. The best listener may lead a band away from predator sounds. The most experienced mother or father may decide when a basin is no longer safe to use. Elders are valued not as rulers but as memories. They hold water-stories: where pools were, where fog gathered, where the mat grew thickest, where the predators crossed in old seasons. But water-stories are not commands. They are maps made by time. A young person may ignore one and die. Or may learn a newer map and save the group.

Death is handled with a strange simplicity. The Anvil has no carved tombs, no cairns, no family plots. The dead are placed with care upon the living ground, sometimes wrapped in woven stalk, sometimes laid in a basin-shaped hollow, sometimes set beneath a plant-tower if the wind is right. The mat grows over them slowly, softly, without drama. In a season, the place may show nothing more than a patch of unusually green moss. Names may be spoken afterward. A mother might say, *the child who was called rain-click is the shade beneath the eastern reed now.* But no one marks the shade. To mark it would be to ask the land to remember what it already remembers by taking it back.

---

The small lives are the loudest.

Insects are the true crowd of the Anvil. They rise from the moss in clouds so fine they look like dust disturbed by sun. They crawl in bright lines along the collars of leaves. They tap, rasp, and drum through reed-stems with bodies no larger than a dew-drop. Some are wingless, some are pale as salt-crystal, some are dark and hard-shelled, some have long, delicate legs that spread their weight so they can walk on the mat without sinking. They drink fog, chew root-felt, carry spores, pollinate moss-flowers, and feed everything above them. At dusk, winged forms spiral above the basins so thickly they seem to pour like smoke from the ground itself. Their sound never fully stops. Even when human camps are far away, the plateau hums with their labor.

The burrowers live beneath the mat. They are small animals, rounded, with flat skulls and powerful claws for digging through root-felt and soft cushion-moss. Their fur is the color of old dust or pale stone, sometimes streaked with green where moss has stained them. They have poor eyes, not because they are blind, but because light here is often too bright, too open, too unreliable. They navigate by smell, sound, and touch. They eat fungus, root-cords, insects, and the soft underlayers of cushion-plants. Their tunnels honeycomb the living ground, and when a person steps into one of these burrowed areas, the mat gives with a soft sigh. This is not a flaw in the ground. It is a sign that the ground is alive. The burrowers are the Anvil’s gentle hands, turning over what people must never dig too deeply.

In the shallow wet basins, mud itself seems to move. Small amphibians hide in the damp folds, their skins patterned like lichen or wet stone. They call in the early morning and again after rain, not from one throat but from a hundred, a thousand, so that the water’s edge becomes a chorus. Water-skimming insects spread across temporary pools in silver lines. Tadpoles, when pools exist, make the mud seem granular with motion. These hollows are temporary kingdoms. A pool may last only days before sinking into the mat or steaming away under the white sun, yet the creatures inside them know the urgency of wetness. They breed fast, grow fast, and store life in ways suited to a place where water is more memory than guarantee.

Above the small lives are the grazers.

They are long-legged animals built for distance. Their hooves do not cut the mat; they crackle against it. Their legs are thin but strong, adapted to cross vast terraces without exhausting themselves. Their coats are pale at dawn, dust-colored at noon, and near the shade of stone at dusk. Some have broad, flat feet like the predators, though wider and blunter. Their faces are long and sensitive, often stained with moss-licking. Their ears are large, because sound is survival on an open plateau. When a herd moves, it is not one animal but many minds listening: a change in insect noise here, a bird alarm there, a faint vibration through the mat from a distant hoofbeat. They are difficult to hunt for exactly this reason: a herd is not a meal, it is a warning system.

The birds come in sheets. Some are high-winged and gray, moving so densely overhead that they cast a net of shade across the land. They do not nest in fixed colonies. They move across cisterns, basins, terraces, and wind-edges, feeding, roosting briefly, breeding opportunistically when rain opens new pools. Others are long-legged, almost like birds with hooves, if one can imagine such a thing, walking through the mats and calling in sharp repeating notes that seem to answer each other across distances. Their eggs are laid in hollows of living cushion, sometimes abandoned after hatching, sometimes reclaimed by moss so that the nest becomes indistinguishable from the ground. From a distance, an empty nest may look like a tiny stone. From close, it may look like a bowl of dew. The birds are not permanent, but their passing shapes the plateau as much as the herds do.

The predators are the most unsettling because they are not dramatic in daylight.

They are lean, long-limbed, with flat feet that let them move without cutting the mat. Their fur is pale as dry grass, or sometimes darker, like the shade beneath a stone, depending on the band and the seasons. They hunt in pairs or loose bands, not because they are gregarious in the manner of pack animals, but because one alone cannot read the full grammar of the Anvil’s noise. They watch from ridges, wait beside water-hollows, and move when the chorus changes. Their most frightening trait is not speed or ferocity but attention. They listen for the sound that has no reason to be there: the sudden lift of a flock, the hush of insects, the pause of a grazing herd, the soft crack of a stalk under a step too light to be a small burrower. They know that on such an open plateau, noise is not merely sound. Noise is information. And information can be eaten.

Predator young are raised in sheltered basins or beneath plant-towers, where the wind carries fewer warnings and the mat gives warmth. The adults do not roar like lowland beasts. Their calls are short, dry cries, sometimes almost insect-like. At dusk, when the herds settle near water and the insects begin their loudest pulse, predator bands can be heard across the terraces: a faint coughing, a click, a low trill, a silence afterward that means something has been lost.

There are also larger predators in the far margins of the plateau, where the land begins to tilt downward toward unseen lower worlds. These are rare. They leave no tracks so much as disturbances: a ring of broken mat, a scattering of feathers, a pause in the insects that lasts too long. But they are not the heart of Anvil life. They are exceptions to the pattern, reminders that openness can contain danger beyond sight.

---

The plant-towers are neither people nor animals, yet they behave like landmarks.

Some bundles of fused stalks and arching fronds rise like broken columns from the living ground. They are not trees in the ordinary sense; they do not grow as trees grow in wet lowlands. They are dense, jointed, and strange, their stems often curved as though bent by wind and then hardened into that shape. Their roots spread outward in woven fans, lifting the mat into benches, ramps, and shallow pools. A person can stand beneath one and feel enclosed by something that is not quite shelter and not quite living creature. Its base may be damp enough to drink from. Its upper fronds may catch fog and drip it down into moss-collars. Its shade may host insects, birds, and sleeping grazers. Its shade may also conceal predators waiting for the shade to move.

When rain comes, the plant-towers sing differently. Broad leaves strike together. Hollow stems whistle. Water runs down fused stalks and pours into basins where it will last for only a day. The living ground around them may darken, swell, and breathe moisture back into the air. Afterward, the towers stand like elders who have survived many brief floods. They are not permanent in the way of stone, but they are permanent enough to serve as meeting-points, drying-racks, wind-shelters, and boundaries. People do not cut them down unless they must, and even then they cut only the dead or dying stalks, leaving the living bundles to continue their slow architecture.

---

The people and the animals exist in an uneasy alliance.

The humans are not top of the food chain. They are too few, too exposed, and too dependent on the plateau’s rhythms to claim that. They avoid predator territory. They move near flocks because the birds may warn first. They follow grazers because the grazers know where water once was and might still be. They avoid basins when the insects fall silent. They do not chase herds carelessly, because a frightened herd can scatter the mat into torn strips, and a torn mat can bleed dry into dust. They hunt, but not as conquerors hunt. They hunt as listeners hunt, using knowledge rather than force.

Some animals tolerate human camps. Small grazers may lick salt from drying racks. Burrowers may be found near hearth-rings, eating scraps and fungi. Birds may nest in abandoned windbreaks, becoming unintentional sentries. But this is not domestication. It is shared presence. The people allow certain animals close because the animals are useful, and the animals tolerate the people because the people do not, in most cases, threaten the animals’ paths. If a camp breaks the mat too much, the grazers avoid it. If humans scare birds from a basin, the birds do not return until the wind has carried them elsewhere. There is no contract written in stone, because nothing that matters here is written in stone. But there is a grammar of trust, and the trust is fragile.

Children learn this early. A child who kicks a mound of cushion-moss may be scolded not because the moss is sacred in the way lowlanders understand sanctity, but because the mound holds water, burrows, insects, seeds, and warmth. Breaking it is breaking a room without knowing who lives in it. A child who makes too much noise near a grazing herd may be hushed with a hand over the mouth and a look toward the far rim, where a predator may be listening. On the Anvil, a careless sound can become a death, and then a hunger, and then a lesson passed down in a whisper.

---

Time is felt differently by all who live here.

The sun at noon falls hard and clean, making everything bright and exposed. The people move less then. They rest in the shade of plant-towers or lie on warm basalt seams heated by the day. The insects intensify. The herds shift. The sky is deep blue, thin, bright, almost too clear. In the late afternoon, shadows lengthen from every tuft of reed-grass, and the plateau turns golden. This is the time when the Anvil feels most alive, because everything begins to move again: birds calling, grazers walking, predators shifting bands, small animals leaving burrows. At dusk, the wind falls, the chorus rises, and the warmth of the ground breathes out through the mat.

Night is not cold in the way outsiders expect. The basalt floors hold heat. The geothermal seams rise gently. The living ground is warm like an animal’s hide after sun. People sleep in shelters open to the sky or pressed into moss hollows, their feet warm against the earth. At night, the sounds change. Birds grow quiet. Frogs and insects dominate. The wind moves through hollow stalks with long, thin notes. Sometimes the ground itself makes a faint wet popping sound as the temperature drops and the living skin contracts. It is not a voice, but it is easily mistaken for one.

In the early morning, fog slides over the ridges. Dew gathers in leaf-struts. The mat drinks it greedily. The sun becomes a white coin in the high air, waiting until fog clears before giving warmth. At that moment, the plateau may seem empty. The wind may seem indifferent. The stone may seem older than life. But then insects lift from the moss, birds scatter across the light, herds move between cisterns, and the people rise with the first wetness of the day, tasting air for water. The emptiness fills with motion, and motion reveals that emptiness was only scale.

---

The people of the Anvil do not think of themselves as living upon the plateau so much as listening to it.

Their greatest art is not carving or weaving or building. Their greatest art is attention. They know which plant-towers drip before rain. They know which moss is wet at noon. They know which lichen-cracks hold water-bulbs through dry seasons. They know the difference between a flock that has risen because of hunger and one that has risen because of fear. They know that wind from the east carries dust, wind from the south carries fog, and wind from the west can mean a cloud is breaking over the rim before anyone sees it. They know when the ground has grown too thin to camp upon and when the mat has thickened enough to sleep on without breaking it.

They do not measure land in acres or ownership. They measure it in water, shade, sound, and recovery. A good place is not a place claimed, but a place that can be used and left. A bad place is not dangerous because it is empty, but because it cannot heal. The Anvil can take almost anything back, but if it is torn too often, if the mat is scraped away, if the root-felt is burned too wide, it may take a season, a generation, or longer to cover the wound again. People remember these scars not by markers but by the difficulty of crossing them: bare stone too hot for sitting, salt rings where water used to be, pale lichen crusts that do not breathe underfoot.

There is a rule among them, rarely spoken, because it does not need to be repeated.

*Do not make the ground a thing you stand upon. Make the ground the thing you stand with.*

This is not ethics in the abstract. It is survival.
