What lives there is not first a body. It is a repetition that has learned to have a body.

At the smallest scale, the room is a country of surfaces, and every surface is a citizen. The lichen is not decoration. Neither is the rust. The blue-green film that appears on the underside of a pipe is an animal in the way a reef is an animal: slow, distributed, patient, and composed of thousands of tiny negotiations between dampness and mineral. It feeds on what the room offers without asking permission: salts from old concrete, iron from bleeding pipes, sulfur from warm water, faint electrical leakage from cable sleeves, and the residue of dead skin and cloth from people who are no longer there. It grows in rings and threads. It stores the room’s climate the way a tree stores years. A human might look at one thin line of it and see only mold. If the line is watched across seasons, it may be seen retreating from heat, swelling near cool drips, brightening in darkness, and dimming when the emergency bulb’s red eye grows brighter. That response is not thought, not exactly, but it is attention.

The moss in the tile joints is another kind of resident. It does not live as a green carpet but as a narrow nervous system, threading itself through hairline cracks where tile meets iron. It is hungry in a very low, very old way. It waits for condensation. It tolerates the heat by curling into itself. When a pipe sweats, it sends pale filaments along the damp path like a hand testing water temperature. It has learned the room’s leaks the way a person learns the floorboards that creak. It knows which seams hold moisture and which seams only threaten it. In some places it has trapped tiny fragments of glass, rivets, and fibers, and the fragments are not litter but architecture. The moss uses them. The room is too crowded for anything to remain merely accidental.

The fungi live in shadows and cracks. Thin white threads run behind warped labels and under the ceramic casing of pipes. They are not mushrooms. They are not showy. They are almost invisible, but they occupy the places where heat and water meet in balance. They break down dead tape, rot the cloth wrapped around conduits, and feed the bacteria that feed the lichen. There is a whole economy here, so slow that a century might feel like a breath. If a cable rots by half a millimeter, if a bead of oil shifts from one seam to another, if a patch of rust flakes away and is replaced by a pale bloom of mold, those are events. They are weather. They are lives being spent.

Then there are the small animals.

A blind beetle crawls across a warm plate, leaving a faint line through the dust. It is the size of a thumbprint, pale at the joints, with a shell the color of wet ash. It does not know what a beetle is. It only knows gradients: warm, cooler, damp, dry, gritty, smooth. It follows the edges of tile and metal because the edges tell it where the room changes. Sometimes it stops at a place where the heat is too strong. Sometimes it enters the dark beneath a shelf and does not return. There are others like it, hidden in the layered clutter, but they are rarely seen together. Their population is kept low by the scarcity of real shelter and by the room’s own cycles of heat and condensation.

Beneath the grates, in black water that looks like oil but is mostly mineral-stained, there may be pale wormlike things with no eyes and too many tiny bristles. They live in the spaces where water is trapped but not moving. They feed on slime. They avoid light. If a tool is dragged across the floor, they tighten into themselves. If a pipe warms enough to release a pocket of air from a joint, they drift upward, not because they are curious, but because the current invites them. A small pale thing slips from under a grate and vanishes into the dark water; that is not a single creature. It may be a fragment of the room’s hidden life, or it may be the young of something larger, or it may simply be one more temporary body made possible by the room’s endless recycling of mineral and moisture.

Even the flies are strange here, if there are flies at all. Not the quick, noisy kind. Something slower. Something that lands near a cold puddle and remains still long enough to be doubted. Their wings may be iridescent from the oil films. Their eggs may be laid in the damp cloth on a shelf. Their larvae may fall into the black pools beneath the tile. But they are so rare that seeing one feels like seeing an exception to the room’s rules.

If you stay long enough, though, the smaller lives begin to assemble into a larger impression. This is the real inhabitant, or rather, the real being that the room has produced out of its own repetition. It is not a ghost. It is not exactly an animal. It is not a machine. It is what might happen if maintenance, decay, heat, water, and habit were left alone long enough to develop a body.

Call it the Attendant.

At first it has no outline. You notice it as an error in the room’s usual darkness. A patch of tile is slightly more reflective than it should be. A shelf of oil-stained jars seems to have rearranged by only a few millimeters. A line of condensation runs down a pipe in a shape too deliberate to be accident. A puddle near the central pump trembles, not because something has fallen into it, but because warmth has shifted underneath. When you turn to look directly, the thing becomes invisible, not because it hides, but because there is no single place where it ends. It is a process wearing the appearance of a body.

The Attendant is made of accumulated repair. Its skin is lichen, mineral crust, damp ceramic, and thin films of oil. Its bones are copper mesh and old wire. Its hair is fungal thread and fiber from rotted tape. Its blood is water with salt in it, moving through capillary seams by heat and gravity. It breathes by temperature difference: warm air expands through the room, draws moisture from pipes, cools against metal, releases droplets, and starts again. Its pulse is the faint tick of the pump’s residual mechanism, though not the pump itself. The pump is one organ among many. The Attendant is the room’s circulation, the room’s memory, the room’s tendency to preserve its own balance.

If one insisted on seeing it in a human shape, it would appear as a bent, damp figure made of layered surfaces. Its shoulders would be shelves. Its arms would be pipes. Its face would be the cracked gauge of the pump, glass clouded, needle resting just past zero. But that is a projection, the way clouds become faces. The Attendant does not think of itself as a person. It does, however, repeat the shapes of people. The room is so full of vanished workers that it has learned their gestures through absence. The height of a shelf, the worn place on a handle, the pattern of footprints polished into a tile floor, the location of oil cans and wire coils—these have become a template. The Attendant moves along those templates as though they were still warm with bodies.

This is why the place feels inhabited even when no person is present. A tool is not quite where you left it. A bolt has rolled to a place it should not have reached. A valve that was stiff now turns with surprising ease. A patch of dryness appears on a tile after hours, as though a hand had wiped it. These are not miracles. They are maintenance. The Attendant cleans itself by moving through the room, collecting dust, salt, and loose particles with a sticky film of moisture. It oils joints by carrying droplets of oil from one surface to another. It tightens what has loosened by depositing mineral crust in seams. It seals small leaks with a resin-like secretion produced by colonies of lichen and bacteria working together. It is a gardener of rust and a surgeon of dampness.

Its senses are thermal and chemical. It does not see with eyes. It sees with condensation. Where it is cooler, moisture beads. Where it is warmer, moisture disappears. These patterns tell it the shape of objects, the presence of air currents, the heat of a body, the weight of a tool placed down nearby. A person stepping into the room becomes a sudden event: heat rising from boots, breath fogging in the air, a vibration through the grate, a chemical trail from skin and cloth. The Attendant reads that intrusion like a ripple in water. It does not necessarily fear it. A person is hot, loud, unstable, and temporary. But a person also carries useful things: oil, metal, salt, heat, the memory of repair. Sometimes the Attendant will move toward a person only to take what the person leaves behind. A drop of sweat. A thread from a glove. A fleck of rust carried on a boot.

It is almost always slower than attention. If you watch a dark corner for minutes, nothing moves. If you blink and watch for seconds, the corner has changed. That is because its movements are so gradual that they are perceived only in intervals. A film sliding down a pipe can appear in one moment as still and in the next as slightly lower. A line of lichen can thicken by a hair’s breadth. A bead of water may travel along a joint for an hour, then vanish into a crack as though the room had swallowed it. The Attendant does not dash or flee. It migrates by accumulation. It leaves a part of itself behind and regrows elsewhere, until the “elsewhere” becomes the center of its activity.

It lives in layers, because the room is made of layers. One part of it lies along the low shelves, where tools rest and jars cloud with moisture. Another part follows the pipe runs, especially the warm ones that seem to hold heat like a body holds fever. Another part occupies the dark gap beneath the grates, where water and oil gather in black pools and the air is cooler. Still another part reaches toward the central pump, not because the pump is its brain, but because the pump was once a focal point of the room’s coordinated labor. The pump is a heart of motion, not of thought. Around it, the Attendant gathers the remnants of purpose: belts, bolts, glass beads, cracked porcelain, coils, salt crystals, and the faint iridescence of old oil. These are not treasures. They are parts of itself.

It has a sleep cycle, though sleep here means something different. When the emergency bulb brightens, the blue-green glow of the lichen dims, and the visible parts of the Attendant become more diffuse. The condensation patterns slow. The small animals retreat deeper into seams. The room appears almost empty. In that state, the Attendant is more like weather than a creature. It lies spread across the surfaces, thin as film, waiting for the bulb to dim or the air to shift. When the bulb flickers lower, the room’s other light rises. The lichen glows faintly again. Pools tremble at their edges. The air becomes perceptibly thicker, as though the space between walls has begun to breathe. Then the Attendant condenses. It gathers into patches, flows into channels, and resumes its slow tending.
