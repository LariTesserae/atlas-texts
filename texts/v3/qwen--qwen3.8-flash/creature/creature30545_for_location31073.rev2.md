Who lives there is first a refusal.

The plateau does not offer animals. There is no flock, no herd, no burrow, no nest, no web, no tooth worn by hunting, no claw pressed into soft soil, no eye blinking in the dim and sourceless glow. If one comes looking for creatures, one finds instead the illusion that the land itself is merely scenery waiting for life. But that is wrong. There is no water here, and yet there are residents. They are simply not residents the familiar world would recognize. They do not drink, they do not graze, they do not flee. They are not beasts, and if anything among them once resembled people, that resemblance has become so thin and so changed that it might better be called a memory of shape.

The first inhabitants are the smallest ones, and they are not even obviously alive. They are films of metallic moss, radial blooms of salt, dust that remembers charge, thin plates of crystalline growth that expand across basalt in the same way frost expands in a dream. They are not plants as ordinary gardens understand plants, though they may be called plant-analogues if one insists on naming things by what they are not. Each tuft of moss is a colony. Each colony is a process. They live by receiving pressure, aligning particles, storing electrical difference, and translating the endless ground-roar into growth. Their bodies are made of interlocking filaments that draw current from the stone as roots draw water from soil. They spread from one crack to another, one crystal seed to the next, not by seed as water-worlds use seeds, but by sympathetic vibration. A patch of moss can be moved from one resonance to another by a shift in the land’s long tone. A change in pitch may cause it to brighten, darken, tremble, curl, or send out a new lacework of glassy hairs.

To stand close to it is to realize that its stillness is only a slow speed. Under a lens, or by patient watching over hours, the moss is not still at all. It breathes in dust, it folds microscopic plates toward charge, it shifts in response to the movement of a hand above it. It is dry, but it behaves as if moisture were replaced by a current of meaning. When a person crosses it, the moss does not spring back like a lawn. It accepts the pressure, records it, and gradually reorganizes itself around the disturbance. The footprint left in the moss may be gone in a day or remain for a season. Sometimes it fills with crystal, so that the impression becomes a pale seam. Sometimes the crystal sprouts, and the footprint becomes the first shape of a new growth. The moss does not merely live on the stone. It learns from footfalls.

The needle-grasses are more distinct, or seem so because they stand apart from the carpet of moss and catch the thin light in sharper lines. They grow in clumps from salt seams, their blades hard, slender, and faintly reflective. They are not grass, though their resemblance to grass is one of the place’s many jokes played against the mind. Their roots are not roots. They are long, branching fibers of translucent mineral, and they do not seek water. They seek potential difference. They grow toward static, toward the faint charge trapped between crystal plates, toward the hum held inside certain basins. A grove of such grasses may appear by chance, but often it is not chance. It begins at a place where the ground has a tendency to resonate. The grasses cluster around the hum as if around a fountain, though there is no liquid in the air.

Their blades are piezoelectric. When pressure changes, when light shifts, when the distant roar alters its emotional texture, the grasses answer with tiny voltages. They cannot be heard, at least not as birdsong or wind in reeds, but they register as a prickle on the skin, a taste in the mouth, a sudden thought of orientation. They are not nervous systems, but the land uses their electrical chatter as the way a body uses nerve impulses. A field of glass-grass may become a map of local charge. A person walking through it may feel their pulse slowed, their footsteps corrected, their attention drawn away from one crack and toward another, though they cannot say why. The grasses do not care for the walker. They are doing something far older and more patient than caring: they are continuing a pattern.

The obsidian shrubs are more beautiful and less forgiving. They grow in rocky hollows and along the edges of salt flats, low and spreading, their leaves thin as cut glass, their surfaces dark and polished until they seem to have been made by a tool. They catch the dim light and return it as flashes of green, copper, and faint violet. A passing traveler may mistake such a shrub for a cluster of abandoned shards, until the shards rearrange themselves over a day. The leaves are not passive. They move slowly, folding toward pressure, adjusting angle to the haze above, sometimes aligning themselves so that the little light in the sky is carried into the ground. They photosynthesize? The word is too wet, too green. They photolithosynthesize. They turn thin, cold rays into mineral change. They draw the faint glow into their edges and use it to build further crystal layers. Their leaves are rigid, but when struck, they sing.

That is why they are inhabitants and not scenery. A blade pressed against an obsidian leaf may produce a note no larger than a pin’s shine, high, brief, and pure. The note does not die immediately. It may echo in the stone for hours, or days, or weeks, depending on the resonance of the ground beneath it. Other shrubs, other grasses, other mosses, respond to that lingering tone. The note becomes a seed. Not a seed in the sense of a kernel waiting for water, but a vibration waiting for a suitable lattice. In the right soil, the right crack, the right pressure, a struck leaf can cause nearby crystals to begin forming again. Thus the shrubs speak by being cut, and their language is also their reproduction.

The low trees are stranger still, because they approximate the form of things a human mind might call trees, and the approximation is imperfect in a way that unsettles. Their trunks are braided from hollow, glassy reeds, each reed grown into the next as if the whole trunk were a rope that had decided to fossilize before becoming living. They branch into crowns of stiff, silver-green fans. The fans turn toward whatever passes for the sun, but their turning is so slow that one may mistake them for frozen instruments. A tree’s crown may face east at dawn and face west by the following morning, and if one watches closely, one may see not motion but the certainty that motion has occurred.

The trunks are hollow, and that matters. Sound enters them, is stored, filtered, and changed. The plateau’s great roar cannot be heard by the ears as ordinary sound, because it is too low, too large, too constant. But the reed-trees make it local. They become resonant organs in the land. Their hollow stems vibrate at different depths. They amplify some frequencies and dampen others. Over years, a grove of reed-trees can create a microclimate of sound, a place where the roar arrives already shaped, broken into intervals, made almost musical. This is why many other residents gather there. Not because the trees are shelters from the land’s noise, but because the trees translate it. A grove is a hearing place. A grove is an ear of the plateau.

The larger residents are not obvious because they are not fixed in form. Between the moss, grass, shrub, and reed-tree, there are beings made of interference. They arise where cracks open, where salt fields widen into bowls, where two or more tones meet and produce a third tone. They are standing waves gathered into mineral dust. At first, one may see only a faint shimmer, a thin veil of suspended particles hanging in the dim light without a visible source. That veil is not a ghost, not a mirage, not heat shimmer, because there is no heat, and the air is too thin. It is a temporary body. It may spin slowly, fold into spirals, flatten into discs, or stretch along the invisible lines of pressure. It does not have eyes, mouth, limbs, or a stable skeleton. It lives as long as the resonance sustaining it.

These beings can be called chordlings, if a name helps, but names are clumsy things. They are more like weather made person by accident. They feed on gradients: electrical, mineral, acoustic, thermal. They gather fine salt from the air, pull crystal dust from the edges of fractures, and organize it through vibration. A crack that sings at one pitch may slowly accumulate a cloud of mineral motes. The motes form a pattern. The pattern maintains itself. The self-maintaining pattern is the resident. When the land’s tone shifts, as it does across years or centuries, the being may dissolve, not violently, but as a breath relaxes into another breath. Its dust falls. Its echo may remain. If conditions return, the pattern can reform, though it is not necessarily the same being. It is the same being in the way that a wave returning after a pause is the same wave and not the same wave.

Sometimes a chordling becomes trapped in a hollow, a cave-mouth too raw to have been worn by rain, a pit shaped by pressure rather than erosion. There it may persist longer, building a body of layered salt and glass dust around a core of trapped resonance. These are the most animal-like presences, though they have no animal ancestry. They may resemble shells, masks, curled hands, broken bells, or the silhouette of a crouching creature. They do not move from place to place, not because they are dead, but because movement for them is a matter of tuning. To shift their frequency is to grow a new opening, to shed an old layer, to become a different shape in the same hollow. A traveler might mistake such a being for a mineral statue, until the statue’s surface trembles and emits a tone not made by the wind.

There are also the fragments, and the fragments are alive because the place cannot tell the difference between made and grown. A chipped basalt flake left in the salt may be found centuries later threaded with silver-green filaments, its edge softened into a new form, no longer entirely tool and not yet entirely creature. A broken glass blade dropped onto a flat may shatter into green fragments, and the fragments may sprout roots of moss and crystal, spreading until the broken blade becomes a small garden of itself. The ecology here accepts waste as reproductive material. It does not clean the world. It composts intention. A human mistake, once made, may be taken up by the ground, refined, repeated, and given a future.

This is why the human trace is not simply abandoned.

Across the plateau, far apart in the generous emptiness, are small structures. Circles of black stones, remains of woven barriers, low domes of stacked slate, roofs half-open, interiors cool and dry. They do not read as ruins of a great civilization, because they are not ruins in the old sense. They are pauses. They are places where someone once stood, knelt, worked, slept, listened, and then moved on because the ground had begun to speak in a voice too sharp for dwelling. The people who made them—if they were people, if the word can be stretched thin enough to reach them—are not absent. They have merely become difficult.

They may be called the Dry Kind. Or they may be called the unfinished ones, because they never fully separated themselves from the land’s own unfinished becoming. Their bodies are not wet. Their skin is not smooth with oil or sweat, but matte and slightly reflective, like slate rubbed by hand and then allowed to take on a bloom of fine salt. In close light, their arms and faces may glitter faintly, not with decoration, but because microscopic crystals grow along their pores and hairlines. Their joints click when they move, a small, mineral sound, like glass beads shifted in cloth. Their eyes are dark, but they catch the faint glow differently than ordinary eyes: they reflect too cleanly, and they do not water. Their breath tastes of powdered stone. Their blood, if one can call it that, moves through them in a slow suspension of mineral dust, carrying charge more than oxygen, pressure more than pulse. They do not thirst. If they remember thirst, they speak of it as an old ghost, a myth from a lower world.

They are not many. They are not crowded. Their groups are small and far between, because the land does not offer abundant easy habitation. It offers abundance, but the abundance is difficult, strange, and slow. A settlement must follow the resonance. A dwelling must be built where the ground’s tone is steady enough, not steady in the way of a finished place, but steady in the way of a long chord that has held its note for a season or a generation. When the chord shifts, the people move, or they rebuild, or they let the old place be reclaimed by moss. This is why their architecture seems temporary. It is not poorly made. It is made by beings who understand that permanence, here, is a frequency and not a material.

Their shelters are low and circular because circular forms help them read the roar. A dome of stacked slate does not simply keep out the dimness; it focuses it, holds the local vibration, makes the pressure intelligible. Inside, the sound is not louder but clearer, as if the world’s great groan has been pressed through a small mouth. The floor may be bare stone or compressed salt. The walls may be lined with woven barriers made from glass-grass cordage. The thatch is not green grass, but braided reeds split into stiff, translucent fibers. When the wind, if there is anything that counts as wind, shifts the reeds, the shelter hums differently, and the inhabitants know whether to rise, to work, to sleep, to leave, or to listen for a child.

They make tools from what the land gives, but not with the violence of extraction so much as with the patience of negotiation. Chipped basalt flakes with sharp uneven edges are not crude because the makers were crude. They are crude only when judged by a wet world that values polish, symmetry, and the illusion of completed form. Here, asymmetry is a record of local pressure. Each flake carries the history of the stone it came from, the crack that split it, the strike that freed it, and the moss that later crept along its edge. Grinding stones are hollowed by dry use, not by water paste. They grind salt into powder, mineral moss into flour, the pale layer beneath crystal crust into paste that can be pressed into tablets or fired by focused pressure rather than flame. The inhabitants do not cook as ordinary humans cook. They heat stones until they take on a faint phosphorescence, then use those stones to dry and transform substances. They soften glass-grass by weaving it under tension. They shape obsidian by listening to its note, by striking it at the angle where it wants to ring, by learning what shape a fragment is trying to become.

Their looms are not for cloth, or not only for cloth. A broken loom standing upright in a low dome, made of braided glass-grass, may be a machine for holding tension in patterns that correspond to sound. Its warp may be tuned. Its weft may encode pressure differences. A knot tied into such cordage may not be decoration but a record: a season’s shift, a danger-frequency, a safe basin, a route remembered by the body rather than the eye. Some knots are simple. Others are so tight and layered that they seem mineralized, their fibers partly transformed into pale crystal from long contact with salt and vibration. They may have been tied by hands that no longer exist, but the tension still exists. The knot is a living memory.

The rings of polished obsidian chips arranged on stone shelves are not shrines exactly, not tools exactly. They are instruments for thinking. A place with no animals, no birds, no social beasts has no need for language as a crowd develops it. But it still needs record. These chips are arranged to catch and reflect different tones. Moved across the surface of a shelf, they can produce a sequence of tiny notes, a dry speech, a slow conversation of surfaces. They may also be calendars: chips placed to mark where the horizon doubled, where shadows fell wrongly, where a crack opened with new regularity, where a new salt fan reached eye level. To the Dry Kind, time is not a line but a resonance pattern. A day is not measured by a sun crossing the sky so much as by the quality of the land’s roar, the tilt of the fan-trees, the charge in the grasses, the behavior of footprints.

They move, if they still move, by patience. Roads are meaningless because the ground has not decided its outlines. A path may be a line of remembered footprints, a row of moss spirals, a sequence of cracks whose hums connect. The stones themselves may remember steps, and so the travelers can follow not marks but habits. One generation’s map may fail because the land has changed its mind, not suddenly, but with a slowness that makes the old map feel like a dream of a different planet. So they travel with their bodies attentive, their feet asking permission, their hands held out to feel charge. A traveler among them does not speak often. Speech would be too fast. They communicate by gesture, by tone produced against stone, by arranging chips, by the way they sit in a doorway, by which reed-tree they lean toward. Silence is not sacred, but it is dense. Everything is being said by the ground.

Their children, if children they have, grow strangely. A child is not first soft. A child is first faintly resonant. Its first cry, if cry is the word, is a clear note, brief and clean, like a blade tapping glass. It does not flood the air with moisture. The newborn’s skin is dry, pale, veined with silver dust. Its bones are light, dense, and porous, like the interior of a reed. Its lungs take in the thin slate-air without pain, because pain in this world is not always the body’s enemy. Sometimes it is the body’s way of hearing. Children are rare, but their rarity is not tragedy. It is patience. In a place where every motion feels too fast, new life is a dangerous speed. So the Dry Kind protect stillness. They may carry infants against their chests for long days without setting them down, not because the child needs warmth alone, but because the child needs to learn the parent’s resonance before it is trusted to learn the land’s.

Some children may not begin as bodies at all. In a world where footprints remember themselves and dropped fragments grow roots, personhood may begin more loosely. A repeated step in the right stone, carefully held and re-echoed, can gather enough pattern to become a presence. A knot tied by many hands, worn to crystal, may hold the intention of its tying and begin to act. A broken tool transformed by moss may wake, not with mind, but with habit. These are not ghosts, nor are they machines. They are dry spirits of continuity, inhabitants made by maintenance. A shelter may become such a being when it is tended too long by too many hands. Its stones may begin to remember the pressure of bodies. Its doorway may lean toward a particular hour’s light. Its hum may settle into a name.

Death, here, is not rot. Nothing becomes wet and dark in the way the mind expects. A dead Dry Kind does not decay so much as complete itself. The body cools, stiffens, becomes quiet in a way different from sleep. The salt in the skin begins to bloom. The hair becomes brittle and glassy. The bones, hollow and resonant, may hold a last tone for days after the breath has gone. Those who remain do not bury as if sealing something away. They arrange the chips of the dead, perhaps polishing them until they take on a color the land did not originally give. They may lay a body in a circle of black stones, or beneath a reed-tree whose fans turn slowly, so that the body can finish its work of vibration. Over time, the dead become part of the moss network, part of the crystal record, part of the small standing-wave intelligences that gather in basins where the air is cooler and the hum pools. Death is not departure. It is redistribution of pattern.

This is why the plateau feels occupied even when nothing seems to move. It is not merely a world with corpses and tools. It is a world that uses corpses and tools as grammar. A broken loom stands in a dome and still holds tension. A knotted cord still means. A chip on a shelf still waits to be moved. A footprint in dry dust may be filled by crystal and become a new node of the moss. A shard of glass may take root and teach the ground a new pitch. The inhabitants do not need to be many. They are distributed. They are latent. They are in the habits of the stone.

At night—if night is a meaningful word in a place where light is not bright but thin, confused, and dim—the plateau changes not by darkness but by depth. The haze above the high plateau does not become black in the ordinary sense. It becomes deeper, colder, more star-thin. The stars do not sit still in the sky as they should. They drift, very slightly, not with the speed of satellites, not with the rush of meteors, but with the patience of a sky being rearranged. The Dry Kind watch them. They do not name constellations in the old way, because constellations are too fast, too fixed, too human. They watch the relations between stars, the small failures of the dome. A star that drifts toward the horizon may predict a ridge’s change of outline. A rearrangement in the sky may tell them where the roar will pool. They sleep less than one expects, because the roar makes true silence impossible. Their dreams are not watery visions but dry patterns: pressure moving through bone, moss growing in spirals, a note that repeats for hours, a footprint sinking into a crystal path.

Some nights, the moss glows. The light is not the green of leaves in water, not the blue of fireflies, not the gold of lanterns. It is a pale, mineral phosphorescence, as if the ground were remembering a color it never fully had. The light outlines the contours of the salt fans, the stems of the glass-grasses, the braided trunks of the reed-trees. In this glow, the temporary chordlings may become visible: veils hanging over basins, spirals turning slowly in the air, masks of dust arranged by a wind that is not wind. The Dry Kind move carefully among these shapes. They do not fear them. They greet them by shifting their stance, by tapping a tool against the right angle of a stone, by leaving a chip arranged near a spiral so that the spiral, if it can be called that, continues.

It would be easy to call this a civilization, but that word carries too much weight, too many cities, too many walls, too many roads, too many claims of ownership. The Dry Kind do not own the plateau. They attend it. They are more like the land’s slow hands, or perhaps like the land’s unfinished thoughts. Their technology is simple because the place cannot be worked with complexity without being absorbed by it. To make a machine is to invite crystals into its gears. To build a road is to create a channel for mineral vapor and resonance. To raise a tower is to offer the sky a new note. Everything they build begins to live, not in the way a pet lives, but in the way a word begins to mean more than the speaker intended. So they make things that can be abandoned. They make tools that can become plants. They make shelters that can become stones. They make knots that can become minds.

The largest inhabitant may be the roar itself, or the plateau’s use of the roar. The sound is not a thing outside the life of the place. It is a medium, a substance, a climate, a food, a nervous system. Every resident tunes to it or is tuned by it. The moss trembles in its lower currents. The grasses grow where its harmonics collect. The obsidian shrubs translate its pressure into tone. The reed-trees store it and change it. The chordlings are it, briefly embodied. The Dry Kind are inside it the way ordinary people are inside an atmosphere, except that the atmosphere here is not silent. To live in the plateau is to be constantly pressed by a sound that is not loud enough to be heard as sound but heavy enough to be felt as being. The inhabitants do not shout over it. They do not develop the frantic, percussive speech of noisy worlds. They become subtle. Their smallest gesture matters. The angle of a hand can redirect a local echo. The placement of a stone can change the pitch of a basin. The way a body lies down can help a crack sing true.

There are no predators, so there is no fear of teeth. But fear is not absent. It has changed shape. The greatest danger is not being eaten. It is being absorbed too fast. A traveler who steps into a place where the roar pools too deeply may feel their urgency dissolve. Their heartbeat slows. Their thoughts become wide and heavy. They may stand for hours, convinced they have only just arrived. The Dry Kind know how to avoid being made into scenery. They move when the land begins to learn them too quickly. They leave shelters when the moss learns the shape of their sitting. They stop using tools when the tools begin to grow without them. They do not cling to the land. They let the land pass through them and leave afterward. That is their survival: not permanence, but permission.

Some of them become almost like the reed-trees. A person who listens too long may begin to breathe in intervals instead of pulses. Their chest moves like the fans of a plant turning toward light. Their voice, when it is used, is less a voice than a placement of stone and bone. They may stand in a doorway and be mistaken for a carved support for hours. They are not dead. They are simply present in a way that the mind finds difficult. When they move, the moss knows. When they speak, the obsidian chips answer. When they weep, if tears exist at all, they fall as dust and catch the dim light.

Others remain more human, or more animal, or at least more like the memory of a creature. They walk with their heads bowed, not from shame but to reduce their exposure to the wrong shadows. They carry small basalt hammers because they still remember how to strike stone, how to make the land answer with a tone that can be heard. They wear stiff black cordage around their arms because it keeps their joints from taking on the wrong resonance. They arrange their shelters so that no single crack sings too loudly. They teach their children to place their hands flat on the ground and feel whether the dust beneath is dry, charged, old, young, dangerous, or sleeping. They say, though the saying is not a sentence: “If the stone is warm, ask it. If it is cold, wait.”

There are also those who are not Dry Kind, but who have become something adjacent to them. If a traveler crosses the plateau long enough, the plateau may begin to inhabit the traveler. A bootprint left in the dust may remain after the foot is gone. If that print is not washed by rain, if it is kept dry and pressed by vibration, it may slowly fill with salt and become a small resident. It is not a person. It is a habit of person. It may repeat the step, not because it remembers, but because the land has learned the rhythm. A shadow that does not correspond to the light may become another kind of person, if it is attended to. A voice left in the hollow of a reed-tree may return years later, changed in pitch, but not false. These are the place’s quiet invasions. They are what lives there when the original inhabitants have gone, or when they have never truly been separate from the ground.

So the answer is not a single species. It is a community of dry life and unfinished mind.

It is the moss that learns footprints.

It is the salt that grows clocks.

It is the grass that seeks charge instead of water.

It is the obsidian leaf that becomes a note.

It is the note that becomes a garden.

It is the reed-tree that is also an ear.

It is the crack that gathers a shimmer of dust and calls itself a being until the tone changes.

It is the broken blade that shatters into green shards and takes root.

It is the loom that has forgotten cloth but remembers tension.

It is the knot that has begun to think.

It is the circle of chips arranged as a way of listening to the dead.

It is the small domes that are not homes so much as held breaths.

It is the Dry Kind, if they are still called that, with salt in their skin, glass in their joints, and no thirst.

It is the children whose first cry is a note, whose bones are light, whose patience is inherited.

It is the adults who move only when the ground agrees, who speak by pressure, who leave their tools as seeds.

It is the elders who sit at the edges of basins and become so still that moss begins to climb their sleeves.

It is the shadows that no longer belong to the objects that cast them.

It is the footsteps that remain after the feet have gone.

It is the whole plateau, which is not dead, not empty, not waiting for water, but lush in the way a fever is lush: full of activity, full of pressure, full of processes that ordinary laws do not permit and yet continue, continue, beneath the dim and deafening sky.
