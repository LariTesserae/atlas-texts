The true inhabitants are not one kind of life but a joint occupation.

At the human center there are the Sinterfolk—though that is not a name they chose to sound poetic, nor a name imposed by outsiders with too much distance. It is a name that came from habit. In their language, *sinter* is the slow mineral crust that geothermal water leaves when it passes through stone: white lips on hot channels, pale rims around vents, chalky bloom on iron where steam has breathed for decades. To be sintered is to be deposited, layered, made durable by gradual accumulation. The people call themselves Sinter because they understand themselves as things forming in water, heat, stone, and long maintenance. They are not a bright nation, not a conquering civilization, not a mountain race with flags and epics. They are the city’s wet, iron-voiced, wool-wrapped residents, and the plateau has sintered them over generations.

They are small-boned but not fragile. Thin air discourages excess mass; cold damp discourages openness. Their chests are compact and rounded, as though their ribcages had learned to hold breath like a sealed vessel. Their shoulders are narrow but often strong in an odd, practical way—the strength of climbing wet ladders, hauling rope, scraping frost, turning valves with a cloth over the hand. Their noses are long and clean, because noses matter in cold places; their eyes are deep-set and often rimmed with faint irritation from steam, dust, mineral mist, and the glare of wet stone. Many have pale skin, the kind that flushes reddish on the nose, knuckles, and ears, then darkens in the rare clear hour to the color of birch bark left in rain. Their hands are broad-palmed, short-nailed, marked by small burns and scratches that never fully fade. Old Sinterfolk have hands like tools stored in a damp drawer: still functional, still precise, but wearing the city into their flesh.

Their voices are low. Shouting is unnecessary when sound travels through iron and water, and dangerous when mist thickens in the lungs. A Sinter voice is trained to carry without volume. They speak in short phrases that end with a downward breath, almost as if every sentence must be paid for in oxygen. When they laugh, it is often quiet, a sudden exhalation, as though laughter is one more thing that should not be wasted too much at this elevation.

Their clothing is part of the architecture. Every Sinter has more layers than an outsider first expects: inner wool next to skin, outer wool woven with hair from hardy plants, pitched canvas over that, then waxed hides where water demands it. Boots are heavy, oil-soaked, sometimes lined with cork or compressed moss to deaden cold through the soles. A boot is not merely footwear but a family object; children inherit them before they inherit plates. Hoods have stiffened brims to throw dripping mist from the eyes. Gaiters are worn over trouser legs to keep moss beads from trickling inward. In workshops, sleeves are bound tight with leather thongs, and every worker carries a small brass hook for hanging a cloak by a vent so the damp can be lifted from the fabric before it reaches the bone.

Their houses are accretive, like themselves. A Sinter home is rarely one style. It begins in squat stone, sunk into the plateau’s own skin. Then timber and plaster are added, then iron, tin, piping, brackets, vents, boxes, platforms, hanging racks, drying poles. The roof is almost always tin because tin sings when water beads and bursts, and the sound tells the household that the night is doing something. Moss grows in the seams not only as intrusion but as seasoning: it seals gaps, softens wind, traps warmth, and—after three rains—is swept back and sometimes used for packing, insulation, poultices, or as a gentle abrasive for cleaning metal. Children are taught which mosses bite with itch and which are safe against the wrist.

A Sinter room is never fully dry, and they have made that into comfort. The center of a home is usually the warm corner: a low stove, an iron bed-box, a shelf of oiled stones, sometimes a small engine exhaust passed through masonry to share its heat. Beds are raised on legs, often with removable tin pans underneath so dripping can be heard and measured. Every family keeps a dry chest. This chest is almost sacred, though they would resist that word. It is made of tight wood, sometimes lined with oilcloth or a mixture of charcoal and lime, and holds the best clothing, papers, medicine, and keepsakes. When it smells of must, someone in the household is ill or the weather has entered through the lid. Opening it too often is foolish; closing it too often is lonely.

The wet parts of the house are not feared but respected. Some lower floors are deliberately open to channel water, so that a family’s cellar may be more river than room. These spaces are not wasted; they cool, they regulate temperature, they discourage fire in certain directions, they carry away silt, and they serve as proof that the settlement was built after the water and not before it. There is no shame in this arrangement. To block such a channel is a kind of violence against the land and against one’s neighbors, because water that is insulted in one place will find revenge in another.

Their names reflect the geometry of the place. A person’s true name is given at birth in a dry room by someone with clean hands—often the mother, or an older woman who can keep water from spilling during the naming—but they are also called by seam-names: the place where they first cried, where their family kept tools, where the nearest bell was heard from the window. A child may become Moss-Left, Tin-Door, Pipe-Seven, Black-Channel, or Second-Cold-Stair. These names are not mere nicknames. They are maps. To speak of a person is to speak, in part, of where the city held them.

They have no temples. Their closest thing to temple is the maintained channel.

A channel that runs clean, clear, and audible is the greatest praise anyone can receive. Families boast quietly over which pipe still sings, which wall still sweats the correct pattern, which terrace still drains before the frost sets. The most respected elders are not the most learned in abstract knowledge, though learning matters, but those who can read the city’s hum. A good pipe-reader can press a bare palm to a metal conduit and know by vibration whether water is moving freely, whether scale is narrowing the bore, whether steam has changed its mouth somewhere below. A good fog-listener can stand on an outside stair and say, from the way mist pools and retreats, which vents have cooled, which stones are about to shift, which lower street will become a river in half an hour.

Their work is mostly maintenance, and they are proud of it in a modest way. Sinter work is not about making things larger or more spectacular. It is about keeping the world from undoing itself. Condensation farms line the upper terraces: long tilted plates angled to catch moisture, their surfaces wiped and scraped in shifts, their gutters checked for blockage. Drying fields sit in shallow hollows, protected from direct wind by stone rims and low walls, where goods, grain, hides, cloth, and fuel are laid out to shed damp. Steam-bellows tenders adjust valves to send breath into workshops, keeping heat from the plateau’s interior moving through hidden belts of stone. Bell-hours are observed not because the bells are sacred, though some are, but because in fog a bell tells you where you are relative to others. The sound says: not that far, not yet lost, not alone.

They harvest sinter with care. Mineral crusts are scraped from vents, channels, and heated stones, then used as mortar, pigment, filtration media, mild abrasive, poultice base, and the white rim on a chimney cap that marks the line where the city’s breath has been rising for years. In some places, whole platforms have grown pale with deposit, and the Sinterfolk treat them with the same respect an orchard owner shows ancient fruit trees: not as geology, but as productive body. They can predict by the color of new sinter whether heat is rising, whether sulfur has altered the water, whether a fissure is opening or merely breathing.

Their medicine is practical and damp-worried. They steam lungs with heated stone, drink bitter lichen broths, rub rendered fat and powdered mineral onto aching joints, apply moss poultices to burns, and bind wounds with waxed cloth. They fear stagnation more than injury: a closed room with no breath, a blocked channel, a chest wound that cannot be opened to air. If a house feels sour, they knock holes in walls, reorient vents, or burn salt and dried resin in the center of a room until the smell lifts. They do not think of this as superstition, though outsiders might call it so. To them, superstition is what happens when people refuse to notice consequences.

They raise children by the hand, not the whip. In a city of wet stone and exposed machinery, violence against a child wastes the one person who still has to learn everything. Children spend much of their early years attached to adults at the hip with cloth loops or rope belts, because the streets are full of drops, steam mouths, and sudden ice. They learn to walk by learning where not to walk. A Sinter child knows by age six which railings are cold enough to stick, which stairs will bead at the second hour after rain, which roofs sound safe and which sound hollow. They learn the difference between a harmless drip and a dangerous trickle by touch alone.

They eat without ceremony. Food is dense and portable: dark breads dried on hot stone, root crops, lentils, eggs gathered from tower birds or kept from small hardy fowls traded upward, salt, dried meats, and a bitter mushroom or lichen broth that helps against mineral-heavy digestion. In wealthy households, or in households attached to old trade lines, there is imported grain, tea, sugar, and sometimes milk from lowland animals. The Sinterfolk do not disdain imported food, but they regard it with mild suspicion. What comes from below must pass through many damp hands and unclear seasons; what comes from above must be scraped, gathered, tended, and earned. Their own food is less pleasant and more honest.

They are suspicious of open space. Not because they fear the sky, though they respect it, but because open space on the plateau means exposure to wind, fog, cold, and the long drop. A large empty room makes them uneasy. A courtyard without a channel or roof is “unfinished.” The ideal gathering place is a roofed square or an iron gallery where many people can stand close, share heat, and still see each other’s faces. Their festivals are not loud. They are shifts of light and heat: the opening of the dry rooms in the first hard cold, the cleaning of the great bell in the last fog season, the hanging of blue lichen bands above a channel that has run clear all year. The greatest compliment one person can pay another is to say, without irony, “Your house keeps dry enough.”

And yet, if one looks more closely, the people are only one part of the population.

The pale-eyed birds are the city’s aerial tenants. They are not beautiful in a conventional way. Their eyes are too bright, their bills too short, their wings long and stiff in a way that makes them look less feathered than cut. They nest in cooling towers, chimney cavities, and the upper hollows of buildings where steam once rose. They hunt the midges, drink fog from angled wires, and roost in places too narrow for humans. The Sinterfolk do not try to keep them as pets. They call them *tower-breathers* and treat them as indicators. When the birds shift nesting sites, something in the air has changed. When they go silent, a new vent may be opening. Children are taught not to take eggs from the higher nests because the birds know the unseen currents better than any human map, and a city that loses its birds will lose one of its ears.

The ground creatures are harder to see. In the deepest moss terraces at the settlement’s edges, small dense-furred runners move at dawn and dusk—creatures that might be called rabbits by someone not living here, but which are not quite rabbits. They have broad feet, short ears, and fur so thick that it holds water in beads like waxed wool. They live in hollows beneath root mats and abandoned platforms, and they are the reason certain moss patches stay aerated. The Sinterfolk leave them alone except in hard winter, and then only in regulated numbers. More often, the Sinterfolk use the ground creatures as they use the birds: not as property, but as evidence that the land still has private life enough to hide from them.

Insects are everywhere the water hides. Near channels and condensation basins, midges rise in slow columns when disturbed. Beetles with oil-sheen shells crawl along pipe seams, feeding on lichen, algae, or the soft rusted skin of old iron. Some people cultivate these beetles in shallow trays as a living indicator of damp balance: too many beetles means the air is soft enough for new corrosion; too few means dryness has entered somewhere it should not. Moths gather at gas jets and weak bulbs, leaving pale dust on glass that must be wiped away each morning. There are also smaller things—mites in the wool layers of insulation, pale grubs in the moss that turn to flies, fungal threads too fine to see that make certain walls bloom overnight with a soft grey fur. These are rarely acknowledged in speech, but they are understood. A wall that starts to smell of warm powder after rain is already speaking.

The moss itself is a resident. It is not decoration. It is not even merely vegetation. It is part of the city’s body. Certain strains are encouraged over others because they seal drafts better, repel flies, absorb sound, hold heat, or turn a color that warns of mineral change. A moss wall can indicate humidity, wind direction, and the age of plaster. In some districts, houses have been deliberately replanted with moss taken from an older building when a new roof is laid, as though moving the household’s second skin from one shell to another. People speak of moss inheritance: if a family’s wall moss dies, they do not call that a disaster, but they may move into the house for a year or more before trusting it.

Lichens, too, are inhabitants, though slower than thought. The pale blues, rusty yellows, and olive films that paint the plateau’s stone are used to mark ownership, warn of wetness, and paint the outer signs of workshops. A good sign-painter knows more than letters; they know which lichen grows against wind, which spreads along stone joints, which will stain metal if pressed too hard against a hot plate. Lichen is also mixed with sinter for pigments that resist fading in fog. When a Sinter door is painted, it is often painted with the same material the city has been using for generations, so that after a few seasons the door and the wall begin to resemble each other again.

Then there are the machines, which have begun to live again.

Most are not alive in any literal sense, but they behave as though the boundary matters less than the city thinks. Engines tick. Belts turn with dry sighs. Pipes sing in wind. Reservoirs hold black water that never seems to evaporate because the thermal draw keeps them in circulation and because the microbial films that grow within them release heat at a slow rate. Great iron drums stand in courtyards and turn once an hour, their original purpose forgotten by everyone except a few stubborn archivists, yet they have become calendar stones: people say they are meeting when the second drum turns, or that the wind is shifting because the drums have changed their rhythm. Fans rotate in long lazy circles, pulling steam away from workshops or sending it into drying galleries. These fans are cleaned and oiled, and when they stop, someone notices as quickly as a farmer notices a silent well.

Maintenance is closer to animal husbandry than to engineering. A Sinter mechanic does not merely repair; they adjust, warn, coax, and listen. They say “hold” to a valve about to stick. They tap a pipe before opening it, as if reminding metal of its manners. They wrap pipes in wool not just for insulation but to give the machine a kind of coat, a softness against the cold, as though a bare pipe in winter would suffer and sulk. In old workshops, machines no longer used are left in place until nature finishes the dismantling. Cooling towers become bird cathedrals. Broken turbines become pools for moss, beetles, and rainwater. Rusted frames become supports for climbing ferns that once would have been cleared away. The city does not mourn this. It reassigns the object to the next life it can enter.

Even the plateau is a resident, and the eldest one.

It does not live as animals live, but it exchanges. Stone gives heat. Water gives movement. Air gives damp. Iron gives rust. Life gives and takes color in the same breath. New steam mouths open where moss shifts overnight. Ice forms in hidden hollows and lifts slabs with patience. Frost creeps into cracks, widens them a hair, and lets go again. Water runs downward through fissures and returns upward as warm sulfur threads, carrying mineral memory from deep places to the surface. The Sinterfolk know this not as philosophy but as household weather. They do not say the mountain is angry. They say the mountain has moved its breath.

They are descendants, mostly, of people who came for work and stayed because the work became indistinguishable from living. Some came for heat, some for minerals, some for the strange purity of high steam, some to escape lowland plagues, taxes, armies, or debts. The settlement’s oldest quarters contain names and tools no longer explained. A door may open onto a stair that ends in a sealed room. A pipe may descend into a cistern with no known source. A reservoir may sit black and patient under a stone lip, its temperature wrong for the season. The Sinterfolk do not always know why these things exist, and they have learned that ignorance can be practical. If a place hums, one may leave it alone. If a place does not dry after long warmth, one may leave it alone. If a place is remembered by no one but still maintained by many hands, it must have been useful once, and usefulness tends to persist.

They keep records without paper. Paper molds, sticks to hands, turns soft, and forgets its shape. Sinter records are cut into tin, pressed into clay tablets fired in steam-kilns, knotted into cords, etched on copper plates, or painted in lichen patterns that age visibly. A well-kept ledger is a stack of thin metal pages with oil between them. A genealogy may be represented by a sequence of channel sounds, a pattern of stones set in a wall, or a particular moss color cultivated in a courtyard for three generations. To lose a record is less terrible than to misplace a dry-room key; a record can be rebuilt if enough people still carry its habits in their hands.

Their law is mostly about water, warmth, and blockage. A person may be fined, shamed, or assigned labor for closing a neighbor’s channel, leaving a stove vent open too long, or allowing silt to enter a shared cistern. The worst punishment is not imprisonment in a cold cell, which most prisoners would prefer anyway, but confinement in a room too wet to sleep and too dry to drink from. The best reputation belongs to those who keep a house in working order, share heat when others’ stoves fail, and can be trusted to notice when the city is beginning to fail at something.

They travel along the plateau’s edges as much as through its streets. The Sinter region is not a single town but a network of terraces, cable tracks, drying fields, steam farms, lower basins, and hidden settlements. From the high shelf, smaller communities descend toward warmer air, their roofs dark in the mist, their bells answering one another at strange hours. Goods move by rail, pipeline, and cable: fuel from the low valleys, metal from the deeper works, salt from evaporated pools, moss-dyed cloth, sinter bricks, dried roots, and machine parts. The Sinterfolk do not control all this trade, but they are its pulse. Their settlements are high places, and high places become visible even when they are not bright.

There are among them a type of person especially suited to this world: the wet-quiet laborer. The person who can climb a slick ladder in rain without complaining, who can carry a sealed tool case through a yard so cold the metal sticks to the glove, who can hear one drip in a wall out of a hundred drips, who can look at a patch of moss and know that a gutter is forming, who can wait an hour by a vent because the vent has not yet finished what it is beginning to say. These people are not always admired outwardly. They are not loud, not charismatic, not rich. But when a winter fails, they are found in the dark, moving through alleys with a tin cup of broth or a spare gasket, because someone has to keep the city from turning into stone.

At dawn, before the settlement fully wakes, the birds circle above the towers. The first work is not in factories but in listening. Men and women press palms to railings, look upward at fog, scrape ice from plates, and tap drains. Steam rises in slow threads. A bell sounds somewhere below. Light appears not as sun but as a thin silver brightening that makes everything look tired but clean. The Sinterfolk move through wet stone as though expecting it, because expectation is a survival trait. They do not walk carelessly. They place feet where water has already chosen to go, where moss gives a little, where iron has begun to darken with damp. They speak softly. They breathe carefully. They know the names of their houses not because they are sentimental, but because their houses are old companions.

Evening comes without drama. The air cools, fog thickens, the steam from vents becomes more visible, and the settlement’s small lights begin to show through wet glass. Forge-light spills yellow across courtyards. Gas jets blink blue near condensation chambers. Oil lamps glow inside rooms where wool insulation has been replaced by hand, and the smell of hot metal, moss, and damp cloth becomes one of the most familiar odors of life. People eat little meals that have been kept warm near pipes, then sleep lightly because a roof may leak, a pipe may burst, a bell may ring the hour of frost. At night, the city does not go silent. It changes its silence into something full of tiny reports: metal ticking, water shifting in walls, stone settling somewhere unseen, moss releasing a slow breath of cold air.

If one insists on naming what lives there, the answer is layered.

The humans live there, but they are not alone. They are the warm-blooded agents of a wet ecology: Sinterfolk with oiled boots and low voices, children reading water by touch, old pipe-listeners with hands like polished iron, moss-farmers scraping pale bloom from a hot rim, condensation-keepers tilting plates toward fog, bell-tenders winding their weights through the damp hours. The birds live there, nesting where the city’s heat makes stone safe enough for eggs. The beetles live there, cleaning seams and warning of rot. The midges live there, clouding the mouths of water. The moss lives there, sealing, softening, absorbing, and sometimes judging the air. The machines live there, not because they think, but because they have been given bodies, rhythms, moods, and names by enough generations of care. The water lives there. The steam lives there. The stone itself lives there, too slowly for any human breath to match, but present enough that everyone feels the difference between a stone that is merely cold and a stone that is listening.

What lives in that place, therefore, is not a population.

It is an accretion: human, mineral, fungal, mechanical, animal, weather-born. It is a city that has learned to inhabit its own erosion. It is a society that does not shine but endures in patches of visible warmth. It is a high world whose people have been sintered by the same forces that make pale rims around their vents, and who know, without needing to say it in full, that every surface is doing something—draining, holding, rusting, growing, cooling, warning, waiting.
