The inhabitants of the bowl are not creatures in the usual sense. They have no eyes, no hunger, no calls, no tracks, no nests, no faces turned toward the sky. Nothing moves through the moss with legs. Nothing drinks from the pools with a tongue. Nothing burrows under the ash to sleep. If a human were to stand at the rim and search for life there, at first the eye might return only emptiness: black stone, wet stone, broken stone, steam moving as though the mountain were breathing but without lungs, water falling and vanishing again without a river.

But the bowl is not empty.

It is filled with life so still, so small, so patient, so bound to surfaces and moisture and mineral that it seems, at first glance, to be part of the stone itself.

The first and greatest citizens of the place are the mosses.

They are everywhere: in cushions, mats, films, threads, sponges, felt-pads, curtains, damp black sheets, green-black carpets clinging to basalt. There are no trees, no shrubs, no broad leaves catching sunlight in an open canopy. The mosses have taken their place, covering the ground in a low, dense, trembling kingdom of smallness. They grow where they can grip, not where they can stand tall. They grow where water lingers, where steam condenses, where a crack holds dust, where ash has softened into a faint suggestion of soil.

Some mosses are black, almost sooty, spreading over wet stone like a stain that refuses to wash off. Others are a deeper green, saturated to the point of appearing blue-black in the low light. A few, sheltered in tiny basins, shine faintly jade where a brief opening in the cloud lets pale sunlight strike them. Near cold ledges they become tight and pale, compacted against frost. Near warm vents they grow loose and sodden, heavy with moisture, their cells swollen until they seem ready to burst.

They have no true roots to anchor them. They hold by tiny filaments, by friction, by wetness, by the slow adhesion of their bodies to stone. If a boulder shifts, the moss moves with it, not as an animal fleeing, but as a living skin stretched over a moving surface. If the boulder breaks, the moss splits, crumbles, dies at the edges, and the surviving fragments catch dust, collect water, begin again in another shape.

This is what the moss does here: not merely grow, but persist by becoming part of the surface.

It grips. It seals. It holds moisture. It makes a place for other things.

In the deepest hollows, where steam pools and the air is almost wet enough to drink, the moss becomes thick and sloppy. It grows in curtains that drip when touched. Its filaments form a tangle that traps spores, ash, mineral dust, fragments of dead tissue, and small bright crystals. Water runs down it in slow paths, darkening it as it goes, and when it dries for a few minutes in a gust, it curls inward, brittle, gray, apparently lifeless. But then another spray of mist, another collapse of cloud against the stone, and it swells again, reopening tiny leaves to the dimness, resuming its silent work.

Higher, on dry ledges, the moss becomes something else: pale, tight, almost crustlike. There it is no longer alone. It shares the stone with lichens.

The lichens are the second civilization of the bowl.

They are not moss. They are not quite plant, not quite fungus, not quite animal. They are a collaboration, a meeting of partners that makes a single organism capable of living where most cannot. They grow slowly, almost invisibly, over glass, basalt, ash, salt-crusts, iron-stained stone. They form mosaics of gray, white, green, rust, ochre, black. They look like old paintings on a damp wall, like maps of nations that have never existed. But they are alive, spreading in rings and islands, colonizing fresh cracks, reclaiming shattered surfaces, turning unstable rock into a community of color.

Some lichens are flat as paint, pressed to the stone, unable to lift themselves even a little. Some are granular, crumbly, forming pale crusts that break into dust when scraped. Others have tiny lobes, edges curling upward as though trying to feel the wind. On the coldest walls they become tight and gray, almost invisible except by texture. On drier sunlit ledges, rare as sun is, they glow pale gold or rust-red, as if the stone had begun to burn without heat.

They live by weather. They live by the slow arrival of mist, by condensation, by the faint moisture carried in the cloud that sits on the bowl. They do not drink like animals. They take the air into themselves, all over, through every exposed surface. They are organisms of permeation. When the fog moves through, they darken. When the wind strips moisture away, they lighten, contract, wait.

There is a kind of patience in them that is not calm but mineral. A lichen may spread across a boulder for years, then be cracked away in an instant by falling rock. Another may begin on a surface no human hand has touched, on a piece of volcanic glass freshly broken from the flow. In that moment, it is already trying to live. It cannot choose the weather. It cannot flee the collapse. It can only attach itself to the edge of instability and grow in the dark.

Between the moss and lichen lies the water.

The pools are inhabited by films and blooms and mats that seem more color than life at first. Green and brown algae spread in shallow water, sometimes as a slick like oil, sometimes as a soft fuzz, sometimes as a tangled hair. They paint the basins in shades that shift with temperature: near a vent, a yellowish green; in cold shadow, a dark olive; in a pool where iron seeps, a rusty brown; in clear meltwater, a faint jade. They grow where light is too weak for larger plants and where minerals are sharp enough to sustain them.

Some pools are acidic, stained with sulfur, faintly boiling at the edges. There are no fish, no insects, no amphibians. But there are microbial communities: microscopic threads and cells that live by chemistry as much as by light. Around hot seepages, near the mouths of shallow vents, there may be mats that feel greasy or gritty under a finger, formed by organisms that consume sulfur or iron compounds and leave behind strange crusts. In cooler water, diatoms and algae build delicate siliceous walls, so small they vanish at once, yet in their millions they can cloud a pool, brighten it, or turn it brown when they die.

These water-lives do not wait for permanence. They exist in pools that may last minutes, days, seasons, or only until the next collapse. They multiply when moisture remains, dry into powder, freeze into brittle films, revive when water returns. They are the true inhabitants of the transient: creatures of the brief wet.

The ferns are rarer, but they are there.

They grow in cracks where more water and less danger gather: beneath an overhang, inside a fissure too narrow for large falling stone, beside a seepage that never fully dries. Their fronds are small, dark, wet with condensation, stiff in cold air, soft in steam-warmed shadow. They do not spread broadly across the bowl. They do not fill the slopes. They are hidden things, tucked into the architecture of the stone.

They reproduce by spores released from the undersides of their leaves, carried by drafts, landing in dust, surviving or failing according to the next hour’s weather. Their life cycle is not a single line from birth to death but a scattered repetition: spores, gametophytes, tiny green hearts growing in damp ash, young ferns rising only to be crushed, buried, scorched, or waterlogged. A fern in this place is an accident that has somehow become a life.

Then there are the fungi.

They are not plants, though they belong to the same dense weave of growth. Under damp ash, inside rotting moss mats, around buried fragments of dead fern, in the sheltered underside of collapsed stone, white threads spread like invisible veins. They dissolve what the mountain has already destroyed and returns to mineral dust. They break down dead tissue, recover nitrogen and carbon, make surfaces usable again, connect one small death to another possible life.

Some are faint and colorless. Others form pale fruiting bodies, no larger than a grain of sand, appearing after a warm rain and vanishing into powder before the next frost. In the deepest, most humid hollows, where dead moss becomes a black sponge, fungal networks may form thin sheets that glisten under the eye when disturbed. There is no great forest floor here, no thick humus, no long cycle of fallen leaves. What exists instead is a minimal decomposition, a slow rot of small things, a fungal patience in wet dust.

The bowl also has its travelers, but not animals.

Life moves through it by wind, water, gravity, and fragmentation. A cloud of spores rises when a dry moss mat is disturbed. A gust carries lichen dust across the ledges. Water flushes tiny fragments of algae from one pool to another. A falling stone scatters moss into new cracks. A fern fragment wedges itself into a moist seam of ash. A spore enters a crevice too dark for anything larger and waits through frost, through steam, through silence.

The inhabitants are not settled.

There is no permanent home. There is no fixed territory. A cushion of moss on one boulder may die when that boulder slides into a hot vent. A lichen crust may be buried under sulfur dust one day and reappear after a wash of rain. An algal film may vanish when a pool evaporates and return when water seeps back through the same channel. A fungal thread may occupy a fragment of dead plant, then be lost when the fragment breaks apart and blows away as powder.

And yet the place is never bare.

If one surface is destroyed, another becomes exposed. If one patch dries, another is soaked. If one crack seals, another opens. The bowl’s inhabitants do not dominate the landscape. They colonize its wounds. They grow in the aftermath of collapse. They take possession of instability and make it habitable.

The mosses are the first to reach into new ash. The lichens claim the driest ledges. Algae stain the pools. Fungi inhabit what has died. Ferns exploit the hidden seams. Microbes make chemistry into life. They are not separate kingdoms here so much as layers of the same tenacious presence.

A boulder in the bowl is never merely a boulder.

Its north face is cold and wet, black with moss, beaded with condensation, sometimes streaked with ice. Its south face is drier, covered in pale lichen, salt bloom, cracked mineral skin. Its underside, where it rests against the ground, is damp, tangled with threads of growth, dust, spores, tiny roots-like anchors. Near its top, a crust of lichens may break away when a drop of water falls from a ledge above. At its base, a thin pool may form after a storm, then shrink, darken, vanish, leaving behind a ring of dead algae and fine silt. The boulder is an ecology because it is a surface, and the surfaces of this place are crowded with life.

A crack is a corridor for organisms.

It holds moisture longer than the exposed stone. It shelters spores from the wind. It collects ash. It hides tiny white fungal threads and green films of algae. Sometimes, in the deepest fissure, there is just enough heat from below and just enough coolness from above to make a microclimate where ferns unfurl. A crack in this bowl is not emptiness. It is a niche, a vault, a nursery, a tomb.

A vent is not alive, but life clusters near it.

At the hot center, nothing survives. Moss is scorched. Algae dies. Lichen cracks. Ferns shrivel. But beyond the direct heat, where steam has cooled into moisture and minerals have been carried into the air, the surroundings can become rich with growth. A vent can create a warm wet wall, a basin of condensed water, a sulfur-stained margin where microbial mats flourish. It can make a patch of ground habitable simply by melting ice, releasing steam, and keeping the air from freezing.

The vent may change. A shift in pressure, a collapse, a new crack, and the vent’s steam moves. The mossy place becomes dry and cold. The scorched place becomes wet and warm. Growth retreats, advances, dies, begins again. The inhabitants do not remember. They simply respond.

There is no food web as animals know it.

Nothing chases. Nothing hunts. Nothing grazes. Nothing burrows to eat. There is no population of predators, no cycle of mouths. The bowl lacks the restless consumption that defines most ecosystems. There is only a slow circulation between mineral and life: water dissolves sulfur, iron, silica, salt; algae and moss and lichens take in what they can; organisms die; fragments fall into cracks; fungi break down what has died; ash and mineral crust absorb what remains; rain carries it downward; steam lifts water into the air; frost seals and releases; collapse exposes new surfaces; growth reattaches.

It is a quiet metabolism without bodies.

A human might listen too long and begin to imagine voices in the moss. That is not because anything is hiding there. It is because the water draining through a saturated mat can sound like whispering, because steam under ash can crackle like a small fire, because frost can snap against stone, because wind can press through basalt columns and make a hollow tone. The place is full of sounds that suggest life because life is present, but the sounds themselves are not calls.

If one tried to describe the inhabitants as if they were a community, one might say this:

The mosses are the dwellers of wet stone.  
The lichens are the dwellers of dry stone.  
The algae are the dwellers of temporary water.  
The ferns are the dwellers of hidden moisture.  
The fungi are the dwellers of dead things.  
The microbes are the dwellers of chemistry itself.  
The spores are the travelers without bodies.

None of them is an animal. None of them is human. None of them is made. None of them builds a thing that remains. They do not cut stone. They do not channel water permanently. They do not shape a shelter. They do not make tools. They do not leave roads or walls or markers. The bowl remains wild not because it is untouched, but because its touchers cannot hold.

And yet they change it.

Moss traps water and slows erosion. Lichen begins to make soil where none exists. Algae contributes organic matter to shallow pools. Fern roots hold fragments in place for a while. Fungi break down dead tissue and release minerals. Microbes transform sulfur and iron into crusts and colors. The living forms are small, but they are not powerless. They are the surface’s slow agents, writing and rewriting the stone with growth, death, decay, and patience.

They do not make a stable world. They exploit instability. They are the first things to return after a collapse. They are the last things to die when a surface changes. They turn catastrophe into substrate. A fallen boulder is not the end of life here; it is a new opportunity, a fresh surface, a crack to hold water, a place for moss to grip.

The bowl’s inhabitants are not beautiful because they are graceful. They are beautiful because they are dense, tenacious, and almost impossible to remove. They are the color of black-green in steam-light, the gray-white of lichen on young stone, the brown stain of algae in sulfur water, the pale curl of a dead fern frond, the faint shine of a fungal thread in damp ash.

They live where no animal would stay, because they are not asking to stay. They are asking to hold.

There is no grand creature at the center of this place. No guardian beast. No hidden tribe. No ancient forest spirit. No bird circling the cloud. No wolf on the rim. No deer stepping over a fallen column. No bear tearing at a steam-warmed boulder. No human fire. No path. No shelter. No footprint. No hand. No voice.

The bowl belongs to surfaces and spores.

It is inhabited by things that grow by taking, things that die and become dust, things that wait in dryness, things that revive with water, things that colonize by breaking apart. It is inhabited by life without appetite, life without movement of its own, life that survives by becoming as close to the stone as possible without becoming stone.

If one stands at the edge and looks carefully, the inhabitants reveal themselves not as creatures but as textures.

The soft black cushion of moss over pumice.  
The crusty white ring of lichen on a ledge.  
The green film in a warm pool.  
The brown flakes of algae at a waterline.  
The pale threads in damp ash.  
The small fronds in a wet crack.  
The fine dust of spores lifted by a gust.

They are not waiting to be seen. They are not performing. They are simply continuing in the place they have made for themselves out of collapse, steam, frost, mineral, and time.

The bowl has no permanent people. It has no stable homes. It has no creatures that walk, sing, hunt, or mourn. It has only a community of holdfasts and films, a society of spores and fragments, a dense and silent life that lives by moisture, by stone, by the constant changing of surface into surface.

And this is enough for the bowl.

It is enough that moss can drink fog.  
It is enough that lichen can color a fresh crack.  
It is enough that algae can bloom in a pool that lasts an hour.  
It is enough that a fern can appear in a seep no animal will touch.  
It is enough that fungal threads can rot a fragment of dead growth into mineral dust.  
It is enough that spores can wait in dry ash until the next rain returns.

The inhabitants of the bowl are not hidden.

They are there on every ledge, in every hollow, beneath every collapse, above every vent, in the dark water, in the wet stone, in the cold wind, in the steam. They are the mosses and lichens, the algae and fungi, the ferns and microorganisms, the spores and fragments, the small living films that make the landscape thick with process rather than bodies.

They live there without leaving tracks.

That is the strangeness and the completeness of the place: it is full of life, but it is not full of creatures. It is crowded with growth, but not with movement. It is loud with industry, but not with voices. It is a bowl where the living do not wander, where the mountain is the only animal, and where the only footprints are the shapes that water, steam, and stone make when they pass over moss, over lichen, over salt, over glass, over the endless young face of the rock.
