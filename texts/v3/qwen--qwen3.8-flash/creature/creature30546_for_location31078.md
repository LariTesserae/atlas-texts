What lives there cannot be introduced with a single name, because it is less a species than a way of filling a hollow.

If you mean “who,” the answer is plural: a parliament of small, armored, dry-bodied things, each one doing a narrow and necessary task, each one dependent on the others in ways no outside observer would easily see. If you mean “what,” the answer is larger and stranger: a sealed industrial ecology, a community of animals, minerals, engines, and slow chemical filaments that has become so tightly integrated it no longer respects the boundary between life and artifact.

No people live there.

There are no mammals, no birds, no reptiles, no tall green things, no insects recognizable in the ordinary sense. There is nothing soft. Nothing wet. Nothing that could survive being left out in ordinary air for long, because ordinary air carries the ghost of dampness, and this chamber would not permit it.

What lives there is hard, pale, jointed, tapping, scraping, sealing, consuming, molting, storing, and rebuilding.

The first resident, if one can call it a resident at all, is the lace.

It grows everywhere, though “grows” is too generous a word. It spreads. It accumulates. It branches in fine crystalline threads across the floor and walls, in pale filaments that look fungal but are not fungal, because there is no moisture to support anything so ordinary as a fungus. It is closer to chemistry that has learned to behave like a body. It metabolises dust, iron particles, trace vapour, and the mineral skins left behind by the lives above it. It consumes no light. It consumes no rain. It consumes the residue of dryness.

This lace is the chamber’s oldest skin.

In some places it is white as frost. In others it is rust-brown or blackened by iron. It forms slow branching maps across the floor, filling cracks where older cracks have collapsed. When heat pulses through the chamber, it contracts minutely, a withdrawal so slight that only the smallest creatures notice it. When a vibration passes through the floor—a footfall, a piston, the snap of a predator’s ridge—it transmits that tremor outward like a signal running through glass.

The animals live on the lace as much as among it.

The salt-walkers are the first thing one notices, if “notice” is the right word for something so densely present. There are hundreds of them on any given panel of floor, thousands in the larger hollows. They are no larger than coins, with shells of white carbonate that look as though they were carved from dried milk. They have six legs, slender and segmented, tipped with tiny knobs of calcium. When they move, their steps make a faint pebble-rattle against the floor, and when many move at once, the sound becomes a hiss of granular rain.

They do not eat in any way a surface-dwelling animal would recognize.

They scrape.

They taste.

They grind.

Their mouths are low, hard beaks, adapted to consume the mineral lace, the dust gathered in cracks, the shed skins of other creatures, the fine grit deposited by machines. They ingest powder and excrete powder. Their bodies are so efficient at waterlessness that they seem to have made a virtue of absence. They do not drool. They do not sweat. They do not leave damp tracks. If one turns one over—gently, carefully, with fingers that will be bitten—the underside is a plate of dry ridges, spiracles, and folding joints, like a mechanism made from shell.

The salt-walkers are grazers, yes, but also builders.

They carry fragments of shell back to crevices. They pack calcium dust into narrow gaps. They press molt skins over eggs to insulate them from the shifting dryness. Their shells are not discarded when they grow; they are taken. A salt-walker sheds its outer casing, and within seconds pale cleaners come to examine it. If the shed is suitable, it is moved to the back wall, where it becomes part of the ossuary-mosaic, a growing stratum of old bodies that is not a cemetery but a wall in progress.

Their eggs are the bead-like clusters found in corners.

Translucent, faintly amber, pulsing with something too slow to call life, perhaps only heat moving through fluid-free tissues. The eggs are laid in sheltered depressions, sometimes bolted to metal fragments, sometimes sealed in resin near engine housings. When the chamber’s dry breath shifts, when the vents warm and the upper panels cool, the eggs respond. They do not hatch on a season. They hatch on temperature, vibration, the rhythm of piston-clicks. A pulse runs through the floor. Three soft crystalline pops. The membranes split. Tiny white grubs spill out, blind, jointed, and already hungry.

They eat the membrane.

They eat the shell fragments beneath them.

They crawl into nearby powder and roll until their outer skins harden, becoming miniature salt-walkers before the day’s temperature cycle ends.

On the upper walls live the scrapers.

They are pale, many-limbed, flatter than the salt-walkers, with broad undersides that clamp to the stone as though they are parts of the wall until they move. They have no eyes. Their heads are ridged, fanlike organs of sensation, tasting air pressure, surface warmth, chemical traces left by other lives. They scrape mineral crusts from stone with grinding proboscides that wear long, polished trails wherever they pass. To an intruder, those trails look like scratches made by tiny tools. In truth, they are feeding marks.

The scrapers are important because they produce fine mineral flour.

The flour drifts into troughs. It falls into the resin mix. It accumulates near vents, where salt-walkers and pupae feed. It is swept by the lid-like creatures into sloped trays beside the engines. In this way, the upper walls support the lower floor. A scraper dying in place becomes a white tile on the surface; another scraper will later consume it, or a cleaner will remove its soft interior and leave the shell to be incorporated into a wall.

The lid-creatures are among the strangest inhabitants.

They live on the machines.

Not “near” them. Not “beside” them. On them.

They are flattened, disc-shaped organisms, sometimes no larger than a thumbnail, sometimes broad as a hand, with undersides that seal against warm metal and ceramic. They absorb heat through their ventral plates and vent it through tiny spiracles along their rims. When the engine below them overheats, they darken. When the chamber cools, they open. When the piston changes rhythm, they click in sympathy, as though they are living washers in the machine’s anatomy.

Some of them are old.

Some of them have been attached to the same gear housing for so long that their shells have fused with mineral deposits, becoming part of the apparatus. When a new engine starts, lid-creatures are already waiting at the seams. When an engine fails, they leave its housing and gather where another rhythm begins. They are not servants, but they behave as though the chamber’s machines were organs to be regulated.

The resin-lickers are small enough to be overlooked.

They crawl across the translucent panels high in the walls, across the upper shafts, across the old frames where ordinary light filters down. They are fingernail-sized, soft-edged, but still plated—everything here has armor of some kind, even at the smallest scale. Their bodies are pale amber near the ventral glands and grey at the shell rims. They move slowly, deliberately, over the panels, and as they move they exhale a cloud of sticky molecules so fine that they seem invisible.

The molecules settle.

They collect dust.

They bind mineral fragments.

Over time, this builds a skin over the walls. The skin hardens. It becomes enamel-like in some places, flaky in others, and it has a faint sweetness, not because there is a flower, not because there is resin from sap, but because the resin-lickers themselves produce it. Their exhalations are the chamber’s perfume: scorched salt, warm dust, chalk, and a thin ghost of sweetness.

This resin-skin is then scraped away.

It is carried into troughs.

It is heated by friction, pressed between stone plates, worked by tiny jaws. It becomes a sealant. It becomes glue. It becomes the amber-like substance that protects eggs, mends cracked shells, bolts metal fragments to the floor, and coats vulnerable surfaces against the relentless dryness. Nothing in the chamber is wasted. Even the breath of the resin-licker becomes architecture.

The cleaners arrive when death happens.

They are fast, pale, and terrible in miniature. Their bodies are narrow, their legs long, their jaws reduced to two sharp tweezers that click and snip. They do not tear flesh as mammals tear flesh. They remove tissue with absurd precision. They pick a carcass clean within minutes, extracting every filament, every gland, every dry membrane. What remains is shell. Shell is not waste. Shell is building material. The cleaners carry shell fragments away, often to the ossuary-mosaic, where they are fitted into the chamber’s endless construction.

Sometimes a cleaner works too long on a predator’s kill.

Then the predator snaps.

The crack closes.

The cleaner vanishes into the gap.

The chamber does not pause. The piston rings once. The resin-lickers resume. A new bead of amber seals the place where the crack had widened.

The predators are the most difficult inhabitants to see, because they are not creatures of open space. They are creatures of thresholds.

They wait in the seams between floor plates, in the dark gutters beneath old machinery, behind curved ceramic beads and the hooks of blackened wire. They are flat, spined, pale, quick. They have no obvious eyes. Their heads are ridged with fine sensory threads, twitching in the dry air like harp strings tuned to heat, vibration, and mineral taste. They know the chamber through motion, through the pressure of footsteps, through the faint chemical signature of prey approaching a vent.

When a salt-walker passes too close to their seam, the ridge snaps.

A flat mouth opens.

Something is seized, pulled into the gap, and dragged downward with a speed too fast to track. Then there is only a click, and the gap closes, and the prey is gone. The floor continues to teem. The machine continues to hum. No alarm is raised unless the rhythm of the kill disturbs it.

The predators exploit the machines as much as the living.

Some wait near vents where the mineral fibers chirp, knowing that small creatures will be drawn upward by vibration. Some imitate the clicking of the piston, luring curious juveniles into the cracks. Some hide in the thermal shadows of engines, where the lid-creatures gather heat. They are patient because the chamber is patient. They are dry because everything here must be dry.

There are the wire-legged sorters, too.

They are among the most delicate inhabitants. Their legs are long and thin as needle-work, their bodies small and armored, their movements almost absurdly careful in a place of such density. They roam among the cages and broken frames, among the old pipes and collapsed trays. They do not build in the way animals build above. They assemble.

They move a fragment of ceramic into a gap.

They bend a wire hook into place.

They carry a grain of calcium to seal a seam.

They reassemble old debris into crude shelters.

A nesting colony may form around a blackened wire hook. A predator-ridge may wait in the curl of a ceramic bead. A metal plate may become a shield-wall, bolted to the floor by layers of secreted calcium. The sorters do not worship these relics. They do not repair them to any original design. They simply use them, because in this world there is no waste, no forgotten object, no material that has lost its purpose.

The relics are old.

Some of them are older than the animals.

There is a plate of dull metal pitted and rounded at the edges. There is a bead drilled through at both ends. There is a strip of wire bent into a rough hook. None of the chamber’s current inhabitants knows what they were for. They may once have been tools. They may once have been parts of a larger machine. They may be markers from a vanished industry that made the pocket before the pocket learned to live on its own.

The inhabitants treat them as terrain.

That is the most human thing about them, perhaps, or the most non-human: they have no myth, no reverence, no nostalgia. They take what is left and put it to use. A shell wall becomes a nest. A broken cage becomes a nursery. A dead engine becomes a thermal ridge. The chamber does not remember its makers. It remembers only its rhythms.
