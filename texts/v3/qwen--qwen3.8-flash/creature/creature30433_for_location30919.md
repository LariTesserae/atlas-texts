What lives there is not a fauna.

If one descends from that thin gray-green water into the blacker green below, the first instinct will be to look for mouths and fins and legs, for something that hunts or feeds or flees. There is nothing of that kind. The fields have no grazing, the curtains of fiber have no parasites, the hollows have no nests. But if one continues to watch the seams, the joints, the rounded tiles, the rope-grooves worn into stone, the clamps gone pale and green with long corrosion, the idea that the place is empty begins to loosen. It is not empty. It is not quiet. The quiet is merely too slow for impatient listening.

What lives there is a body of living repairs.

Not repairs as if something recently broke and must be fixed. Repairs as a mode of being. The first inhabitants, if “first” can mean anything in a landscape that has spent more time being made than being abandoned, are neither born nor hatched nor seeded from soil. They are set. They are arranged. They are accreted. They begin as intention left over in material: a caulked gap, a band of tar, a metal staple, a depression worn by rope, a shallow channel scored by repeated working. Something made, then left in place, then overgrown, then worked on by currents, then folded again by fiber, then sealed by mineral dust. Out of that sequence, and with the right chemistry and the right stillness, a being begins to exist.

The place has no animals. The law of it excludes animal-ness. What exists instead is a plant-microbial-ceramic continuum: things that grow, things that coat, things that bind, things that absorb, things that hold current, things that glow when the water changes. These are not organisms in the sense of one body walking or swimming from another body. They are organisms in the sense of a pattern of organization that continues to maintain itself by adding material, excluding material, sealing cracks, braiding fibers, and reading chemical gradients. They are alive because they persist, and they persist because they are continually remade.

The smallest recognizable inhabitant is not large. It is, at first, little more than a patch of growth that has learned to prefer its own edges. On a rounded tile, a pale olive mat spreads in a broad strap. Nearby, a darker mat grows over a joint. Both are, individually, just growth. But when the current passes over them in the long season of winter, when the mineral dust thickens, when the water cools into the lower terraces, the two patches do not merely coexist. One begins to grow over the other with a strange regularity. It forms a rim. The rim channels water. The channeled water carries more chemical nourishment into the second patch. The second patch becomes denser, then darker, then slightly domed, like a cushion. Between them, the joint widens by decay in some places and narrows by mineral addition in others. The seam becomes a boundary. The boundary becomes a body.

That is what one might call a seamling, though the name is only a convenience. A seamling has no face, no brain-case, no limbs, no obvious mouth. It is a living knot of material relationships. Its core may be a fired tile, partly softened by time, partly fused with lime-rich clay. Around this core, layers of filamentous growth build and decay, thicken and thin, bind and loosen. It is often covered with a felt of moss-analogues, dark and velvet-like, but the covering is not decoration. It is a skin, a breathing surface, a place where bacteria exchange minerals with the water. Beneath the felt, fine braided fibers spread through gaps and along old seams. These fibers do not carry blood. They carry currents of chemical exchange: calcium, iron, sulfur compounds, fragments of dissolved clay, particles of copper, suspended mineral dust. The being metabolizes by drawing water through itself, not from outside into inside, but through its own layered architecture.

If one disturbs such a seamling with a slow passing current, the bacterial mat may brighten at its center, a dull metallic glow like bruised metal, then fade. This is not a reaction of fear. There is nothing like fear. It is a chemical disclosure: the disturbance changes the oxygen and mineral balance, and the glow follows. The being reveals itself as a system with gradients. It has a “front” and a “back” only because one side receives more current, and the other side grows into the shelter of a neighbor. It may lean, thicken, or flatten in response to the pressure of water passing over the shelf. Its growth can appear directional, as if it is looking toward something. But what it looks toward is not a thing with eyes. It looks toward favorable conditions: warmer venting, cooler seepage, better mineral balance, the shelter of a larger formation.

The first law of the place governs all of this. Nothing is born without being made.

A new being does not begin with an egg, a spore, a seed, a birth-mark, or even a clear separation from parent and child. It begins with a prepared place. A mature formation may, through the slow pressure of its own body, press a block into a gap. It may extend a braid of fiber over an empty joint. It may trap a band of lime-rich mud and then seal that band with another growth, creating a chamber. It may leave behind a caulk of tar from an older construction, and that tar, in the cold water, becomes a surface where chemiluminescent mats attach. The place must have intention in it, or at least the ghost of intention. The seamlings do not arise from raw nature. Raw nature is absent. They arise from making that has continued too long to be called architecture.

Some beings are more obviously made than others. The clampborn are one of the most common forms. They begin around metal staples, the kind driven into tile and stone to hold layers together. After centuries under water, the iron and copper alloys corrode, swell, flake, and form green-black halos. In those halos, mineral-rich water becomes especially useful to certain filamentous growths. The growth begins as a fuzzy rind around the clamp. Then it thickens into a collar. The collar catches dust, then fibers, then smaller bits of broken tile. The being develops a hard inner lattice of corroded metal and ceramic dust, and a soft outer mantle of growth. To an observer, it resembles a stone stump wearing a collar of velvet and braided moss. If the clamp remains strong enough, it acts like a spine, or a node, or a hinge. If the water changes, the whole structure can shift by a degree or two, not by muscle but by contraction of saturated fibers and by gradual rearrangement of mineral load. Clampborn are not fast. They do not hunt. They do not gather. They simply set themselves in favorable joints and grow with terrible patience.

Other beings are less architectural, more vegetal, more like a field that has discovered boundaries. In shallow hollows, broad fields of pale algae grow in long ribbons. These ribbons can merge into curtains hanging from the edges of collapsed terraces. When the current is steady, the curtains sway as if in weather. When the water turns cold and thick in deeper depressions, the curtains become stiffer and more translucent. Within certain zones, the field does not grow uniformly. Patches become denser, darker, layered like felt. These patches are not individual bodies in the strict sense, but neither are they mere clumps. They have continuity. A field-patch may maintain its pattern for centuries, expanding by one millimeter a year, contracting by another millimeter when a vent closes, retaining a shape despite replacement of all the material at its outer edges. One might say that in this place, a field can be an individual if the field has edges.

The deepest depressions, where the water becomes black-green and nearly still, are home to the long weavers. These are beings formed from tough, narrow leaves and braided ropes of fiber. They grow from the mouths of fissures where mineral-rich seepage rises. Their bodies are not compact. They are distributed. A long weaver may consist of dozens of strands, each strand anchored in a different crack or joint, then gathered into a central braid that rises and falls with the faint water movement. It has no obvious head. It has a center only because certain strands carry more chemical traffic than others. If a strand is lost, the weaver does not die at once. It may continue as a frayed cluster of filaments for years, feeding itself through remaining anchors. If enough strands survive, it may grow new ones, reaching out like fibers of living rope.

Around vents and seams where water exhales from below, growth becomes brighter, whiter at the center, darker at the edges, radiating outward in fan-shaped colonies. These are the vent-fans. They are among the most beautiful things in the place, but beauty here is not incidental. The whiteness is not innocence; it is concentration. The fans glow faintly because they are saturated with chemiluminescent microbes. They live on chemistry, and their beauty is a record of chemistry. When a vent strengthens, the fan brightens and expands. When the vent cools, the edges turn green-black, then gray, then slowly lose structure. A vent-fan does not move away from death. It remains where it was made and dies into a paler stone. Other fans grow around its remnants, sealing its body into the architecture of a future mound.

There are also dust-collectors, though the word suggests activity that might be misleading. A dust-collector is a surface shaped by slow currents so that mineral particulates settle into its folds. These surfaces may look like soft hills covered with gray-green moss, or like broad lips around a joint, or like the flattened top of an old block that has been overgrown until it resembles a cushion of dust. The dust-collector does not chase dust. The water brings dust, and the structure catches it. If the catch is good, the dust binds with bacterial mats and becomes part of the body. A good dust-collector can grow thick enough to change the flow over it, which then makes it better at catching dust. It is a being made by a feedback, a self-shaping trap, except that no prey enters. What enters is material.

The place has no appetite, but it has selection. A growth does not need hunger if it can favor the conditions that sustain itself. It can seal over a crack that brings too much poison, widen a channel that brings nourishment, pull itself down against a cold pocket of water, or grow away from another growth that blocks its current. In the absence of predators and grazers, the drama of life here is not chase and capture, but boundary and chemistry. The beings compete without violence. One may shade another with an overgrown filament mat. Another may release a chemical film that discourages neighboring growth from spreading. A third may simply grow faster where minerals seep richer. They push, dissolve, accrete, exclude, enclose, and merge. Their struggles are patient and invisible, like the movement of stone under pressure, except that stone here is alive with bacterial skin and woven fibers.

They communicate, if “communicate” can be stretched to include any exchange of information. Their communication is not sound. Sound is nearly useless in a world of soft, cold, heavy water. Their communication is chemical, mechanical, and optical in the slowest possible sense. A being changes the water around itself: it releases mineral compounds, alters oxygen levels, leaves suspended particles in patterns. Another being reads that alteration by the way its own fibers contract, its own mats shift color, its own growth thickens in certain directions. They can also signal by glow. Disturbed chemiluminescent mats flash or fade. If one seamling presses against another, or if a current forces water through the first body and into the second, the second may brighten at the point of contact. That is not a greeting. It is a record: the first is here, this way, with this chemistry, at this time. It is a sentence made of pressure and glow.

Their “language,” if one insists on that word, is built of repairs. A being’s body contains its history as visible layers. The oldest layer may be tile. Above it, a seal of clay. Above that, a band of tar. Above that, bacterial mat, then filaments, then a braid of fiber, then dust, then newer growth. The sequence matters. A clamp inserted from one side and grown over from the other is not the same as a braid threaded through an empty joint and then sealed. The placement of fragments tells where the current has been, whether mineral venting rose, whether a neighboring structure shifted, whether the water cooled or warmed, whether some earlier being dissolved into dust and became food for the next. A mature seamling is a manuscript of materials. The oldest ones are not necessarily the largest, but they are the richest. Their bodies are palimpsests: a tile from one age, a clamp from another, a mat of bacteria that has replaced itself thousands of times, a braid made of fragments from a dozen vanished relatives.

The beings do not reproduce by making offspring separate from themselves as a parent might. Reproduction is a form of construction, sometimes deliberate, sometimes accidental. A mature weaver may extend a strand into an open gap where the current is favorable. That strand may anchor, thicken, gather dust, develop its own rim of mineral exchange, and eventually become an individual. But the boundary between parent and new being remains blurred. The strand may continue to share material flow with the parent for centuries. Or it may be sealed off by a new growth, cut by a change in pressure, enclosed by mineral deposit. Only then does it become sufficiently distinct to be called another being. Other beings may fragment during slow collapse, then the fragments attach to nearby joints and begin again.
