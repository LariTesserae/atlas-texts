Who lives there? Mostly people, though the basin nearly erases them at first. It is not empty because it lacks inhabitants; it is empty because the inhabitants have learned to live at the level of the pipes. They are not crowds in the ordinary sense. They do not gather in public squares. They do not shout down wet streets like market folk or line the railings to watch things pass. They are fewer than four hundred, perhaps—just enough to keep the channels clear, the pumps oiled, the glass wiped, the joints checked, the ledgers marked, and the salt lines traced with chalk. They are spread through the basin the way water is spread over stone: thinly, constantly, and never in one place long enough to make it feel occupied until you begin to notice the signs.

There are small wet footprints by the tram rails. A pair of boots drying in the heat beside a vent. A handrail polished bright where many palms have passed. A kettle warming on a pipe joint. A curtain of oiled cloth stirred by steam near a doorway. A child’s handprint left in the dust on a low wall before the moisture softens it and makes it disappear. If you stand long enough, the place fills with people, though they are never as dramatic as the crabs or the birds. They are the thing that makes the basin a town and not only a machine.

Their bodies carry the climate. Their skin is seldom dry. Their hair stands damp around the neck and temples, and it frizzes in the steam regardless of how often it is cut. Their hands are calloused not in one broad place but along all the edges, the palms and fingertips worn thick by metal. Many have small old burns near the wrists or ankles from steam leaks, pale patches where skin healed without ever seeing sun. Their eyes tend to be red-rimmed, not because they cry, but because the air is hot, wet, and full of fine salt. Some wear tinted goggles made from cracked glass and rubber, because the worklights shine off wet metal for hours. Their faces look soft where the heat swells the skin, and hard where oil, iron, and salt have darkened it.

They dress for moisture. Most wear clothes that began as dark canvas, denim, or wool and are now the color of the basin: rust-brown, slate-gray, tar-black, brick-dust red. Boots are essential—steel-toed, rubber-shouldered, sometimes patched with melted tar. The uppers are often wrapped in strips of old belt or wire so they hold firm on wet concrete. Gloves are fingerless or split at the thumbs, so hands can grip wrenches and keys without slipping. Coats are waxed cloth or oilskin, hooded, with cuffs that bind tight and hems that drip. In the pump hall, people sometimes work shirtless or in undershirts despite the wet, because the heat from the iron is enough to cook the air, but at the edges of the basin, in the cooler marshy corners, the same people can be shivering and damp all at once. Their pockets sag with tools: brass keys, wire, spare gaskets, chalk, oil-stained rags, bits of fish, and sometimes a dried saltgrass seed head they keep for no reason at all.

They are not owners of the basin so much as its tenders. Their work is maintenance taken to the point of devotion. A pump hall does not need builders; it needs people who will keep it from changing. The basin’s residents are very good at this. Some specialize by sound: a “listen-runner” is someone who lies on the wet floor near the machines and places an ear to the plates, or presses a long screwdriver to the casing and hears whether a bearing is beginning to speak wrong. Some are packers, who stuff oil-soaked rope and flax into gland joints to keep steam from escaping. Some are brine-walkers, who skim algae from the canals before it clogs the weirs. Some are salt-gaugers, who measure the white lines on stone and note when they have moved by the width of a fingernail. Some keep the tramway joints greased, some clean the glass roof from catwalks, some trim rust from railing posts with a wire brush so slow and regular that the metal seems to be polished by patience alone.

They do not build new structures. That is one of their most striking habits. If a concrete apron breaks, they pour a patch to match its shape. If an iron bracket cracks, they remake it to the same thickness and bolt it into the same place. If a dwelling wall leaks, they tar it from outside and move a bucket. If a pipe needs replacing, the new section comes from stock, painted to disappear into the old. Expansion is not their concern. The basin already fits. A family might take over the same three rooms that a previous family occupied for twenty years, scrape out the salt, hang a new curtain, and leave the floorboards alone. The town does not grow outward. It maintains inward.

The streets—if they can be called streets—are never silent. The people speak as little as possible in the loudest places, because voices get lost and the air feels thicker when someone shouts. In the pump hall, workers often communicate by gesture: thumb to the mouth of a pipe, flat palm lowered to mean “too much steam,” two fingers tapped twice on a gauge face to say “watch this one.” On the walkways outside, people speak in short, flat sentences, not because they are cold, but because there is always enough noise that long speech becomes wasteful. They say what is needed: “The east joint.” “Your turn.” “Mind the plate.” “The birds are back early.”

In the cooler edges, where the pumps become a distant thump and the steam thins, voices sound almost human again. People laugh in doorways, and their laughter is low and quick, as if embarrassed to be too cheerful in a loud place. They have gossip, too: which old man has started sleeping on the gantry again, which child learned to name valves before colors, which woman can taste a failing gasket from across the room, which family boiled their fish wrong. It is ordinary talk, but it is never loud enough to drown the basin’s own voice.

Their homes are plain and damp. They live in single-story dwellings with blackened brick foundations and low roofs of corrugated iron or tar-sealed timber. Doors open inward so the steam cannot press them shut. Floors are raised, or the people simply sleep on platforms. Beds hang in the air from chains or are built on stacked cinder blocks. Clothes are pinned to overhead lines where they never fully dry. Oil lamps burn low in windows. Kettles sit on hot pipe sleeves in kitchen corners, and the water they hold always tastes faintly of iron unless it is filtered through charcoal and basalt, or collected from the cleanest condensation trays.

Fresh water is their only true luxury. The basin is full of water, but it is hot, briny, sulfurous, algae-slicked, and unsafe for drinking unless carefully managed. So people have cisterns, ceramic filters, brass faucets, and collection channels under glass. They measure this water more anxiously than they measure anything else. Children learn not to drink from low puddles or runny seams. Families keep marked cans and jars. Someone in every household spends part of each day checking the fresh supply. In the evenings, when the pumps still thump but the light is soft behind glass, there is a domestic ritual of rinsing hands, warming water for washing, and sharing cups of something boiled from saltgrass, imported leaves, or whatever bitter green can be grown in the pockets of soil near the southern wall.

Food is mostly brought from somewhere beyond, though no one speaks of it as a market or supply chain. It arrives as hard flour, oil, salt, tinned goods, dried beans, seeds, and sacks of tubers. But the basin provides fresh things too, and the people eat those carefully. They catch fish from the canals when the fish move slow in the warmed shallows. They trap crabs in pipe mouths with bits of bait and string. They collect eggs from the ledges beneath the bridges where small birds nest, though not so many as to empty the nests, because everyone knows that the bird-count matters. There are thin gardens in the imported soil: tough saltgrass, pale root crops, some leaves too bitter to eat in quantity, grown in boxes because the ground itself refuses them. Algae is scraped from certain channels and dried into brittle sheets. Everything is salted or smoked over pipe heat. Their kitchens smell of hot oil, iron, fish, and wet stone. They do not complain about it. They have learned that some foods taste better if you eat them standing near a vent.

Children live there as completely as adults. They are born in small rooms where the rhythm of the pumps enters the walls like a second heartbeat. They learn to sleep through thumps and clicks. Before they learn to walk well, they learn to avoid hot pipes and open pits. By the time they are tall enough to lean on railings, they can name most valves by silhouette. Their play is not soft or silent. They race crabs across shallow concrete. They tie fishing line to the railings and dangle scraps. They catch moths with nets made from torn cloth and wire. They count birds perched on the gantries and argue about which heron is standing in which channel. Some of them collect bottle caps, brass shards, salt crystals, and small pieces of colored glass from the office block windows. They make necklaces of wire and dried algae. They climb low walls, but never high ones, because they understand from an early age that falling into a pipe trench in a place like this is not an adventure.

The older children help. They carry oil cans, run messages, hold lamps, count fish in the traps, wipe condensation off gauge faces, and learn to read the salt line. By twelve or fourteen, most can tell if a pump stroke has changed by the smallest fraction, or if a joint is beginning to leak by the new smell in the air. They are not solemn in the way of old monks, but they are serious in the way of people who know that the machine must keep its rhythm. Their pride is not in novelty but in accuracy. A child is praised not for building something new but for doing a familiar thing better than before.

The people are not alone, and they are not even the most numerous inhabitants. The crabs are. They live in the pipe seams, under the railings, beneath the concrete aprons, in the briny hollows by the walls. They click over wet stone with a dry, metallic sound that makes them seem part of the machinery. Children watch them with intense focus, but adults mostly step around them unless a crab blocks a trap inlet or gets into a clean channel. The crabs eat dead things, scrape algae from stone, and clean the edges of water where no human hand reaches. If a crab becomes too bold and pinches a sleeping child, it is returned to the wet side of the basin with more annoyance than cruelty. The town could not be this clean if the crabs were not working.

The fish are quieter but no less real. They live under the narrow canals, pale-sided and soft-bodied, drawn to the warmth. They school where there is no open river and spawn in the protected corners behind iron plates. People eat them, but they are also watched as signs. If the fish vanish too suddenly from a channel, someone checks for a new leak, a too-hot vent, or a change in the salt line. If they gather in great numbers under one bridge, someone leaves a bucket there overnight to see what the catch is. The fish are part of the basin’s balance as surely as the gauges. They are not decorative. They are indicators. They are food. They are one of the things that make the town feel alive rather than merely functional.

Birds claim the upper world of the basin. Herons stand in the channels like sentinels, and the people do not try to move them. Some herons seem to know exactly which ledges are safest, which fish schools are thickest, which bridges give the cleanest sight lines. Their stillness is useful. Children are taught to look at a heron’s feet to see if the water level has changed. Old workers are known to swear by one or another specific bird: the one who always stands by the north wall, the one who never eats from the south drain, the limping heron who has survived for years because it learned to avoid the steam surge. The smaller birds live in the steel ribs above the pump hall, in the gaps where iron meets glass, in the shelter of trusses where no wind reaches. They come back at dusk to the same perches. The people expect this. They count them the way they count fish.

Insects are everywhere, though they are often mistaken for dust until light catches them moving. Moths beat softly against the worklights. Flies cluster on damp brick. Beetles run through saltgrass. Midges breed in condensation pans. These creatures are not welcomed in the kitchens, but they are tolerated in the canals, the ledges, and the margins. They feed the birds and fish. They eat what the machines shed. The people scrape away some, leave others. They know that if the insects vanish entirely, something has become too hot or too toxic, and if they swarm too thickly, there is standing water where it should not be. Even the smallest flies are read by the town.

There is a microbial life too, though no one names it. Algae films spread in green-brown skins across still pools. Iron bacteria leave dark specks on concrete. Sulfurous breath from below feeds strange pale growths in the cracks of the basalt. Salt crystals bloom in the shaded joints like slow white flowers. The people do not let these organisms run wild, but they do not eradicate them entirely either. They manage the boundaries: here scrape the algae away, there let it grow; here wipe the salt until it returns; there leave the iron stain because it protects what is beneath. The basin is not sterile, and the people are not clean in a bright, chemical sense. They are balanced. Their hygiene is practical, not obsessive. They wash with hot water when it is available, but they also understand the value of a thin protective film on skin and metal.

The residents have their own private places, though there are not many. A woman who keeps a jar of dried saltgrass on her sill. An old man who sits by the lock gate and listens to the outside water even though nothing comes in. A boy who has drawn a pump in charcoal on every wall of his bedroom until the plaster is ruined. Two sisters who share a window and hang feathers from it to catch the steam. A foreman who eats breakfast in silence on the high walkway above the pump hall, where the thump is loudest and the heat makes his clothes cling. A heron watcher who has given names to birds and refuses to explain the names to anyone. A child who sleeps with a crab trap because it is the first object that seemed to belong to him.

Their religion, if they have one, is not obvious. They do not pray loudly. They touch a railing when they pass it, not as superstition so much as habit, the way people touch a doorframe for luck. When a pump stroke changes in a way that frightens everyone, they stand still for a long moment and listen as if waiting for a diagnosis, not a miracle. If the sound returns, someone will mutter a word for relief. If the sound fails to return, they move with sudden precision and do not speak for hours. There is no priesthood of iron, no temple to the valve, no formal worship of steam. But there is reverence, all the same. They respect the machine as one respects something that keeps one alive without asking permission.

What they fear is not collapse. Collapse seems too large, too dramatic, too final. Their fear is of drift. They fear a salt line moving one inch too far. A channel deepening by a finger-width. A gauge that always reads one notch too high. A bird that stops returning to its ledge. A joint that hisses where it used to hum. They fear not sudden violence but the smallest change that suggests the basin is beginning to become something else. That is why their ledgers are so meticulous. That is why every child learns to compare old marks and new marks. That is why an old repairman’s greatest compliment is not “you built well” but “you kept it right.”

Their humor is dry and brief, because long speeches do not travel through steam and thump. They joke about the way everything smells of metal and salt, about the crabs acting as if they own the town, about the herons behaving like judges, about the way one’s boots never dry no matter how long they hang. They tell stories not about great escapes but about small catches: the time a man noticed the pump had changed rhythm before anyone else, the time a child named a fish that no one thought would come back, the time the whole town stood at the edge of a channel and watched the salt line drop by one hair-width and not rise again. These stories are repeated often enough to become rituals. They are told in a lowered voice, in the dry spots, when the machines are not too loud.

The people do not think of themselves as trapped. The wall is not a prison to them. The unknown beyond is not a dream. They are more concerned with the next valve than with distant horizons. If anything, they treat the outside like weather: real, but not part of the daily discipline. They do not ask what lies beyond because there is so much to keep working. If someone leaves, there is not a farewell crowd. If someone arrives, they are fitted into the existing roster. The basin does not make room for change; it makes room for people who will become part of the rhythm.

In the evenings, the worklights make the place glow like something seen through breath on glass. Steam hangs lower than it should and thickens around the buildings. Shadows pool beneath the iron. The pump hall hums with that same unchanging rhythm, and the water runs with its same thin sound over the same stone. A woman walks home along the wet street, her boots slapping in shallow channels, a coil of rope over one shoulder, a jar of collected condensation in her other hand. A child crouches near a pipe seam watching crabs. A man leans on a railing with a cloth in his pocket and a gauge reading in his memory. A heron lifts from a ledge, folds its wings, and drops into the channel without changing the water’s surface.

What lives there is not one thing. It is a small human community, a machine population, and a dense animal world, all folded into one another so tightly that no boundary between them feels final. The humans need the pumps. The pumps need the humans. The crabs need the channels. The fish need the warmth. The birds need the ledges. The insects need the damp glass. The people need the animals as signs, as food, as companions, and as proof that the basin is still alive rather than merely operating. Together they make a town of iron and salt and steam, where life is not wild in the natural sense but no less abundant for being enclosed.

The place does not grow. It does not progress. It does not become. The people who live there are like the rest of it: organized, maintained, persistent. They are not heroes of a new world. They are caretakers of a balanced one. They wake to the same heat, walk the same wet ground, listen for the same thump, and keep the same small boundaries from moving by even the width of a handprint. And because they do, the basin is never abandoned. It is never ruined. It is never silent. It is alive in the ordinary, mechanical sense: with bodies, routines, hunger, children, habits, birds, crabs, fish, oil, steam, salt, and the sound of water running over stone in a town that does not need to change in order to continue.
