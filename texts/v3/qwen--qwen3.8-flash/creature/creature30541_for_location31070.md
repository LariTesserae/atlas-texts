What lives there is not a population in the ordinary sense. There are bodies that walk, swim, and breathe, yes—but they are separated from the plateau the way a tongue is separated from a mouth. The place is not inhabited by animals; it is inhabited by functions. The few creatures that move across its courts and basins are mobile organs, glandular messengers, living valves, unfinished thoughts of the same body that made the steps, the arches, the cisterns, the weeping rim, and the bright wet hum in the air.

They do not gather, because gathering implies need, and this place has been arranged so finely that need appears only as maintenance. They do not crowd, because their numbers are dictated by the water’s line, by the membrane’s pitch, by the exact count of pores in a living surface. They are few, deliberate, fixed as notes in a chord that neither modulates nor resolves. If one vanishes, another is already beginning somewhere nearby, in a cistern or a seed-pit or the soft fold of a leaf-wall, fully shaped before it is completed.

The plateau itself is the first and largest inhabitant.

It is one continuous organism made to resemble geography. The moss-skin, the hardened lichen plates, the fibrous pavement, the bark-membranes, the calcified edges, the hollow stems, the ridged leaves, the swollen stems that slope like terraces—all of these are parts of a single wet body that has learned to behave as a city. It has no boundary that can be called “ground” rather than “plant.” It has no interior where roots might hide, because the roots are not attached to anything; the roots are the surface, the arch, the basin, the gutter, the wall. It knows things not through eyes or ears alone, but through pressure, mineral taste, the temperature of moisture, the exact delay between one drip and the next. It thinks by gradients. It remembers in bands of calcium, in the width of rings around a tower’s mist, in the particular curve a wall keeps after a thousand years of standing as if it had just remembered how to stand.

Its inhabitants are its differentiated life: those parts of itself that have separated enough to move, to taste, to pulse, to carry, to sing, to repair.

## The Cistern Keepers

The pale legless thing moving through the cisterns is one of the plateau’s most perfect and most unsettling beings.

It has no scales and no ordinary skin. Its body is mostly water held in a translucent shape, like a length of living glass that has been folded into ribs. Those ribs do not support the animal as bone supports a human; they float it, tension it, open and close with a slow hydrostatic intelligence. Along its length are fins, but they are not fins in the fish sense. They are folded membranes, wide and soft as inner petals, edged in a pale calcium fringe. When the water is still, the fins lie flat against its body like a closed book. When it turns in a cistern, the fins spread and the creature appears briefly like a drowned orchid.

It has no face. At one end there is only a vertical mouth-slit and a crown of sensory fronds, each frond a thread of white tissue that tastes the mineral weight of the water, the angle of the surface, the exact position of a droplet. It does not need eyes. It perceives by pressure and by the chemical speech of the ground. If a pool rises by a fraction, it feels the change not as sight but as a question asked by its whole body: *Where should this water go?*

Its function is not to live in water as an animal lives in a lake. Its function is to make the water behave as the plateau requires. It moves through channels like a valve, like a tongue, like a soft hand testing the shape of a cup. In narrow gutters its body becomes ribbon-like and presses against the sides. In broad shallow cisterns it flattens until it looks almost like a leaf floating just below the surface. When a basin fills too high, it coils near an overflow hole and releases or retains water by the exact amount its membranes will allow. When the basin begins to dry, it presses itself against the wall and exudes a stored moisture from its skin, feeding the hollow as if the water it carries is a debt to be paid.

It does not hunt. It does not devour smaller creatures. It feeds by absorption, by drinking mineral dew, by allowing water to pass across its translucent ribs and take from it the salts and sap-borne nourishment it needs. Its metabolism is slow and wet. Its waste is not waste. Anything it excretes becomes part of the surface: a thin calcium film, a hydrophilic coating that makes water cling in perfect beads, or a slick that guides a droplet down a wall until it finds its appointed groove.

The Keepers do not reproduce by mating. They bud when the architecture requires them. In the curve of a newly finished cistern, a pale swelling forms on the rim of the living floor. It grows into a translucent drop-like body, ribs appearing inside it like wet lace. When the cistern’s water reaches the correct level, the creature detaches and enters the pool as if it had always been there. Its first motion is not confusion. It already knows the shape of the water.

If a Keeper is damaged, it is not dragged to the edge or left to rot. The plateau receives it. The water carries it to a shallow basin, where its membranes soften, its ribs dissolve, and its body becomes a bright film on the floor. In a day or a week, new moss grows through the film. The next pool may be shaped slightly differently, and a new Keeper will rise from the rim.

## The Porewalkers

The small beetles crossing the open courts are not insects in the ordinary sense, though they carry the memory of insects in their legs.

They are broad-backed things, no larger than a palm’s width, with legs too many to count at first glance. Their shells are not hardened chitin alone. They are living leaves, flattened and fused, with pale veins running across their backs like river deltas seen from high above. The leaf-shells photosynthesize, but not for growth alone. They use light to regulate their internal moisture, and they release that moisture in tiny measured exhalations as they step across the ground. The effect is a faint click in the pavement each time a leg touches the living skin: not a sharp insect sound, but the sound of a damp seed tapping against wood.

They cross the courts at intervals so exact that their movement looks like clockwork. This is because their metabolism is driven by the ground’s swelling and contracting, by the capillary pulse that runs through the plateau when warmth rises and mist condenses. When the skin of the ground expands by a degree, the Porewalkers emerge from the pores. When the moisture level lowers by a breath, they vanish again. They are not traveling to a destination. They are completing a circuit.

They maintain the surface. The plateau has no dead matter, no falling leaf that fails to find a place, no dust, no drift, no litter. But living systems can still overproduce: a moss film may thicken too much, a pore may narrow, a calcium edge may grow out of tune, a droplet may gather where it should disperse. The Porewalkers correct these small errors. They eat only excess living surface—microscopic buds, overly wet skins, tiny ridges that would interrupt the flow of water. They do not consume the architecture; they prune it, polish it, and repair it.

As they move, they deposit a resinous paste from their hind legs and the underside of their shells. This paste enters the pores of the ground and hardens into a pale lichen-like plate. The plate then becomes part of the pavement, part of the living skin, part of the city’s floor. Thus the Porewalkers are also the architects of the smallest detail. A broad court may look smooth and continuous, but beneath it is a history of passage: the invisible tracks of thousands of small bodies moving in fixed intervals, maintaining the exact porosity of the ground.

They are the plateau’s immune cells and gardeners at the same time. They do not recognize one another as individuals. If two Porewalkers meet, they pass around one another in a curved path that is itself a kind of speech, because their bodies leave a faint chemical line in the moisture. That line may alter the next drip’s route, or cause a nearby membrane to shift its hum, or prompt a cistern Keeper to move one fin nearer to an overflow hole.

Their life span is not measured in years but in completeness. When a Porewalker finishes its circuit and enters a pore, it does not die in the animal way. Its leaf-shell separates, softens, and becomes part of a new plate. Its legs dissolve into fibrous threads. Its body joins the ground, becoming a hidden node in the skin. From that node, other Porewalkers may later emerge, carrying the faint pattern of the one before them.
