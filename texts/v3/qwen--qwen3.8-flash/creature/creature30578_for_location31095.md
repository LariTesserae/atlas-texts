The slow pressure is mostly water, and water has nowhere urgent to go. It sinks through clay dust, wells as damp along the rim, gathers in shallow pans, evaporates, returns. In that slow movement Tallowroot is not a place with inhabitants so much as a body whose organs are roots, fungi, insects, toads, people, and the faint shine of fat on stone.

The chief inhabitant—the one that makes the name necessary—is the tallowroot. It grows in low crowns above the grey floor, each plant spreading like a green hand that has been pressed flat by weather. Its leaves are ridged with dust, stiff at the edges, translucent near the center where the light passes through a faint oily skin. They are green from their living film and grey from the fine granite meal that settles in them. When rain comes, the dust runs in little rivers into the base of each crown. When rain fails, the dust bakes into a paste around the root shoulders.

Below the ground is where the tallowroot becomes extraordinary. Its taproots are pale, swollen, slick, almost the color of marrow. If cut, they do not bleed water so much as weep a pale grease: a tallow made of fats, resins, sugars, water, and mineral salts. The plant stores fat in them the way other plants store starch, sealing the substance in long translucent cells where it can keep through drought and frost alike. It smells faintly of candle smoke, wet stone, and something green that has been kept too long in a jar. In the hottest months the surface roots soften with heat and give off a little of this grease; in the rains the pools take it on and shine like dishes of milk.

The tallowroot is not a simple plant. It is a compact of living and mineral. Its roots host pale fungal threads that carry phosphate and zinc from the worn granite; its leaves carry cyanobacterial skins that fix greenness into the surface; its dust traps lichens that grow in tiny cracks no larger than a fingernail. A mature tallowroot patch is therefore a slow republic. It feeds itself, shelters insects, seals wounds with wax, catches water, makes soil, dies by melting, and from that death feeds a newer generation. The bowl is old enough that no one would call it wilderness anymore. It is a managed thing, but managed without hands, by pressure, dampness, and patience.

When a tallowroot dies, its fat does not simply rot. It hardens in the clay into white or yellow pans. These pans become beds for fungi called candlecaps, which fruit in the damp as thick white stalks with pale-yellow hoods. They smell like rendered fat and cellar air. Their spores are carried by ants, beetles, and toads, but also by wind, because the bowl has no trees to slow it. In time the candlecaps consume the wax and turn it back into fat-soil, a greasy, slightly sweet substrate in which new seedlings can anchor. Thus the root’s body is never finished: it leaks, hardens, molds, melts, and is reborn in a circle that can take decades.

There are other plants too, but they are less decisive. Feather-mosses grow in the damp hollows where seeps darken the granite. Dust-ferns unfurl only after rain, their small fronds the color of lichen, curling back into brown threads when the dry returns. A low grey grass with oily seeds forms cushions in the rim gravel, and on wet mornings it holds dew so long that the whole bowl appears frosted, even when no frost has fallen.
