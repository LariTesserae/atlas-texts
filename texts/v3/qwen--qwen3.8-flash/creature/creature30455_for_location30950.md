There are no inhabitants in the human sense. No village clings to the hummocks, no road cuts the peat, no fence leans against the fog, no fire blackens the basalt. But the place is not empty. It is populated by a dense, layered, slow-moving civilization of small and larger lives, each one fitted into the wet silence so completely that the distinction between animal, plant, mineral, water, and weather seems almost artificial.

What lives there is, first, the land itself, if one can call that an inhabitant. The plateau behaves like a body: it drinks, sweats, filters, breathes, stores, decays, and rebuilds. But it does not live alone. It is inhabited by moss as if moss were the primary architect of the world. Sedge follows, then peat, then fungi, then the water itself threading through them all. Around these foundations move birds, frogs, salamanders, voles, beetles, worms, spiders, small predators, and perhaps the quiet hoofed grazers that cross only where the ground is firm enough to bear them.

If you ask “who lives here,” the answer is: almost everything that can live by staying low, moving softly, and accepting water as the first condition of being.

The most numerous residents are invisible ones. Beneath the moss, inside the peat, along the wet skins of stones, and in the shallow mineral water beneath floating mats, microbial life works without pause. Bacteria and other tiny organisms digest fallen stems, dissolve fibers, release gases, and precipitate iron and sulfur into ochre streaks, dark nodules, and pale mineral crusts. They are the refinery workers of the basin. They make the bog into a place of slow chemistry: carbon separated from living tissue, nutrients released back into water, metals gathered into little stones, dead matter converted into future soil. Their presence is not dramatic. You do not see them, but you see what they leave behind: rust-colored veins on rock, bubbles rising in pools, warm hummocks, gas-smelling hollows, fungal blooms, and the faint mineral taste carried in the fog.

Fungi are also citizens, and perhaps the truest engineers of the terrain. They thread the roots of sedges, form networks through wet clay and peat, spread beneath moss domes, and fruit suddenly along rotting stems. Their pale shelves and translucent caps emerge quietly in the damp mornings, as though the ground is putting on new skin. They eat the dead, bind the living, and make the landscape cohesive. In such a place, fungi are not decoration. They are infrastructure. They help the moss hold water, help the sedge draw nutrients, help the hummock resist the pull of erosion, help the basin become a continuous living mass rather than scattered patches.

The mosses themselves deserve their own name as inhabitants, because they behave less like plants and more like a collective organism. They form deep cushions over basalt, fill hollows, soften edges, and build up peat year by year. They condense fog into droplets, draw water upward by capillary action, and release it slowly when the air warms. They make the ground feel spongy, silent, and alive. Walking across them is like walking on a surface that is still becoming something. Their colors are not bright in the way of tropical green. They are dark green, gray-green, blackish in shade, rust-brown where iron stains them, pale and translucent when newly wetted. In the dawn, each cushion may hold thousands of droplets so evenly that it looks paved with glass beads.

Sedge tussocks are the upright residents of this low world. They rise in dense rounded clumps, narrow-leafed, ribbed, water-beaded, their roots braiding through wet peat and clay. They give the plain its quilted texture. Between them are spaces, but not empty spaces: spaces of floating moss, shallow channels, dark pools, insect tubes, mineral scum, frog eggs, salamander shadows, and trapped air bubbles. Where sedge roots are thick, they form arches and platforms over the water, little living bridges. A creature walking carefully can move from hummock to hummock without ever fully breaking the surface of the mat.

On the higher ridges and better-drained margins, dwarf shrubs grow in tight clusters. They are woolly-leaved, low-branching, dark-eyed with small berries. They are not trees, and they do not stand tall. They live close to the ground, sheltered from wind and fog, their leaves adapted to keep moisture and resist chill. They feed small insects, offer cover to ground birds and mammals, and provide seeds for animals that pass quickly through the damp tangles. Their roots help hold the firmer parts of the terrain, and their fallen leaves feed the peat with new carbon.

The invertebrate life is thick and nearly continuous. In the wet peat, fly larvae twist through root channels. Midges and mosquitoes breed in shallow pools. Beetle larvae move through rotting stems. Aquatic insects crawl through the dark water beneath floating mats, translucent and delicate until they become something else. Earthworms thread through the sponge of dead plant matter. Millipedes pass slowly over moss and fallen leaves. Spiders build small, precise webs between sedge blades at dawn, weighted with droplets that turn them into temporary jewels. When the sun thins the fog, the whole ground seems to glitter with tiny movements.

Some of the most characteristic residents are the small crustaceans of the hummock water lenses. They are too tiny to notice unless you scoop a handful of wet moss and watch it shimmer. They move in the capillary films, in the shallow channels, in the floating pools, feeding on detritus, grazing microbial mats, becoming food for larvae, frogs, birds, and perhaps fish in the deeper channels. They belong to the half-hidden economy of the basin, a population that turns decay into flesh without ceremony.

Amphibians are especially at home here, because the air and ground are so rarely dry. Salamanders with mottled backs and pale bellies hide beneath moss shelves, under stones, inside the wet tunnels left by voles. They move through the peat like soft dark threads. In the shallower water, they lay eggs that cling to submerged fibers, and their young breathe through their skin in the oxygen-poor pools. Frogs live in the margins between moss and water. They do not shout. Their voices are wet, intermittent, swallowed by the fog, as though the damp air has absorbed part of their song before it can travel. Some have long slender legs for leaping from hummock to hummock. Others move like small stones, staying motionless when approached, their skin beaded with water until they seem indistinguishable from wet moss.

Fish, where there are deep enough pools, live beneath the floating mats and the orange-brown mineral films. They are not visible from above unless the fog clears and the water lies still. They feed on larvae, aquatic insects, and the small animals that fall or swim into their channels. Their presence adds another layer of life below the surface: slow darting movement, occasional bubbles, the faint disturbance of moss roots, and the quiet maintenance of the water-food chain.

Birds are the most visible animals, and even they are adapted to the landscape’s restraint. They have long toes for spreading over floating vegetation without breaking through. Their feathers are dense and slightly oily, beaded with water. They move carefully, head tilted, listening for insects in the moss. They are not noisy birds. Their calls are short, soft, and localized. If a wading bird steps across a mat, the sound is little more than a gentle compression of wet fiber. A bird taking flight gives a soft whisper of wings, as if wrapped in cloth. In the drier ridges, small ground birds hop through lichen cushions, eating seeds, spiders, and tiny insects. Their breast feathers are thick and downy, their movements quick but careful. High overhead, rare large silent birds with broad wings and muted brown feathers circle when thermals rise above the basin. They do not cry often. When they do, the sound seems to come from far away, as if the plain is a vast room and their calls are echoing from its edges.

Mammals, too, live in packed numbers, mostly small. Wool-tailed voles move through tunnels beneath the sedge and moss. Their fur is damp at the tips. Their runways aerate the peat, break through dead plant matter, and carry spores, seeds, and water. Their burrows become accidental seed banks and shelters for other creatures. When a vole emerges, it is cautious. Its eyes are dark and wet-looking, its nose quick against the fog. It listens more than it sees, because the moss deadens visual clarity and carries vibration better than distance.

There are larger herbivores, if “larger” can be said in a land of small things. They are goatlike animals with broad hooves, dark eyes, and dense, wool-backed bodies. They graze only on the firmer ridges, where the vegetation is thick enough to resist sinking. Their footprints fill slowly with water after rain. Their droppings appear in neat clusters, feeding moss and attracting insects. They do not roam wildly. They move through the landscape with the same caution the ground demands: low-headed, deliberate, careful of hidden wet places. They are shaped by the marsh as much as they shape it.

Predators live here lightly. Stoat-like mammals slip between roots and moss domes, hunting voles, birds’ eggs, and ground-dwelling animals. Foxes with pale bellies and wide paws move through the fog, their paws spreading over the spongy ground, their hearing tuned to tiny sounds beneath the vegetation. Small raptors drop onto the marsh without a sound. Even the predators are quiet. They conserve their noise, as if loudness were dangerous in a place so packed with sensitive life. In such a landscape, a sudden call or crash might reveal not only prey but predators, competitors, weather, and the animal’s own vulnerability. So they hunt by patience, stillness, and soft movement.

What lives there also includes processes that behave like lives. The fog moves across hummocks like a slow animal. Steam vents hiss in the cold mornings, not dramatic geysers but breathing points in the plateau’s hidden geothermal logic. Shallow pools release bubbles at regular intervals as gases collect and escape. Hollows beneath moss mats fill and drain and refill as water pressure shifts. These are the land’s pistons. They do not have bodies, but they are inhabitants of the same order as salamander and moss: they participate in the metabolism of the plain.

There is a strange electrical life in this place too. Wet peat, iron-rich water, moss fibers, wind, and the friction of moving animals create a low static charge in the surface layer. On still evenings, the fog may taste faintly metallic. Small sparks sometimes jump between wet root ends when a bird steps through a dense tangle. This is not a creature, exactly, but it behaves like a nervous flicker in the living matrix. It makes the land feel aware. It gives the animals another sense to navigate by: not sight or sound, but a subtle charge in the air, a premonition of wetness, pressure, movement, or weather changing nearby.

The residents do not live in open display. They live in interstices: under moss, inside root masses, beneath floating mats, behind hummocks, in the margins of fog. If you stand in the middle of the plain, you may see very little at first. The sedge sways. The pools reflect gray sky. The moss looks unbroken. But as your eyes adjust, the landscape fills with small presences. A beetle rasps across lichen. A frog’s eye blinks from beneath a floating thread of fungal growth. A vole pushes through a moss mat, disturbing the surface for only a moment before the soft ground closes behind it. A salamander slides from under a stone into darker water. A bird steps carefully across a sedge arch. A grazer lowers its muzzle into a thick clump and pulls a mouthful with a wet tearing sound.

Their way of living is one of packed coexistence. Nothing has room to be wasteful. Their bodies become food. Their waste becomes fuel. Their burrows become water channels. Their footprints become pools. Their feathers carry spores. Their claws crack mineral nodules. Their bones return iron and calcium to wet ground. The ecology is saturated, not merely diverse. It is a crowded quiet, a world where nearly every empty surface is claimed and nearly every death becomes material for life.

They are not loud because the land forbids loudness. Moss absorbs their footfalls. Fog swallows their calls. Dense vegetation diffuses their movement into a soft, continuous texture. A large animal running sounds low and cushioned. A bird taking flight gives only a whisper. Even thunder arrives flattened, more felt than heard, rolling across the marsh like a distant bed frame shifting in another room. The animals have learned this silence. They communicate through vibration, scent, stillness, tiny movements, and the faint electrical sense the saturated ground can offer. A warning may be a sudden freeze, not a call. A territorial boundary may be a scent mark on moss, not a cry. A predator’s presence may be known by the way the voles stop moving, the way a frog’s throat stills, the way a bird’s tilted head does not lift.

If one tried to name the whole resident, it would not be a species. It would be a community: moss-and-water-and-peat-and-root-and-fungus-and-larva-and-frog-and-bird-and-fog-and-static. It is a living system in which each element appears separate but is actually a fold in one dense organism. The hummocks are its organs. The channels are its veins. The fog is its breath. The peat is its memory. The microbes are its digestion. The animals are its movements and moods.

At dawn, the inhabitants seem most themselves. The air is cool and wet. Breath ghosts from the fur of voles and the mouths of the grazing animals. Droplets line each sedge blade like tiny lanterns. The moss holds them so thickly that the ground appears made of glass. A small pool in a hollow between hummocks has a skin of orange-brown mineral. White threads of fungal growth drift beneath it. A bubble rises and pops, sending a faint tick across the surface. Under that skin, something shifts: a larva, a tiny fish, a frog embryo, a piece of decaying leaf. A bird steps across the floating mats, its toes spreading, its feathers beaded with mist. It tilts its head and listens. Beneath the moss, a vole moves through a tunnel, leaving a faint trail of disturbed peat. Farther off, a wool-backed grazer eats with slow, wet sounds. Fog curls between the tufts, moving not as wind but as its own current.

In that moment, the place is fully populated, though no one appears to be in charge. The residents do not announce themselves. They do not build monuments. They do not clear fields or raise towers. They merely continue their quiet industries: condensing water, trapping carbon, cracking stone, feeding soil, laying eggs, hunting small animals, spreading spores, breathing, rotting, growing, storing, releasing.

What lives there is, in the end, a civilization of smallness and saturation. It is an inhabited world without a human heart in it. The people are absent. The population is total.
