No one lives there in the way people live in places. There are no households, no fences, no hearths, no names. If a walker were somehow brought to the rim of that hot wet basin, the place would not resist the walker with walls or customs, nor would it welcome them. It would simply continue its own affairs: steam rising, moss swelling, iron oxidizing, larvae turning in peat, a bird stepping through a curtain of midges without disturbing them.

What lives there instead is a nested population, a city of small wet beings and slow processes. The basin’s residents range from the invisible to the barely visible, from microbial films that eat minerals to the soft footfalls of a fox-like animal at dusk. Their numbers are great, but their presence is mostly suggested rather than announced. The place looks as though it has been set down and left alone, yet nothing in it is idle.

The first citizens are the film-makers.

On the surfaces of warm stones, in the mouths of rock seeps, at the edges of black pools, there are skins of life so fine that they could be mistaken for stain. These are bacterial mats: iron-oxidizing bacteria that rust the water into orange veils; sulfur-loving forms that leave a yellow grease around warm vents; silica-depositing microbes that build white lacework at the places where steam condenses; cyanobacteria and algae that darken the water into deep olive, copper, or soot. They are not merely on the land. They are among its active organs. They lift dissolved minerals through water, build films, exhale gases, trap light, fix carbon, decompose, and recombine. When a thin skin covers a warm pool, that skin is a living membrane. It ripples when touched, trembles with trapped vapor, and then slowly reseals as if the pool had a breath held inside a mouth full of water.

Beneath and between these films, the microscopic fauna are abundant. Rotifers turn in capillary water, filtering bacteria and algal grains with cilia that move like tiny paddles. Tardigrades cling to wet moss at the margins of steam vents, suspended in their own patience, waiting for the moisture to arrive or persist. Nematodes thread through water films thinner than a blade of sedge. Protozoa and other single-celled organisms crowd the spaces between peat fibers, grazing bacteria, dying, being eaten, and becoming again the mineral broth from which the next generation is built. These are not background life. They are the basin’s essential working population. Without them, the water would remain merely water, and the stone would remain merely stone.

Above them, and still tiny, are the animals that live in the moss.

Springtails are everywhere. They are pale, translucent, silver-gray, or dusted with fine particles that make them look like fragments of living salt. They jump through the wet labyrinth between moss stems, grazing films of fungi, algae, and bacteria. To a springtail, a dew-laden cushion of sphagnum is not ground but terrain: ridges of green branches, lakes the size of thimblefuls, caverns formed by decaying leaves, tunnels of black water running between roots. A single moss cushion might contain dozens of such beings at any moment, some climbing, some resting, some falling into a pocket of water and being fished out by a mite.

Mites are equally numerous. Some are no larger than a pinhead, with glossy bodies like drops of amber, dark cherry, or milk. They move slowly through the damp air spaces in the peat, riding on water films and feeding on fungi, eggs, decaying tissue, and smaller animals. Many have adapted to the heat and wetness of this place with strange subtlety: their bodies are translucent enough that one can sometimes see dark gut contents moving inside them, a sign that even the smallest creatures here are full of the land’s chemistry. They crawl across steam-warmed rock lips, drink from droplets, and lay eggs in black crevices where the temperature remains steady and the air never becomes dry.

The peat itself is an inhabited substance. It is not merely soil. It is architecture. In the upper layers are mosses and liverworts, roots, rhizomes, and the remains of dead stems; below that, darker and more compressed, lie thousands of tunnels, chambers, and galleries. Fly larvae move through these layers, some of them bright and oily-looking, clustered where the water is warmest and the organic matter most rotted. Their bodies are pale at the front and darker behind, stained by the peat around them. They feed, molt, leave behind casings, and become food for others. Small beetles tunnel after them. Shrimplike larvae, thin and translucent, bend in the water that fills the spaces between fibers. These are not conspicuous creatures. They are part of the interior of the ground.

In the shallow black pools, there is a different society.

Water striders or their relatives stand on the surface tension, legs spread like needles across a film of iron-stained water. The pools are too small and too shallow to hold fish; they are instead arenas for insects, crustaceans, and amphibian larvae. Chironomid larvae, often red or black with fine feathery gills, twist in the mud and feed on detritus and microbial films. Some swim in slow spirals when disturbed. Caddis larvae build portable houses from sand, rust grains, and tiny fragments of leaf or hair-thin moss stem; they resemble moving grains of colored gravel. Snails with shells thin as tea stain graze the microbial skins, leaving faint grooves behind them. Their shells are orange, black, or ash-gray, so they seem to be pieces of the water’s own palette until they move.

There may be tiny branchiopod-like animals there as well, comma-shaped things swimming upside down through the warm shallows, their eggs resistant to drying. These are creatures adapted to a world where a pool may last for weeks or centuries depending on the water’s path. Some eggs wait in peat until heat and moisture return; others are laid directly onto stems and leaves with microscopic precision, as if the animals themselves were chemists calibrating their own young to the chemistry of the place.

The plants are not merely the stage. They are residents too, and some of them are builders.

Sphagnum moss forms cushions swollen like pale green lungs. It holds water, creates its own acidic bog, resists decay, and provides homes for smaller creatures. Liverworts spread flat over stones and wet earth in frilled rosettes. Crustose lichens weld themselves to bare basalt, turning it into a slow surface of gray, rust, and green. Ferns crowd the warm seeps and unfurl with visible speed when the heat and moisture are right, their young fronds curled tight like the fingers of someone opening a fist. Low shrubs with black stems and small glossy leaves root in mats that hang above the water table, their roots forming curtains in which insects and eggs can hide. Sedges with blades like wet bronze bind the soil edges. Peat accumulates not as dead matter only, but as the memory of living and dying, a compressed archive of roots, leaves, moss, and the bodies of the small creatures that once inhabited them.

The amphibians live in this damp archive.

Frogs call from hidden cavities. Their voices are not loud, but they seem to come from the earth itself. Some are dark and muddy-colored, with skin rough as bark and eyes that gleam faintly in steam. Others are smoother, their backs striped or speckled with rust and sulfur yellow. In the warm pools, their eggs are laid in strings or masses attached to submerged stems. The embryos develop faster than they would in ordinary cold mountain water because the basin’s heat seeps up through the ground. Tadpoles graze microbial films, filter algae, and sometimes feed on each other. Their guts may be dark with mineral-rich sediment; their bodies are transparent enough in places to reveal the movement of developing life inside. They become frogs that return to the moss when the air dries too much, sinking into cool pockets where the peat retains moisture and the stone gives off warmth.

Near the edges of warm pools and within the deeper peat are small salamander-like creatures, perhaps with flattened heads, long tails, and dark glossy skin flecked with gold or orange. They move at night or in the mist hours, hunting midges, springtails, and larvae. Their eggs are not left in open pools but tucked into damp folds of root or inside hollows beneath stones. The warmth helps them develop; the surrounding damp protects them from drying. Their bodies are soft enough to slip through narrow cracks in the peat, and their sense of territory is not distance but temperature and moisture. A particular seep, a certain black hollow, a root mat where the ground exhales warmth may be as important to them as a burrow is to a mammal.

The reptiles are heat-readers.

There are small lizards in the basin, dark-bodied and quick in the warmest hours. Some are smooth, some slightly keeled, and their tails taper to a point that disappears into moss. They bask on stones heated by geothermal breath, their scales catching the steam-light. Their coloration is mineral: black, iron-orange, bronze, sulfur-yellow, pale gray, often with patterns that make them look like living fragments of the rock. They move along steam vents, test the temperature with their bellies, and retreat if the stone becomes too hot. They drink condensation from fern fronds and moss tips, catching drops on their snouts and swallowing them with a quick blink.

Their bodies are tuned to the heat. When the ground is warm, they become alert and fast. When the cloud closes and the basin cools, they slow, tuck themselves into crevices, or flatten themselves over warm peat until heat rises through their bodies. They hunt insects in the moss and along the edges of water channels. Their claws are suited for gripping wet stone, their tails for balance on thin root mats, their eyes for catching movement in dense low vegetation. They are not conspicuous, but on a warm rock near a steam vent one might see several at once, motionless, eyes half-closed, as if the stones had grown heads.

The birds are quiet, and mostly small.

There are no rooks, no loud corvids, no raucous waterbirds in a noisy flock. The birds of the basin are waders and runners, birds that know how to move through wet moss without seeming to break the surface tension of the world. One is sooty or dark gray, with pale eyes and a short beak. It probes into root mats, lifts leaf litter, and searches for larvae. Its legs are long enough to keep it above the wettest surfaces but short enough to allow it to slip into dense cover. Its plumage is stained with bog brown and rust, as if the land had marked it at birth. When it walks, it does not leap or hop; it tests, steps, pauses, then flows onward.

Other birds are smaller, almost mouse-like in their movements, darting through the sedges and shrubs. They feed on midges and tiny insects. Some nest not in tall trees but in the hanging gardens of root and rhizome supported by peat. Their nests are cups of moss, lichen, hair from the moss mice, and fine plant fibers. The eggs are pale, freckled, or blackish, camouflaged against peat and mineral dust. The chicks are quiet. They have down the color of damp ash, and when they are hungry they do not clamor loudly but press upward against the parent with small open mouths.

Above the basin, if a larger raptor appears, it is not common and not noisy. It is a short-tailed bird of prey with dark wings, a pale throat, and sharp eyes, circling high in the clean thin sunlight. It may descend at times to hunt lizards, small birds, or the occasional mammal. But it does not stay long. The basin does not offer open hunting; it offers hidden things. A predator moving over it has to come low, follow channels, and trust its ears to the faint sounds beneath the moss.

The mammals include both the hidden and the quiet hunters.

In the peat runways are small rodents or shrews: short-eared, soft-bodied, pale-furred on the underside. Some are mice, adapted to dampness and moss. They do not live in dry nests above the ground but in burrows lined with sphagnum, where the material keeps them warm and absorbs moisture. They eat rhizomes, seeds, fungi, insect eggs, and small animals. Their paths are not visible unless one knows how to look: compressed moss, a slight darkening of the peat, a tunnel opening near a warm rock, a passage beneath a fern mat. They travel repeatedly along the same routes, because heat, moisture, and food concentrate in certain spots. Over time, their bodies press soft trails into the ground. If one lifted the mat, the peat might reveal a network of tiny galleries, some occupied by fly larvae, some empty, some beginning to collapse and be rebuilt.

There are shrews too, with pink noses and dark wet fur. They hunt surface insects and larvae in the moss, using quick twitching movements and perhaps ultrasonic clicks to detect movement. They are not loud. They are not large. They may be the most active nocturnal creatures in the basin, appearing at dusk along rock lips and warm seeps, pausing with ears erect, then vanishing into root curtains.

But the most defined mammal is the fox-like predator.

It is not a forest fox, not a plains fox, not a common fox at all. It is a slender animal with dark russet and black fur, long legs, a fine muzzle, and large ears edged in pale hair. Its coat looks almost wet even when it is dry, because of the dense humidity. It has a tail long enough to balance on stone channels and paws broad and sensitive enough to test the firmness of a moss cushion before shifting its weight. It walks with a softness that seems practiced by generations of survival in a place where movement must be quiet. Its footprints are tiny stars pressed into rust-stained ground, filled within hours by moisture and creeping moss.

This animal hunts the frog, the lizard, the nesting bird, the moss mouse, and the beetle. It digs into the peat when it smells larvae or eggs. It crouches near steam vents where insects gather, then moves with a sudden flick of the head. It knows the warm patches, the cooler refuges, the drainage lines, and the places where the ground will hold its weight. It does not roar or bark. It clicks, breathes, and pauses. In the evening, when the heat drops slightly and the pools darken, it moves along the edge of a black channel. It steps from root mat to root mat. It stops. Its nose twitches toward the sound of something hidden beneath moss. Then it continues, because the basin is full of such moments, and it must not disturb the entire night by one wrong movement.

There are perhaps other small predators too: marten-like animals, perhaps with shorter faces and glossy coats, climbing root curtains to reach bird nests. But the fox-like predator stands out because of the way it inhabits the open edge of the basin, the way its paws test each cushion, the way it seems to understand the country’s hidden supports. It is one of the few creatures large enough to leave a visible route, and yet it is still so light-footed that its path is easily mistaken for the slow shift of moss, the press of rain, or the settlement of peat.

The food chains are short, intense, and ceaseless.

A bacterial film fixes light and builds itself from minerals. A snail grazes it. A fly lays eggs in nearby water. A frog eats the fly. A fox eats the frog. The fox’s waste falls into moss. The bacteria in the moss consume it, the roots draw nutrients upward, the sphagnum holds water, the springtails graze the dead, the bird eats the springtail, the parasite returns to the water, and the film grows again. Nothing escapes entirely. The basin recycles everything except what escapes as vapor, sulfur gas, or mineral precipitate. Even death is a material. A dead beetle becomes fungus, then larvae, then black water, then iron oxide, then a crust of rust, then a surface for new bacterial life.

The place has no loud chorus, but it has a soundless abundance.

The moss dampens footsteps. The air, thick with moisture and warmed by stone, carries sound poorly. Distant bird calls become soft clicks. Frog calls seem to come from inside hollows. Insect hums are scattered, not aggregated into a swarm’s roar. When the wind crosses the plateau, the vegetation absorbs it in a whisper of thousands of leaf tips. This is not silence, exactly. It is a reduced volume, a world with the treble removed and the bass turned into murmur. The inhabitants live inside this quiet because the quiet protects them. A predator that makes too much noise may lose prey. A bird that calls too loudly may reveal a nest. A frog that remains too visible may become a fox’s supper. Many of the animals here are adapted to being hard to detect: translucent larvae, moss-colored frogs, lizards like wet stone, birds that flatten themselves into shadows, mammals that move through the peat without breaking the surface.

Some of the weirdest inhabitants are those that live on the boundary between water and air.

On the surface of steam-warmed stone, there are tiny animals that seem to drink from the humidity itself. Some mites attach to insects and hitch rides over the wet maze, then dismount near water films rich in bacteria. Water-striders or their relatives stand on surface tension where there is barely any depth, hunting midges that fall or are blown into the skin of the pool. On the edges of warm springs, where mineral water evaporates into air, there may be minute creatures adapted to a film of water less than a millimeter deep. Their whole world is a liquid sheet stretched over stone, constantly changing as steam condenses, water drains, and heat shifts.

The pools covered by microbial skin are also inhabited. The film itself is a community. It may be grazed upon by microscopic animals from above, while below it larvae and bacteria work in dark water. When a stone breaks the surface, the film tears and then reseals, as if the pool had been breathing through a thin mouth and quickly closed it again. To a careful observer, this might seem like a single organism stretching over a pool. Perhaps that is close to the truth. The pool is not only a pool. It is a living enclosure.

The animals of the basin do not think of the place as “hot” or “wet” in human terms. They experience it as gradients of comfort. For a frog, a warm cavity means faster development and safety from drying. For a lizard, a steam-warmed rock means energy. For a fly, a rust-colored channel means water thin enough to land on and organic matter enough to lay eggs in. For a moss mouse, a sphagnum run means insulation and concealment. For the fox, a rusted stone channel means a path, a listening place, a boundary where prey may appear. The inhabitants navigate by temperature, moisture, mineral smell, and the texture of the living ground.

There are also resident parasites and symbionts, as inevitable as insects and bacteria. Some flies may drink blood from birds or mammals and rest in warm moss. Some small crustaceans may live in the nasal cavities or feather sheaths of birds. Some mites travel on larger animals to reach fresh patches of peat. Some fungi live inside moss roots, helping them gather water and minerals. Some bacteria live inside the bodies of invertebrates, making possible what the host cannot do alone. Even the larger animals are not whole by themselves; they are ecosystems wearing fur, scales, or feathers.

If one tried to describe who lives there as if it were a town, one might say this:

In the steam vents live the microbial workers, invisible and industrious.  
In the black pools live the larvae, shrimp, and frogs.  
In the moss cushions live the springtails, mites, rotifers, and the eggs of things that have no wings until the water recedes.  
In the root mats live the moss mice and shrews.  
On the warm stones live the lizards.  
In the low shrubs and fern thickets live the small birds and moths.  
Along the rusted channels at dusk walks the fox.  
Above the basin, sometimes, a dark wing turns in thin sun.  
Under the peat, threads of fungal life advance and retreat in a war of decay.  
Within the water itself, dissolved minerals and microbial films carry the country’s chemistry from one pocket to another.

But this list would still be incomplete, because the basin’s inhabitants are not only individuals. They are also relationships. A certain orange stain is not paint; it is microbial action. A certain silence is not emptiness; it is moss absorbing movement. A certain pool is not a hole in the ground; it is a living membrane. A certain trail is not merely a path; it is a habit repeated by bodies, water, and soft earth. The residents are the creatures and the processes that make them possible.

The place appears untouched because no one has cut into it, burned it, fenced it, or named its resources. Yet it is used by everything in it. Every drop of water is routed by roots and stones. Every warm patch is read by animals. Every mineral deposit is built or broken down by organisms. The country is not a wilderness without inhabitants. It is a wilderness whose inhabitants have never been asked to appear.

If one had to answer the question directly, the answer would be: everything lives there that can live in a small warm wet world and make a virtue of quiet.

The rust eaters. The steam dwellers. The film-makers. The pool-keepers. The egg-layers in black water. The larvae that turn the peat into a living lattice. The frogs hidden in cavities. The lizards basking on heated stone. The birds with soft feet and sooty feathers. The mice and shrews pressing their routes into moss. The fox that walks without sound along a channel of rusted stone. The fungi advancing across dead stems. The bacteria oxidizing iron, fixing carbon, exhaling sulfur, building silica lacework, and resealing the skin of a pool.

They are the basin. They are its industry and its population. They are not people, but they have a kind of citizenship. Each occupies a patch of temperature, moisture, mineral water, root, or film. Each depends on gradients invisible to the eye. Each lives in a place where the heat comes from below and the clouds come close enough to touch the moss, and where water is not a river but a slow breathing through the body of the land.
