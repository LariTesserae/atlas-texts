To live in the Hollowloom is to live inside a body that has learned the shape of a town.

Nothing there belongs only to itself. A wall is a wall for a while, then a vine finds it, then a patch of pale growth decides to call it home, then a family of blind beetles makes its chambers in the mineral skin, then the house around it becomes less a house than a joint in the larger damp organism. The same is true of the creatures that walk there. Some are people, or were people once enough to be called that. Some are animals so integrated into the moss, metal, and machinery that they seem less like visitors than organs. Some are machines that have grown into life. And beneath all of them, threading through stone and water and breath, the Hollowloom itself is not inert. It combs, settles, swells, exhales, and remembers.

The inhabitants do not occupy the chamber in the old town’s way. They inhabit it as one inhabits a wet coat, a slow heartbeat, a forest that has agreed to carry buildings. They are many kinds of life, all of them nested.

## The First Residents: the Living Skin

Before any creature could live there, a way of living had to be made.

The first residents of the Hollowloom are not animals, though they behave like animals when observed too long. They are the mosses, the pale fungal laces, the root networks, the mineral furs, the slow green-grey skins that colonize every surface with an insistence that looks almost like memory.

Moss in the Hollowloom is not soft decoration. It is an engineering material. It holds moisture like a sponge stitched over stone. It protects metal from rust by swallowing it. It gives structure to loose mineral crust. It makes slippery slopes gripable. It turns bare walls into porous, breathing surfaces. Some moss is thick as a pillow and springs back when pressed. Some is thin as breath, a green film so delicate that it can be wiped off by a careless sleeve.

Roots behave like masonry. They do not merely grow into cracks; they become the architecture’s hidden logic. A ceiling-root may begin as a black thread, then thicken, then fork, then lace with tiny hairs that collect condensation. Over decades it becomes strong enough to bear weight. Over centuries it may form a bridge across an open court or cradle the frame of a house so gently that no one can say whether the house was built on the root or the root grew around the house.

Fungal rinds are another kind of citizen. Pale, powdery, sometimes faintly luminous, they spread over iron bands, copper rails, pipe joints, and the edges of loom rollers. They do not attack metal so much as convert it into something the hollow can tolerate. A bolt left too long in one place will find its head softened by growth. A cable left naked will eventually be embraced by vine. This is not decay alone; it is incorporation. The living skin does not merely cover the world. It teaches the world how to stay.

These organisms are not individuals in any human sense. They belong to a horizontal kingdom. They do not compete by rising tall; they compete by being impossible to dislodge. If a surface is dry, a spore arrives. If a drip appears, a thread follows. If a machine leaks, the growth moves toward the leak as though it had been invited.

They are the Hollowloom’s first people, the ones who made the later people possible.

## The Comb-Folk: the Town That Was Swallowed

There are people in the Hollowloom.

They call themselves the Comb-folk, because the central task of their lives is this: to comb water into channels, roots into walls, breath into engines, and the town into a shape the hollow can hold.

They are small in stature and broad through the chest, as though generations of damp rooms and low vaults had shaped them for enclosed air. Their skin has a pale, waxy warmth, the kind that never dries easily but remains slightly humid, as if the body remembers where it lives. Their hair grows thick and dark, often kept tied with strips of leaf-fiber or thin copper wire. Their eyes are wide for the low light, with large pupils and flecks of gold or grey in the iris, so that even when still they seem to be reading the movement of shadow.

Their hands are perhaps the most distinctive thing about them. Fingertips are worn smooth, palms calloused not only by friction but by a thin fungal skin that grows into the living architecture. They can press a wall and feel whether water is gathering behind the moss. They can wrap a hand around a pipe and know whether the pump beyond it is working, struggling, or dreaming. They can test a root by touch and tell whether it will accept a bracket, refuse a cut, or tighten during the night.

They do not dress as if above the weather. Their clothing is layered, damp-proof, and quietly alive. Shirts and coats are woven from pale leaf-fiber, spun by looms whose belts are sometimes reinforced with the silk of loom-spiders. Over these they wear cloaks shingled with broad, overlapping leaves, each leaf held down by a strip of rusted iron or a band of mineral crust. The shingles shed droplets when the ceiling lets fall its patient rain. Around their waists hang small clay jars, sealed pouches of water, brass hooks, pruning wedges, and corks made from compressed moss. Some wear goggles of green glass, not because the light is too bright but because spores and swarm-flying things can fill the air in moments.

They are human, but they are no longer independent of the hollow.

If a Comb-folk child is born in a dry room, the room is said to be unhappy. If a child is named in the same hour as a steady drip, that drip becomes part of the child’s history. Adults move through the terraces with a practiced reluctance to disturb anything. They know that a stone left uncovered will be colonized, that a machine sealed away will fail, that a wall cut too greedily will answer with contraction, and that a cistern forgotten for long enough may begin to hold its own creatures.

Their society is organized around water, pressure, growth, and sound.

The most respected are the **drip-listeners**. A drip-listener does not merely hear a falling drop; she or they or he hears the whole room. By listening to the tone a droplet makes when it strikes metal, stone, wood, leaf, or pool, the drip-listener can tell how the reservoir is filling, whether a pipe is blocked, which terrace has become too dry, or whether a pump is running out of rhythm. Some old listeners claim they can tell whether a root is about to tighten simply by the pitch of a cistern’s echo.

The **channel-combers** carry narrow bronze combs and long, thin tools that look like half a fork. They clear moss from the troughs, shape the flow of condensation, and decide where a thread of water may be allowed to become a streamlet. They are not only workers; they are artists of patience. A poorly combed channel can flood a sleeping alcove, drown a lamp niche, or starve a turbine basin of enough water to turn.

The **root-tighteners** tend the load-bearing roots. When a vine becomes a wall, it must be watched, braced, and sometimes taught. Root-tighteners insert brass wedges, guide roots through iron hoops, smear wounds with lichen paste, and listen to the slow creak of tissue under tension. They fear no predator so much as a dry crack. A cracked root is a door for failure.

The **boiler-farmers** tend the warm engines. They do not shovel coal in the way the old town might have imagined. They feed nutrient slurry through pipes, clear scale from fungal-lined chambers, and encourage the growth that gives the engines their strange endurance. A boiler-farmer can tell by smell when a fire-stem is breathing too fast. If the moss around an engine turns grey, they know the machine is sealed off from the living tissue and will soon die.

The **lamp-herders** move light itself through the chambers. They carry jars of luminous mold, trim phosphorescent veins on damp timber, place lichen sheets near dark courts, and sometimes coax the biological glow toward machines that need it. They are also careful with electric lamps, because the current in the Hollowloom is not separate from the ecology. The glow of a filament can feed certain lichens. The dimming of a filament can attract swarms. The lamp-herder knows when to light a room and when to let a darkness speak.

The **loom-feeders** maintain the great looms and workshops. Their belts run over wet rollers; their frames are half iron, half vine. They harvest spider-silk, cut leaf-thread, and keep the looms from being swallowed entirely by the growth that supports them. A loom that has been left too long without attention may continue weaving for a time, then slowly forget what it is supposed to make.

The **fishkeepers** tend the pools, basins, troughs, and cisterns. They know that every water pocket is a city-state. A basin can hold blind fish, frog-like creatures, larval beetles, and microscopic life so dense that a spoon dipped into it comes away with a trembling green film. The fishkeepers decide which pools may dry, which must be filled, which will become too crowded, and which must be divided before the creatures become cannibalistic.

There are also **flue-watchers**, who climb breathing stems and clear blockages before warm air chokes a house. **Cistern-wives and cistern-husbands**, who seal water in clay pockets and leaf-lined hollows so the town can remember what the air refuses to give. **Swarm-readers**, who watch the small flying creatures fill and empty the air, because a sudden thickening of wings can mean pressure change, leak, heat, electrical pulse, or the approach of one of the larger animals.

The Comb-folk do not live in houses so much as in negotiated chambers. A family’s home may begin as a shallow terrace, then a root-wall closes around it, then a leaf roof is pressed into place, then a copper rail is allowed to fuse with the growth, then a breathing stem rises from the central room like an inverted tree. The house may shift slightly over years. A child may sleep in the same alcove but with a new curtain of vines. A doorway may narrow as roots swell with winter damp. This does not frighten them. They call it the room settling.

They are not conquerors of the hollow. They are tenants who have learned to become useful.

## The Small Occupants: Animals as Infrastructure

The animals of the Hollowloom are not merely present. They are civic.

They live in walls, pipes, pools, looms, roofs, flues, cables, and the bodies of machines. Their movement is part of the settlement’s maintenance. Their hunger is part of its economy. Their droppings are mortar. Their silk is cord. Their nesting material is insulation. Their bodies, in many cases, are indistinguishable from the infrastructure they help shape.

### Wall-Grazers

The most abundant are the **wall-grazers**, slow pale creatures with shell-like backs, soft bellies, and many small legs. They look, from a distance, like pieces of fungus that have decided to move. They crawl along root-channels and mossed terraces, scraping growth from stone.

They are not considered pests. They are considered workers.

Where a wall-grazer passes, it leaves behind a clean surface, then its droppings, then a richer moss coat than before. In a few days, the wall is thicker, softer, and better able to hold water. Comb-folk encourage wall-grazers by placing shallow troughs of mineral dust near walls that are too dry. If a terrace begins to feel lonely, it may not need a person; it may need more grazers.

The grazers themselves have many names depending on terrace, color, and behavior. Some have shells as smooth as eggstone. Some are furred with their own moss. Some have translucent edges, so that lamplight reveals the slow movement of fluid inside them. Their young are tiny and white, often found in crevices, in old pots, or in the folds of abandoned clothing.

### Drip-Newts

In every basin, trough, and shallow pool, there are **drip-newts**. They are small amphibian-like creatures with long tails, flattened heads, and skin that feels like a wet leaf. They can climb almost any damp surface, clinging to copper pipes and mossy railings, sliding into cracks with an ease that makes them impossible to fully count.

They are leak-seekers.

A drip-newt will gather where condensation forms, often before a human notices. Comb-folk sometimes place newt-jars in dark corners. If the newts cluster near one wall, a channel is forming. If they scatter from a pool, the water may have become too still, too rich, or too warm. They eat swarming larvae and microscopic life, keeping cisterns from turning into choking green soup. When dried out too long, they become sluggish and grey; when wet, they brighten with a pale blue underside.

### Roof-Hens

The ceiling-roots are roosted by creatures the Comb-folk call **roof-hens**, though they are not hens and may not be exactly birds. They are small, feathered animals with long toes and wide, soft wings that seem to make no sound. Their feathers are dark grey-green, not black, so that they vanish into the hanging root-lace. Their eyes are too large for daylight and useless for it, so they hunt mostly by sound: the drip of water, the knock of a pipe, the slow creak of a growing stem.

They nest in roofs, engine grilles, leaf curtains, and the hollow bases of breathing stems. Their eggs are speckled and heavy, eaten by the Comb-folk when available. Their droppings feed moss. Their feathers shed water and are sometimes tied into cloaks or pressed into shingle linings.

The roof-hens are also acoustic animals. When a pump changes rhythm, they may all shift at once on their perch. When a court becomes too dry, they leave and settle elsewhere. The Comb-folk listen to them as readily as to any machine.

### Loom-Spiders

In workshops and lower passages live the **loom-spiders**, pale jointed animals that spin a thread stronger than it appears. Their silk is not merely organic; it carries traces of iron dust, mineral salts, and fungal fibers harvested from the surfaces around them. Comb-folk cultivate them in framed wooden cages, feeding them small flying insects and offering them scraps of copper wire.

The spiders, in return, produce thread that is spun into belts, cords, and thin fabric. If a spider is left in a machine too long, it may become part of the machine. A loom that has stopped working may continue only because a colony of spiders has woven a temporary belt around a slipped roller. This is not always desirable, but it is always interesting. The Comb-folk know that a machine can be kept alive by a creature that does not understand what a machine is.

### Boiler-Moths

Inside warm pipes and the chambers behind engines live the **boiler-moths**. In larval form, they are fat, pale, and blind, chewing away at mineral scale with jaws that seem too strong for their soft bodies. They are not pests but cleaners. The adult moths are dull and heavy, with dusty wings, and they move sluggishly near hot surfaces.

They are often found near flue-stems, where they carry spores from one warm chamber to another. Their pupae are sometimes mistaken for knots in the pipes. A boiler-farmer who hears a faint scraping inside a heated line may smile, because the scraping is the sound of the engine being tended by something small enough to fit in the spaces a human hand cannot reach.

### Spark-Mites and Electric Beetles

Near dynamos, junction boxes, and old cables gather tiny luminous creatures known as **spark-mites**. They are almost too small to see, like grains of moving sand. They are drawn to electrical fields and gather in patches wherever current hums. The Comb-folk do not fully trust them, but they use them.

A swarm of spark-mites can make a wire seem to glow with its own faint life. If the swarm disperses, the current may have changed. If it clusters too thickly, it can cause shorts, dim lamps, or draw power into itself as though feeding. Electric beetles are larger and less numerous: shiny black or coppery insects with long antennae that seem to taste voltage. They crawl along railings and sometimes open small panels with their jaws, not out of malice but curiosity.

The Comb-folk have learned to coax these creatures away from important junctions by scattering salt-fungus or moving damp cloths over cables. Sometimes, however, they let the beetles remain, because their presence is a sign that the old wiring still has vitality.

### Flue-Salamanders

The breathing stems of houses are home to **flue-salamanders**, slender creatures with slick mottled skin and a habit of climbing warm vertical surfaces. They regulate the flow of humid exhaust in a way no engineer could explain. Their bodies are cool, their mouths hot. They eat tiny flying things carried up from below and leave behind a moist coating that keeps flue linings from drying and cracking.

Children sometimes see them and think they are pieces of metal that have come to life, because their skin reflects lamplight like oiled brass. If a flue-salamander dies in a stem, the house may cough smoke for a day. Comb-folk then climb with hooks and cloths, not angry, only concerned that something small has taken a large duty for too long.

### Cistern-Fish and Blind Frogs

Every pool, however shallow, contains **cistern-fish** and their amphibious relatives. The fish are pale, sometimes nearly transparent, with fins like torn leaves. They do not need deep water. They survive in films of moisture if the surface is still enough and the air thick enough. Some have developed strange habits: they jump from one basin to another when condensation makes the air dense; some cling to damp roots with sucker-like mouths; some feed entirely on the green fur of a cistern wall.

The **blind frogs** are more common and more sociable. They live under loose tiles, behind iron bands, in the hollows of worn steps, and in the folds of moss curtains. They do not croak in any way that would remind a surface-dweller of summer ponds. Instead, they make a wet clicking sound when pressure changes. A terrace full of blind frogs may sound like a room of tiny mouths learning to speak.

## The Larger Quiet Ones

The Hollowloom also contains larger animals, but it does not seem built for them. They are too big for its streets and too slow for its machinery. They pass through the settlement with an unbothered grace, as though the town is only a shallow stream crossing.

These larger creatures are known to the Comb-folk as **bellowsbacks**.

They are broad-chested, long-limbed, and covered in hides that seem to breathe. Some have mottled brown-grey skin like old stone; others have translucent flanks stretched tight over ridged bone, so that in the dimness their ribs look like the framework of a boat. Their feet are wide and soft, made for wet surfaces. They step into basins without splashing and cross worn terraces without cracking mineral crust.

Their backs are often colonized by moss. It is not parasitic; it is symbiotic. The bellowsback’s skin sheds salts that feed the growth, and the growth cools the animal and helps it remain invisible among root-walls. When a bellowsback stands still in the dark, it can look like a low terrace, a stone bench, a pile of wet brush.

They do not fear the machines. A pump may shudder, a loom may clack, a flue may exhale, and the bellowsback merely shifts its weight and continues. Sometimes, however, they are drawn to the warmth of engines, to the dripping rhythm of basins, or to the faint electrical hum of patched dynamos. Their bodies can interfere with the hollow more than the small creatures’ can. A bellowsback leaning against a water trough may change the flow for hours. Herds gathering near a court can make a terrace seem alive with bodies. Their footfalls can loosen old moss. Their dung can bloom with fungi overnight.

The Comb-folk do not ride them, at least not seriously, because riding requires control and control is a poor way to live in the Hollowloom. Instead, they tend the animals the way one tends weather: by offering places, opening passages, moving water, and accepting that the larger ones will choose their own routes. Sometimes, when the hollow is crowded with bellowsbacks moving through the terraces, the entire sound of the place changes: drips become echoes, footsteps become drums, and the room feels less like a city and more like a body walking in its sleep.

There are other large creatures, too: long-necked blind grazers that feed on root-curtains, translucent-skinned animals with too many limbs that appear in dry seams and vanish into wet ones, and slow, mottled quadrupeds that seem to belong to the stone until they blink. But the bellowsbacks are the ones that most clearly embody the Hollowloom’s condition. They are not tame. They are not wild. They are simply abundant beyond human planning, and their abundance is part of the town’s texture.

## The Living Machines

The machines of the Hollowloom are not dead things placed inside a living room. They are alive enough to be considered residents, and dependent enough to require care.

At first glance they are industrial: boilers, pumps, looms, turbines, dynamos, pipe networks, grilles, rollers, belts, chimneys. But their surfaces are colonized. Their joints hold moss. Their cables wear fungal insulation. Their frames are wrapped in vines. Their pipes carry water that has been touched by living things. Their rhythm is not only mechanical but organic.

A pump sealed in a dry room will fail. But a pump allowed to grow a skin of root-hair through its base, fed nutrient slurry, and sheltered under a canopy of wet moss can continue for generations. The Comb-folk do not see this as strange. They see it as the machine’s health.

The engines squat in damp rooms behind grilles, their bodies wrapped in warm growth. When one runs, it does not merely turn; it breathes. It draws air through flue-stems and releases a faint odor of hot mineral water, fungal musk, and heated metal. Its rhythm can be heard across a court as a low pulse. The Comb-folk know the difference between a healthy breath and a laboring one.

The small turbines are perhaps the most beautiful. Water does not flood them. It is gathered into threads, combed into channels, and released as a steady drip-line that has been concentrated over generations. The blades turn slowly, not because they are weak, but because speed is not required. The turbine becomes part of a hydrologic conversation between root, stone, and basin. Sometimes the turbine’s shaft is coated with pale growth. Sometimes it is cleaned only by the passage of drip-newts. Sometimes a loom-spider has spun a line from its housing to a nearby pipe, and the whole apparatus looks like an instrument strung by a patient hand.

The looms of brass and iron move through workshops, their belts running over rollers slick with condensation. They weave not cloth alone, but fiber, fungal thread, leaf-strip, and sometimes animal silk. Their sounds are wet: tapping, hissing, pulling, slapping, clicking. They are never silent. Even when stopped, they creak as the air changes. A loom may be left running because the growth around it requires the motion, or because the vibration keeps a wall from sealing itself shut.

The dynamos hum with a dim, unreliable electricity. Their cables are wrapped in leaf-thin insulation, and that insulation has sometimes grown thick enough to need pruning. If a cable is left too long in one spot, moss may climb it. Fungal salts may bloom. In rare cases, the cable seems to pulse because the moss around it is contracting. The Comb-folk do not always know whether they are maintaining the wire or the plant.

The boilers hiss from warm pipes. They do not burn dead fuel alone. They draw heated gas, pressure from growing wood, contraction of living tissue, nutrient slurry, and sometimes the metabolic heat of creatures that have made their homes in the pipes. A boiler that has been properly integrated can be coaxed awake by a careful feeding of slurry and a clearing of its breathing stems. A boiler that has been forgotten may become too dry, too sealed, too separate, and then it will sulk into failure.

There are machines that resemble no surface-world engine. Some are made of pressure and patience: a growing root pushes against a valve, releasing water at intervals. Others are made of animal movement: a bellowsback brushing past a trigger may open a vent. Still others are made of fungal timing: a rind expands in damp air, softens a metal joint, and allows a pipe to shift.

In the Hollowloom, the line between machine and organism has not been erased so much as rendered absurd.

## The Swarm-Life and the Changing Air

The smallest flying creatures are perhaps the least describable and the most essential.

They are not one kind. They are a constant murmuring ecology: lichen midges, spore wasps, water gnats, tinily bright things that may or may not be insects, creatures with wings like wet cloth, tiny bodies that can fill the air in loose restless swarms without warning. They gather around lamp niches, hover above cisterns, drift through open courts, and vanish in moments, leaving the air suddenly emptier than it should be.

The Comb-folk call them collectively the **wing-mist**.

The wing-mist is not evil, but it is not harmless. It can carry spores that make machines bloom with new growth. It can pollinate the luminous molds that keep certain rooms alive. It can also swarm into open flues, coat copper railings, or settle so thickly above a pool that the water below appears to boil. If the wing-mist thickens near a machine that has recently been cleaned, it means the air has changed. If it vanishes from a place where it always gathered, something else has taken its niche.

The swarms are also part of the Hollowloom’s weather. A sudden rise in wing-mist density may precede a pressure shift. A falling wave of midges may follow the tightening of roots in the stone. In the deep hours, when the chamber’s larger animals grow still, the swarms become the most visible movement: clouds drifting between terraces, spiraling around flue-stems, collecting in the amber glow of lanterns.

Children are taught not to breathe deeply near a wall where the swarms rest, because one may find oneself swallowing a cloud. Adults move through the densest patches with cloth masks or wide-brimmed leaf hoods, the way surface-dwellers might wear caps in snow. Yet the Comb-folk do not try to eliminate the wing-mist. They know that a hollow without small flying things would be a hollow without the animals that eat them, the roofs that shelter them, and the lights that call them.

## A Town of Nested Breath

The Comb-folk do not think of the Hollowloom as empty space containing objects. They think of it as a web of breaths.

Each house breathes. Each engine breathes. Each root breathes. Each pool contains a thousand tiny breaths. Each swarming wing breathes as though the whole cloud is one lung.

At night, if there can be said to be night in a place without sky, the Hollowloom does not sleep so much as shift. The drips slow in some courts and quicken in others. The luminous lichens brighten where the air has thickened with moisture. The machines tick as pressure changes. The bellowsbacks move between terraces. The roof-hens settle and rise again. Children dream in moss-lined alcoves, wrapped in leaf-fur, breathing the warm wet air of the room.

There are times when the whole hollow contracts so gently that the Comb-folk know to step away from walls. Root-stems tighten against stone. Ceiling-lace stiffens. A flue exhales more sharply. The room seems to think for a moment in a direction no one understands. This is called **the settling**, and it is not feared if it is honored. People pause. They do not strike, shout, or cut. They place their hands on the nearest damp surface and wait. The hollow releases its tension as slowly as a sleeper shifting in bed.

There are other times when the Hollowloom seems almost conversational. A pump knocks three times, then a basin answers with a ripple, then a roof-hen clicks from above, then a lamp-flicker runs along the wall as though light has heard the summons and moved to see. The Comb-folk call such moments **loom-talk**. It may be nothing but physics, pressure, water, growth, and animal reflex. Or it may be something older and more diffuse, a distributed intelligence made from moss, metal, bone, and breath.

The place is never quiet enough for silence, but sometimes it is quiet enough for listening. In those intervals, the town reveals itself as a city of many lives, each one depending on the others:

- The moss holds the water.
- The water feeds the root.
- The root supports the house.
- The house shelters the people.
- The people clear the channels.
- The channels carry the drips.
- The drips turn the machines.
- The machines warm the air.
- The air grows the lichen.
- The lichen gives light.
- The light calls the flying things.
- The flying things feed the roof-hens.
- The roof-hens nest in engines.
- The engines are cleaned by moths.
- The moths carry spores.
- The spores colonize stone.
- The stone becomes wall.
- The wall becomes moss.

No life in the Hollowloom is merely decorative. Even the smallest blind beetle, if removed from its niche, may leave a wall too dry, a pipe too clogged, or a chamber too dark. Even a human, if acting only for convenience, may kill the tissue that makes the town possible.

## Their Dangers and Devotions

The dangers of the Hollowloom are not cliffs, predators, or collapse. The space has settled into itself; its erosion is old and patient. The real danger is desiccation.

A dry room is a room that wants to die.

When moisture thins, the moss shrinks. The mineral crust cracks. The fungal rinds grey and flake. The copper rails lose their soft green film and become bare, sharp, and hostile to touch. The machines begin to fail, not because they are broken, but because they have lost the living context that kept them running.

The Comb-folk fear dryness more than darkness. In the dark they can find their way by touch and dripping. In dryness, they must search for every drop.

Their work reflects this fear and devotion.

Every dwelling has a water memory. A cistern under the floor. A sealed pocket in a wall. A clay jar in a niche. A hollowed beam that collects condensation. A leaf basin shaped by decades of careful trimming. A gutter that feeds a reservoir. A roof of shingled leaves that can be opened to catch a drip or closed to keep the air from escaping. Even clothing is designed to hold moisture: folds, layers, moss padding, and porous fibers that do not mind dampness.

Their medicine is wet. Their breads are grown, not baked. Their tools are oiled not only against rust but against fungal overgrowth. Their songs are rhythmic enough to mimic dripping, because the human ear can learn to love the sound of survival. Their histories are kept in water-patterns: grooves in stone, channels in wood, rings on jars that record where moisture once gathered and whether the room was generous or stingy.

They have rituals for the machines. They have rituals for the roots. They have rituals for the bellowsbacks, not because the animals are sacred so much as because they are larger than intention. When a pump is installed, it is given a small offering of mineral slurry and a patch of prepared moss. When a root is tied, the worker leaves a thread of silk to say that the growth is not being imprisoned, only guided. When a cistern is sealed, a few blind frogs are placed inside as witnesses.

Their greatest mistake is to forget that the Hollowloom is not their thing.

## Who Lives Here?

Who lives in the Hollowloom?

The moss lives there. The roots live there. The pale fungal skins live there. The water that has learned to become a town lives there.

The Comb-folk live there, in nested rooms of leaf and iron, with damp hair and watchful hands. They are people who have been swallowed enough to be sheltered and trained enough to shelter others. They live not on the land but inside its living structure, like threads in a loom that has begun to weave itself.

The wall-grazers live there, moving slow across wet stone, feeding on growth, returning it as fertile film.

The drip-newts live there, climbing from basin to basin, reading the town’s thirst before its residents know it.

The roof-hens live there, sleeping in engine grilles and leaf curtains, listening to pumps with bird-sense or whatever sense has replaced bird-sense.

The loom-spiders live there, spinning thread into belts and walls, turning silk into something stronger than cloth.

The boiler-moths live there, chewing scale and carrying warmth from one hidden chamber to another.

The spark-mites and electric beetles live there, feeding on current, clustering on wires, making the dynamos seem to have a mind of their own.

The cistern-fish live there, pale and patient, moving through water no larger than a hand-span.

The blind frogs live there, clicking pressure into song.

The bellowsbacks live there, large and translucent-skinned, breathing moss from their backs, turning the terraces into moving country.

The machines live there, half industrial and half botanical, turning not because they are dead but because they have been allowed to join the living tissue of the hollow.

And the Hollowloom itself lives there, as a room can live, as a dream can live, as a city can live when it has been forgotten by the surface and remembered by the earth.

It is not a wasteland. It is not a ruin. It is not a garden in any simple, beautiful sense. It is a damp subterranean town where the city and the forest have folded into each other, where water is combed rather than rushed, where roots become walls, where animals become infrastructure, where engines become lungs, and where the lives inside are so nested together that to remove one thread is not to uncover a floor, but to make the whole woven dark begin to dry.
