It is inhabited, first, by a household that cannot quite be called human-only, nor animal-only, nor plant-only, because the Drip-Hall has spent so many generations folding its occupants into its own made body.

If one were to give the people who live there a name, the name they use among themselves is simply **the level-keepers**, though outsiders might call them the **Hallborn**, or, less kindly, the **lower-room kin**. They are not a great tribe. They are not a lost civilization. They are a dense, practical family of dwellers, perhaps forty or fifty souls at any one time, more if the lofts are crowded, fewer if some have gone upward and do not return. They do not speak of the surface as a memory, because they are not nostalgic people; they speak of it as a place where the air is too thin to hold a thought, where the light is too generous and therefore too careless. Here, light is rationed, warmth is negotiated, water behaves as though it has a mind, and every living thing must account for itself before it is allowed to settle.

They are short, broad, warm-blooded people. Their feet have grown wide from years of balancing on rounded flagstones; their toes curl against damp edges; their ankles have learned, in sleep, to catch themselves before falling. Their shoulders are worn smooth at certain heights, as if the walls had taken impressions of them. Their knees are stained with clay and lime. Their hands are perhaps the most human part of them: cracked at the palms, strong at the fingers, capable of telling by touch whether a basin has gathered too much water, whether a rope has swollen, whether a pot has developed a hairline crack that will betray the room in the night. They carry small bone toggles, reed stoppers, bronze rings, cloth sponges, clay seals, and little pouches of dried grain in their apron pockets. Their wool clothes are never fully dry. They smell of warm dung smoke, milk, chalk, snail slime, wet reeds, and the particular mineral coolness of water that has traveled through pipes too long.

They are born inside. That matters. A child of the Drip-Hall does not first learn the sky. A child first learns the drip. It learns which ceiling tiles sweat first when the wind shifts above, which floor grooves fill fastest, which basins ring with a bright note and which give a dull, dangerous hollow. It learns to name things not by shape alone, but by behavior. The southern moss is not only moss; it is “the slippery one by the door,” or “the moss that has been told to stop.” The fern in the corner trough is not only fern; it is “the leak-hider,” and therefore untouchable for now, unless the room decides otherwise. A child’s first word is often not mother or father but something like *drip*, *goat*, *pot*, or *too much water*.

The Hallborn speak softly, because the room amplifies noise into obligation. They do not need to shout to be heard; they need to choose which sounds are allowed. The bronze plate is the one voice they permit above all others. When someone strikes it, the whole house of creatures answers: quail clatter, goats lift their heads, rats freeze in the clay tubes, bats stir in their niches, snails climb a little higher or retreat a little lower, and the water seems to pause in midair, listening. The plate is not used for ceremony except insofar as ceremony is anything that makes many beings agree on the next action.

Among them there are roles, though not fixed castes, because a role here is more a posture one adopts toward the room than a title one inherits.

The **Drip-Count** is one of the most respected. This person, often an elder, sometimes a child with strange patience, sits near the door at the hour when condensation begins to run, and counts. Not the drops themselves so much as the pattern. Three drops into the bowl near the grindstone means the southern pipe is opening. A long silence from the ceiling above the snail jars means the air has thickened and the room is about to give itself an indoor rain in one of its chosen corners. The Drip-Count does not claim to know the cause. They know the habit. In the Drip-Hall, habits are the truest form of weather.

The **Grinders** tend the Grinding Pot and the saddle querns. They measure grain, wet it, dry it, and move it through the day in clay scoops and reed baskets. They know when the water at the pot’s center becomes too clear, which can mean a spill has not yet been found, and when it becomes too cloudy, which can mean something has died where it should not have. They keep the starch rim clean at certain hours and leave it at others, because the snails are considered guests at the pot, and guests are sometimes fed. The Grinders’ arms are blackened at the elbows from leaning on smoke-darkened clay, and their hands are permanently roughened by basalt rollers. They are practical, irritable people, because grain is the room’s blood and blood must be measured.

The **Goat-Keepers** live with the vertical herd. The goats are too many for the horizontal hall, but they are exactly enough for the vertical one. They stand on mats woven of reed and thin wood strips, above baskets, above pits, above channels. Their hooves clatter whenever routine changes. They are not sentimental creatures. The most senior goat is often a broad, bad-tempered, gray-licked animal who knows the sound of the milk bucket before anyone enters the room and whose sneeze is trusted as a weather sign more reliable than several human elders. Goat-Keepers milk them into shallow bowls, skim the milk, and carry some to the snail jars as a rare treat. They also collect dung cakes from the pits, press them, dry them, and burn them in the low braziers. To them, the goats are not property so much as a warm engine of transformation: grain in, dung and milk out, heat, pits, mats, and smell all part of the room’s metabolism.

The **Loft-Wardens** manage the rats, mice, bats, and the upper shelves. This is high work. They climb rope-and-wood ladders, repair niches, hang clay tubes, and listen for the small knock of rat claws inside the tube-system. They are not rat-catchers; they are rat-employers. Rats are fed grain at set hours and allowed to run through the tubes, where their bodies knock seed pods loose and their feet pull husks into baskets. The Loft-Wardens collect these husks and return them to the pits or use them for kindling and stuffing. They never kill rats inside the tubes. If a rat dies there, the blockage is removed carefully, and the body is taken to the lower pit, because the old people say a dead rat in the tube brings misfortune. The younger Loft-Wardens sometimes say that a dead rat in the tube brings water, which is true enough: the blockage changes the flow, and the room responds. The rule is superstition with a wet edge.

The **Basin-Tenders** work low, among the crabs and snails. They clean the shallow basins, skim what floats, and move snail jars away from newly wet channels. They watch the crabs click against the ceramic and know, from the sharpness or dullness of their tapping, whether the water is right. If the crabs are too quiet, the Basin-Tenders worry. If the crabs are active, they are reassured, though not always safe; when the room grows too quiet and the water seems well, a crab may be caught for a meal, for medicine, or for salt. The Basin-Tenders are the ones who notice the small silver trails across the tiles and know which snails have climbed too far and which have not climbed enough. They are patient, slow people, or become so in that work, because haste breaks jars.

The **Moss-Judges** tend the green law. This is not gardening in the surface sense, where one admires abundance. Here every green thing must be interrogated. The moss at the threshold is scraped back because it is slippery. The moss near the north shelf is left because it hides a crack no one wishes to repair. The ferns in the corner trough are auspicious, or perhaps merely convenient, because they mark where a slow leak has found a comfortable hiding place. The vines in high pots are allowed only as long as they do not touch the floor; if they do, they become too eager, and someone cuts them down. The sprouted grain trays are fed grain water and watched for mold; if mold appears, the tray is dumped into the lower pits and lime is poured over it. Moss-Judges are often quiet, severe, and respected, because they understand the room’s appetite and are willing to say, *not here, not yet, too much*.

The **Fire-Herders** keep the braziers alive. They mix dried dung, oil, and reed fiber, feed the flames, move the iron rings if the clay lips begin to crack, and adjust the heat so the room remains close but not suffocating. They know where the lizards gather and do not shoo them away. They know that the bats are drawn to the warm air above the flames, but not too close, and that the quail need the warmth without the glare. Fire-Herders have faces with the particular darkness that comes from years near low smoke: eyes always slightly watery, brows faintly singed, cheeks shaded where soot settles.

The **Lamp-Movers** work at night. When the oil lamps are shifted from shelf to shelf, the shadows are not banished but invited to sit in useful places. They move a lamp to reveal a crack here, hide a basin there, brighten the passage near the door, darken the lofts where the bats prefer stillness. They do not fear shadow, because the Drip-Hall has taught them that darkness is storage. A shadow can hold a goat’s sleeping shape, a rat’s pause, a snail’s silver trail, a child’s fear, or a broken pipe’s shame. To illuminate everything is to accuse the room of being nothing but what it is at first sight.

These people do not own the hall in any legal sense that would satisfy a surface landlord. They belong to it as much as it belongs to them. Each family has a level and a routine. Each person has a groove they walk so often that the stone remembers them. They can identify one another not only by face or voice, but by the particular way each body disturbs the room. One person’s foot always catches the threshold groove. Another’s shoulder always grazes the tile near the Grinding Pot. A child’s breath sounds different when passing under a low shelf. An elder’s staff rings differently on wet stone. If someone is missing, the room seems to hold the wrong note.

But they are not the only inhabitants.

The goats are among the most visible, though they are rarely still enough to be contemplated. They are small, stocky, and surprisingly confident. Their hooves have worn the wooden platforms into shallow dips. Their horns are polished on brick edges. Their eyes are horizontal and calm, even when their bodies are frantic. They sneeze in a way that sounds like a question. Their milk is thin in winter and thick in summer. Their wool is combed, twisted, and felted into mats that are too thin to be soft but useful enough to catch moisture and wring back into pots. They are allowed near the Grinding Pot only at certain hours, because if they startle, their hooves make the room feel too large.

The rats are more hidden but no less organized. They live in stacked pottery lofts above the door, in a vertical warren of broken bowls, sealed pots, and reed nests. Some are brown, some gray, some cream. Their claws are bright little tools. They have names in the Loft-Warden’s mind, though not in human language: one may be the fat runner, the late sleeper, the tube-jammer, the thief of dried pods. They are fed grain and expected to work. If they do not knock enough husks loose, their portion is adjusted. If they become too bold, they are moved to a higher loft. If they become too many, the bats are fed, the lizards are encouraged near the floor, and the grain stores are sealed with clay. No one considers the rats a plague; they are a household labor class with poor manners.

The bats are the night-breath of the hall. They hang in niches carved into the ceiling vault, tucked so far into the stone that at first glance they look like lumps of old mortar. When they drop down, they make the air above the light shafts look stitched with movement. They are not shooed out at noon, because their flight path helps keep the quail calm. The Loft-Wardens collect their droppings from certain high shelves, not with delight, but with the dull acceptance that no creature lives for free in the Drip-Hall. The bats eat the insects that gather above the braziers. Their feeding is a service rendered, and the room accepts the small dry sounds of their passage as part of its chorus.

The quail and pigeons are the businesspeople of the high shelves. They do not sing with ambition. They call in short, practical bursts: *there, here, now, not now, too warm, too many, more grain*. Their feet tap continuously, like small dry rain on wood. They roost in tiers near the warmest wall, and their feathers are collected for brushing moss, marking seeds, and stuffing the smallest pouches. Their eggs are eaten with the same seriousness as all other food here. They are not beloved, but they are respected. They keep the birds’ presence as a signal. If the quail become too quiet, someone checks the air. If they become too loud, someone checks for a lizard where a lizard should not be.

The snails are the slow archivists. They gather in the dark lips of overflowing basins, under shelves, around the rim of the Grinding Pot, and along the mortar seams where condensation leaves its trace. They climb in silver trails that the Basin-Tenders can read like a ledger. They drink milk when it is offered. They taste sweet according to old belief. They are neither fully food nor fully workers. They are indicators. If the snails stop climbing, the air has changed, or the room has decided not to admit movement. If they gather too thickly in one basin, the water is too still, too rich, or too neglected. Children are allowed to touch them, but only gently, because the snails are old creatures, and in a room made for speed, anything that moves slowly deserves a kind of deference.

The crabs are the room’s tiny inspectors. They live in shallow water basins, clicking their claws against ceramic with bright, irritated certainty. They eat what floats into them. They clean the basins in small, crabbed ways. They are caught only when the room has grown too quiet, which is the wrong kind of quiet, not the quiet of sleep but the quiet of a stopped mechanism. If the crabs cease their clicking, someone must look at the water, because bad water can be silent before it becomes dangerous. They are not admired, but they are useful in the way a cracked cup is useful: it shows you how full the table was before the leak began.

The lizards are the unpaid guards. They sit on warm stone near the braziers, their bodies matching the color of aged clay so closely that they seem like small living fragments of the architecture. When they move, it is with sharp confidence, more mechanical than mammalian, as if the room had decided to make a tiny predator out of tile and patience. They eat smaller beetles. They take rat young if the lofts are disturbed. They are not fed directly, not housed, not named, and not mourned when they vanish, but no one removes them. They belong to the room’s defensive instinct.

The beetles are the crawling geography under every rim. Fat, iridescent, and persistent, they make the undersides of pots into territories. Their dry whisper across tiles is one of the hall’s faintest sounds, yet it is always there. They are eaten by lizards, avoided by goats, ignored by people, and tolerated by moss. They are part of the room’s secret economy, the kind that functions best when no one counts it.

The plants are watched with suspicion and care. Moss creeps over lower walls and thresholds, green and slick and necessary, until it becomes too slippery. Pale lichens stain stone near the shafts, making the light look older than it is. Ferns grow in clay troughs, their fronds curling in damp air, never lush, never absent. Vines hang in high pots and are allowed to trail only as far as they remain useful. Sprouted grain lives in shallow trays, green lawns of almost-future food, kept moist and examined daily. The plants are not celebrated. They are not beautiful in the surface sense of being allowed to grow into what they wish. They are beautiful because they persist, because they earn their place, because they have learned to live inside a made climate that tolerates them only so far.

Even the molds are inhabitants. They appear at the wrong time on grain trays and are killed by lime. They appear at the right time on reeds and are left to soften the fibers. They are not welcomed, but they are not denied either. In the Drip-Hall, the line between useful and pestilential is not drawn by nature; it is drawn by habit, work, and the water’s current mood.

And then there is the room itself.

The Drip-Hall is not merely a place where beings live. It lives. Not as a beast, perhaps, nor as a god, but as a climate, a body, a remembering. It sweats at known hours. It rains in one corner and not in another. Its walls darken where moisture has chosen its old routes. Its floor develops channels from repeated use until water obeys them more faithfully than it obeys the pipes. Its tiles remember when the wind shifts above. Its silence becomes a warning. Its chorus becomes a comfort. To the Hallborn, the room is a co-resident, a fellow household member, a thing with moods that must be respected. They do not pray to it, exactly. They listen to it. They argue with it by adjusting pipes, scraping moss, moving baskets, lighting fires, closing basins, and striking the bronze plate. The room answers by dripping, steaming, brightening, dimming, crowding, or thinning its sounds.

The inhabitants live within this reciprocity. They rise when the condensation begins to run. They move snail jars when the indoor rain gathers in its favorite corner. They bring grain trays under the mica panes when the upper air changes. They wring reed mats when the room exhales too much damp. They let water run to loosen the noise when the hall grows too sharp. They light oil lamps when the shadows become useful again. They do not dominate the hall, and they do not submit to it. They negotiate with it. They are the people of made walls, and they have made the walls into a people.

If one asks what lives in the Drip-Hall, the answer is therefore layered.

There are the Hallborn, the level-keepers, the drip-readers, the goat-keepers, the rat-mind, the basin-watchers, the moss-judges, the lamp-movers, the children who touch the water in the Grinding Pot and pull their feet back when the goats startle.

There are the animals, from the hooved and bellowing goats to the quiet, rim-climbing snails, from the industrious rats in their clay tubes to the bats stitching darkness with their wings.

There are the plants, the small mosses, pale lichens, curling ferns, trailing vines, and green trays of sprouted grain, each one tolerated under terms.

There are the insects and beetles, the unseen bacteria, the mold, the lime films, the mineral crusts, the dark trails in the grout, the stains shaped like branches, veins, and hands.

There is the water, behaving as though it were an inhabitant, returning to its chosen paths, filling its chosen basins, leaving its chosen marks.

And there is the hall, the made body, the constructed world, the small weather system, the loud and breathing pocket below the surface.

At midday, when the broad slab of daylight comes through the nearest shaft and falls across the Grinding Pot like a flat river, all of them are visible at once if one knows how to look. Dust dances in the light. Beetles freeze at its edge. A goat stands in the warm strip and twitches its ears until the light moves on. A lizard watches from a brazier stone. A child presses a palm to the pot’s rim. A snail finds the leftover starch and climbs. A rat knocks a dry husk in its tube somewhere above. A bat hangs in a niche, folded, patient. The quail tap. The crabs click. The water drops. Someone strikes the bronze plate, and the whole made world shifts by one breath.

That is what lives there: not one creature, not one household, not one species, but a dense and attentive agreement between many kinds of life and a room that has learned, over generations, to call them all its own.
