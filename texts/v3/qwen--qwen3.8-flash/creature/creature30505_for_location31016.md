What lives there are the Keepers.

There may be three of them, six, nine—numbers rise and fall with the water as if they, too, were governed by some hidden tide table. They are not drowned. They are housed. The Warm Sump has not swallowed them; it has learned their names and, having learned them, has made itself the shape of a home.

They came from above long enough ago that the above is now only a rumor in their bones. Some were engineers. Some were lamplighters. One, perhaps, was a tram driver who returned for a lunch pail left on the dashboard. One was a child. One was a mother. One was a clerk who had locked the door from the outside and lost the key when the water rose. They stayed for reasons, then stayed after the reasons became impossible, then stayed after staying became a body. Their old municipal roles did not disappear; they softened, fused, and grew into something else, like brick becoming reef.

Their bodies have gone the way of everything here. They are pale, but not the white of moonlight; they are the pale of wet paper held against old copper. Their skin carries a thin film of calcium and mineral shine, warm to the touch, almost soft, almost alive in the way that a long-loved object seems to have a breath of its own. Beneath that film, their flesh is the green-brown of the water itself, lightly translucent in places: along the wrists, behind the knees, under the throat, where the blue lines of veins can be seen moving with a slowness that has nothing to do with human circulation. Their hearts are present but quiet. The flywheel does more of the work.

They have gills, if that word still fits. They are not neat gills like a fish’s, not symmetrical things. Along the ribs they open as faint vertical slits, lined with the texture of old lace or wet velvet, darkened by moss, brightened by copper. When the flywheel descends, the slits part; when it climbs, they close, as though the body of each Keeper is synchronized to the machine’s breath. They have lungs, too, but those are smaller now, private organs, useful only in the upper pockets of air where condensation forms. In those pockets they can speak, but only as one might speak in a chapel, because the sound comes out damp and broken.

Their eyes have changed most.

The whites are gone. What remains is a milky glass over something deep, like shards of ceramic insulator polished by water. Their pupils are wide and slow to shift. They do not see the Sump the way a human sees a street; they see pressure, warmth, color, and vibration. They see the green halo around old lamps as one might see breath on a window. They see the faint amber glow of moss beds. They see the copper bacteria films on iron as thin streets of light. The rain from the ceiling, where it bends away from certain invisible architectures, is to them a kind of script. They can read where the water has been thinking.

They do not wear clothing so much as they wear accumulated history.

Old raincoats have fused into their shoulders, softened into a kind of second skin. Brass labels remain stitched in: PUMP 2, VALVE 4, TRAM LINE E, MAINTENANCE. Sometimes the letters have dissolved into leaf-shapes, and the Keepers touch them with their fingers as if reading a name written on a grave that is not a grave. Belts have become lattices of copper wire and barnacle shell. Pockets hold glass shards, tiny shrimp husks, flakes of paint, and fragments of the ceramic insulators they have gathered from the gaps between buildings. Their hair is filamentous, greenish, often tangled with eelgrass and long strands of kelp-like growth. In the highest air pockets, moths sometimes settle in it, thinking it is part of the architecture. They allow this.

Their hands are the most unsettling and the most tender. The fingers are long, slightly webbed with thin membranes that may once have been moss but now behave as if they belong to a body. Each fingertip is capped with a plate of calcium or old metal, worn smooth by valves. When they touch the pump, the touch is not like repair. It is like listening to a heartbeat. When they touch a plant, the plant bends a little toward them, not because it is alive in the way of ordinary plants, but because it recognizes maintenance.

They live everywhere and nowhere, because in the Warm Sump, everywhere is already occupied by water.

At high cycle, when the liquid lifts and brushes the underside of the rain ceiling, they rest in the streetcar that has become a chapel. Its windows are gone, but the Keepers have filled the frame with suspended curtains of filamentous green, so the seats glow faintly. Its wheels are anchor points. Its ceiling has become a nest of iron mesh, rope, shell, and tiny glowing buds. They do not sleep there in the human sense. They drift into a state like floating just above the bottom, bodies curled, rib-slits opening in time with the flywheel.

At low cycle, when the water sinks a few inches and barnacled thresholds appear, they work.

This is when they scrape the calcium from the iron gates. This is when they clean the lamp glass with the inside of their sleeves. This is when they adjust the valves not because the valves are failing, but because the Sump requires correction, the way a living body requires correction. They carry small cups made from brass nozzles, from broken glass insulators, from the tops of old bottles. They collect condensation where it falls thickest and pour it back into the channels where the water is too dense. They do not speak aloud, but their movements are a kind of ritual.

They tend the moths.

This is one of their quietest occupations. In the upper air pockets, where water thins into wet vapor and the emergency lamps cast a greenish haze, pale moths rest on gauges and pipe fittings. Their wings are damp and folded. The Keepers do not frighten them. They approach by the side, hands behind them, gill-slits half closed. If a moth has been still too long, a Keeper will touch the gauge with one finger, sending a tremor through the metal, and the moth will flutter once. If the moth does not move, the Keeper may carry it to another pocket, higher, drier, closer to the lamp. This is not a kindness the moths understand. It is not a kindness the Keepers understand either. It is maintenance.

They tend the eel.

Beneath the transformer case lies the oldest of them, or perhaps the first among them, though the distinction has worn away. It has lost its limbs, or perhaps never had limbs when it became what it is. Its body is pale and eel-like, with a faint blue line beneath the skin like a fish’s skeleton. It rests in a hollow of rust and calcium, motionless except when the flywheel turns. Then it rises slightly, as if hearing its own name. The Keepers call it Nozzle-Mother, though they may also call it something else, something closer to the hum of copper coils. Young Keepers press their foreheads to its flank during the thirty-seven-second rest, letting the rhythm enter them. They do not worship it. They remember it. It remembers them.

The fish are not pets. The shrimp are not food alone. The plant-fronds brushing the tram wires are not decoration. Everything lives there as part of the same slow municipal organism. If a Keeper passes through a channel where blind shrimp cluster in the crevices of brass fittings, the shrimp will flick at suspended particles and ignore the hand. If the Keeper pauses, the particles will continue to hang for a while afterward, disturbed only by the Keeper’s breath, which is so small that it resembles the exhalation of the water itself.
