Ask what lives in the Pocket of Brine Rain, and the first answer is not an animal. It is a condition.

Life here begins as a bloom, a softness, a thin film of something that has not yet decided whether it is mineral or living. It lies along the newest stone like a breath caught in glass. Pale bacterial mats spread in chalky veils across raw surfaces. They are not plants. They are not animals. They are not quite either. They are the first colonists, the ones who make the rock think it is soil. They consume the faint chemical seepage of the deep water and return it as light: a milked glow, a low phosphorescent patience. They cling to edges where the brine rain gathers, where the first heavy threads of cooled salt pull down from the ceiling and lose themselves in the dark. In these mats live the smallest citizens of the pocket, so numerous they are barely countable, so small they move like dust that has decided to persist.

If one insists on a creature, begin there.

The pocket is full of creatures, but its true inhabitant is a crowded patience. No being owns it. No species governs it. Its citizens are not individuals in the way surface creatures understand themselves; they are nodes in a living network of walls, streets, currents, mouths, filaments, shells, and secretions. A tube worm is a tower. A barnacle colony is a pavement. A molted exoskeleton is a temporary shelter. A drifting egg mass is a road, a nursery, and a meal all at once. The inhabitants live inside one another’s structures so completely that the boundary between resident and residence becomes thin, unreliable, almost meaningless.

There are tube worms, and they are among the most visible architects of this place.

They live upright in tubes of mineralized shell, their bodies soft and segmented inside narrow, hardened corridors. Their crowns rise from the tube mouths like small flames. They wave, flatten, contract, and extend again as the brine rain changes pressure. Each crown is a fan of filaments, lined with tiny cilia, catching particles, oxygen, chemical traces, the faint taste of dissolved metal. They are not flowers, though they seem to bloom in the dark. They are mouths and gills and antennae at once, living instruments tuned to the slow weather of the pocket.

Some tube worms are pale as bone. Others are rust-colored, green-tinged, or nearly translucent. Their tubes grow in clusters so dense they form skyline after skyline. They lean against one another for support. Their shoulders fuse. Their bases become buttresses. When one breaks, another settles nearby and begins to cement itself into the wound. The worms do not plan this. They do not build in the way a builder builds. They add themselves, generation after generation, until their combined habit looks like intention. They create vertical streets along the wall. They create balconies for small crabs. They create shelter for amphipods who press close in the gaps between tubes. They create a lattice so fine that a tiny isopod can hide inside a single cluster as though it were an entire forest.

The tube worms are citizens of the upper city, the rainward city, the city of ledges and crowns.

Below them, or perhaps beside them, depending on how one chooses to map a place that does not acknowledge maps, there are barnacles.

Barnacles are the hard floor-become-creature. They stand on their heads, open to the current, their legs fanned out like tiny feathered hands reaching into moving water. They are sessile, anchored, ancient in habit but fresh in place. They form pavements. They form rough mosaics of conical walls, pale with blue edging, their plates worn by current and pressure. In some places the barnacle floor rises in mounds; in others it flattens into a crowded square. They close their doors when the brine thickens and opens when the water steadies. They are not still. They are waiting with bodies.

Small creatures walk over them as though they were cobblestones. An amphipod hops from shell to shell. A juvenile crab steps carefully along a barnacle ledge. A cleaning organism, perhaps another barnacle, perhaps a tiny worm, picks at the plates of an older one, removing sediment and parasitic growths. The barnacles do not notice or perhaps notice only as pressure, as a faint disturbance in the boundary between safety and exposure. In this pocket, every body becomes a place to go.

There are bryozoans too, lace-makers, colonial animals so small they seem more pattern than creature. They spread across shells, tubes, mineral crusts, and stone in intricate networks of chambers. One bryozoan zooid is no larger than a pinhead, yet together they form walls that look like frost. They filter-feed with crowns of tiny tentacles, each opening no wider than a blink. In the dim pocket they resemble written symbols left by some forgotten script. The city is covered in their pale, fractal handwriting.

Among all these structures, the molluscs live.

Some are limpets, flattened against stone, gripping as though the rock itself might drift away if released. They rasp the biofilm with tongues made for slow grazing. Their shells are low domes, unshowy, brown-gray-green, adapted to pressure and patience. Other molluscs are snails, tiny as grains of sand, carrying spiral houses on their backs. They climb shells, tube-worm walls, and barnacle ridges with a delicacy that seems almost absurd in such a heavy place. Their bodies are soft under their coils; their movements are cautious. They live as though the world is made of edges that might cut.

There are bivalves too, partly buried in new sediment, shells half open, tasting the water with folded gills. They are not flashy. They are not large. They filter. They pause. They close when the brine rain turns thick. They are the quiet accountants of the pocket, managing particles, counting currents, making order out of drifting dust. Their empty shells become architecture. A broken valve, long dead, becomes a roof over a nursery channel. A scallop fragment becomes a bridge across a gap too wide for a small creature to trust. A pair of hinged shells, furred with bryozoans, becomes a house within a house. The molluscs do not merely inhabit the pocket; they leave behind the furniture of its future.

The crustaceans are the movers.

They are the most restless citizens, though “restless” in this cold place is relative. They move with calculation. They test the water with antennae that may be longer than their bodies. They step as though the surface could become slippery at any moment, and sometimes it does. Their shells vary in color: beige, slate blue, translucent green, pale pink, all muted by the dim light. They are small enough that a shadow, if there were one, would still be smaller.

Amphipods are among the most numerous. They have curved backs and sudden movements, like notes plucked in an almost silent room. They climb barnacle walls, dart between tubes, and gather in small swarms where detritus settles. They clean, graze, scavenge, and hide. They are everywhere, yet they do not dominate. They belong to the city the way dust belongs to movement: always present, always passing.

Isopods are broader, flatter, more careful. They move in low, deliberate steps, their segmented bodies sliding under shell lips and between worm tubes. They are grazers of the thin, valuable life: algae, bacterial films, decaying molt fragments. In the scarcity of this pocket, a patch of biofilm is a commons, and isopods are the quiet claimants who arrive, feed, and withdraw without seeming to notice they have altered the balance of a whole surface. They are also passengers. An isopod may ride along the edge of an egg string, clinging to the silk as though it were a rope stretched over a dark canal.

Crabs are the engineers of disturbance.

Some are tiny, only a few millimeters across the carapace, but they have the posture of beings who believe themselves substantial. They wedge into shell piles. They pull sediment aside. They inspect tubes with cautious claws. Some have mismatched claws: one small and precise, one larger and blunt, perhaps for holding, perhaps for cracking, perhaps for pushing aside another creature’s wall. They climb barnacle ledges. They move under shell roofs. They patrol the drainage hollows. They are predators and scavengers, builders and destroyers, residents and temporary weather.

A crab may take a molted skin from a crevice and drag it across the floor. It may enter an empty worm tube and remain there for hours, not because it made the tube, but because the pocket is full of borrowed rooms. It may disturb a bivalve’s half-open shell and scatter a cloud of mineral dust. That dust may settle elsewhere, becoming part of another creature’s foundation. Nothing here is only itself. Every body is a potential building material, a food source, a shelter, or a map for someone else’s movement.

There are polychaete worms, too, the long, segmented, bristled inhabitants of the sediment and the tunnels.

Some live inside tubes, like the tube worms, but more secretive, more withdrawn. Others crawl through the soft floor with bodies that move like slow waves. Their parapodia ripple; their bristles catch the mineral dust. They dig, rework sediment, and leave behind faint channels that may become drainage paths for younger, smaller creatures. They are not flashy, but they are necessary. The pocket’s lower floors depend on them. The sediment breathes because they move through it. The grain becomes rearranged because they do not understand themselves as architects.

Some polychaetes extend only their heads from their tubes, tasting the rain. Others leave their homes entirely and drift through the brine curtains, cloaked in segmented bodies, like small travelers moving through a city that cannot see them. They may carry eggs. They may feed on detritus. They may become prey. In this place, those categories overlap constantly.

There are shrimps, translucent and quick, though “quick” is a relative term in a world of cold. They fold their antennae along their bodies and move through shell corridors with a cautious, rippling propulsion. They are almost invisible until a limb catches the faint glow. They are predators, scavengers, cleaners, and food. A shrimp may probe the barnacle field with one long feeler, sensing the edge of a small fish, then withdraw beneath a scallop fragment. The absence of its body is one of the city’s small changes, one of its silent renovations.

There are small crabs with pale legs, small amphipods with too many eyes, and other crustaceans whose forms would be difficult to name if one tried too hard. Many are translucent. Many are soft-edged. Many are built for concealment. The pocket favors bodies that can become edges, lines, glimmers, not shapes that insist on themselves.

Among these are the rare vertebrates, the larger-bodied citizens, though “larger” still means only a few centimeters at most.

There are fish that look as though they were designed without color in mind. Pale, slender, with oversized heads and mouths slightly too wide for their bodies, they rest beneath shell roofs and inside the hollows between fused worm tubes. Their eyes are too large for the darkness, catching what little light there is and returning it as tiny pinpricks. They are not bright, not sleek, not obviously beautiful. They are adapted to a world where motion costs more than it would in warmer places. They conserve. They hover. They wait.

Some resemble cusk-eels more than surface fish. They are soft-bodied and pale, almost eyeless in appearance, though they are not blind; they are merely not dependent on the kind of sight that expects light. They lie in hollows, mouths slightly open, tasting the water. They may strike with a suddenness that seems impossible in such a calm place. One moment there is an amphipod clinging to a barnacle wall; the next there is not. No splash. No cry. Only a shift in pressure, a slight bend of a current, and a mouth closing around a small absence.

There are goby-like creatures as well, transparent or near so, hovering with fins spread like wet paper. They do not dart like familiar fish. They hold themselves in the moving water as though their bodies have been given a task too delicate to rush. Their heads are large relative to their bodies. Their movements are strange, almost tentative. They belong to the upper and middle levels, where the rain is thinnest and the light has not yet surrendered completely. They may watch a tube-worm crown tremble. They may pass through a curtain of brine so slowly that the fluid seems to part around them by courtesy.

There are larval forms that may not remain at all.

They arrive from outside through the hair-thin seam, or they are born inside and drift upward by accident. Some are no larger than grains of dust. Some are translucent ribbons of tissue. Some belong to species that may never touch the pocket again after being swept away. They are the city’s travelers, its temporary population, its uncertain future. Many are eaten before they can become permanent. Many leave before they can become residents. But their passing changes the city. A larva may settle into a new sediment bed and become a worm. A drifting egg may be caught on a mucus thread and become a nursery. A passing jelly-like ribbon may trail stinging filaments that alter the behavior of a small shrimp for hours. The pocket is never closed, even when sealed. It is porous. It leaks life.

Occasionally, something stranger comes through the brine rain.

A juvenile siphonophore, ribbon-thin and nearly invisible, may drift through the upper veil. Its cells are too small to distinguish; its body is a cooperative organism made of tiny specialists. It does not build the city, does not clean the lanes, does not settle into the shell terraces. It passes through like a message written in an unknown language. A tiny squid, mantle only a few millimeters long, may hover with black eyes large for its body. It does not seem frightened. It seems observant. It watches the pocket with a stare too deliberate for its size, then retreats through the rain. It may be a hunter, a wanderer, a doomed guest, or a resident so new to the city that it does not yet understand its own belonging.

All these creatures are small, but their smallness is not the point. In this place, smallness is a civic virtue. A body too large would obstruct the streets, consume the thin resources, and destroy the delicate architecture of living surfaces. The pocket favors the compact, the layered, the indirect. It rewards creatures who can become part of the environment rather than masters of it.

Even the largest resident, the pocket itself, is not a single organism. It is a composite creature made of mineral and living tissue, water and pressure, birth and decay. The bacterial mats are its skin. The tube-worm towers are its spine. The barnacle pavements are its floor. The shell ramps are its roofs. The mucus threads are its nerves. The currents are its breath. The molting cycles are its shedding skin. The spawning events are its dreams.

This is not metaphor in the way a city is like a body. Here, the analogy is too loose. The pocket is more literal than that. It is an arrangement of bodies that behaves as one system without being one animal. It has metabolism. It has growth. It has waste. It has repair. It has reproduction. It has predation. It has mutual dependence. It has rhythm. It has no brain.

The citizens of the pocket do not know one another as individuals, but they know one another as conditions.

A crab does not recognize a worm. It recognizes an opening, a texture, a probability. A shrimp does not fear a fish by name. It fears a displacement of water. A amphipod does not know it is cleaning a shell. It knows only that detritus is edible. The rules are local and sensory: taste, touch, pressure, shadow, chemical trace, the faint taste of metal. The inhabitants live by immediate knowledge, not by long-term memory. Yet across generations, their combined actions make a city.

The predators move like weather fronts. A too-wide mouth enters a shell lane. The current shifts. Small bodies tuck into barnacle gaps. A tube crown flattens. The fish passes. The absence is more dramatic than the strike. The city resumes.

The scavengers move like cleanup crews without flags or tools. A molted exoskeleton appears in a crevice, pale and crinkled, abandoned by its owner in the night that is not night. It is not trash. It is shelter. It is mineral. It is surface. It is food for bacteria. It is a home for something too soft to survive in the open. It is a bridge for a young crab learning to climb. It is a landmark for an amphipod navigating a newly flooded shelf. The scavengers strip it of edible tissue. The grazers pick at its biofilm. The burrowers break its edges. Eventually it dissolves back into the pocket’s slow circulation of matter.

The cleaners are everywhere. Some amphipods pick at the plates of barnacles. Small worms live in the waste channels of molluscs, feeding on expelled particles while preventing blockage. A curled isopod rides an egg mass and may eat the drifting silk strands or simply remain until the current changes. A shrimp removes detritus from the shell of a larger creature, gaining a few crumbs and in return improving the flow across its host’s surface. These relationships are not generous. They are necessary. In such density, every body must become useful, or it becomes buried.

The larvae are the future and the uncertainty.

They spin in eddies like motes of living salt. Some settle. Some are eaten. Some drift out through the unseen seam into a darkness that is not the pocket’s darkness. Those that remain must find a place where the current is mild enough, the mineral surface suitable, the predators too occupied, the biofilm thick enough. Settlement is a decision made by chance and chemistry. A larva touches the right surface and changes. It begins to build. It becomes a resident. It becomes a wall.

In this pocket, a home is rarely built in a straight line. It is built by accident, repetition, and survival.

A worm tube may begin as a shelter for a single creature. If other worms settle near it, their tubes may fuse. If barnacles colonize the fused structure, it becomes harder, more permanent, more vertical. If shells accumulate around it, it becomes a platform. If the current carries larvae past it, it becomes a nursery. If a predator uses it as ambush, it becomes a hazard. If a scavenger uses its abandoned crown as a bridge, it becomes a street. The same structure is never only one thing. The inhabitants do not need names for these roles. They simply use them, modify them, abandon them, rebuild them.

There are residents too small to see.

They live in the mineral skin, in the spaces between crystal, in the pores of bacterial mats, in the hollows left by dissolved shells. They are archaea and bacteria, perhaps fungi, perhaps organisms so unfamiliar that human categories strain to fit them. They process sulfur. They fix minerals. They create the faint glow that lights the lower city. They are not background scenery. They are the metabolic base. Without them, the pocket would be only stone and water. With them, it becomes a place where life can be dense enough to appear urban.

The tiny, almost invisible creatures include those that might be mistaken for debris. A speck of white may be a juvenile mollusc half buried in new crust. A thread of transparency may be a nematode moving through sediment. A cluster of dots may be a colony of diatoms or a patch of chemosynthetic filaments. A flake may be molt. A pulse may be a ciliate. A cloud may be a group of newly hatched larvae caught in a microcurrent. The boundary between life and matter is thin, and the pocket lives in that boundary.

Some inhabitants are barely visible because they are made of mucus and light. They cling to the underside of shells, forming films that shift with the current. They are grazed, scraped, rebuilt, and consumed. They are not dramatic. They are the city’s lubrication, its first growth layer, its quiet glue.

The whole place is governed by the brine rain.

The creatures do not love it or fear it in any human sense. They answer it. When the rain is gentle, they extend. When it thickens, they withdraw. When it stops, they move with rare boldness, as though the world has been given a small permission slip. During the gentle rain, tube-worm crowns open. Shrimp cross lanes. Fish hover. Barnacles feed. The city is visible. During the heavy rain, the city disappears into itself. The inhabitants do not abandon the streets; they become the streets. They fold into shells. They close doors. They tuck bodies into tubes. They flatten against walls. They let the rain pass over them, through them, around them. The city remains, but it contracts, becomes secret, becomes architecture again rather than activity.

The rain also feeds. It carries particles. It disturbs surfaces. It brings chemistry. It causes mineral dust to settle. It creates the slow, constant renewal of surfaces. A crust may form over a shell path. A bacterial bloom may cover a broken tube. A fresh layer of carbonate may glue two surfaces together. The creatures live in a place that is always being repaired, not by intention, but by the same process that is always being inhabited.

The light, weak as it is, matters.

The tube worms see through contrast, not color. The small fish navigate by the difference between glow and shadow. The amphipods use the faint luminescence of mineral edges to locate shelter. The bacterial mats do not need light to live, but the pocket’s creatures need edges. They need to know where a wall ends and water begins. They need to find the boundary between tube and gap, shell and floor, safe and unsafe. The light is not illumination. It is punctuation. It tells them where the next word is.

The sounds are almost not sounds. They are pressures.

A leg on shell. A claw on tube. A jaw closing around a particle. A molt sliding across a ledge. A body folding into a chamber. A current tapping an arch. The pocket absorbs these. The soft tissues damp them. The density scatters them. There is no echo, because there is no open space large enough for the water to confess itself back. The silence is not empty. It is full of small events happening without permission.

If one could name a citizen, perhaps one would name the city itself.

But if one insists on plural beings, then the inhabitants are these:

the pale bacterial mat that begins everything by making the stone usable; the tube worm that grows upward into the falling rain, fanning its crown against the current; the barnacle that makes pavement of its own body; the bryozoan that writes lace over living and dead; the limpet that scrapes the scarce film from a fresh mineral crust; the snail whose shell is a grain of stone and whose life is a slow spiral of caution; the bivalve that filters and closes and half-burries itself in the new sediment; the amphipod that moves like a punctuation mark through the barnacle fields; the isopod that grazes with the patience of a cleaner; the crab that disturbs and borrows and builds by destruction; the polychaete that tunnels and tastes and carries its soft body through the lower city; the shrimp that folds itself into the water as though trying to become absent; the translucent fish with oversized eyes, resting beneath a shell roof; the larva that arrives by accident and may become permanent; the molting crustacean that sheds its own shelter; the scavenger that consumes the shed and thereby maintains the streets; the egg rider that clings to silk and drifts through lanes; the tiny squid whose black stare seems to contain more attention than its body can justify; the siphonophore ribbon that passes through the rain like a visitor who has not been invited and yet changes the air—or water; the bacterial glow at the lowest level, where the new sediment settles and the pocket’s metabolism hums below silence.

They do not form a society because they have no society in the way humans recognize one. They form a city because they are forced into proximity by density, scarcity, cold, and the need for shelter. Their movements repeat. Their wastes become foundations. Their deaths become architecture. Their bodies become places for other bodies. Their habits accumulate until the whole seems engineered, though no one engineered it. Their relationships are not laws. They are tendencies, patterns, pressures, flows.

The pocket’s inhabitants live by a logic of layers.

Above, the fragile life: bacteria, filaments, drifting threads, a single transparent thing hovering like a suspended seed. Below that, the vertical suburb of tube worms and barnacles, crowded with crabs and amphipods. Below that, the middle city, shell lanes, mucus bridges, eddies where larvae turn. Below that, the dark sediment and nursery hollows, the predator’s mouth, the new mineral floor, the drainage gap. The districts are not separated by gates. They are separated by pressure, current, light, and the small decisions of bodies too poor to travel far.

They live with borrowed time.

The pocket is young. It has no ancient stones. It has no mature reef. It has no settled landscape. The raw walls are still becoming. The sediment is still learning. The tubes are still joining. The shells are still being arranged. The creatures live in the middle of becoming, which may be the only way any life can live. They do not know the place is unfinished. They do not need to. They add to it by being born, feeding, molting, dying, and leaving behind the materials for others. The city grows because life is sticky and persistent. It grows because the creatures cannot help building the next room by finishing the previous one.

They are patient. Not because patience is a virtue, but because cold makes haste expensive. They move only when movement is necessary. They extend only when extension is worthwhile. They close only when closure is needed. They strike only when the prey is close. They flee only when the pressure wave says they are seen. Their lives are not dramatic because drama costs energy. They are economical. Every motion has a return. Every shell has a purpose. Every current has a consequence.

They are also not lonely.

Loneliness requires an awareness of absence that this place may not have. Still, there is a kind of intimacy here. Bodies press against other bodies. A shrimp passes through a tube cluster so dense that its antennae touch ten organisms in the space of a breath. A crab steps over a barnacle field so crowded that the shell plates seem to argue with one another. A fish lies beneath a shell roof that once housed a living mollusc. A worm extends beside another worm, their crowns nearly touching, their tubes fused by mineral deposits. There is no privacy in the pocket, because privacy requires space, and space is occupied by life.

There is no center, except the drain.

The drain is where the pocket makes itself livable by letting go. Brine gathers, cools, pools, and slips away through a hidden gap. This is not a sewer in the human sense; it is not dirty or shameful. It is circulation. It is the city’s exhale. Around it, the density may be greater because water is slower, because particles settle, because some creatures prefer calm edges. Some predators may rest nearby because prey gathers where the current hesitates. The drain is therefore also a place of danger, a threshold, a quiet mouth.

There is no center, except the ceiling.

The ceiling is where the rain begins. The water there is cold, dense, mineral-touched. It gathers, thins, then falls. The creatures above it live by its mood. They may cling to the underside of a shell overhang, sheltering from the first fall. They may taste the change before it arrives. The ceiling is not a roof in the sense of protection; it is a boundary that generates weather. The pocket’s climate is made above, and its inhabitants live by the rhythm of its falling.

There is no center, except the new stone.

The new stone is where the pocket is still growing. Glassy shards. Mineral films. Tiny crystals. It is not finished, not safe, not stable. Some organisms avoid it. Some settle on it. Some turn it into shelter within a generation. The new stone is the future of the city, though the city has no idea what a future is. It simply continues.

The inhabitants do not think about the pocket.

If they think at all, they think in the way small animals think: by gradient, by taste, by tension, by memory too short to be called memory. A shrimp knows the lane because the lane has the right number of shells. A crab knows the molting court because the shed skins offer temporary cover. A worm knows the upper wall because the current carries enough particles and not too much heat. A fish knows the hollow because it smells of previous fish and small prey. They do not map the city. They move through it because their bodies remember what works.

And yet, if one watches long enough, the pocket seems to have character.

It is cautious. It is crowded. It is quiet. It is mineral. It is hungry. It is tender. It is not gentle, but it is not cruel in a theatrical way. Predation is small. Birth is small. Death is small. The city absorbs everything without mourning. A shell becomes rubble, then floor, then shelter, then wall, then dust, then foundation again. A body becomes molt, then food, then mineral, then light, then structure. The pocket recycles its own inhabitants with the patience of stone and the insistence of life.

If a single word were needed for the thing that lives there, perhaps the word would be “continuation.”

The inhabitants are not a nation. They are a persistence. They are the repeated act of making places livable by being too small, too many, and too patient to leave. They are the city made by bodies that cannot conceive of cities. They are the architecture of survival without intention. They are the brine rain’s audience, the dim light’s witnesses, the raw stone’s first tenants.

They are the pocket’s living answer to the question of why anything is here.

The tube worms lean into the falling rain and say, with their feathers, that the water can be tasted, that the particles can be caught, that the cold can be tolerated, that the dark can be used. The barnacles cement themselves in rows and say, with their shells, that pressure can be made into floor, that time can be turned into pavement. The molluscs leave their empty valves in terraces and say, without knowing they are saying it, that death can become infrastructure. The crustaceans molt and say, by the sudden softness of their new bodies, that growth requires risk, that shelter can be shed, that a home can be a temporary skin. The tiny fish rest and say, with their too-large eyes, that light is enough when one only needs edges. The bacterial mats glow and say, in the language of chemistry, that life can begin by making stone useful.

All of them together say something the pocket itself seems to know without knowing: that even a hidden bead of unfinished stone, even a narrow seam no wider than a hair, even a room of bitter liquid and failing light, can become crowded, organized, alive.

If one looks closely, the inhabitants are everywhere, though none of them seems important enough to describe alone.

They are in the shell lane, where a current sweeps particles along a narrow groove like wind through a street market. They are in the molt court, where abandoned skins lie crinkled in the dark like discarded papers. They are in the nursery spiral, where larvae settle in soft sediment and broken shells. They are in the drainage hollow, where brine pools before vanishing. They are in the tube-worm skyline, where tiny crabs climb parapets. They are in the barnacle plaza, where amphipods hop from one living tile to the next. They are in the mucus threads, where drifting eggs hang like beads and a curled isopod grips like a traveler at a railing. They are in the dark lower levels, where the predator with the too-wide mouth waits with a stillness that is not peace but preparation.

They are not a parade. They are not a cast. They are more like the city’s continuous pulse: one movement after another, one small life replacing another, one structure growing over another, one absence becoming another creature’s opportunity.

The pocket’s true inhabitants are, therefore, the pattern itself.

But the pattern is made of flesh.

It is made of tube worms whose crowns open like slow flames. It is made of barnacles whose doors close with a soft, almost inaudible snap. It is made of amphipods whose antennae twitch through the dim. It is made of crabs whose claws test walls that were once other crabs’ shelters. It is made of fish whose eyes are black mirrors in the mineral glow. It is made of worms whose soft bodies move through sediment as though sediment were a living tissue. It is made of shrimps that fold themselves into the current. It is made of larvae that drift in the brine rain, neither resident nor visitor, only possibility. It is made of bacterial mats that spread their chalky light over the newest stone, making the pocket less like a wound in the rock and more like a beginning.

They do not know they are making a city.

They know only the way down, the way across, the way to the safe crevice, the way to the particle current, the way to the place where another body has already opened a room.

And still, they build.

And still, the brine rain falls.

And still, in the hidden glass bead of unfinished stone, the tiny citizens live their cold, quiet, crowded lives, moving through shells, tubes, mucus lanes, and mineral courtyards, each one small enough to be overlooked, each one part of the ceaseless arrangement that makes the pocket breathe.
