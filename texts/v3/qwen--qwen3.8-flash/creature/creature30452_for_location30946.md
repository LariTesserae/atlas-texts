What lives there is not a people. It is a scattering of peoples, a thousand small occupations of dampness, each one unaware of the others except as sound, smell, and movement. Nothing builds. Nothing stores. Nothing makes a place out of another place. The hollow is inhabited in the way a body is inhabited by weather: by cycles of moisture, warmth, death, and renewal, not by architecture.

If one comes down into the basin and stands at the edge of the fresh rock ridge, the first truth of the place is that the eye wants a community it cannot find. There is no forest of creatures, no field, no nest, no den, no swarm so large that it becomes a visible cloud. There is only a rhythm of presence and absence. A pool shines like a small moon in the grey floor, and around it the stone teems. Beyond it, the ground may be empty for hundreds of meters. The life is there, but it is there only where the conditions allow it: where water remains, where mineral crusts trap dust, where a ledge holds heat, where a roof overhang shelters from the thin falling drip, where guano has softened the chalk into a black fertile paste.

The true first inhabitants are not the animals the eye can name. They are films. Thin, living skins cover the wet stone. Algae spread in pale green veils wherever the faint light reaches, so slight that they look less like growth than like a stain the stone is remembering. Bacteria cloud the rims of pools with a film that gives them a milked appearance. Fungi, if they can be called fungi, grow in the dark interiors of cracks, white roots threading through detritus too fine to see. In the mineral crusts, microscopic organisms feed on chalk dust and dissolved organic matter. The hollow begins with these tiny skins and residues. They are the place’s first words. Everything larger is spoken in relation to them.

The pools are the loudest of the small cities.

A pool may be only as wide as two hands placed together, or as wide as a small pond, but its population is almost unbearable. Blind fish hover in the cold water like fragments of pale glass. Their bodies are so translucent that the slow pulse of something dark inside them may be seen through their own sides. They do not seem to see the eye looking down at them. They move by pressure, by taste, by the tremor of wings and legs in the water above. They are not beautiful in the ordinary sense; they are beautiful in the way a cold tool is beautiful, because every part of them is fitted to an environment that has no light to waste. Their fins are delicate and almost colorless. Their mouths are small and always searching the surface, the bottom, the walls, the undersides of mineral rims.

Around them move things that look like pale insects until one realizes they are crustaceans, or perhaps insects whose descendants have learned to live in water the way fish have learned to live in air. Blind isopods climb the white mineral rims and disappear into the water with a suddenness that makes the surface look as if it has swallowed them. Shrimp-like creatures hang just beneath the surface film, their antennae trembling in currents too small for the hand to feel. When a drop falls into the pool, they scatter like dust disturbed by breath. When a bat squeaks overhead and the water vibrates, they cluster again. They have no fear of the eye because they have no eyes, only a knowledge of pressure and motion.

There are snails too, or things very close to snails. Their shells are thin and translucent, often stained by minerals into yellow or grey. They graze the calcite rims with tiny teeth that are almost too patient to hear. They make the shallow grooves in the stone look as if someone had passed a soft needle across it again and again. Their slime dries into faint silver tracks. They are slow residents of the pools, not swarming like the shrimp or darting like the fish, but clinging, feeding, resting under mineral lips. If a fish snaps near a snail, the snail does not flee; it simply closes into a smaller world. It has learned that the best defense is to become a stone.

At the edges of the pools, where water becomes dampness and dampness becomes rock, the next layer of life waits. Crayfish-like creatures, no larger than a thumb, sift through wet gravel with long, feathered antennae. They are pale as wet paper, but their bodies have a harder sheen, as if they are armored with the same mineral that rims the water. They do not hunt with speed. They hunt with patience. They feel the gravel for movement, for dead flesh, for larval tremors. A pool disturbed by a footstep becomes a storm in their world; a body falling into the water becomes a feast for dozens of antennae in minutes.

The wet walls are not walls to them. They are roads, farms, hiding places, breeding grounds. Where condensation leaves a continuous film across limestone, tiny creatures ride it like insects crossing a bridge that is always being made and unmade. Springtails jump from mineral grain to mineral grain. Mites, no larger than a pinhead, move in dense communities across the blackened guano of the ledges. Flies and midges gather in films of slime where algae grow in the light’s memory. Their eggs are laid not in a nest, because no such thing exists here, but in the thin wet crevice, the dust pocket, the edge of a drop that has dried into a crust.

Moths are among the quietest inhabitants and yet, in the aggregate, one of the most visible.
