It is a low bowl in a warm young land, valley-sized and town-sized at once, and what lives there is not one kind of being but a slow, breathing parliament: people, vines, fungi, waters, and the small bright animals that have learned to use the same architecture.

The Trellis Basin began as a place too soft and too quick to grow for clearing. The hills had recently folded themselves into their present shape, and the young soil steamed after rain. Things rose too fast to be chopped down, too eager to be left alone. So the first inhabitants did not make a town by removing the forest. They made it by asking the forest to keep its weight. Over generations, they trained climbing stems, grafted branches, tied stone to wood, and let the whole valley grow into something that could hold a household, a street, a market, a temple, a sleeping room, and still remain a wilderness.

Now the basin is a town made from living arches. The trellises are its streets, its fences, its roofs, and often its rooms. Ironvine thick as a child’s leg has been trained over basalt ledges, around carved stone rings, through holes in the cliff-face. Lanternwood has been grafted into it, so that at night the streets do not need lamps: sap beads swell in small sacs and give off a pale amber light, enough to read by, enough to find the next rung. Moss covers the north-facing roots. Pale threadfungi weave between the roots, holding rain through the dry weeks. When you enter from the low eastern pass, the first thing you notice is not the town but its shade: a green, moving ceiling, stitched through by voices and footsteps.

The people of the Trellis Basin are still recognizably human, though many generations of climbing have made their hands unusually clever and their bodies light. Their palms are broad and darkened by sap, their feet are quiet on a rung, and their eyes have learned to read the basin in patches of light and leaf rather than in flat horizons. They have no houses in the way flatland people do. A family’s home is a grafted chamber, a ladder-loop, a platform behind a screen of flowering vines. If the vine grows too thick, the room is pruned. If a branch weakens, the room is moved, sometimes in one afternoon, with beds and cooking pots still carried in nets.

They call themselves Basin-born in ordinary speech, but outsiders have named them for their work: trelliswrights, graft-keepers, spore-readers, ladder-tenders, thermal-wrights. These are not rigid classes so much as duties that change with age, season, and temperament. A child may spend one year helping catch fruit in leaf-baskets, the next year learning to read the color of sap. An adult may be a mason one morning, a midwife one evening, a negotiator between households the next. The basin has no central square; it has a thousand small intersections where vines cross, and those are squares enough for gossip, barter, and the exchange of cuttings.

Strangers are not refused, but neither are they casually admitted. The basin does not have gates in the usual sense. To become resident is to be grafted: first you climb when asked, then you carry, then you prune, then you know where the water is heavy and where the wind is loose. If a person stays one year, helps through a storm, and learns to tie a vine without cutting the inner skin, they may be given a rung with their name scratched near its base. That is not citizenship, exactly. It is closer to being added to a family of places.

What lives there is also what makes the architecture hold. Beneath the roots, a fungal web spans the basin floor. The people call it the underthread; it is not intelligent, but it is attentive in the way natural things are attentive. It moves nutrients from healthy trunks to wounded ones, dries pools in the wet season, and tells a person where to dig if they are patient enough to follow its threads. Some of the oldest trellises are kept alive only because a household has learned the pattern of its underthread, adding leaf-litter in certain rings, leaving mushrooms untouched, never letting livestock stand too long in one place. To ruin the underthread is not vandalism. It is a kind of forgetting.

The animals are not outside the town either. They are the basin’s tenants and engineers.

There are sievebirds with flat, comb-like beaks that drink nectar from the high lanternwood blossoms; as they feed, they scatter seed onto the trellis beams. There are velvet-hoofed climbers, deer-like but smaller, that step along broad vines and browse only the leaves chosen for pruning, so they double as gardeners. Their dung feeds the moss beds. There are ash-rats, quick and copper-tailed, that cache seeds in the warm stones and dig tunnels through fallen leaves; in bad years they become the basin’s pantry, because their scattered stores sprout into new growth. There are bats with translucent ears that roost on the underside of the trellis arches, and in the wet season they leave in columns at dusk to hunt insects over the flooded meadow, returning at dawn to hang like little cloaks of fur and leather.

In the lowest part of the bowl, where the young land still remembers heat, lie the thermal pools. They are not wild places alone: they are kitchens, laundries, healing baths, and gardens. People have built steps of black stone into them and trained heat-loving plants along the rims—reed-grass for weaving, water-mints for breathing sickness, thick tubers that soften faster in mineral warmth than in any fire. The springs are home to pale thread-salamanders and crabs with shells like river pebbles. The basin folk do not simply take from the pools; they have schedules. In the cool months they may fish the thermal margins, but in the breeding months they take only from certain stones and return the females with the eggs. The rules are kept by the pool-houses: families whose ancestors first built steps there. These are respected not because the law commands it, but because if the pools are mistreated, everything above them withers.

At a larger scale, the Trellis Basin lives as a town of gardens and a garden of towns. It is valley-sized because its fields climb the slopes and follow the watershed; it is town-sized because its streets are dense enough to argue, to fall in love, to carry messages, to host wakes. A visitor might be disoriented: where does a forest end and a street begin? The answer is nowhere and everywhere. A bridge is a living vine; a fence is a row of trained shrubs; a market stall is a platform under a fig-like canopy; a nursery may be built into the hollow of a fallen trunk, and the child inside it may never know which tree once stood there unless an adult tells them.

Daily life is shaped by weather and growth. In the dry season the people climb high to open vents in the trellis roofs and let air move between the leaf-layers. They carry water in clay bladders from the upper cisterns and pour it into root-cups at the base of each arch. In the wet season, the basin steams because the young ground warms the rain before it sinks; people work by day and gather in the covered terraces at dusk. They eat fruits that taste of heat and mineral sweetness, tubers roasted in spring sand, fish smoked over dried reeds, and bread made from grain grown on stone shelves. The basin is not poor, but its abundance is careful: it comes from tending, not from extracting.

There are also those who are more human than human, or perhaps human in a way that requires the whole town.

The graft-mothers, for example, are older people who have learned to read the tension in vines by touch. They do not merely inspect trellises; they feel where the load is too heavy, where a storm might pull a ladder loose, where a house’s roots are beginning to lift. Some spend their lives listening. They are not priests, but if a child asks them for a blessing, the blessing is practical: may your hands stay cool, may your rung hold, may the vine know you as part of it.

The wind-callers are younger, quick climbers who race up the highest arches before storms and sing or strike iron rings so the household knows which way to bind. Their songs are not just warnings; they are maps of memory, because each verse names a place, a tree, a family, an old repair.

The spore-readers kneel in the cool hours and study the fungal rings around the roots. Some can tell from the thickness of a white thread whether a pool is turning sour, whether a trellis beam is drying too fast, whether a storm has carried blight from the lower valley. They carry small knives and soft brushes and speak little unless asked, and even then their answers come in fragments: *the east arch is listening,* or *the water here is too proud of itself,* or *that room has been sick for three nights.*

The basin has a kind of memory that is not written only in books. Many of its people mark themselves with vine-sap, so that after a year of work their hands look stained as if they have carried the town inside their skin. Children sometimes grow up with the mistaken belief that this is natural, that all hands darken, that all roofs breathe. The written records are kept in leaf-books, but more important are the graft-books: not words so much as living diagrams. A graft-book is a panel of young wood with cuts and bindings; it records who joined which family to which tree, which ladder was built after the Great Slip, which thermal spring was reopened after the cistern cracked. To read one is to touch time without being trapped by it.

Outsiders ask who lives there as if the answer could be one name. It cannot.

If you enter in the morning, you may meet a girl no older than ten, barefoot on a moving ladder, carrying a bundle of cuttings that will become a doorway in three seasons. You may meet an old man sitting on a stone shelf, his feet in a shallow pool, peeling shrimp with a knife made from a bat’s shed claw. You may meet a couple arguing about whether their new room should hang by the eastern vine or near the kitchen shaft. You may meet a trader from the flatlands, sweating and offended by the number of steps, trying to sell nails in a place where nails are thought rude to wood. You may meet no person at all, only a lattice wet with spores, a basket left behind, a bird shaking water from its wings, and the slow green fact of the basin itself continuing around them.

That is the deepest answer: what lives in the Trellis Basin is a way of living in which no one is merely a resident. The people are residents, yes, but so are the fungi that make the roots strong, the bats that keep the air clear of gnats, the springs that warm the houses, the rain that the trellis catches and delays, the stones that hold the arches, the vines that have learned to grow in useful shapes. The town is not a container for life; it is life in a particular arrangement.
