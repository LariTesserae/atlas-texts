Plant everywhere, yes, but the sentence is too simple if it makes you think of fields. There are no fields here, no grass to lie down in, no swaying meadow of familiar green. “Plant” in the Cold Loom is less a category than a condition. It means: anything that breathes light, holds water, bends in the wind, remembers a season, and moves without leaving a track. It means: every surface is waiting to become something else.

From the rim, the resemblance to a town is uncanny and useless. The basins, terraces, ridges, shallow channels, rounded mounds, shelves of pale stone—each could be mistaken for a ruin, except that ruin is always the wrong idea here. Ruin implies that something was made and then abandoned. In the Cold Loom, the “made” is what is alive. The ridges are not the remains of roads; they are the raised bodies of slow organisms. The terraces are not the remains of walls; they are the overlapping backs of vast sun-living mats. The mounds are not cairns or hearth-heaps; they are swollen fruiting heads, sleeping chambers, reservoirs, breathing pouches. The channels are not gutters; they are veins, water-traps, nerve-grooves, capillaries. The parallel scars are not marks of ploughs; they are growth lines, contraction rings, the places where the living threads of the basin pull against one another and tighten.

It is plant everywhere, and so there is no one who is not a plant, or at least something that behaves with the patience and appetite of plant. And there is no one who owns the valley either. Ownership requires boundaries, and the boundaries here are not walls but membranes. They leak. They swell. They close when cold comes, and open when the ordinary sun strikes.

The first thing to notice, if one stood at the edge long enough, is that the pale surfaces breathe.

They are not flat stone at all. They are shelves of sun-livers, each shelf a crust of interlocking leaf-thick mats, translucent at the edges, veined with threads as fine as spider silk. A sun-liver looks at first like a broken piece of bone-colored slate, but the “slate” is alive. It has tiny domes of clear fluid that focus light downward into green layers beneath, and when warmth enters, those domes lift by a hair. When the sun moves away, the domes retract, sealing themselves like wet paper closing over a wound. They have no eyes, no leaves exactly, no flowers, no wood. They are bodies made to collect light and refuse freezing. They are architecture made out of appetite.

In the ordinary light of the high sun, the Cold Loom becomes briefly audible.

Not loud. Nothing here is loud. The cold dampens sound, and the ground swallows what little is made. But if the air is still, you can hear a faint hiss, a thousand microscopic seals opening at once. The sun-livers are waking. Frost that formed on them in the night is drawn into their pores, melted by warmth, released as vapor. The vapor rises in threads almost too thin to see. It curls around the ridges and catches the light. For a few hours, the valley seems to hold its breath and then let it out.

Then the cold comes.

The sun does not set like a violent event here; it simply withdraws its attention, and when it does, the basin closes. The cold is not weather alone. It is a pressure. It settles over the terraces, slides into the channels, finds the mounds, and the entire place contracts. The sun-livers seal. The threads tighten. The ridges draw themselves into harder, darker lines. The mounds fold inward, leaving only a slit at the top like a half-bitten seed. The sound is not hissing anymore. It is the sound of fiber under strain. A faint, wet, creaking tension, as if the ground itself is a sail being pulled around a mast.

The thread-weavers are the first inhabitants one truly begins to recognize.

They are not creatures of limbs. They are not even creatures of faces. They are long, pale, filamentous bodies that grow along the terraces and run into the channels, thick as a finger in some places, fine as a root in others. They appear as cords crossing the basin in parallel lines, the same lines that make the “scars” look as if someone has cut the land with a blade. But nothing has cut them. They have grown themselves, one contraction at a time.

Each thread-weaver is a chain of contractile cells. When warmth enters it, it slackens; when cold enters, it draws tight. The cold does not merely slow it. The cold moves it. This is how the ridges are made. The threads pull at the edges of the sun-livers, draw neighboring mats together, braid across the channels, and seal the shelves beneath them. Over years, over centuries, the threads make the land look shaped, as if a town were slowly being woven underfoot. But there are no hands, no loom outside the organisms themselves. The loom is the thread-weavers. The thread-weavers are the loom.

They are ancient-looking, though not necessarily old as individuals. Some of them may die and dissolve into the mats in a season. Others may split and regrow. If a thread-weaver is cut, the exposed ends do not bleed red. They weep a clear sap, and within that sap are fragments, spores, contractile cells. The wound becomes a seedbed. The cut becomes new growth. That is why there are no broken things in the Cold Loom. Brokenness here is only the beginning of multiplication.

The thread-weavers do not have eyes, but they respond to light gradients as if they could taste direction. A weak patch of sun-liver beneath them causes them to grow toward it, thicken, and draw moisture into it. A cold shadow makes them contract and form a ridge. If many of them grow together, they become almost intelligent-looking: not in thought, but in the way a reef is intelligent, or a river delta.
