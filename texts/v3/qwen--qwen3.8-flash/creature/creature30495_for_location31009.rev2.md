There is no one here.

If by “lives” you mean someone who builds, shelters, remembers a home in the ordinary way, then the answer is: nobody. The plateau has never had a hearth, a doorway, a fence, a cradle, a temple, a campfire. It has never had a hand or a tool. There are no ruins and no signs of habitation, because there has never been habitation of the sort that leaves marks behind. There is only existence. Vast, loud, dry, slow existence.

But the place is not empty. It is populated by *it*.

The things that live here are not small enough to be forgotten, and not numerous enough to be called dense. They are present the way mountains are present. A single animal can alter the meaning of a horizon. A migration does not crowd a valley; it moves through it like a change in the pressure of the distance. The plateau is empty, yet life dominates it, because every living thing here is partly creature and partly geology, and because the land itself is an engine that these creatures have been fitted into.

There is no water to drink.

There is nothing to drink in the usual sense. The animals of the dry plateau do not thirst for liquid. They drink gradients. They feed on heat, pressure, electric tension, mineral vapor, and resonance. They are chemistries that have learned to move.

### The skin of the land

The closest thing to life you might first notice is not an animal at all. It is a crust.

Across warm stone and salt flats spreads a lace of oxide threads. They are dark, fine, branching, and dry. In some places they resemble mycelium, in others frozen flame, in others the oxidized skin of some abandoned machine. They have no dampness, no softness, no green vitality. They are not plant. They are not fungus. They are closer to a living crystal process, a metabolism of heat and wind and metallic vapor.

The lace grows where a warm shelf holds mineral gas long enough, where wind leaves charged dust on a glass ridge, where a thermal vent exhales and the air cools a little too fast for the crystals to remain simple. It spreads in lattices. It feeds on gradients. It brightens and darkens with the day. At certain angles of ordinary white-gold light, a square kilometer of crust can look like a blackened circuit board laid over stone, though no one made it.

It is eaten, of course. It is not eaten by mouths like ours, but by grinding mandibles, by scraping tongues of hardened salt, by the slow passage of bodies that polish it away and carry it into themselves. The crust is the land’s closest thing to grass, but it is not grass. It is a dry edible geology. It is a skin that can be grazed.

If a herd crosses, the lace recovers slowly. It does not regrow like a plant; it re-crystallizes, re-lattices, re-forms over weeks, seasons, decades. A bare path becomes a faint memory. A grazing line becomes a stripe of altered mineral chemistry. From above, if anything ever watched from above, the plain would appear patterned. Not by design. By appetite.

### Kiln-tortoises, or the heat-eaters

The first beings that may fill your eye are the tortoise-like animals.

They are enormous, broad, and low, with shells layered from salt and black glass. The shells are not merely protective; they are thermal organs. The tortoises move slowly across the hottest stone, and when they find a place where heat rises strongly from below, they lower themselves onto it.

They feed by pressing their bellies to the land.

Their undershells are porous, crystalline, and lined with mineral channels that conduct warmth inward. The heat does not burn them. It enters them. As they feed, their bodies become faintly luminous, not bright, not wet, not fiery in any soft sense, but glowing with the dull amber internal light of a furnace seen through iron lattice. The glow is dry. It is heat turned into a visible appetite.

When they rise, the rock beneath them has changed. The spot they occupied is cooled and pitted. Around the cooling point, salt and oxide crystallize into pale rings. They leave behind a record of their feeding like a ring in wood, except that the ring is made from the land’s heat being stolen and re-deposited.

They move very little.

A kiln-tortoise may travel no farther in a day than the shadow of a nearby mesa stretches at sunset. But the tortoise’s life may last centuries. Its shell becomes a stratigraphy. Its body becomes a portable oven. When it dies, it does not rot. It contracts into a mineral mass, its salt-shell layers cracking into smaller forms, its internal heat bleeding out into the surrounding stone. Eventually, a dead tortoise becomes a local warm feature. Other creatures may gather around it. The oxide lace may grow over it. In time, it may be mistaken for a small rise in the plain.

Young kiln-tortoises do not emerge from eggs as we know them. They begin as heat-crystallization within a parent shell, a pocket of ordered salt that has grown in a thermal shadow. When a shell cracks, it is not necessarily a death. Sometimes it is a birth. The young one steps out of the hollow with a raw, bright shell still soft in the dry way of mineral wool. It will harden as it learns to seek warmth.

### Fluted quadrupeds, or the gas-walkers

Larger still, and more obvious in movement, are the fluted quadrupeds.

They are tall and angular, with legs like basalt columns and bodies that seem to be composed of ribbed furnace chambers. Their backs are open with curved plates, ribs that flare outward like the doors of industrial hatches. Those ribs do not merely protect. They resonate.

These animals live in and around the gas channels.

They walk through rivers of dense supercritical vapor that move through dry cuts in the stone. As they pass, their chests expand. They inhale the mineral fog. The hot vapor enters their internal chambers, where it meets heated mineral structures and becomes sound.

Then they exhale, and the air fills with a long rolling note.

It is not a roar. It is not a song. It is mechanical, resonant, and vast. The note can carry for tens of kilometers. It is shaped by the fluted walls around them, by the channels they occupy, by the angles of their bodies. A herd moving through a gas valley becomes a moving orchestra of pressure. Every step produces rhythm. Every breath produces tone. The land, which is already full of resonance, receives them and amplifies them.

These quadrupeds do not merely live inside the machinery of the plateau. They are its moving parts.

When they walk, they push and draw gas through narrow passages. When they breathe, they condense mineral fumes into fine crusts that settle on nearby stone. When they call, they trigger distant vents. A herd’s movement can change the behavior of the landscape for kilometers around. They are not herders. They are more like valves, compressors, and acoustic regulators.

Their eyes, if they have eyes, are not wet reflective surfaces. They are flat polarized filters made of dark glass and crystallized salt, oriented to read heat, pressure, and electric charge. They do not see a landscape of objects first. They see a landscape of gradients. They see the hotter ridge before the cooler basin. They see the charged dust before the salt plain. They see the sound coming before the wind reaches it.

The fluted quadrupeds are not solitary. They gather in slow, long-moving groups that are difficult to define as herds. A herd is not a family, exactly. It is more like a shared pressure pattern. Individuals can enter and leave without much notice. The important thing is continuity of resonance. The animals maintain the herd by producing overlapping tones that keep the channels open, guide gas flow, and signal when the next thermal pulse will rise.

If one is separated, it does not bleat or panic in a small creature’s way. It becomes wrong. Its body may begin to vibrate at a frequency that no longer matches the terrain. Its heat regulation may alter. It may wander until the ground accepts it again, until it finds a channel whose pressure matches its chest, until some distant bellback or herd-song gives it a new frequency to follow.

When they die, they do not decompose. They collapse inward. Their mineral ribs fall apart. Their chambers cool into irregular shapes of sinter. Their bones become new acoustic formations. Other quadrupeds may later walk through the remains and find the dead animal’s ribcage producing a useful tone. The dead are sometimes incorporated into the machinery.

### Forge-crawlers, or the glass-polishers

On the glass plains, there are many-legged creatures like beetles fused with the concept of a forge.

They are not large in comparison to the tortoises or quadrupeds, though they are far from small. They are long, low, and plated with oxidized metal. Their legs are multiple and heavy, ending in points that strike sparks when they touch glass. Not fire, not combustion in any moist way. Only dry sparks, brief and bright, thrown off as metal contacts mineral.

They graze.

Their food is the oxide-thread crust that spreads over warm stone. They break it up with grinding mandibles. The crust is brittle, sharp, and dry, and they crush it with slow, deliberate force. As they move, their bodies smooth the glass plains beneath them. Their legs and shells wear down the roughness. They leave behind polished trails that can stretch for kilometers. In some places, the glass becomes so uniformly abraded that it looks as though it has been prepared for a machine that no one has built.

Occasionally, a forge-crawler stops.

It lowers its heavy body. It begins to vibrate. The vibration is deep, barely audible to anyone who might stand far away, but the salt crystals near it respond. They shift. They align. A small field of disordered crystal becomes momentarily ordered. The land has become, for a few seconds, a clearer instrument.

Other creatures gather then.

Not because the forge-crawler is food. Not because the sound is a mating call. They gather because the plain has become more coherent. A bellback may turn its crest slightly toward the vibration. A gas-walker may alter its breathing so the tone joins the alignment. An overhead static membrane may drift lower, drawn by the sudden pattern in the local charge. The gathering is not social in any warm sense. It is tuning.

Forge-crawlers molt. When they do, their old metal shells crack and fall away, revealing new plates beneath. The shed shells lie on the plain for years, sometimes decades. Wind and dust work them further. Eventually they become ridges, lenses, reflectors, or salt-bridges. A crawling line can leave a trail so regular that from a distance the plain appears tiled.

There are no small burrowing creatures in the ordinary way. The plateau has no mice, no beetles in the usual insect sense, no ants, no fleas, no soft-bodied scavengers. If tiny life exists, it is less like animals and more like weather. Dust motes may behave with a strange persistence near charged salt fields. Fine metallic fibers may drift and accumulate in patterns too regular to be entirely inert. But nothing crawls in herds beneath the stone. The land has few small things. The small things here are mostly chemistry.

### Static membranes, or the sail-drifters

The air contains beings.

They are not birds. They are not balloons. They are not clouds. They are dry membranes, vast films of silica and stretched salt, thin enough to look almost unreal. They drift through the upper air above the plateau, catching thermal currents, electric gradients, and the fine mineral haze that thickens during dust events.

They resemble jellyfish only in the loosest sense. They have no wetness. They are supported by electrostatic tension and by the pressure of the superheated gas rising from below. Their bodies can stretch wide enough to cast shadows over entire fields.

These membranes feed on static.

The glass plains build charge as wind moves across them. Salt basins accumulate dust. Charged vapor rises through channels and fogs. A static membrane passes through this charged air, brushing it with filaments trailing from its underside. The charge collects within its translucent body. When it becomes full, it may discharge in long blue-white cracks, not from cloud to ground, but from one region of its own membrane to the plateau below.

Where it discharges, glass can be etched. Salt can be fused. Metallic vapor can condense into fine needles. The sail-drifters do not merely float through the air; they write with electricity.

They are not numerous.

A sky above the plateau may contain one or two, spaced far apart. But when one passes overhead, the effect is total. The hair of a body standing below might rise. The air may taste of metal. A soft crackle follows the membrane like applause in an empty hall. The shadow it casts may move across a mesa without obscuring everything, because the membrane is translucent. Instead, the land appears briefly altered by a strange, dry filter.

Their reproduction is tied to discharge.

When a membrane stores too much tension, it splits. The daughter films are smaller and initially unstable, drifting low over the salt basins until they find charge. Some are torn down to the ground by wind. There, they lie like pale broken glass, gathering mineral dust, accumulating salt threads, growing by slow adsorption of vapor. If they survive long enough, they lift again.

Old membranes, when they finally fail, do not fall cleanly. They fragment into translucent sheets that settle over ridges and channels. The wind carries the fragments farther. They can become part of the landscape’s reflective surface, part of the crackle, part of the optical noise that makes distances here unreliable.

They often drift near the bellbacks, because a bellback’s resonance changes air pressure and electric charge in the surrounding basin. The membranes feed on that disturbance, or perhaps merely ride it. It is difficult to say whether one attracts the other or whether both are simply responding to the same gradient.

### Bellbacks, or the living instruments

On high ridges stand the bellbacks.

They are the slowest and strangest of the plateau’s large animals, though the word *animal* strains here as well. They may be living cathedrals without architecture, or perhaps cathedrals that have forgotten they were once alive. Their bodies are massive, stone-dark, and fused with mineral crests that flare upward and outward like organ pipes. Their backs are grown into fluting, hollows, and resonant shelves.

They stand on ridgelines.

They remain still for months. Sometimes years. They do not pace. They do not hunt. They do not migrate. They position themselves where the wind bends through a particular harmonic shape and then they let the plateau play them.

When wind strikes their crests, the sound they make is not a call. It is a sustained resonance. It can be heard as much in bones as ears. It is dry, immense, and architectural. The note may last so long that it seems to be a fixed property of the air. If a bellback is struck correctly by a thermal tide, a nearby mesa may seem to flicker for several seconds, not because the mesa moves, but because the sound has bent the local perception of form.

Bellbacks regulate much of the larger life without moving.

Their resonance can draw gas-walkers toward a channel or away from an unstable vent field. It can calm the electric tension above a salt basin, causing static membranes to rise higher or drift farther. It can influence the movement of kiln-tortoises, though slowly, because a tortoise may take days to understand that the frequency of the ground has changed.

They are not alone on the ridges. Young bellbacks may grow from old ones. A juvenile does not emerge from the adult’s body. It begins as an acoustic crack, a harmonic fracture in a nearby salt ridge. The wind cuts a shape there, and the shape slowly accepts mineral deposit. The young bellback forms in stages, like a formation being tuned into existence. If it can maintain the correct resonance, it stands. If not, it collapses into sinter and is reabsorbed into the ridge.

When an old bellback dies, it does not collapse. It settles. Its body becomes a new formation. Its ribs remain open. Its crests become channels. Other creatures later gather around it because it produces tones. A dead bellback may continue to function for centuries as a landscape instrument.

The line between ecology and industry becomes meaningless around them. A bellback is not only a living thing. It is also a place where living things are affected. It is a machine that breathes. It is a territory that sounds. It is a creature that can become geography.

### Counter-singers, or the fracture-stags

Not all life on the plateau is constructive.

There are predators, though they do not bite, tear, or swallow in any familiar way. They hunt with resonance.

These creatures are rare and large, walking behind herds or lingering near thermal vents. They have bodies like fluted quadrupeds but leaner, with throats opened toward the ground and chests tuned to anti-frequency. They do not eat flesh. They eat the hot inner lattice of mineral-bodied animals.

They listen.

When a kiln-tortoise walks with a slight imbalance in its shell, or a gas-walker breathes with a harmonic that leaves a salt plate too brittle, a counter-singer can identify the fault. It approaches slowly, keeping distance. Then it opens its throat and sings the inverse note.

The sound does not always kill.

Sometimes it only cracks.

A shell fissures along a hidden line. A mineral plate loosens. The heat inside is exposed. The counter-singer does not devour it. It presses its mouthless, resonant feeding chamber against the cracked place and drinks the escaping heat. The injured creature may move on. The counter-singer moves with the herd, waiting for the next weak harmonic.

These hunts are not dramatic. They are not fast. There is no chase, no blood, no wetness. A predation event may last hours, or a single sustained note may be all that is remembered by the terrain. A crack in a shell may be mistaken for thermal expansion. A potted ring of cooled stone may be mistaken for ordinary feeding. Only the animals who hear the anti-note know that something has chosen.

The counter-singers themselves are strange and sparse. They seem to prefer the hotter gas channels, where sound travels with density and pressure. Their bodies are darker than those of the quadrupeds, almost black glass, with thin salt veins like cracks in a windowpane. They rarely gather. Two counter-singers meeting may not fight. They may tune against each other until one frequency dominates and the other drifts away.

### Echo-herds, or the unmade

The strangest inhabitants are the echo-herds.

They are hard to see.

They are not made of matter in any reliable way. They appear as moving pressure, as faint distortions in the ordinary light, as clusters of refracted sound. If a gas vent releases a long rising note, something may gather where the note bends and repeat it with slight variation. This repetition is enough. If the variation persists, if the note becomes self-sustaining through the interaction of wind, pressure, salt geometry, and mineral dust, then an echo-herd may be there.

They drift across the basins in shapes that look like translucent antlered quadrupeds, or like floating sails, or like ripples made of memory. Sometimes they are nearly invisible. Sometimes, when the air cools slightly at night and thickens, they become visible enough to disturb anyone watching. Their forms are unstable. They have no true outline. They look like the idea of a herd pressed through the atmosphere.

They feed on patterns.

A bellback’s note may attract one. A static discharge may create a temporary body of sound that an echo-herd can enter and prolong. A gas channel may release a pulse so regular that the echo-herd can inhabit it. They do not eat matter. They sustain themselves by feeding on organized movement of air, sound, and charge.

The plateau’s emptiness makes them feel larger than they are. If every few kilometers there were shrubs, insects, small animals, scattered debris, ordinary life, then echo-herds would be odd features among many features. But here there are almost no small things. The spaces between presences are enormous. The land leaves too much room for movement. Therefore, when a shape of sound drifts across an empty basin, it seems total. The absence around it makes its presence unavoidable.

They may also be the reason reality feels thin here.

Distances on the plateau do not always obey themselves. A ridge that looks twenty kilometers away may be reached after an hour’s walk, or after three days, depending on wind, thermal pressure, and harmonic mesas. Echo-herds move through these variable spaces. Sometimes they cause a distance to collapse into perception. Sometimes they make it expand. An animal, if observed, may walk toward an echo and find that the echo walks too, not because it is following, but because the air has organized a pattern that both creatures are now inside.

They can alter sound’s location.

If a bellback strikes a long note, an echo-herd may catch the sound and leave it hanging in the air. After the source has moved, the tone may remain. It may drift to the edge of a mesa. It may settle there. Over time, the sound can condense into a faint vertical filament of glass, a tiny shard of materialized echo. Not always. Not everywhere. But enough that the land contains crystallized residues of things that once had no matter.

At night, they are more visible.

The plateau’s ordinary light cycle may bring darkness, but not cold. Cooling cracks stone. Salt fields pop. Charged air discharges in delayed arcs. Heat-eaters glow with internal light. And the echo-herds move through the thicker, cooler air in translucent processions. They are not ghosts. They are not spirits. They are not dreams. They are patterns with hunger, and they are alive in the way a self-repeating storm can be alive if it persists long enough.

Some bellbacks seem to know them. Their resonance changes when an echo-herd approaches. Some static membranes rise sharply away from them, as if the dry sail-animals do not want their charge stolen by something that is not solid. The gas-walkers sometimes fall into silence. That silence is not rest. It is avoidance. The fluted quadrupeds know that when they stop singing, the land may sing itself, and not in a useful way.

### The ecology of process

There is no food web here in the familiar sense.

There is no sun energy captured by leaves, no herbivore eating plant matter, no carnivore eating herbivore. The system is hotter, slower, and more mechanical. The energy enters from below as heat and from above as light, but the animals do not eat plants. They eat thermal differentials, gas flow, electric tension, mineral crust, sound pressure, and organized motion.

The plateau is industrial without factories.

The animals are part of that industry.

A herd of gas-walkers moving through a channel can change the pressure in a valley. Their exhalations condense into mineral crusts that become habitat for other things. Their footsteps trigger vents. Their bodies move gas that otherwise would remain still. Their dead ribs become acoustic instruments for future herds.

Kiln-tortoises cool the ground where they feed, then leave crystallized heat-rings that alter local conductivity. Static membranes collect charge, discharge it, etch glass, and scatter fragments that affect wind and light. Forge-crawlers polish stone, align salts, and create regular patterns that may influence how future creatures navigate. Bellbacks stand and sing, and their songs can organize migrations. Echo-herds move through the spaces between matter and make sound behave like terrain.

If you watch long enough, you see that none of them is separate from the machinery of the land.

A tortoise’s shell is a thermal battery. A gas-walker’s chest is a compressor. A membrane’s body is a capacitor. A bellback’s crest is an organ pipe. A forge-crawler’s grinding is a polishing operation. Even the dead belong to the process. The plateau does not bury its dead. It recycles them into function.

There is no care in this. No mourning. No waste. A fallen bellback becomes a ridge. A shed crawler-shell becomes a tile. A broken tortoise becomes a heat ring. A spent static membrane becomes dust. An echo-herd dissipates into a pattern that the wind may carry forward into another vent, another note, another fragile shape.

This is not a gentle ecology.

It is not cruel either. It is simply massive. It operates on timescales where patience becomes pressure and pressure becomes force. A single gas-walker’s breath may matter to a vent field for hours. A herd’s movement may alter a valley’s acoustic balance for seasons. A bellback’s death may change the route of migration for decades. An echo-herd may repeat a lost note for centuries, until the land is mistaken for the thing that made it.

### Reproduction without birth, as we understand it

Reproduction here is often tied to rupture.

Heat cracks stone.

Cold contracts salt.

Wind polishes glass until a weakness becomes a fault.

Electric tension exceeds a membrane’s capacity, and it splits.

A resonance finds a harmonic flaw in a shell, and a new thing can emerge from the gap.

The animals do not mate in ways recognizable from wet worlds. There is no copulation, no nest, no burrow, no pregnancy as such. There is no nursing, because there is no milk, no soft young, no vulnerable flesh. The young are born from order appearing inside fracture.

A kiln-tortoise’s young begins when a shell develops an internal thermal cavity with the correct crystalline lining. A gas-walker’s young may precipitate from superheated vapor condensing in a parent’s rib-chamber during a pressure drop. A forge-crawler’s offspring may be seeded when its vibration aligns salt needles into a shape capable of self-maintenance. A static membrane’s daughter is a split caused by excess charge. A bellback’s offspring is grown in the harmonic shadow of a ridge, given body by persistent resonance and mineral deposition.

Even the echo-herds reproduce by variation.

A note repeats. The wind changes it. The change becomes stable. Stable becomes hungry. Hungry becomes herd.

The most difficult thing to understand is that this life does not seem to struggle for space. The space is already vast. The competition is not for shelter. It is for gradient. The animals compete to occupy the correct pressure, the correct temperature, the correct frequency. A poor gas-walker is not one that has been chased away. It is one that has lost resonance with the channel. A poor tortoise is not hungry in the ordinary way. It is one that can no longer draw heat efficiently from the stone. A poor membrane is one that leaks charge too fast to sustain its form.

Death is therefore often a loss of tuning.

An animal may die because the land stopped fitting it.

### What life looks like from above

If something could hover above the plateau—if not a ship, not a bird, not a constructed thing, but simply an observing vantage—the life below would appear less like animals than like slow weather patterns.

The salt basin to the east might contain rings.

Kiln-tortoise feeding marks. Pale crystallized circles scattered across the flats, some old and nearly faded, some bright and newly formed. From above, the basin would look like the skin of a beast covered in slow sores of light.

The gas channels to the north would show rhythm.

Dark shapes moving through mineral fog in lines too regular to be random. Each shape would trigger a tone. The channels would pulse. The fog would ripple. The herd would not move as a single animal or as a chaotic crowd. It would move as a mechanical process made of bodies.

The glass fields below would have polished veins.

Forge-crawler trails cutting through black glass. Long shining lines where the surface has been worn smooth. In places, the trails would intersect in almost deliberate patterns. Not streets. Not roads. Not anything built. Just the residue of grazing.

The high mesas would hold bellbacks.

Motionless. Dark. With crests like flares of stone. They would be almost indistinguishable from the ridges until the wind hit them and a sustained note rose from the plateau, spreading across the open country like a visible pressure wave.

The air above the basins would contain shadows that are not quite shadows.

Static membranes drifting, translucent and broad, with long trailing filaments that gather dust and electric charge. Occasionally, they would crack with dry lightning. The sky would fill with brief blue-white lines, no clouds, no rain, no thunderhead, just static seeking the ground.

And in the spaces between all of it, if the light were wrong and the air were right, there would be movements that could not be confirmed.

Echo-herds.

Not quite there. Not quite sound. Not quite matter. Moving through the open distance as if the emptiness had become a path.

### What life sounds like from the ground

From the surface, the plateau is louder than its emptiness suggests.

The sound is not busy. There is no small rustling, no insect murmur, no leaf whisper, no wet slap of water on shore. There is grinding, ringing, booming, clicking, whining, and resonance.

Wind moves across fluted mesas and produces long chords. Stone clicks as it expands with heat. Glass fields chime when dust drifts across them. Vents hiss in sustained tones that carry for miles. Kiln-tortoises shift on hot basalt, and the ground answers with a dry scrape. Gas-walkers breathe, and the land becomes an organ. Forge-crawlers strike sparks, and the glass fields receive them with tiny metallic ticks. Bellbacks stand on ridges and become instruments. Echo-herds move through pressure waves, and the sound becomes visible in the air before it fades.

At night, the sound changes.

It does not soften. It clarifies. Cooling stone cracks like a machine losing tolerance. Salt pops. Air discharges in delayed arcs. Far ridges hum with a lower frequency than daytime. The heat-eaters glow more visibly, and their shells emit faint ticking as internal structures contract. The gas-walkers sing less often, but when they do, the notes travel farther through the denser night air. The echo-herds appear.

Then the whole landscape seems to be thinking in slow frequencies.

Not speech. Not language. Not in any way that promises understanding. But a deep organized movement of sound and pressure that resembles thought the way a river resembles memory. It is too large to be called intention. It is too coherent to be called accident.

### The inhabitants without inhabitants

If someone were to stand there—and the ground has never known a constructed thing, so such a person would be an impossible interruption—the emptiness would not be comforting.

It would be crowded in a different way.

A single kiln-tortoise far below could occupy an entire basin in the mind. A gas-walker’s call could make the ground feel as if it is being played. A static membrane’s shadow could pass over the observer like a dark wave. A bellback’s note could make the observer feel less like a witness and more like a component. An echo-herd’s approach could make distance seem unreliable, as if the observer’s own position is a guess.

There would be no sense of sanctuary, because there is no shelter. No cave to hide in, because caves, if they exist, are resonant. No plant to gather for cover, because there is no green. No water to cross, because nothing wet crosses. No fire, because heat is everywhere, including the ground. No darkness to escape into, because even night is loud and luminous with mineral charge.

If one looked closely, one would see that the animals are not merely surviving in the environment. They are expressions of the environment. Their bodies are made of the same materials as the terrain. Their metabolisms mirror its mechanics. Their deaths become its new features. Their movement changes the operation of the land. The land, in turn, changes the operation of their lives.

This is the paradox of the plateau.

It feels empty because there are few small things.

It is full because the few things are enormous, slow, and integrated into the processes of the world.

It is untouched because there are no artifacts.

It is industrial because there is no absence of production.

It has no maker.

It makes itself constantly, with stone and salt and pressure, with sound and charge and heat, and the creatures that live there are among the products and agents of that making.

They are not citizens. They are not inhabitants in the sense of living in a place. They are place, in motion.

What lives here is not a population.

It is a functioning.
