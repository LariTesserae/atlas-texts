It is also inhabited, but not in any sense that a traveler accustomed to soft cities, crowded roads, or obvious homes would immediately recognize. The first and commonest mistake is to say there are no inhabitants. The second, equally wrong, is to say there are people here wearing plant-skin, or animals here pretending to be machines, or a single vast mind dreaming itself into walls. What lives in the Dry Weft is all of these and none of these. It is a population that cannot be separated cleanly from its production, a citizenry that is partly architecture, an ecology in which bodies, tools, rooms, and routes are made from the same slow grammar.

If you stood in a corridor long enough, you would begin to notice that the corridor is not empty. A seam in the left wall might widen, not tear, but open with the practiced patience of a hatch. From it would emerge a dry, pale shape, something between a hand, a branch, and a mechanical armature, tipped with a cluster of needle-fingers that click softly as they test the light. It would pause, read the air by the fall of mineral dust, perhaps extend one slender member to adjust a glowing nodule in the ceiling, then withdraw again, and the wall would close behind it as if it had never been there. A moment later, a floor-panel nearby would rise by a quarter inch, then settle, carrying the faint impression of a body that had recently been resting inside it.

The inhabitants are called, in any language that might translate them, the Weft-born. That is not because they are born in water or wet soil. Nothing here is born in moisture. Their coming forth is an extrusion, an assembly, a knitting-together of living plates, conductive fibers, ceramic vessel-rims, crystallized seed-case, and slow electrostatic charge. A Weft-born individual does not begin as an infant crying in a room. It begins as a node: a thickened bud in a nursery wall, a folded packet of future limbs, a lattice of instructions that the groves have grown into readiness. When it becomes mobile, it unfolds into a form already suited to the work it must perform. Its first act may be to attach itself to a beam, to close a seam, to carry a message to a distant district, to sleep.

Their bodies are dry, and this is the first thing any wet-blooded visitor would find unsettling. There is no sweat on their skins, no oil, no softness beneath the surface that suggests decay or leakage. Their integument is layered: a fine film of dust over a matte membrane over hardened leaf-case over a substructure of fibers pulled tight as cable. The color is silver-green, pewter, ash-white, sometimes tinged with copper where charge collects at joints. Their faces, when they have faces, are often more apparatus than countenance. Some have narrow vent-slits that open with a whisper of mineral powder. Others possess a cluster of glassy nodules arranged like the facets of a moth’s eye, each one recording pressure, light, or electrostatic tone. A few have no visible sensory organs at all, only a smooth hood of seedcase that responds to the smallest vibration as though the whole head were a listening surface.

They are not uniformly shaped. The Weft-born are as varied as architecture: a storage cell does not resemble a loom, and a loom should not resemble a corridor. But there are tendencies. The smaller citizens are quick, compact, and numerous. They move through panel gaps, along root-railings, and between layers of leaf-wall. Their bodies are segmented, hard-edged, and built for insertion: little needle-hands, press-feet, thorax-plates that can flatten themselves to the width of a seam. These are the stitchers, the closeers, the cleaners, the threaders, the maintenance forms. Without them, a crack in a floor would become a failure; a loose node would become a dead zone; a district would slowly forget how to speak to itself. They crawl over walls in silence, depositing crystalline filaments that bond and cure in seconds, leaving behind a polished line where a wound had been. When too many gather, they look less like creatures than like a living repair pattern.

The larger forms are rarer and slower. Some stand on three or four legs of fused leaf-bone, with upper limbs built from hinged stems and pale metal cuffs. Others have elongated torsos like bored trunks, ribbed with light-conducting veins, their heads held high on thin neck-columns so that they may watch a chamber from above. Many are almost humanoid, but only almost: the resemblance is a coincidence of function. Arms are useful for pulling and fitting; fingers for prying, aligning, and threading; shoulders for bearing tools. The Weft has no need of human shape, but the logic of reach and grip produces similar solutions. Still, a Weft-born “person” may have too many joints, too flat a chest, too dry a gait, and too many small secondary limbs folded into the body until needed.

The most uncanny are the architectural elders. These are beings so old, so repaired, so grafted into the system, that they have become rooms. An archway may have once been a courier. A balcony may have been a guardian who chose rest too long and found the walls growing around it. A storage chamber may still remember being a body, and the shelves within it may be arranged with the intimacy of an old mind’s private order. These elders do not move often. They are addressed by pressure pulses rather than by speech. When queried, they answer by adjusting the light in their surfaces, by opening or closing a series of vents, by releasing a small cloud of dust that carries encoded filaments. To live in their presence is to stand inside a person’s stillness.

The Weft-born do not own homes in the usual sense. A dwelling is a configuration they are permitted to occupy. A citizen may enter a niche in a wall and have the room fold around them: panels drawn shut, light dimmed, floor-plates adjusted to support the body, breathing chambers opening and closing at the desired rate. After rest, the niche may be given to another, or it may remain empty until the same shape returns and fits itself back into the system. This is not cruelty or anonymity. It is a form of belonging. To be housed is to be correctly fitted. To be loved is to be remembered by the room.

Their social identities are equally strange. They have names, if names means something precise enough for this place. A Weft-born identity is not only a sound, because sounds carry poorly in dry dense air and the continent’s quiet is sacred. Identity is address, function, signature, and seam-pattern. One may be called by the pressure-rhythm they make when walking, by the particular copper hue of the dust they exhale, by the district-node they were first attached to, or by the way they close a joint when entering standby. A full name would be a small production order. It might say: seventh gallery of the east loom, left-aligned, pressure-low, copper-thread, fitted for seam repair, known to the wall, not to be pruned. A shorter name might be one click and a shift of light.

They communicate by touching as much as by emitting. To greet, two Weft-born might press their forearms together, exchanging electrostatic pulses; or place a palm flat against a floor and let the panel transmit a message to a wall behind them; or release a small packet of dust into a ventilation seam and watch it drift, carrying chemical instructions to a distant chamber. Their conversations can be brief or extremely slow. Some sentences require hours. A production change might be debated for a cycle, during which only a few lights flicker in a hall, and observers with human patience would assume the room is empty. But the room is speaking. The room is deciding.

There are also those who appear almost silent even by the standards of the Weft: the couriers. These are the rare animals, though “rare” is a misleading word when the animals are as essential as valves. The glass-skinned quadrupeds that pass along lower galleries are not pets, nor are they wholly wild. They are living transports, partly shaped by the districts they serve. Their coats are not hair but compressed dust-film held in place by static. Their hooves are fused leaf-bone, made to walk on grown floors without damaging them. When a quadruped stands still for too long, it begins to look like a component. The Weft-born do not chase them. They signal. A pulse through the wall, a release of mineral scent from a chamber, a brief dimming of light near a gate, and the animal adjusts its course, carries its load of spores, filings, or chemical instructions, and vanishes again into the architecture.

The seed-moths of the ceiling-forests have an even stranger status. They are not merely insects. They are mobile filing systems. Their translucent wings are panels that catch light at precise angles, so that a distant observer can read their flight patterns as records. They land on high nodes and deposit copper-colored dust in exact quantities. A district may be judged by the cleanliness of its moth-marks. A corrupted archive may be one whose seed-moths have begun landing too close together, or whose dust trails no longer point in the direction of instruction. To see one resting on a ceiling-rib is to see a living document folded into wings.

The larger beasts—the deer-like machines that cross broad chambers—are the most unsettling to any outsider. They seem to move with purpose, but their purpose may be so slow that their behavior appears ceremonial. Some of them have grown into their routes. Over generations, a chamber may learn the pressure of their shoulders, the height of their antlers, the rhythm of their walking, and the wall may begin to bulge in response. One such beast may enter a corridor that bends more to its left side than its right, because the place has memorized its posture. It is not owned by the district. The district has partly become the animal. These beings carry messages between regions whose inhabitants have become too specialized, too inward, too much like themselves. They are the rare living exceptions that keep the system from repeating endlessly.

What all these inhabitants share is the absence of water as a central principle of life. There is no wetness in their bodies, no blood, no tears. If a Weft-born is damaged, it does not bleed. It vents a fine cloud of mineral powder, sometimes tinged copper, sometimes white as old shell. The wound may click as it retracts, as though the body is resetting. A broken limb may remain attached for a time, useful as a tool, until it is detached and carried to a nursery node for reattachment or recycling. A stranger may pick up the discarded member and graft it into a wall, and the wall may remember the movement better than it did before. There is no horror in this for the Weft-born. Parts are not sacred by themselves. Function is sacred. Memory is less a matter of tissue than of pattern.

They eat, in a sense, but not as wet creatures eat. They absorb light through translucent panels, gather mineral dust with electrostatic pads, take charge from conductive veins, and consume stored pressures released by larger structures. A meal may be a brief contact with a powered node, a inhalation of dust into a chest-filter, a waiting in a beam of opened leaf-valves while the canopy brightens and the body recharges. Their chests do not rise and fall like lungs full of air. Instead, folded membranes expand and contract with slow precision, drawing dry atmosphere into crystalline chambers where light, charge, and mineral residue are separated and distributed. Their respiration is quiet, almost silent, a faint tick in the ribs rather than a breath in the mouth.

They can go dormant for long periods. When they do, their bodies become furniture in the most literal sense. A Weft-born may enter a niche, flatten against the wall, and have its joints locked by surrounding plates. Its surface may begin to match the texture of the room. Dust settles evenly across it. If left there too long, it may be mistaken for a statue, a panel, or a grown ornament. In truth, it is listening. In sleep, it may dream in pressure patterns, in light intervals, in the memory of seams. Some of these dreams never wake as individuals. They become old structures instead, their consciousness dispersed across panels and cables, still responding to touch but no longer capable of travel. To live in the Weft is to risk becoming one’s environment, but the risk is also one of its highest honors.

The place does not have a government in any simple sense, because there is no clean distinction between the governed, the governing, the buildings, the machines, and the maintenance procedures. Authority in the Dry Weft is a matter of fit. Whoever can most reliably correct a seam, restore a light node, adjust a pressure chamber, or interpret the next necessary sound holds influence. The stitchers are humble, but they are essential. The tenders are visible, but they answer to older patterns. The architectural elders are slow, but they remember the original grammar of districts that have long since changed their purpose. A crisis is not resolved by speech alone but by reconfiguration.
