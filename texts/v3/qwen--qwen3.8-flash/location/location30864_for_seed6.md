**The Cistern of the Quiet Engine**

High above the tree line, where the wind is thin enough to feel like a rumor, there is a bounded place that should not be so green. It is not a valley, nor a forest, nor a ruin. It is more like a chapel of machinery that has been drowned, then replanted. The space is small enough to be grasped in a single walk-around, a hamlet-sized world behind bolted glass, but inside that smallness it feels cathedral-quiet, as if every surface were waiting for a sound that would take days to arrive.

There is no natural ground here. No dirt, no root-torn soil, no exposed stone, no patch of untouched earth. The floor is a manufactured plate, riveted to a deeper shell of steel and ceramic. The walls are made in sections, fitted with gaskets, reinforced with ribs, lined with pale vitreous coating that reflects the water’s glow. The ceiling is another made thing: a lens of translucent panels, segmented by dark bands, through which ordinary daylight enters. The light is not strange. It is white, diffuse, and honest, like noon in a greenhouse, like the flat brightness of an overcast sky translated through water. It falls without color into the room and seems to thicken as it reaches the lower depths.

The room is filled, almost completely, with warm water.

It stands in the vessel like a trapped flood, clear at first glance but faintly mineral in its depths, tinted toward green-gold where the plants hold it in suspension. The surface is near the ceiling, a narrow breathing layer of humid air above it, but below that, for all practical purposes, this is a submerged landscape. The water does not move like a sea. It moves like something inside a machine: currents rise and fall in slow, deliberate columns, folding around pipes, pressing softly against sealed glass, carrying bubbles that seem to take hours to reach the surface. The warmth is immediate. It is bath-warm, body-close, the temperature of a room that has been heated by machinery rather than by sun.

Everything is new.

There has not yet been time for wear to soften this place. The edges of the plates are sharp. The weld lines are bright and clean. The bolts have not begun to darken, the glass has not scratched, the metal has not blistered or peeled. The water, too, is young; it carries a faint factory taste, not of rot or stagnation, but of new seals, rinsed valves, and poured stone. There are no old scars here, no places where centuries of motion have made smooth curves. It is as if the room emerged only moments ago from an oven, and only after that did the garden begin to grow.

That garden is what makes the strangeness.

Even though there is no soil, it is lush to the point of congestion. Vegetation claims every surface: moss-like sheets crawl across the floor plates; pale root-filaments hang from suspended bars; bulbous aquatic rosettes open from sockets in the walls; long, translucent fronds sway through channels that were made for something else. There is not one unused square of surface. The place is packed so completely that it feels less like a body of water containing plants and more like a woven structure where the plants and the machinery are the same thing. Cables are dressed with green skins. Pipes wear collars of fern-like growth. Valve wheels are half-strangled by pale tendrils that have grown into their spokes. The water itself seems crowded, suspended in drifting sheets of algae and seed-dust, threaded through with the shadows of roots that have no ground to search.

No animals live here.
