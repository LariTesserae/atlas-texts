At the bottom of a country that has lost its hills, the basin opens.

It is not a small place. The eye begins at the nearest dark mirror of water and does not stop until the low ridges of silt and stone blur into the dim horizon. The land lies beneath the surrounding world like a shallow bowl, or like the lid of a wound that has never been permitted to close or heal. From here to there it spreads in layers: wet meadow, reed bank, black mud shelf, mossy island, half-made rock terrace, forested hollow, and all of them crowded together in the hot, low air.

The heat is immediate and total. It does not come from a blazing sun, because there is no obvious sun; there is only a fixed dim brightness, as if the whole basin has been placed inside the amber of a late afternoon that refuses to end. The temperature sits in the lungs like warm water. Nothing cools. Nothing rises as breath. There is no wind, because there is no direction for the air to go. The wetness is too heavy for drying, and the basin is too evenly held for storms.

It is wet everywhere.

Water is not a river here, nor a lake, nor an ocean, nor rain falling in a single place. It is a condition. The soil is saturated just beneath its surface. Every stone weeps. Every root is damp. Every leaf has a bead clinging to its tip, trembling with the intention of falling but never committing to it. Pools lie in shallow depressions, their surfaces smooth and dark, reflecting the low red-gold light with a clarity too perfect to be natural. The margins of the pools are not clear banks but marsh-edges, places where mud and water and plant have forgotten where they end. Fine black silt feathers out under the surface like smoke held in glass.

The water is near enough to touch, and the air is so close to the ground that it seems the basin might be entirely filled with it. Condensation stands in the open spaces like faint curtains. It does not drift. It glimmers in the dim light, and sometimes, at the edge of sight, a drop will shine as if it has just appeared, though nothing has moved to make it.

The geology is raw.

This is not worn land. The stone has not been polished by ages. It has not been softened by repeated seasons. It lies in the basin as though still being thought into shape. Clay shelves rise from the mud with sharp, fresh edges. Shards of black basalt appear in rows too regular to be accidental but not regular enough to be built; they are only the world proposing a structure and then pausing before completing it. Pale limestone benches stand near the shallows, their faces clean and angular, without pits or rounded corners. Some cracks in the rock are deep enough to suggest canyons, but the canyons have never opened further. Some hollows are long enough to suggest river channels, but no water has yet accepted the job of carving them.

Erosion is present as an idea only. There are beginnings everywhere: little notches where a stone might split, shallow runs where mud might wash away, delicate fractures that could become gullies if time were allowed to do anything at all. But time is not allowed. Nothing collapses. Nothing settles. Nothing dries into dust.

The vegetation is overwhelming.

It fills every space the water permits. The ground is not soil alone but living mat. Mosses grow in dense cushions, black-green and slick, spreading between the feet of reeds. Ferns uncurl in thickets so crowded that they appear to be made of one another, each frond touching the next, their leaves glossy with condensation. Broad, fleshy plants rise from the shallows with leaves like open hands, their undersides pale as bone. Grasses and sedges stand in the warm water with blades so thick and waxy they reflect the dim light in long streaks. Higher vegetation forms a tangled green canopy in the middle distance: low trees with trunks of twisted fiber, shrubs drooping under leaves shaped like hearts, vines that do not climb because there is nothing unfinished about their reaching.

The green is lush and layered, but it is not growing.

It has the appearance of growth frozen at full richness. No leaf droops from age. No branch breaks under new weight. No flower opens or closes. No fruit falls. The vegetation looks saturated with life, dense with color, soft at the margins, brilliant in its dampness, but none of its parts have moved toward becoming anything else. It is as if the forest is a complete sentence that cannot be continued.

The light makes the lushness heavier.

There is no bright noon here. The illumination is low, close, and strange, as if the basin is lit from within by the heat of its own wetness. The colors are deep rather than sharp. Greens darken to black where shadows gather. Browns turn almost purple. The pale minerals of the raw stone glow faintly, and the shallow pools carry a dull red reflection that cannot be traced to a sky. In some places, the dimness seems to cling to the air itself, making distance look soft and near things look wetter than they should. Shadows lie on the ground without moving. They look as if they could be peeled away, but they never change shape.

Smell fills the space between heat and water. There is the green smell of crushed leaves that have not been crushed. There is the mineral breath of fresh stone. There is the sweet heaviness of moss. There is the faintly electric scent of wet earth, and beneath it, something else: the smell of life so dense that it has no room to waste or decay.

The animals are everywhere.

They are not rare, not shy, not scattered into cautious niches. They are packed so closely into the basin that the place seems made of them as much as it is made of water and vegetation. Frogs sit in the shallows by the thousands, their backs dark and slick, their eyes bright as wet beads. Fish lie just beneath the surface of the pools, mouths slightly open, fins held wide as if they are about to turn, though they never do. Insects crowd the humid air above the reeds: broad-winged beetles, pale-bodied flies, things like sparks that might be wings or might be reflections. Along the low mud banks, creatures stand in herds and knots, their bodies pressed almost together, their breathing paused, their skin damp with the same fine water that covers every leaf and stone.

Some are ordinary in shape, enough so that one can guess what they are: birds with broad, low-set wings; lizards with soft, shining scales; mammals with thick fur matted into wet points; crabs with pale legs sunk into the mud. Others are unusual in ways that feel local, inevitable, and only slightly wrong. There are creatures with shells that resemble leaves. There are animals with eyes set too far back on their heads, as if they need to watch their own movement. Some have translucent ears, some have tails that end in flat, paddle-like growths, some have fur that grows in spiral ridges, some have throats expanded in permanent calls.

They are alive. That much is unmistakable. Their bodies are glossy, tense, breathing, watchful. Their feathers and fur are clean. Their eyes are bright. Their skins hold moisture. Nothing about them is dry or dead or abandoned. But no action begins. No creature steps forward. No beak opens wider. No tongue flicks out. No foot breaks the surface of the water and makes a ripple.

There is no before and after in the animals. There is only the continuous present of their presence.
