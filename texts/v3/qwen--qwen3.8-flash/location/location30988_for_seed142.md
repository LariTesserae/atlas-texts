**The Stillworks of the Upper Anhydrite Table**

The place begins at a height where the air has grown thin enough to forget what moisture is. There is no water here, not as river, rain, dew, or seep, and not even as the memory of water in the shape of stone. What lies beneath the sky is an ancient table of dry mineral, high and broad and nearly flattened, as though the entire world had once been pressed flat by a slow, patient hand. The terrain does not rush upward into sharp peaks. It rises first into long, low ridges, then into shelves of pale grey and black stone, then into a vast plateau that opens toward every horizon with the quiet indifference of a dry sea.

The color of the land is mostly darkness softened by mineral warmth. The ground is not black as a void, but the black-brown of old iron, the ash-white of fused anhydrite, the dull blue of deep basalt, and the translucent grey of salt crusts that have never been wet in the way living bodies understand wetness. In places, veins of glass thread through the rock, pale as frozen breath, their edges rounded by eons of windless erosion. Elsewhere the surface appears to have been sanded to a final profile, as if every crack had been studied and allowed only its last possible form. Nothing looks newly broken here. Nothing looks unfinished. The plain appears to have completed itself so long ago that completion has become its deepest nature.

It is warm, though there is no bright sun. That warmth comes from below and from the machinery of the place: a low, dry heat that rises through the stone and settles against the skin like the warmth of a wall after nightfall. The air carries the faint taste of metal and dust, not the sharpness of water or the richness of rot. It is clean in the way a sealed vault is clean, and it does not move much. There is no wind to speak of, no shifting weather, no season with intention to arrive. Clouds do not form overhead because there is no liquid vapor to gather them. What lies above is a vast darkness of fine suspended haze, lit not from outside but from within, by the old furnaces and the slow glow of warmed metal and stone. The darkness is not empty; it is full of faint, static light, red as banked coals and grey as cold smoke.

From one high point, the landscape opens into a regional sprawl: a basin here, a broken wall of ridge there, a long terrace of pale salt beyond, and beyond that, the suggestion of a valley so wide and ancient that its edges have softened into the plain. The scale is not narrow or intimate; it is the scale of a town, a working land, a forgotten province. Yet nothing clusters too tightly. The presence of things is sparse. A low wall may stand for a mile with nothing else to break the line of the horizon. A road of fused slag may run beneath the sky for hours, flanked only by the shapes of stopped machines. Settlements exist, but they are not huddled. They are separated by great dry distances, like islands in an ocean of mineral stillness.

The place is urban, but it is also geological.

The built world has become so old that it no longer seems to have been imposed upon the land. It belongs to the land now, in the way that a fossil belongs to stone. Foundries rise from the plateau not as clean, tall buildings, but as long, low complexes of blackened masonry and ceramic tile, their roofs thick with dust that has settled into permanent patterns. Warehouses lie half-buried, their doors fused with salt crust, their interiors still containing rows of empty racks or stone-lined pits. Chimneys stand in crooked lines, but they emit no smoke, because the engines that once made smoke have long since moved into another kind of operation, one without beginning or end.

The city—if it can be called one city rather than many—appears as scattered urban fragments connected by infrastructure. There are roadways cut directly into the salt shelf, wide enough for processions but too quiet for traffic. There are elevated walks of riveted iron, their surfaces oxidized into a matte black film, passing from one terrace to another. There are shafts, pits, and boreholes arranged in repeating intervals, some capped with iron discs, others open to a deep, warm darkness below. There are rows of houses built from fused slag and carved mineral blocks, all similar in outline but worn in different degrees, their windows narrow and dark, their roofs rounded by time rather than pitched to shed rain. There are plazas of flat basalt, and beneath them, vaulted underpasses lined with pipes whose contents have not moved in the ordinary sense for ages beyond counting.

This is industrial construction, but it is not the loud, advancing industry of expansion and production. The technology present here is heavy, mechanical, and elaborate. Great flywheels sit in sunken chambers, their rims coated in fine dust that never accumulates beyond a certain thickness. Piston rods extend from walls into sealed cylinders, moving with a rhythm too slow to hear from a distance. Gear trains of blackened metal turn inside open towers, their teeth meshing without shriek or spark. Belts run over pulleys, taut and silent, carrying no cargo that could be named. Boilers stand in rows, their surfaces warm but unlit, as if they have learned to generate heat without flame. The place hums, ticks, and holds pressure, but it does not produce new things.

It is a city built to maintain the state of the world.

The machines are not in decay. They are not in repair. They are not running toward a goal. They have achieved a kind of permanence: every movement is a recurrence, every cycle a preservation. Heat enters, heat leaves, and yet the temperature does not change. Metal is stressed, metal is cooled, and yet no new fracture opens. The engines operate within a closed equilibrium so complete that their work resembles prayer or ritual: repeated, exact, and incapable of improving the world because the world has already reached its settled form. The land is warm, but there are no gradients of heat to drive a storm or melt ice. The plain is high, but there is no weather to descend upon it. The city is industrial, but there is no smoke, no progress, no waste, and no hunger.

Because the environment has been stripped of water and vegetation, it contains no familiar life. There are no trees, no mosses, no lichens, no grasses, no creeping roots, no fungal forests in the usual sense. Nothing green breaks the dark mineral palette. The surface is not barren in the way a dead desert might be, however, because barrenness implies the possibility of life having failed, whereas this place seems to have never accepted it in the first place. What lives here is not plant life, nor anything that resembles it. Instead, within the pores of the anhydrite and along the seams of cooled slag, there are pale, threadlike networks that are closer to mineral growth than to biology. They are not quite fungus and not quite crystal. They spread through stone in slow, branching lines, feeding on chemical tension without leaving the body of the rock. In dim light, if one presses an ear near a wall or lies upon a slab of salt, there may be a faint sound like dry sand adjusting in a sealed vessel. These threads do not bloom. They do not fruit. They persist.

Fauna is rare, but present.

In the deeper cracks of the plateau, where the warmth from below gathers and the darkness is nearly absolute, small lithovores move at intervals measured in years rather than minutes. They resemble no animal one would expect from a world of stone. Their bodies are thin plates of calcium-like shell over segments of flexible black tissue, and their legs end in points that do not pierce rock so much as ask it to open a little. They feed by rubbing themselves against the veins of mineral threads, drawing sustenance from a chemistry that has nothing to do with leaves or blood. They are slow, solitary, and almost invisible, and when one does appear it feels less like the sighting of a living thing than like the accidental revelation of a small, hidden equation.

There are also things that move through the air without wings in any biological sense: suspended crystalline forms that drift upward when the temperature inside a shaft reaches a threshold that it has reached forever, then descend when the threshold recedes. They are not quite creatures, not quite machines, not quite particles. They may have a metabolism, or they may have a rhythm that only resembles one. They pass through the dark like slow sparks that do not burn. The locals—if any remain, or if the word means anything here—call them “ash-shivers,” though no one is certain whether the name is recent or as ancient as the stone itself.

Sound is rare and carefully shaped by the emptiness.

The Stillworks is mostly quiet, but not silent. Silence would be too clean for such a place. Here the quiet is layered: the tick of a gear somewhere below, the long metallic creak of a cooling bridge, the whisper of dust particles sliding along a polished shaft, the muffled thud of a piston finding the end of its travel, the soft crackle of a thermal seam in the salt. If one stands on the high plain, away from the structures, the dominant sound is the warmth itself, or the feeling of it, as if the stone were breathing through a mask. If one enters the settlement, the machinery becomes a slow heartbeat heard through the soles of the feet. There is no wind to carry voices, no rain to drown them, no birdsong, no insect chorus, no bark, no rustle of leaves. The sounds that exist are isolated and precise, and for that reason they feel more meaningful than noise.

The atmosphere is one of immense stability. There is no erosion in the active sense, no fresh collapse, no new landslide. The cliffs do not retreat. The salt does not recrystallize into new shapes. The roads do not crack further. Even the dust seems to have learned its final arrangement. The land has become so old that it is no longer merely a place where time has passed, but a place where time has arrived at a resting state. It is not frozen, exactly, because motion still exists within the city and within the mineral veins. But that motion is so perfectly balanced that it produces no visible change. A wall does not age. A gear does not wear out. A crack does not deepen. The world is in equilibrium so deep that it can afford to move forever without altering itself.

Because of this, the place feels strange—not chaotic, not broken, but wrong in a subtle and permanent way. The darkness is too complete to be night, and too steady to be weather. The warmth is too constant to be a day, and too dry to be a season. The machines are too old to be modern, and too perfect to be abandoned. There is no city of people, or perhaps there is no people left to claim the city, yet there is no ruin. There is no desert, yet nothing lives that would be recognized as life. There is no water, yet the stone appears to remember shapes that water alone could create. There is no wind, yet the dust moves in patterns that suggest a long, gentle breath held inside the world.

One walks across the plateau not toward a destination but deeper into the idea of the destination.

A road begins beside a row of low kilns whose mouths are sealed with black glaze. The road is not made of stone in the usual way; it is fused mineral, pale as old bone, and its surface has been worn by a footfall that no longer exists. Alongside it stand the shells of pumping houses, their roofs gone, their walls thick with salt crystals that grow in precise hexagonal lattices. In the open air, iron pylons rise at uneven intervals, supporting cables that hum with a low static charge. The ground beneath them is dry and warm, and sometimes a faint blue light spreads across the salt for an instant, then vanishes, then does not vanish but merely waits again.

Beyond the kilns, the terrain dips into a shallow basin the size of a market square. The basin floor is polished black, and the walls are pale with anhydrite bands. There are no plants, no pools, no moss. In the center of the basin stands a machine like a circular loom made of basalt and bronze, its arms extended but not moving. Around it are benches of fused glass, too regular to be natural, too worn to be recent. If one sits there long enough, the darkness seems to become attentive. Not alive. Not malicious.
