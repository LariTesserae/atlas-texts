At the Sinterwork, the world is being assembled in the same posture forever.

You hear it from the far rim before you see anything truly clear: a constant, layered noise, not like a single river or waterfall, but like ten thousand small mouths working together. Hiss. Crack. Slap. Chime. The wet heat comes first, then the sound settles into the body, then the eye adapts to the dimness and the place becomes legible. It is a low basin, open to a wide horizon, and it smells of salt, hot stone, shell, and something faintly fungal, like the air after an old bathhouse drains.

There is no plant life here. No green, no grass, no reeds bending in the wind, no bushes on the ridges, no trees to cast shade. The land looks as if it is made of bone, salt, glass, and damp mineral skin. Where a marsh might normally be soft with roots and stalks, the Sinterwork has crusts. Thin white efflorescences bloom over darker surfaces. Iron-brown films stain the shallow pools. Pale, chalky sheets ripple outward from the waterline, hardening in the warm air as if the water is sweating stone onto itself. The atmosphere is so saturated that droplets form even in places where the ground appears dry. They gather on ledges, run in tiny silver threads down faces of crystalline sinter, and fall into channels with an almost musical continuity.

The basin lies low, almost too low, as though the land had been pressed down by a slow hand. The horizon is broad and open, but not emptily open. It is filled with forms. Far out across the flats, the terrain lifts into long terraces, shallow ridges, and irregular mounds that might be mistaken for ruins if not for their rawness. They look unfinished. That is because, in a sense, they are always unfinished.

The geology here is emerging rather than worn. Nothing is rounded by long erosion. The edges of the mineral shelves are sharp, glassy, or freshly crystallized. Obsidian-black plates lie tilted at awkward angles, their surfaces pitted with steam scars. White aragonite ridges rise from the brine in combs and lips, their teeth still wet and gleaming. Salt crusts crackle in thin flakes where the water has retreated an inch overnight, but it never retreats far. The pools are warm, often almost skin-warm in the shallows, hotter near the threaded vents where the steam begins. Water is not confined to channels; it is near everywhere, just beneath the surface, rising in capillary dark patches, pooling in footprints, condensing on ceilings of low mineral arches.

This is a wet place, but not a flowing place. It has more the character of breathing, seeping, exuding water. The land appears to sweat. Rocks weep. Steps are perpetually slick. Channels cut through the terraces carry brine away only to return it elsewhere in vapor, then drip, then deposit it again as mineral skin. The water does not seem to travel from mountains to sea as it would in an ordinary marsh. It circulates nearby, trapped between the warm ground, the saturated air, and the porous crusts of the city that has grown on them.

The Sinterwork is urban. That is the second thing you notice, once the first roar of wet stone fades into pattern. It is not a city of towers, not a city of roads lined with neat houses. It is a settlement built into the raw crust of the basin, and it is dense to a degree that feels almost organic. Platforms crowd one another. Walkways cross at multiple heights. A terrace may have a lower shelf beneath it, a chamber beside it, and a sloping roof of mineral plates above it. Nothing stands alone. The built forms lean, press, and connect. Walls rise from natural ridges, then continue into hand-laid stone. Stairways begin as cracks widened and stepped by people, then terminate at small plazas ringed with low basins. From a distance, the whole city resembles a coral reef turned mineral and salted by human habitation.

The scale is that of a town, perhaps spreading across a valley-sized bowl, but the density is higher than such a scale would normally allow. Near the center, or what passes for the center—a broad, shallow depression where the vents are thickest—the structures approach and layer over one another. There are always more edges than sky. There are always more hollows than flat floors. If you stand in the dense quarter, your hand can almost touch someone else’s wall across a gap narrow enough to drop a shell into and not wide enough to escape if it lands there. The space is full. Not just built full, but inhabited full.

There is primitive craft everywhere. Metal is rare and simple: soft copper pins, a few thin beads, perhaps one or two blades that have been cold-worked and polished by hand. The main tools are stone, shell, bone, and chipped obsidian. Scrapers made from black volcanic glass are worn to the shape of thumbs. Awls cut from long animal bones are used to pierce sinew thread through gut membranes stretched between stone frames. Hammers of dense river pebble—river stones being scarce here, so perhaps carried from far beyond the basin—rest beside salt mortars and grinding slabs. Nothing industrial intrudes. There are no wheels turning in the streets, no iron beams, no powered looms, no great machines. What order the Sinterwork has comes from human patience, mineral growth, and the repetition of small actions performed by many people over a long time.

The settlement is built not from wood, which does not exist here, but from the land itself. The people cut slabs from sinter deposits after they have hardened, or they shape clay dug from warmer pits near the vents, mixing it with salt and crushed shell until it binds. They stack small stones and seal them with mineral paste. They raise roofs as shallow curves, so that dripping water slides along and falls into channels below. They build walls in alternating layers: dark basalt-like shards, then white salt-crusted clay, then rusted fragments of iron-rich mineral, then more white. The pattern repeats with variations across the town, giving the place a look of layered bedding, as though houses are not constructed so much as sedimented.

There are doorways, but many are only holes scraped wider in a crust wall. There are roofs, but some are little more than stretched membranes over a frame of ribs and stone anchors. There are floors, but floors in the Sinterwork are often sloped, because the builders learned early that level ground collects brine. The streets are channels or raised paths. The plazas are depressions with drains. The houses are niches. The city is not a collection of boxes placed on land, but a modification of land into boxes, shelves, pits, lids, and passages.

Dim light hangs over all of it. The sky above the basin is rarely clear. A thin veil of steam and mineral haze filters the sun into something pale and diffuse, never bright enough to cast hard noon shadows, never dark enough to be night. The result is a permanent, low-contrast twilight. The edges of the buildings glow faintly when wet, but colors remain muted: bone-white, iron-red, sulphur-yellow, charcoal black, brown like boiled bark. At the vents, the air shimmers with warmth, and the light bends in soft waves. Farther out, where the crust thins, the ground looks as if it has been dusted with powdered shell. The dimness makes the dense parts of the city seem deeper than they are; you look into one gap and see several recesses, another walkway, a hanging basin, a moving shape, and then another shape, all layered and half-shadowed.

The sound is never far, and it is never singular. It is the first and last thing the Sinterwork demands. Hissing steam from the lower vents. Ticking and cracking as mineral plates expand or shift slightly with heat. The wet slap of dripping from roof to channel. The grinding of stone underfoot. The low, bubbling gurgle of shallow pools disturbed by creatures. The faint, metallic ringing of wind passing through porous ridges perforated by countless small holes. Then, on top of all this, the animal noise: not one cry, but a chorus of small ones. Brine flies buzz in dense veils. Crabs click their claws against salt shelves. Fish snap at surface film. Birds cry from the upper terraces, where nesting holes are cut or dug into the sinter. The people add their own sounds, but they do not dominate them: stone scraping stone, a mallet tapping a chisel, voices pitched low enough to compete without exhausting themselves, laughter sometimes rising briefly into the steam and disappearing.

The ecology is abundant but strange if measured by ordinary forests. With no vegetation, the world has built itself on other foundations. The primary living surfaces are microbial mats and fungal growths. These are the Sinterwork’s equivalents of meadow, scrub, and forest. They spread across mineral crusts in skins of ochre, rust, cream, grey-blue, and black. Some are thick like felt; others are thin as dust. In the warm, sulfurous shallows, mats build low shelves that ripple over themselves.
