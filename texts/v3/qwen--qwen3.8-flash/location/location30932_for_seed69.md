## The Weeping Table

It is a highland without a visible horizon.

Not because the land is hidden by mountains, fog, or distance, but because there is no light in which such things might be seen. The darkness here is not night. Night implies a sun somewhere else, waiting, or at least the memory of one. Here the absence is total. The sky is not black because it is empty; it is black because it is full—full of dense air, full of vapor, full of warm cloud that presses against the world like a wet hand over an eye. The plateau sits high above the cloud sea, so high that its stone breathes out heat, but the atmosphere is still thick, layered, and humid. This is not the thin, clean cold of mountains. This is an upland soaked to the marrow, steaming with warmth, where every surface sweats and every stone feels like a tongue left in the dark.

The land is vast. Vast beyond the reach of a single weather event. Clouds do not pass over the Weeping Table so much as they migrate across it in slow, continent-wide tides. Rain begins in one district and continues in another for weeks. A storm may be born in a southern marsh, grow into a wall of moving water, then die in a northern basin with a sound like stone swallowing metal. From a distance, the plateau is not a place one can map by sight. It is measured by echo, by current, by pressure, by the way the wind changes when a mountain face turns it aside. Its scale is felt in the lungs before it is known by the mind.

The terrain itself is ancient and settled. This is not new rock. It has been rounded, fluted, undercut, split, polished, and rebuilt so many times that it has come to rest in its own character. The high mesas are broad, their edges worn into soft curves by countless seasons of wind and water. Between them lie wide basins of black peat, shallow lakes of still warm water, and river channels that have forgotten urgency and learned patience. Some ridges resemble the backs of drowned animals, their stone spines exposed and weathered smooth. Others stand like broken walls, not because of any war, but because the plateau is a table of sediment laid down long before the current dark, and erosion has carved its legs away one by one.

Water is everywhere, or nearly so. It is not always open water, not always a river or lake or rain. More often it is a condition. The rocks are wet. The air is wet. The soil is wet. The bark of the dark trees is wet. Moss holds water like a sponge that has decided to live forever. In the mornings—if mornings can be said to exist without dawn—the plateau drips. It drips from stone ledges into shallow bogs. It drips from leaves into the mouths of burrowing creatures. It drips from the undersides of cloud banks that hang just above the ground, turning whole fields into a fine mist that never fully falls and never fully clears.

The rains are heavy. They arrive as a pressure first: a thickening of the air, a taste of metal and wet fungus, a low change in the way the wind carries itself through the high grass. Then the water comes down in sheets and needles and drums. It beats on broad leaves, on stone, on water already waiting on the surface. The land accepts it as a returning guest. Channels fill. Bogs swell. The peat lifts and sinks like a breathing body. Water runs down the flanks of mesas through established grooves, little rivers carved by the same currents over centuries, and gathers in wide, shallow bowls where reeds grow in dense clumps and the surface trembles with the movement of small creatures below.

And yet the warmth remains. The plateau is high, but it is not cold. The rock gives off heat from deep below. Geothermal vents open in the marshes like hidden mouths, sending columns of warm water and vapor into the dark air. The temperature is close to that of living things, around the level of breath, slightly too warm, slightly too close. It makes the water smell rich, almost mineral-sweet, and encourages growth on every surface that will hold it. The heat is invisible to the eye—there is no eye that matters here—but it can be felt as a soft blanket of warmth moving through the low places, and as a sting in the nostrils when one stands near a steam pool.

The vegetation is lush, almost too lush, as though the world is made mostly of water and warmth and has chosen to cover itself in plant life out of habit. But it is not green. Green is a word that belongs to sunlit things. Here the vegetation is black, gray, blue-black, ochre, ash-white, and deep red-brown. It grows in dense layered curtains, in mats, in thickets, in hanging skeins, in carpets so deep they swallow the feet. The forest is not a forest of clear trunks and open understories. It is a compression: stem against leaf, root against fungus, branch against dripping moss. The air is not merely damp with vegetation; it is full of it. Spores ride every current. Fibers loosen and drift. Dead fronds fall in silence that becomes part of the larger sound.

The trees—if they are trees—have no broad leaves to catch light. Instead they grow thick, leathery fronds and folded, pleated growths that seem made to gather vapor. Some are short, thick, and rounded like mushrooms. Others are tall, pale-stemmed things with caps wider than a house, their undersides ridged with gills that drip condensation in slow, musical patterns. There are also the thrum-vines: long, tough strands of growth that spiral up from marsh roots and stretch between the high stone faces. They are not beautiful in any visual sense; beauty is a hard word in this place. But they are marvels of function. Their surfaces are lined with tiny crystalline hairs that catch vibration. When wind passes through them, they hum. When rain strikes them, they ring. When large animals brush against them, they release pulses of scent, spore, or heat. The plateau’s ecology has learned to feed on motion, sound, chemistry, and warmth.

The ground is layered in depth and density. There is the surface mat: wet, spongy, alive with creeping rhizomes and pale rootlets. Below that is the peat layer, black and soft and holding water so long that it sometimes seems to hold time. Below the peat are mineral strata, some porous, some sealed, some threaded with electric ore. Below that, in places, is the hollow sound of underground water moving through ancient channels. This is a land with strata. Everything touches everything else. Roots press into metal remnants. Moss climbs over broken machinery. Steam from deep faults warms fungal mats above. The boundary between geology and biology is not sharp; it is a negotiation.

Fauna is abundant. Not merely present, not scattered, but abundant, pressing into every niche with the persistence of life that has no fear of the dark. Small creatures swarm through the marshes and the underside of the fronds: click-midges, blind beetles, silk-bodied crawlers that move like water over land. Larger animals stand in the shallow bogs in herds, their bodies broad and low, their eyes absent or sealed shut under skin. Their heads are shaped for listening: wide, flared, cupped, ridged. Some have combs of bone or keratin along their skulls that send and receive vibration. Their movements are slow, deliberate, and highly coordinated. They do not need to see one another. They know the position of the herd by touch, by breath, by the rhythm of hooves on wet ground.

Predators move through this abundance with practiced patience. They are not large in every case; many are small, quick, and terrible in proportion. They hunt by pressure changes, by heat, by scent, by sound. In the high grasses, things with segmented bodies and long, sensitive forelimbs wait for the tremor of grazing prey. In the water, flat-backed hunters rise from the muddy shallows with mouths like wide, lined bowls, snapping with speed that can be heard. In the air above the plateau, vast flocks of membrane-winged animals drift through the vapor, their wingbeats making a continuous whisper over the land. When one of them is taken by a hunter, there is a brief, sharp ripple of sound, then the swarm adjusts and continues. Life here is so abundant that death is woven into the daily atmosphere.

The sound is loud. Loud is perhaps too simple a word, because it is not only volume, but density. The plateau is never silent, and it is never quiet except in rare pockets beneath overhangs, behind stones, in the deepest hollows of fungal roots. Water speaks constantly: drip, run, splash, pool, fall, hiss. Steam hisses. Rain drums. Wind rasps through fronds. The thrum-vines sing lower notes. The animals call in clicks, grunts, resonant moos, wing-whirs, footfalls, and strange, layered vocalizations that can travel for miles through the dense air. And beneath all of this is the mechanical sound, because the land is industrial.

Not heavily populated, not city-crowded, not a sprawl of buildings. The structures are scattered, small, and often alone: a single pump-house on a ridge, a shed of blackened stone beside a thermal vent, a gantry of cables rising above a marsh, a half-buried engine in a valley that still turns without tending. These are not ruins in the usual sense. Some may be old. Many are still working. They sit lightly on the land, as if the plateau were a vast field in which the machines were placed here and there, each one responsible for a process much larger than itself. A single building may feed power into channels that run for miles. A solitary boiler may heat a water table. A lone factory mouth may open into a cliff face and release its noise into the weather.

The industry is loud, warm, and electric in ways that do not require light. Cables run through the peat and stone, their currents felt as vibrations, tasted as metal, sensed by creatures with electrical organs near their mouths or tails. Machines grind and cycle with a regularity that has become part of the ecology. Huge wheels turn behind open housing. Pistons move. Belts shift. Water is pumped, filtered, boiled, cooled, and redirected. The factories do not roar so much as breathe and clank, but their breath is steam, and their heartbeat is gearwork. They are not illuminated by fires, for fire is too bright to hide even when unseen—here fire would reveal itself by heat and smoke, by sound and scent. The machines are powered by geothermal pressure, by falling water, by stored currents, by the plateau’s own long-running metabolism.

This gives the land a strange, hybrid ecology. The machinery is not separate from life. It is habitat. Steam vents warm fungal gardens. Cable lines become perches for silent, winged animals. Water channels built by industry fill with blind fish whose lateral lines are so sensitive they can detect a gear turn from a mile away. Peat grows over abandoned tools until the metal becomes part of the bog, soft and useful, no longer foreign. Some machines seem almost biological because they have been so long alive in this environment, and others seem almost geological because they have been worn by the same forces as the stones. The plateau has learned to use them.

The weirdness is subtle but persistent. The world has rules, and some of those rules are not the usual ones. In the lower marshes, rain sometimes does not fall straight down. It seems to hesitate. Tiny beads gather in the dense humid air, trembling with static, held up by a field so common that no creature notices it, until the weight becomes too much and they drop all at once in a sudden clatter. The weeping rocks sometimes weep in patterns: a rhythm, almost like speech, repeated through generations until the sound becomes familiar, comforting, wrong. The thrum-vines do not merely respond to vibration; some appear to grow toward it, leaning closer to the loudest factories, as if sound were sunlight to them. There are pools so still that when a stone is dropped into them, the echo does not stop. It continues beneath the surface, returning minutes later as a warmth at the fingertips. There are places where the darkness feels not like absence, but like material—thick, warm, almost tactile, so that one does not see into distance, but presses through it.

Yet the land is not chaotic. It is established. The erosion is mature. The water cycles are known. The creatures have their migrations and hunting grounds. The machines have their rhythms. Everything has settled into a balance, though not a balance that rests still. It is a moving balance, like a breath held at the top of an inhale, or like a wheel turning so smoothly that it appears still. The plateau is in its own state. It is not broken. It is not waiting to be fixed. It is simply itself: high, wet, dark, warm, loud, industrial, alive.

The atmosphere is dense with presence. Water and air and life and machine are so thoroughly mixed that no single element stands apart. A breath taken on the Weeping Table is not clean. It is full. It contains mist, spore, mineral scent, warm steam, the ozone of electrical currents, the musk of living bodies, the faint bitter note of wet iron. It coats the inside of the nose. It settles on the skin. It is tasted in the back of the throat. To move through the plateau is to pass through layers: the cold film of mist at the edge of a marsh, the warm breath of a steam pit, the damp tangle of fronds, the hard vibration of a stone channel, the soft collapse of peat beneath a boot, the metallic thrum of a cable line, the sudden open space where a cliff drops into nothing that cannot be seen but can be heard as water falling far below.

The weather is active and constant. Fog rolls in not as a curtain but as a living mass, climbing slopes, filling basins, then withdrawing again when the geothermal heat shifts. Rain may last for days, then stop not all at once but in stages: first the heavy drops, then the fine spray, then a lingering mist that hangs for hours before dissolving. The wind is a river as well, moving through the plateau’s corridors between mesas and ridges, bending the thrum-vines into long, singing arcs. In places, the wind meets the updraft from steam vents and forms spinning columns of droplets that rise and fall in endless cycles, audible from far away as a low whistling roar.

Predator and prey exist in tight, noisy intimacy. Because no one can see, everything must be listened for. A step in the mud is a broadcast. A splash is a signal. A wingbeat may be a lure, an alarm, or a dinner bell. Herds move in formations that make it difficult for predators to isolate a single target. Birds of prey—though “birds” is a poor name, since they have too many membranes and too few eyes for such a comparison—hunt by listening for the faintest tremor in the water. Burrowing things listen upward. Crawling things listen sideways. Fish listen through the river itself. The whole fauna is tuned to sound, and therefore the whole ecology is loud: not because the animals are noisy by nature, but because the world demands noise. In darkness, a creature that cannot be heard does not exist.

The scattered built structures make the plateau seem inhabited by a smallness that is also enormous. There is no metropolis here, no city skyline pressing into the dark. There is only a lone hut set against a rock, with its walls grown through with moss and its roof too heavy with condensation to be dry. There is a factory built into a cliff, no larger than a house, but with a tunnel that descends far beyond comprehension. There is a rail spur ending in a marsh, its tracks partly sunk and overgrown, yet one machine on it still clicks at regular intervals, resetting itself, moving forward a hand’s breadth, clicking again. These structures suggest that whatever people once built the industrial network are gone, changed, absent, or simply out of sight. Their remains are faint marks, partial remains, occasional artifacts embedded in the mud: a wheel, a cable anchor, a metal plate worn thin by centuries of dripping water, a bone carved with a spiral pattern, a ceramic vessel no bigger than a fist filled with seeds.

The darkness has no stars, no moon, no dawn. Time is not marked by the passage of light. It is marked by the cycles of rain, the warming and cooling of steam, the migrations of animals, the rhythmic turning of distant machinery. Some cycles are short: a storm passing in an afternoon that has no afternoon, a predator call ending in a wet silence, a gear completing a revolution. Some are long: the slow thickening of peat, the gradual wearing of a mesa’s lip, the migration of thermal vents from one basin to another over generations. The land has its own chronology, written in erosion and echo.

What emerges is a landscape that feels both ancient and industrially present. It is not a wasteland. It is not a dead machine. It is not a primordial swamp, because the swamp has been worked, pumped, redirected, heated, and woven with metal. It is also not a ruin-field, because the ruins are too alive. The plateau is a functioning world, one in which water, stone, plant, animal, and machine have all settled into roles. The darkness is the most complete fact of it, but the darkness does not stop the land. It only forces the land to be louder, warmer, denser, stranger, and more intimate than it could be under light.

A traveler moving across the Weeping Table would not see the marshes. They would stand in them. They would feel the wet ground lift and settle under their weight. They would hear the trees breathing. They would smell the steam. They would taste the mineral air. They would sense the cables nearby, first as vibration, then as a faint pull against the teeth. They would know where the predators were by the way the smaller sounds stopped. They would know where the machines were by the way the natural noises changed around them—water running differently, wind bending differently, animals avoiding or exploiting a particular patch of stone. In the end, they would know that the place has no need of light. It already has warmth, movement, sound, life, and industry. It already has depth. It already has a kind of sight: the dark’s own, made of touch and echo and the long memory of wet stone.

The Weeping Table continues. It rains. The water gathers and runs. The fungi expand by slow degrees. The herds click and shift through the bogs. The engines turn. The cables hum. The plateau breathes out heat and takes in water. Its darkness is vast, its life is loud, its machines are scattered but stubborn, and its world is fully itself: a high, wet, warm, dense continent-scale landscape where absence of light has made presence of everything else impossible to ignore.
