A low bowl of clay and black brick lies at the bottom of a long, warm slope. It is not a city in the way the word usually implies, nor a farm, nor a forest. It is a small settlement of wet industry, no wider than a cathedral nave and its cloister, perhaps a few hundred yards across, held behind a ring of levees and outer ditches. Inside that boundary, the ground never dries out completely. If a hole were dug even shallowly, water would bead in the walls. If a stone were turned over, it would come up dark and glistening. The place has been shaped so thoroughly by water that the distinction between soil, foundation, channel, and floor has softened.

There is a frequent rain here, warm and close, arriving with no great drama but leaving everything changed for a day. The roofs collect it at once, tilting it into iron gutters and glazed clay spouts. Water runs across the streets in wide, shallow skins, pooling in the low places between paving stones, slipping into side channels, beading on railings, dripping from cable-hangers, falling in long threads from the undersides of balconies. It also comes from below. The levees are built over porous strata, and the inner walls of brick, stone, and root-lined concrete weep slowly, especially at the joints, where pale mineral streaks and dark green moss have grown in parallel. Some surfaces seem to sweat even when there is no rain. Cold iron pipes stand in rooms of warm air, and condensation forms on them, collecting at their bases in small puddles that are then carried away by the canals.

The town is dense with plants, but not with wildness. The greenery is structural, economic, necessary. It is a living scaffold and a processing medium at once. Willows and alders have been trained through brick frames, their branches woven into the outer skins of houses and sheds. Mangrove-like prop roots descend through grates into shallow water below, knitting themselves into the foundations over years. Roofs are not empty planes but terraces of reed beds, mosses, and low, succulent ground-cover plants, held in trays of soil so thick with roots that rainwater drains slowly through them.
