## The Latticed March

It is a highland without stone, a marsh without mud, and a city that has never known a citizen.

You reach it at the top of a long, warm ascent—not up a mountain of rock, but up a growing shelf of living construction: a plateau formed from compressed fiber, fused bark-plates, hollow root-columns, and vast membranes of translucent plant tissue stretched tight as parchment over water. The place is called, if it is called anything, **the Latticed March**, though no one has named it. The name belongs to the pattern of its channels and arches, and that pattern is everywhere visible, repeating itself across valley-sized distances, opening outward in long curves and straight water-streets until it dissolves into heat-shimmer on the horizon.

The land is high, but there is no cliff face, no boulder field, no exposed mineral stratum to explain it. What you see is the upper surface of an enormous biological construction. From below—if you could look through the steam and the low clouds that ring its lower reaches—it resembles a forest turned inside out and raised into a tableland. Its supports are not trees in any ordinary sense, but great buttresses of woven stem-fiber, swollen at the base, split into hundreds of load-bearing strands. Some of these structures are wide enough to form plazas. Others stand like towers a hundred paces apart, their tops fanning into roof-like discs. Between them stretch open courts of wet, shining fiber, broad and empty, the scale not intimate but regional: the view extends for leagues of constructed marsh.

Everything feels made, but not built in the human way. There are no tools, no looms, no cut stones, no mortar, no engineered joints. The making is purely biological: growth responding to water, heat, pressure, light, and age. The “ground” beneath your feet is not soil. It is a living plate, a stratified tissue of old generations and new, fibrous in texture, slightly elastic underfoot, and never truly dry. It gives faintly when pressed, as if over something soft and wet, because beneath it is indeed soft and wet: a deep layer of saturated root-mass and black, warm water. The surface is stitched together by ridges of hardened plant secretion, pale as bone but not bone, forming lines that look for all the world like paved seams.

The place is wet in a way that goes beyond standing water. Water is near the surface everywhere, but it does not only collect; it rises, condenses, runs, falls, and weeps from surfaces. The air is hot, and the heat belongs to the March itself: vast metabolic activity within the living substrate, slow fermentation in the lower wet layers, and the trapped warmth of a highland basin under a strong sun. At ordinary elevation, such humidity would be oppressive. Here, at altitude, it creates a strange, shimmering atmosphere: bright normal light overhead, but heat rising visibly from the pools and channels, so that the open courts seem to stand inside a slow furnace of steam and sun.

This is not rain in the usual sense, though rain comes often. The March has its own internal weather. Broad roof-leaves, some wide enough to shade a whole platform, collect moisture during the night and release it slowly during the day. Condensation beads along the undersides of these canopies, drips into gutters carved into living stems, and feeds channels that run along the length of the plateau. Other moisture rises directly from the surface through porous “weeping columns,” pale fibrous stalks that exude water from their tips in a steady, glassy flow. Pools form where these flows meet, perfectly still for a moment, then disturbed by the falling of a leaf-roof or the sudden release of pressure from a hidden chamber below. The result is a landscape of constant dampness: dripping, running, standing, seeping, evaporating.

Vegetation does not merely grow here; it constitutes the environment. There is no bare ground on which plants are set. The ground is made of plant matter. The walls, arches, buttresses, roofing, channels, floors, and floating platforms are all living or recently living plant structures. Some of it remains green and growing, with broad, glossy leaves, pale ribbed stems, and curtains of hanging fiber that move slightly in the hot air. Some of it has hardened into materials more like architecture: bark-plates cured by heat into curved panels, fibrous mats sealed with sap, and stem-cores left behind after older tissues die back, becoming skeletal corridors. Nothing here is inert. The place is all-living-mass, a total plant ecosystem that has become a total constructed landscape.

Because there is no animal life, the silence is not empty but strange. There are no birds, no insects, no grazing creatures, no movement through the undergrowth except the motion of plant parts themselves. That absence makes the sounds seem even stranger. The March is loud—not with voices, but with mechanical noise without machines. Wind moving through the open lattice creates a deep, shifting moan. The hollow stems and arched chambers act like enormous pipes, tuned by growth into different pitches. Some of them whisper, some hum, and some emit short, resonant cracks when pressure changes. Water surging through the channels produces a continuous liquid percussion: splash, gurgle, jet, pour. When one of the great roof-leaves collects enough condensation to shift, the whole structure creaks with a dry, groaning report. New fractures pop like distant whips. In the lower chambers, where heat and pressure build, water forces itself upward through narrow valves and bursts out with sharp, rhythmic thumps.

The place is young in its forms. That youth is visible in every surface. Edges are sharp. Fractures are fresh. The great supporting columns show clean splits where they have been forced apart by pressure below. The channels have not yet been smoothed into round, old shapes; their walls are angular, sometimes almost deliberately geometric, as if the living plate had broken along straight lines. There is no ancient wear here, no long settled erosion. The tableland appears to have recently emerged into its current shape and is still arguing with itself about the details. Whole districts may have been open sky until only a short while ago, their floor-plates newly joined, their first channels barely formed. The March has not yet become comfortable. It is still building and unbuilding at the same time.

And this unbuilding is not occasional. It is the rule of the place.

The landscape is turbulent. The heat that lifts water and drives the internal weather also makes the structures unstable. Turgor pressure in the stems changes with the day: swelling in the morning, tightening under noon heat, sagging again as the temperature dips. Channels widen or narrow depending on the state of the living walls that contain them. A platform that felt solid at dawn may, by midday, have risen slightly, its edges curled upward by internal water pressure. A buttress that supports one section may be forced into new growth by the slow shifting of weight above it. Old parts die; new parts replace them; water reroutes itself through fresh cracks. The Latticed March does not simply exist. It pulses.

Its ecology is a closed, all-plant system, but not a simple one. Different forms have become specialized into functions. Some plants act as structural material, thickening their tissues into load-bearing ribs. Others work as waterproofing, secreting glossy coatings that keep water on the surface instead of letting it vanish into the substrate. Some function as drainage, growing in long straight tubes that guide liquid downward. Others serve as reservoirs, storing condensation in swollen chambers and releasing it gradually. Some species grow rapidly, filling cracks and forming new membranes over open water. Others grow slowly, becoming the hardened “bones” of the March. They do not interact like animal predators or prey. They negotiate through water, pressure, light, and time.

There is no soil, so nutrients move through dissolved films on the surface and through capillary channels inside the living plates. Dust and airborne minerals are collected by sticky filaments and washed into reservoirs by condensation. Spores and seed-motes drift in the hot air, settling on wet ledges, where they germinate into new growths. A fallen piece of older tissue may itself become a scaffold for fresh organisms. The whole system is self-contained, self-repairing, and self-rearranging. It is not “wild” in the familiar sense because nothing here is left to grow in the random direction. It is also not “cultivated” in any intentional sense. It is simply a landscape whose growth has become architecture.

Visually, the place is beautiful and unnerving. In the bright normal light of the highland sun, the open courts gleam pale white and green. Long channels of water stretch like roads toward the horizon. The roof-structures cast sharp, moving shadows across the wet floor-plates, because the leaves are never quite still. Distant buttresses look like ruin fragments, except they are new. Some of them seem to have been shaped for purposes: supports, shelters, bridges, arches, vaulted halls. The eye reads them as constructed, but the construction is accidental, ecological, biological. It is a city whose blueprint was written by heat and water.

The scale is expansive enough to make any single view feel like part of a larger pattern. You might stand in a wide court where a dozen channels meet, and see beyond it a long, straight ridge of raised fiber forming what could be mistaken for a boulevard. Further out, smaller arches span gaps where water has carved through the surface, and beyond those, the plateau begins to dip into a broad, shallow basin, its edges fringed with hanging curtains of moisture-dripping growth. The eye keeps finding more features: terraces, bridges, domed thickets of overlapping leaves, isolated columns with crowns of pale blades. Yet the density remains sparse. There is great distance between presences. Much of the visible space is open air, water, and flat shimmer. The emptiness has weight. A structure may rise alone in the middle of a basin, small against the light, and you realize how vast the emptiness is that surrounds it.

Because of that openness, sound travels unusually far. A single burst from a pressure chamber can echo across a mile of wet plate. The creak of an old beam can be heard as sharp, almost like a door being forced shut in a distant hall. Wind through the lattice produces long chords that rise and fall with the heat. The place is loud, but it is not busy. It is full of noise without hurry, movement without animal urgency, structure without architect, construction without intention.

The atmosphere is thick with a peculiar kind of stillness-in-motion. Air is hot and wet, but not stagnant. At ground level, it moves in slow drafts, pulled through the hollow cores of columns and back out through porous plates. These drafts create localized gusts in the open courts, lifting strands of fiber and sending small ripples across pools. In other places, the air feels motionless and heavy, saturated with steam rising from a black reservoir. The normal light makes everything visible, yet heat haze distorts the edges of distant structures, making them seem to tremble. The March is always there, clear and undeniable, and always on the verge of rearranging itself.

What is most unsettling is that the whole place behaves as if it is built for something, but nothing uses it. The architecture implies function: covered walkways, raised platforms, sheltered corners, channels sized for transport, openings positioned to catch light and wind. Yet there is no body, no mind, no animal that walks the passages. If a leaf-roof curves overhead like a vaulted ceiling, it does so because it has to shed water or resist heat. If a buttress angles like a support, it does so because the living plate beneath is under strain. The design is real, but it is not designed. It is not accidental either. It is grown.

The erosion parameter here is low not because the place is untouched, but because it is newly formed and has not yet had time to become round, worn, and subdued. The sharpness is part of its character. New cracks split the surface into clean planes.
