## The Chittering Terraces

The place announces itself first as sound, then as pressure, then as a wet warmth against the skin. It is not a valley, exactly, nor a cave, nor a forest, though all three seem to have been layered together and left to settle for so long that the boundary between them has disappeared. It is a high, open shelf of terraces and overhangs, spreading across a mountainside that no ordinary traveler would think to cross, because every surface has already been occupied.

There is no untouched ground.

That is the first rule of the place: everything is made. Not by hands, not by tools, not by plans, but by the accumulated labor of wings, legs, mouths, roots, spores, and secretions. The landscape is a construction site that has forgotten how to be empty. Raw stone, if it exists at all, is only a hidden skeleton beneath the visible world. What you see, touch, smell, and stand upon is built: compacted guano, calcium crusts, mud folded with chewed plant fiber, fungal mat, spider silk, shed larval cases, feather dust, dried egg membranes, rotting moss, hardened nest pulp, root lattices, and thin layers of mineral precipitate left by slow water. These materials are not arranged neatly; they are fused. Floors become ceilings. Walls become shelves. Pillars become arches. The whole region looks like a city grown from droppings and dampness, though no one built it, and no one owns it.

The scale is open enough to overwhelm the eye, yet dense enough to make distance feel crowded. From one terrace you can see several others above and below, each wrapped in living texture. They do not stretch to the horizon, but they do extend into the wider mountain, a series of broad ledges, recesses, and overhangs where fog settles and then lifts. There are gaps between terraces, but even the gaps are bridged by webs, root threads, calcite curtains, and nests. The place is regional, valley-like, a town-sized sprawl without streets. The air is full of small motions: things rising, things falling, things crawling sideways, things settling again. If you cup your hands in one place, you may gather moss, insects, larvae, water, egg shells, fungal threads, and a tiny living creature that scurries away. If you press your face close to a wall, you may find that the wall is breathing: thousands of small bodies in crevices, the whole surface alive from edge to edge.

The terraces are high, above the ordinary cloudline for much of the year, but they are not cold. Something about the mountain holds heat: deep thermal pockets, warm stone, a sheltered basin, perhaps the lingering breath of volcanic or geothermal rock beneath the shelf. The warmth makes the place feel human-scale in its temperature, like a room recently occupied. It is a warm dark. The air is thick with moisture and organic scent, but not stagnant. It is the kind of warmth that makes everything grow, everything stick, everything decompose, and everything feed something else.

Water here is not dramatic. There are no rivers, no lakes, no great waterfalls. There is dampness everywhere, and yet water is intermittent. The terraces know condensation as their primary gift. In the cooler parts of the day, moisture beads on mineral crusts and moss. In the warmer parts, it lifts and sinks again in a slow cycle. Pools exist, but only in depressions: shallow bowls of dark water ringed by slime and tiny roots. Some puddles appear after rain sweeps through the upper cliffs, then vanish as the warm air drinks them back. Drip points are constant but gentle. A wet film runs across the faces of the overhangs, then dries, then returns. There is no flood; there is no drought either. The place sits in a damp equilibrium, where everything is slightly wet and everything is waiting for water to arrive.

Light is scarce. The open terraces are protected from the full sun by fog, by height, by the angle of the mountain, by thick cloud that sometimes forms beneath the shelf like a gray sea. The darkness is not black. It is a living charcoal, a green-black, a brown-black, a color changed by moisture. In the deepest recesses, visibility is limited by the wet air itself. A faint sheen on the walls gives them a gray-green glow. In some places, low light allows mosses and algae to persist; in others, fungi carry their own pale luminescence, not bright, not magical, just a quiet scattering of spore-light. It is enough to show edges, enough to make the darkness textured. It is not light for looking at the landscape from afar. It is light for close living: for a moth to find a crevice, for a spider to feel a thread, for a creature to understand what is solid and what is merely wet.

Vegetation, by the usual measure of forests, might seem thin. By the measure of this place, it is lush. Moss is the true forest. It thickens across the terraces in deep mats, in ridged cushions, in long trailing beards, in felted skins over every surface that can hold water. Liverworts form tiny green roofs in the crevices. Ferns are rare, but where they appear they grow densely, their roots binding the built floor into something nearly soil-like. Algae coat the wetter ledges with a slippery olive film. Fungi dominate where the air is warmest: pale caps, black threads, white fuzz, and bracket structures that fuse the nest layers together. The plant life is not tall. It does not reach for the sun. It reaches for dampness, for nutrient, for grip. It is lush in the way that bread is lush with mold: soft, close, layered, and almost too abundant.

The animals are what truly make the place. There are so many that the air itself seems textured with life. Bats cluster in the higher recesses, hanging in dense sheets so thick that the ceiling looks like a moving cloth of black wings. Cave swifts or birds of the overhang occupy the upper ledges, their nests plastered to the walls and fused with the guano crusts and mud beneath them. In the lower floors, insects churn: crickets, beetles, moths, larvae, flies, cockroaches, isopods, mites, small predatory bugs with quick movements and dull bodies. Spiders weave curtains between the terraces, their webs catching dust, feather fragments, and insects. Some webs are so dense they become walls, layered with captured remains and reinforced by silk and spit. If you stand still, you may feel a fine vibration pass through the ground before you see its source. Sound here is not produced by one creature. It is produced by all of them at once.

The noise is deafening. It is not a single roar, but a woven roar: wingbeats, clicks, rasps, chirps, stridulations, the wet scraping of larvae, the dry crackle of chitin, the soft thud of bodies, the occasional sharp call from above, the constant low rush of air passing through packed nests. The sound seems to come from the surface itself because the surface is alive. There are no quiet moments. Even when one terrace seems still, another is full of movement. Even when the bats pause, the insects continue. Even when the insects pause, water drips, and the drip joins the rest. Sound fills every open space. If you speak, your voice is swallowed. If you shout, it becomes part of the texture. The place has a volume that belongs to it alone, a volume maintained by countless small lives.

Yet for all its noise and crowding, the landscape changes slowly. The dynamic is not violent. Nothing erupts, collapses, races, or floods in dramatic fashion. The terraces thicken by layers. A nesting floor may rise a hair’s width in a season. A patch of moss may spread over a guano crust in a year. A fungal mat may consolidate a crumbling wall. A collapse of one ledge becomes a ramp; a ramp becomes a terrace; a terrace becomes a roof for something beneath it. The cycles are gradual: warmth and coolness, moisture and drying, nesting seasons, insect breeding pulses, cloud sweeps, occasional rain, slow mineral deposition. The landscape is not finished. It is not ruined. It is established. Erosion here has long since ceased to be a force tearing the place apart; it has become a sculptor of settled forms. The edges of the terraces are rounded.
