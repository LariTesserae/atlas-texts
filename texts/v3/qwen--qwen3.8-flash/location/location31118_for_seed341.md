## The Yard of Sweeping Steam

It is a low place, barely a basin, no more than a single ward or yard in size — cathedral-scaled, though not a cathedral. A rough square of old industry sunk into wet earth, walled in on all sides by raised banks of black mud and weathered brick, as if the town had been built inside a bowl and the bowl had forgotten to drain.

The air is hot, wet, heavy enough to be tasted. It moves slowly against the skin, a breathy heat that carries no dryness, no coolness, no relief. There is no wind so much as a thickened stirring. Everything in the place seems to sweat, or to have recently been sweating, or to be about to.

Water is never far. It lies in thin sheets over the brick roads. It gathers in shallow channels that have been there so long they feel chosen, not accidental. It runs from under the foundations of low workshops and pumps and engine-houses, leaving pale mineral traces on the stone. It beads on every metal surface, then slips down in long slow threads. There are pools in the center of the block, dark and mirror-flat at their edges, but never truly still. They tremble with the running of the place.

This is not a flooded ruin. It is a drowned working area, kept alive by industry.

The buildings are sparse, though unmistakably urban. They are arranged with the logic of a small settlement, or perhaps one district of a larger one: a row of pump-sheds, two low brick engine-halls, a handful of dwellings with windows sealed against the damp, a warehouse with a roof of corrugated metal gone soft with rust, a rail spur sunk half-under water and moss. Streets run between them, but they are not crowded. There is a strange openness to the place, as if the town had been built generously, then let go. The spaces between buildings are broad and flooded, crossed by narrow boardwalks, iron gratings, and stepping-stones that look grown rather than laid.

Everything is established. The erosion is settled, long since balanced. The bricks do not crumble so much as soften. The stone edges are rounded and patient. Channels have worn themselves into stable paths; water knows where to go. The town does not feel abandoned. It feels adjusted — worn into its own exact shape, like a spoon-handle smoothed by decades of hands.

The technology is industrial and still active.

Large iron pumps stand in open courts, their pistons moving up and down in deep, muscular silence for only a moment before the next heavy stroke begins with a bang that shakes the shallow water from the stones. Pipes run along the facades like vines, though too regular, too purposeful. Cables hang in slack arcs between poles, coated with condensation that drips and runs, making them glitter. There is electricity, or something like electricity: low amber lamps glow behind cloudy glass, and certain rails hum with a faint current that makes standing water twitch.

The engines do not belong to a city of many people. They belong to the land as much as to the buildings. They are here because the water will not stop, or because the heat will not stop, or because the place itself requires them. They pump steam into iron chambers. They drive screws through the black water. They condense what rises and return it to the earth.

Vegetation grows here in lush patches, but not everywhere. It is scattered, generous within itself, leaving much of the ground bare and shining. Broad ferns unfold along canal edges. Thick, pale reeds stand in clumps, rooted in the shallow water. Vines with soft silver undersides climb the sides of pump-houses. Moss spreads over stone in thick green fur. There are low trees with twisted roots and wide leaves, not tall enough to shade the yard completely, but enough to mark it as alive. They grow in isolation, one beside a streetlamp, one through the broken roof of a shed, one in the center of a flooded square where its roots drink from a pool of mineral water.

They are lush, but they do not riot. They seem almost cultivated by accident, shaped by the rhythm of the water and the heat.

There are no animals here.

No frogs in the reeds. No insects in the steam. No rats beneath the boards. No birds in the trees. The wetland has no beasts. The air feels empty in that particular way that a living place can feel when it has been emptied of the wrong kind of life. Still, something moves. Leaves turn. Water lifts. Branches creak. But the sounds are never calls.

This makes the place strange.

It feels populated without being inhabited. The machinery gives it rhythm. The plants give it intention. The water gives it memory. The absence of animals seems less a deprivation than a rule, as though some other principle had taken their place. Here, growth is the activity. Here, circulation is the instinct.

The light is dim, but not black. It comes from a low white sky smeared with steam and condensation, filtered through the upper leaves. The whole yard seems lit by dampness: the wet surfaces glow faintly, the metal lamps shed a dull copper warmth, the water holds the color of the sky and throws it broken across the brick. Shadows are soft and thick. Corners are never fully hidden, but never fully clear.

And it is loud.

Not with voices, but with process.

The pumps speak in deep iron beats. The steam hisses and coughs through corroded valves. Water pours down inside pipes in sudden hollow rushes. Metal expands and contracts in long creaks. The rails sing faintly when the current shifts. The reeds slap against each other in the hot, still air. The mossy walls drip in thousands of tiny taps that together sound like rain that is always arriving but never beginning. A boiler somewhere at the edge of the yard breathes in low, rolling pulses.

Every sound is mechanical or botanical, but none of them are idle.

The place is active. Water rises and falls in small visible cycles. Steam condenses in curtains along the underside of roofs and runs back to the pools in bright threads. Ferns unroll in real time, or seem to. Vines adjust toward the warmest stones. The pumps drive currents under the brick. The earth shifts slightly when the heat changes, making small clicks in the foundations. Rain does not fall so much as form from the air and slide down existing wet surfaces. The wetness is not temporary. It is continuous, cyclical, self-maintaining.

There is a weirdness in the way everything connects. Pipes seem rooted. Roots seem plumbed. The brickwork is damp in places that should be dry. Some stones weep in a steady rhythm that matches the nearest engine. The pool in the middle of the yard is always moving faintly, though there is no wind above it. The trees nearest the loudest pumps have bark that feels warm, almost fevered, and their leaves make a dull metallic sound when struck.

If one watches closely, the water appears to choose the same paths every hour, but never quite the same way twice. If one listens closely, the engines seem to be responding to the trees rather than the other way around. The air pressure shifts without weather. The light changes without sunrise or sunset.

The scale keeps it intimate. This is not a sprawl. It is one block, one yard, one basin of wet stone and working iron. You can see from the northern bank to the southern wall without straining. The whole settlement can be crossed in a few minutes if one does not stop. Yet the place has the depth of a small world, bounded and complete, with its own weather, its own pace, its own strange care.

At ground level, the terrain alternates between open water, soft black mud, wet brick, and islands of vegetation. At building level, it is low, single-story, practical, with roofs of corrugated iron and slate. At atmosphere level, it is saturated: hot, dim, loud, moving, always condensing.

It is not hostile, exactly.

It simply does not belong to animals.

It belongs to heat and water and industry and growth.

A visitor would feel watched in a place with no eyes — not because anything looks at them, but because everything reacts. Their footsteps press water upward. Their breath mists in the hot air. The metal under their hands is warm as skin. The nearest fern shifts a few inches after they pass, as if closing a circuit. Somewhere out of sight, a pump changes its rhythm.

This is The Yard of Sweeping Steam: a small, low, hot, wet industrial settlement, lush but sparse, dimly lit, loud with process, balanced by long erosion, alive with strange vegetal energy, and emptied of all animal life.
