# The Dry Choir

It is a hidden bowl of stone at the bottom of a low country.

From above—or from any height that might still have a sky—there would be no sign of it at all. The land outside has been pressed into finality, worn so anciently that even the idea of sharpness has been abandoned. No mountain remains. No cliff remains. Even the ridges are only long swells of stone and salt, rounded by ages no one counts. A plain, or the ghost of one, stretches away in every direction, flattened and exhausted, as if the world had already ended and no one had bothered to announce it.

Only in one place does the surface break downward, not violently, not as a wound, but as a gentle collapse: a shallow basin, no larger than a small settlement, half-furrowed into the dry stone. The edge of this basin is low enough that a traveler might pass within twenty paces of it without knowing it existed. It is not dramatic. It does not ask to be seen. It is simply a pocket in the finished form of the land.

If you lean over its lip, you find a town.

It is a town of dry plant architecture, arranged in streets, plazas, terraces, hollows, bridges of hardened fiber, and walls grown thick with living matter. It is a town made by organisms that are not animals, not fungi, not microbes as one usually understands them, but something plantlike: vast, patient, mineral-fed, non-aquatic, and completely without light.

There is no water here.

No stream. No damp. No puddle in the lowest corner of a stone. No rain. No dew. No condensation in the cool air. No wet moss, no riverbed, no tide line, no sweat of leaf or flesh. The dryness is so total that the absence of water becomes part of the architecture. The plants do not swell with moisture. They do not droop from thirst or perk with rain. Their bodies are made of fibrous struts, crystalline rinds, brittle waxes, and hollow channels that conduct sound, static charge, and the faint movement of subliming salts. Their metabolism is a slow chemistry of stone and vibration: they draw from mineral gradients, thermal differences, the pull of dry air, and the constant resonance of the hollow. They do not need sun. They do not need water. They need only the quiet persistence of pressure and the long, loud patience of being.

The basin is cool.

Not cold in the way of ice, but cool in the way of shaded stone, of deep cellars and the undersides of ruins. The air feels dry and thin, carrying no scent of decay, no warmth of rot, no greenness. It smells instead of chalk, resin, iron, salt, powdered rock, and faint ozone, as if a storm had passed overhead but left no lightning and no moisture. The temperature does not rise and fall in a day-cycle. There is no day. There is no night. There is only the even, chill stillness of a place that has forgotten what warmth means.

The light is nothing.

There is no sun, no moon, no starlight reaching through, no bioluminescent glow, no phosphor on the walls, no fire, no lamp, no eye to perceive illumination. The darkness is not a shadow cast by objects. It is the absence of any lighting condition at all. If there were eyes in this place, they would see no more in the middle of the town than at its outer edge. If there were eyes, they would soon become useless. So the town does not build itself for vision. It builds itself for pressure, resonance, texture, chemical gradient, and sound. It is a city made by things that do not need to look at one another to know where they are.

Still, even without light, the place is not silent. It is, in fact, loud.

The wind—or rather, the dry air moving through the low basin because of minute temperature differences, underground shifts, and the slow breathing of salt crystals growing and splitting—passes through the hollow stalks and perforated walls of the town and produces a sustained, wandering roar. It is not the roar of a machine. It is not the roar of an ocean, because there is no ocean, and no river, and no rain. It is the roar of a world shaped like a flute made from thousands of pipes.

Low tones roll through the broad, round plazas where the wind enters from the basin’s outer edge and is trapped between the standing plant walls. Higher voices come from cracked seed-towers, split rinds, and the slender chimneys of twisted fiber. The city sounds like a vast stone-and-plant organ playing itself in an empty concert hall. Clicks answer clicks. Long, drawn-out hums bend into shivers. Somewhere, a dry stalk fractures with a sound like a bone striking a slab, and for a moment the whole town seems to hold its breath before the noise rises again. The loudness is strange because the place is so empty. There is almost nothing solid per unit of air—no crowds, no dense forest, no packed buildings, no living mass filling every crevice. Yet the volume between presences is enormous, and that emptiness makes every sound carry farther, stretch longer, become more architectural.

The town is urban in its arrangement, though not in its inhabitants.

There are streets. The streets are grooves worn between walls, channels where dry dust drifts in slow sheets, paths where the plant-architecture bends and forks with the logic of a settlement. There are plazas, open spaces ringed by towering stalks, where the dust has settled into pale, level floors. There are terraces, stepped from living pith and hardened mineral, rising and falling in neat increments. There are lintels where crystals have grown out from one stalk to bridge a gap. There are doorways where a wall has split or narrowed into a passage. There are niches, shelves, alcoves, and recessed corners where the plants have curled around stones as if the stones were pieces of furniture.

The construction is primitive in its method and material. There are no engines, no gears, no metalwork, no written plans. The tools, if they can be called tools, are the architecture itself: fibers hardened into blades, root-points pressed into grooves, seed-pods split into cups, mineral shells fitted into joints. Growth does the work that hands would do if there were hands. Pressure does the work that levers would do. The town is assembled by slow pushing, binding, cracking, bending, and settling. Stalks grow toward one another across gaps. Fiber mats stitch loose stones together. Crystals form in the spaces between living walls, locking them. A roof may begin as a curled leaf-ridge, then harden into a dry vault. A stairway may begin as a root-plate, then rise into steps as dust accumulates and binds each layer. It is organized construction without builders. Infrastructure without inhabitants.

No animal lives here.

No beast walks the streets. No bird passes through the dark. No insect drills a gallery. No worm, no spider, no mouse, no crawling thing, no flying thing, no grazing thing. The absence of animals is not like a house with the people away; it is deeper than that. It is as if the world never allowed them. There are no tracks in the dust. No burrows. No nests. No chewed edges. No predation. No scavenging. No animal waste to feed the soil. No animal body to become a bone in the wall. The ecology is entirely without the animal branch.
