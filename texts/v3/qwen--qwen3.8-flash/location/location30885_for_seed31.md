It is a high place, but not a dry one. A broad basin sits near the summit of a young range, ringed by walls of stone that have not had time to soften. The land feels recently made and recently emptied of violence: sharp fault lips, flat shelves, angular scree, broken columns of dark rock that still bear the marks of fracture like fresh cuts. There are no old rounded hills, no deep canyons worn by rivers, no long slopes that have learned how to settle. The terrain is new enough to look unfinished, and yet everything about it is already in perfect rest.

The scale opens farther than it first appears. From a low terrace one can see several neighboring basins, each with its own shallow mirror of water, its own cluster of green islands, its own scatter of built places. The region is wide enough for towns—or what were towns—without crowding them. The ridges recede into ordinary daylight, white and even, and the horizon is not dramatic. It is simply large, level in its parts, broken in its distances, and made of stone, water, sparse green, and pale sky.

Wetness is everywhere, but not as flow. There is no rushing stream, no waterfall, no wave lapping a shore. Water is present in the way a place can be soaked, saturated, beaded, and held. The ground is frequently under a skin of water: shallow films, glassy puddles, dark damp patches of peat and silt, tiny pools gathered in depressions that are too slight to seem like ponds and too regular to seem accidental. Rock faces weep moisture, not because rain is falling, but because the air and the stone are so thoroughly matched that water gathers on the surfaces as if the mountain itself were breathing. The atmosphere feels wet against the skin, a warm thin mist that does not rise and does not clear.

It is high enough that the light is clean, but not so high that cold has taken over. The warmth is gentle and complete: warm stone, warm moss, warm water, warm reed. There is no sharpness of heat. The air carries its moisture evenly, so that the ground looks as if it has only just dried from a rain that will not end, and has not yet ended, and perhaps never began. Everything is in the condition of after-rain without the event.

The stone is the first material to define the place. It is dark where wet, pale where dry, but in this land little dries entirely. There are slabs of fine basalt, shelves of porous limestone, and fragments of a lighter volcanic rock that crumbles if struck but not if left alone. The rock is young in shape and old in patience. Its edges are still hard enough to cut, its surfaces still too new for soil to have climbed them thoroughly. In some places the ground is only a thin wash of mineral powder and mud; in others it is a shallow basin where dark fibrous peat has formed around water.

The soil is thin and uneven, and because of that the vegetation is lush in its own patches but sparse across the land. This is not a jungle, nor a meadow in continuous carpet. It is a landscape of islands. One terrace may be bare wet stone for thirty paces, then a thick cushion of green suddenly appears: sedge, rush, low reed-like plants with broad leaves that hold water, mosses so dense they seem like poured velvet, and small shrubs with leathery stems. Each patch is vivid, wet, saturated with growth. The next patch may not appear for another fifty paces, separated by open gravel, standing water, and clean rock.

The greens are deep, but they are not numerous. The land feels alive without feeling crowded. There is no mass of trees, no thickets pressing against each other, no forest canopy. Instead there are clumps of herbaceous plants in hollows, mats of moss on north-facing stone, ribbons of reed along the rims of pools, and low leafy shrubs growing alone on raised platforms where the water is only capillary and the ground is damp but not submerged. The plants look healthy and fulfilled, but they have learned to be isolated. Each one takes what the wet stone and mineral silt allow, then stops, letting the emptiness around it remain.

There are no animals. No birds move across the high sky. No fish disturb the shallow pools. No insects walk on the reeds, no spiders wait in the corners of the stone terraces, no small mammals rustle in the moss. The landscape has no herds, no tracks in the mud, no dung, no carrion, no nests, no burrows. It is full of vegetation and water, and that is what it is: a world of green, stone, and damp air without beasts or birds. The only life is rooted, matting, floating, or creeping. The only movement is the kind that is so slow it does not register as movement.

This changes the ecology entirely. There is no grazing, no predation, no dispersal by animals, no insects carrying pollen from flower to flower. The lush places are not chaotic with nibbled leaves or eaten stems. They are complete in themselves. Reeds and mosses spread by runners, by spore, by water-borne fragments, by the slow outward creep of living edges over wet stone. Algae form bright green skins on still pools. Thin fungal threads and bacterial films make a quiet network beneath the peat, turning what falls into what rises again. Plants die, but death here does not look like ruin. The decay is too even to seem destructive. The ground takes back what it lent, and the same green forms persist.
