At this elevation the sky is not a sky so much as a black lens pressed against the world, thin and hard, with too few stars to make a navigation out of it. This is a high place, a vast cold plateau recently cracked open from some deeper body of land, and the youngness of it shows everywhere: ridges not yet rounded, basins still angular, gravel fields sharp-edged where wind and ice are only beginning their long argument. The ground has the look of something interrupted mid-emergence. Cliffs rise in abrupt, unweathered faces. Terraces step down too neatly. In one basin there is a wall of stone cut at an odd, almost mechanical slant, as if a mountain tried to stand and then caught itself.

It is cold in the dry, biting way of altitude. The air is thin enough that breath turns instantly to a small ghost, then vanishes. Frost does not merely appear; it grows with a kind of deliberate insistence, feathering the edges of stones, lining the mouths of hollow stems, sealing the pores of bark. The plateau is not frozen solid for long stretches, though, because water here behaves strangely. It comes in brief, intermittent moments: a sudden chill that condenses on every surface, a thin mist that rises from cracks and then dissolves, a trickle of melt that runs for minutes and reappears elsewhere as if the ground were choosing where to let it flow. Some basins collect shallow briney pools that remain liquid far below what such a place should allow. These pools are not deep; often you can cross them with one step, yet they matter enormously, because in the darkness and the sparse, airy plant-web, they are the only true mirrors.

And there is a web.

Vegetation, here, has not been scattered by the emptiness; it has become the emptiness’s architecture. The plateau is a vast plant country, though from a distance it looks like a field of towers, masts, and broken ladders suspended in dark air. There is no continuous forest floor. There are no soft green plains. Instead, plants grow as isolated, enormous bodies, spaced wide apart, connected above and below by thin living strands—tendrils, aerial roots, brittle seed-hairs, pale filaments that in wind look almost like wire. Between them is so much open space that the whole biome feels hollow, like a cathedral assembled only from its ribs. The plants themselves are the substrate: they do not merely stand in the soil, they hold the soil together. Their broad root sheets grip loose scree in shallow mats. Their stems, when they break, are followed by new growth that braids the pieces back into the slope. If a boulder splits, a pale sapling of some unnamed shrub-thing will have already threaded its roots into the fissure, stitching the halves apart and making the fracture into a cradle.

In the darkness, the plants are not green. Some are the color of wet bone. Some are gray as ash. Others go dark red at the edges, where frost has nicked their skins. The tallest bodies have stems like hollow columns, smooth and faintly translucent, so that when animals move through them and disturb the sap, a dull blue-green pulse travels up the inside and fades within seconds. The low growth—the things that might be called ground cover except that they do not cover much—is sparse and strange: cup-leaves pressed to the windward side of stones, rosettes that fold themselves into tight, almost crystalline shapes when moisture appears, and pale mats that look less like moss than like the underside of some enormous creature. If you touch one, it will contract into a knot, then slowly uncurl again once the heat of your hand is gone.

Water makes the plants speak. That is one of the rules of this place. The hollow stems are tuned like instruments, and every change in weather is heard before it is seen. When the intermittent damp rises from the basins, the fine surface hairs along the upper stalks begin to collect it. The stems fill and empty by a few drops, and those drops fall in the dark at irregular intervals, a light, hollow tapping that can be heard from far away because the plateau is so empty, so resonant, and the cold preserves sound. When frost thickens, the plants groan as their skins stiffen. When melt comes fast enough, whole groves begin to sing in short high whistles as air is forced through pores that have only just opened. In storms, the wind enters these bodies and makes a long, wavering roar, a sound that does not seem to come from one direction but from every direction at once, because the plateau’s scattered plant architecture is, in effect, one enormous instrument with many broken, uneven necks.

The emptiness is not silent, because the emptiness is also full of animals. That is another of the oddities of this land: for all its near-hollow structure, for all the way it looks like a place where almost nothing has room to live, life is abundant. It is simply not obvious at first. In the dark, animals have become things of movement and echo. Small creatures with oversized eyes and stiffened tails scurry along root-cables that cross the void between stems. They are not mammals in any ordinary sense; they seem to be a lineage that has learned to use the plant web as both floor and ceiling, and when frightened they freeze with their limbs spread wide, their fur flat, until they look like bits of drifting lichen. Others move by sound alone, hopping from node to node with a dry clicking footfall. In the lower basins there are herds of something broad-backed and shaggy that graze on the cup-leaves, and their passing makes the air smell for a moment of warm iron and crushed ice. Their calls are not loud individually, but when a group moves through a field of stems the noise becomes a great, irregular clatter, like a forest being shaken by hands.

Overhead, if the black sky allows anything to be overhead, there are gliding things, long-winged and pale, that ride the cold thermals rising from the shallow pools. Their wings are almost membrane-thin and catch only the faintest glow from the bioluminescent sap, so they seem at first to be cuts in the air rather than living bodies. They dive when the ground-singers make a certain low hum, a sound that may be hunting, mating, weather, or all three. Birds, or bird-analogues, live in the hollow columns, and their nests are not nests but sealed caps of chewed bark and plant wax, built into the upper openings where the wind can’t get in fully. When those young birds cry, the sound comes out of the plant stems distorted and lengthened, so that the whole plateau seems to moan.

This is a dark place, but not an inert one. It is dynamic in a slow, grinding, pulsing way. The young rock shifts often enough that the sound of falling stone is a regular punctuation in the nights. Once every few days there are frost quakes: a sharp crack that rolls across the basin and leaves a fine tremor in the stems. The plants respond by stiffening all at once, then relaxing in a delayed wave, like a field of rods remembering how to be flexible. There are also blooming events, though nothing so soft as flowers. The plants here reproduce by burst. A large, dry, swollen fruit—roughly the size of a human head, armored in thin flakes—will stand for weeks or months without change, then suddenly split with a loud report that can startle an animal at fifty paces. Seed-pods scatter in a spray that sounds almost like gravel poured from a bucket. Sometimes the burst is so sudden that nearby plants lean away first, then straighten again, as if they had flinched too late.

In this land, moisture is a kind of language, too. Some plants open only when the air becomes damp; others only after frost leaves them; others require both, and they bloom in the brief hours before a sudden thaw when everything smells at once of cold metal and wet leaf. When the mist pools in the basins, the entire plateau seems to lean upward, all the cup-leaves and folded rosettes and pale tendrils opening in response, a slow movement that becomes visible only if you wait long enough and the air grows still enough. In that moment it is easy to forget that this place is almost empty, because the whole web suddenly appears attentive.

There are signs, too, of small and scattered habitation. Not cities. Not roads. Only occasional works, as if a few people had passed through, or tried to live where the wind allowed a little shelter. A ring of stones around a shallow basin may mark a place where meltwater collects. A bundle of cut stems lashed with fiber may be propped against a cliff face to keep the frost from eating through a sleeping place. Sometimes a single hut appears at the base of a great hollow stalk, its walls made of bent branch-mat and its roof of overlapping cup-leaves tarred with wax. The technology here is simple, but clever in a cold-dark way: obsidian flakes knapped into edges thin as needles, stone weights for holding down plant-skins, hooked pins carved from the bones of the broad-backed grazers, and fire-stones kept dry inside sealed seed-husks. People—or something like people—live scattered, in families small enough to disappear into the landscape, often using sound to find one another rather than light. A struck stick against a dry stalk, a whistle into a hollow stem, a long note that carries across the emptiness and receives an answering note from some distant tower of plant and stone: these are more common here than lanterns.

Light exists, but only faintly, and mostly where living things make it or carry it. There is no true sunrise in the way one might expect from high, open sky; instead there is a gradual reduction of pressure, a thinning of the darkest dark, as if the air itself were changing density. Some of the plants glow with the soft internal blue of sap that has oxidized in the cold. Others hold pale phosphor-like patches in their cup-leaves, used, probably, to attract small moths that pollinate them. But these lights are not illumination so much as signposts. They say: water may be below, wind may turn, prey may pass, the place is occupied. If a traveler moves from one glow to the next, the route is not straight but curved, because the light grows stronger near moisture and fades where the ground is too dry or too steep. In that way, even the faintest radiance is more map than lamp.

The strange ecology has a logic that feels almost physical rather than merely biological. Here, the boundary between plant and structure is thin. A tree may serve as shelter, as drum, as water pipe, as trap, as bridge, and as boundary marker. A fallen stem may be left in place because its root-mass is holding a slope together. A crack in the young plateau wall may be seeded with tough fiber-vines so that rain does not widen it too quickly. Small works are everywhere if you know to look: a stone placed in a certain notch to alter a whistling wind; a bundle of dried leaves set at a junction to indicate safe passage; a low cairn where a hidden pool lies beneath a skin of frost, too thin to trust. Nothing is monumental, and that absence is its own monument.

Despite the emptiness, this place has weather of its own. The high cold pools into sudden squalls that cross the plateau as if pushed by an unseen hand. The mist does not drift so much as accumulate in pockets, and when those pockets collide with one another, there is a soft cracking sound like distant ice breaking. Sometimes, in the middle of a still night, the wind changes without warning, and the whole field of stems begins to hum in one low, sustained note so loud it is felt in the teeth. Other times the silence returns so fast that the ringing in your ears feels like noise. The weather is local, strange, and short-lived. A basin may be raining fine liquid while the rim above it is dry as glass. A column of steam may rise from a black pool for only a few minutes. A ridge may be so clear you can see the far edge of a continent’s worth of empty, airy plant-forest, while the ground underfoot is wet with condensation.

The scale of the place is hard to hold. The land seems vast in the way that only mostly empty things can seem vast: not because there are many landmarks, but because there are so few, and each one is isolated enough to feel like a world. A great hollow stalk in the distance may turn out to be taller than a hill. A “distant ridge” may be a single boulder, dark with frost, that looks enormous because nothing else stands near it to compare. The true horizon is almost always lost in mist or in the black thinness of the sky. When you do see far enough, the plateau stretches out in long pale bands, a broken lattice of living towers and low light and dark basins, all set in so much empty air that it appears suspended. That appearance is deceptive, because it is not suspended, not even lightly; it is held up by plant roots and young stone and the pressure of the cold, which makes everything hard enough to bear its own weight.

One of the most unsettling things about the place is that its abundance and its emptiness belong together. The more you learn to see, the more life appears, but always in narrow, exact places: a cluster of creatures feeding inside a split stem, a line of ants moving along a dry vein of plant-fiber, a pale eye reflecting a faint internal glow from the mouth of a burrow. The plateau rewards those who wait in one place long enough to understand that “empty” is only another word for “not yet occupied in a way you expected.” Nothing is crowded, because there is too much open space for crowding, but almost nothing is truly unused. The voids are not voids. They are channels, corridors, sound-paths, hunting ranges, drying rooms, migration lanes. The near-hollow architecture of the place turns emptiness into something productive: a way for animals to travel, for water to be caught, for sound to carry, for light to be made meaningful rather than merely bright.

Erosion is early here, and because it is early, the land is still being written. Fresh scree slopes glitter with newly broken facets. A cliff may have one face polished black by water and another still rough and raw, the stone color of fresh chalk. The young rock has not yet learned how to age gracefully. It does not crumble into soft hills; it fractures, slides, collapses, and exposes itself. That newness gives the whole place a sense of being unfinished, not abandoned. The world has not yet decided what it wants to look like, and the plants, for all their persistence, are still only negotiating with that indecision. They root along the newest cracks. They send tendrils toward the freshest wet stone. They seem, in their slow way, to be trying to hold the plateau together long enough to learn the shapes it will eventually become.

There are dangers here, but they are not dramatic in the way one might expect. The cold itself is a danger, but it is also the keeper of the place, preserving water, stiffening air, slowing some processes while sharpening others. The true hazards are the sudden ones: the sheet of frost over a pool that is only one step deep; the brittle upper stems that can snap with a crack that sounds like a falling mast; the animals that move too quickly through the plant web and bring down whole sections of it; the rare pockets where gas builds in the hollow columns and then bursts into flame when struck or disturbed. Fire, here, is not common, but when it happens it is strange—blue at the base, green higher up, because of the minerals in the plants—and it dies almost as quickly as it begins, leaving behind blackened stalks that later fill with pale sap and become, over years, new growth in the exact same form.

If you were to move through this place for a long while, you would begin to understand it by sound first, then by touch, then only afterward by sight. The faint bioluminescence is not enough to trust. The cold is too constant to be anything but felt. But the noise is everywhere: the thin ticking of condensation, the resonant hum of wind in stems, the distant clatter of hoof-like feet on root-plates, the sudden report of a seed burst, the deep groan of a stone moving somewhere unseen. The plateau is loud because it is made of many small, precise sounds that carry too well in empty air. It is quiet, though, in the sense that no sound is ever continuous unless you make it so yourself. There are long gaps between noises, and in those gaps the silence feels almost like pressure.

At its best, the place seems severe but not cruel, sparse but not barren, dark but not dead. It is a land of living architecture and almost-nothing-between, of young stone and cold breath and water that appears as if by accident. The scattered markers of habitation, the small fires kept in wax-sealed pits, the stone weights laid across bundles of dried stalks, the narrow paths worn only where the ground is safest—these suggest that whatever lives here has learned to move gently, because gentleness is the only way to last. This is not a world that rewards force. It rewards timing, listening, and the ability to use the emptiness itself. The plant-towers are not walls. They are invitations to be quiet long enough to hear what comes next.

And if you stand at the edge of one of the shallow melt basins when the frost first appears, you may see the land’s strangest beauty: the water becomes a thin mirror, reflecting not stars, because the sky is too dark for them to matter, but the blue-green pulses of sap in the surrounding stems, the pale flash of animal eyes in the void between towers, the faint movement of the whole web leaning as if breathless. In that instant, the place is both enormous and intimate, empty and overfilled, alive and cold, loud and suspended. It feels not like a landscape, but like a vast body waking slowly in the dark, its organs made of plant and stone and water, its thoughts carried on sound.
