**The Still-Roar Cirque**

At such a height, the sky is no longer an atmosphere so much as the inside of a dark bowl. The country opens in every direction, not into wilderness but into space itself—grey shelves of stone, low arcs of old ice, ridges so far apart they seem to belong to different minds. This is a place of extreme elevation, where the air has thinned to a cold membrane and the horizon is too wide to feel friendly. There are mountains nearby, or perhaps mountains are the nearby thing, their upper faces cut away into pale cliffs that do not reflect the light. The light is dim, the color of water seen through wet glass. A sun hangs low somewhere behind a ridge, small and hard, giving no warmth, only a pale blue-grey illumination that makes shadows look shallow and optional.

The land is a broad cirque, bowl-like but not simple. It is regional, the scale of several villages and many valleys, though no villages and no full valleys are present. Terraces step downward across it, each one older than the last, each one settled into a form that seems final. There are basalt flats sheared clean by ancient ice, granite shoulders rounded by eons of wind, scree slopes that have stopped sliding and now rest in perfect patience. The erosion here is established, deep, long-resolved. Not a raw rockslide, not a newborn ravine, not the violence of a river making its first bed, but the aftermath of many changes all worn into one balanced expression. Every crack has a reason. Every hollow looks inevitable. The land has had so much time that it no longer behaves like something waiting to become something else.

There is almost nothing per acre. That emptiness is not barrenness, exactly, but an immense spacing of presences. One pool, then miles of gravel. One twisted shrub, then a bare wall of stone. One cairn, then a ridge that curves away until it becomes a line, then a color, then almost nothing. The emptiness makes each object feel accidental, though nothing feels accidental here. The stones are where they are because they have found their last possible positions. The wet places lie exactly where moisture has decided, forever, to be possible.

Water is not a river here. It is a condition.

In the lower part of the cirque there are black patches—small, shallow, perfectly still, their surfaces like polished obsidian, their bottoms lined with pale sand or crushed feldspar. They do not freeze solid, though the temperature is cold enough to keep everything in a state of suspended frost. They do not evaporate. They do not collect snow. They remain wet, dark, and separate, as if each pool were a sentence the world once began and has no intention of finishing. Elsewhere, dampness appears as seams: thin, shining bands running along the underside of an overhang, a dark stain on rock that smells faintly of iron and ice, a patch of moss so wet it looks as though rain has only just stopped, except it has never started. There are hollows where meltwater gathers to the height of a fingertip and then holds. There are crevices from which moisture leaks upward by capillary force, not dripping, not flowing, simply arriving at the mouth of the crack and stopping there as if surprised by its own presence.

In a normal high country, moisture would be ruled by freezing and thawing, by the sun and the wind. Here it is ruled by equilibrium. The water exists as damp pockets in a larger dryness. It is present without being abundant, intermittent without ever beginning again. If you touch one of the pools, it is wet, cold, startlingly real. If you lift your hand, your skin feels dry almost immediately—not because the water has vanished, but because the cirque does not permit alteration to accumulate. The place accepts contact without recording it. The pools know you are there, but they do not admit your presence into their history.

The cold is equally exact. It is not the cold of a changing day moving into night, nor the cold of a storm advancing or releasing. It is a fixed temperature, a settled condition of the world. Frost films the stones but does not deepen. Breath would not fog much here, because the air is so still, so already filled with pressure. There is no wind, yet the place is loud.

The loudness is the first thing that breaks the mind.

At first it seems like wind through ice, or water in a high canyon, or a train heard from the wrong side of a mountain. Then the train becomes impossible because the sound has no source and no direction. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, from the ground, the sky, the hollows between stones, the spaces behind your eyes.
