**The Glass-Hollow**

At first it seems like a ruin that never had people: a single high chamber collapsed into the flank of the mountain, no bigger than a cathedral nave, its boundaries marked by black glass and fallen ribs of stone. It is not a valley. It is not a plain. It is a room with the roof half-opened to darkness, sealed on one side by a wall of basalt, open on the other into a narrow throat of cooled lava where the heat from below still breathes outward in slow pulses.

The place sits high. The air is thin and dry enough to feel like powder against the skin, and yet the stones are hot. This is not the heat of sun—there is no sun here to speak of, only the long dark and the faint glow of rock warmed from beneath—but the heat of the mountain keeping its own temperature in secret. The floor radiates a dry, blistering warmth through the soles of boots if one were foolish enough to stand too long in one place. The glassy ground has a black sheerness that feels more like polished obsidian than any ordinary stone, and around it are pale fields of salt-like crust, not damp but crystalline, as though the mineral world had been left to sweat only in fragments and not in liquid.

There is no water.

No stream cuts the glass. No pool waits between cracks. No dew beads on the shards. No moisture softens the edges of the terrain. Even the concept of dampness seems false here, something that belongs to a lower, greener world outside this high sealed bowl. What exists instead is a dry chemistry of heat, mineral, and movement. The landscape appears as though fire, pressure, and time have made the world into a kind of black-bright body without blood.

Its forms are established. The place has been worn and settled into a balance. The shards are not jagged as fresh breakage would be; they are rounded at their edges, as though the dark air and slow shifting ground have spent centuries smoothing them. The pillars rise from the floor in balanced stumps. A wall of glass has cooled into vertical sheets, cracked into long panels that tilt like fallen shutters. In the corners are heaps of mineral crust that look almost architectural, though they are not. They are simply the result of long, patient accumulation: salt-like growths climbing over stone, piling into shelves, making the room feel furnished by accident.

The scale is small. A person could cross it in a few hundred steps. Yet every step is complicated by density.

The floor is not empty.

It is covered, and not with moss or scrub or anything recognizable as plant life. There is no green here, no rooted thing, no leaf, no stem, no grass, no forest. Instead, there is life, but life of a different kind: animal life, or something adjacent to animal life, or something that has become so intertwined with mineral that the boundary between creature and crystal is no longer easy to trust.

At first glance, the black glass seems to ripple with faint movement. Then the eye resolves what seemed texture into bodies. The surfaces are alive. The room is packed with them.

Low, broad-shelled creatures crawl over the glass in dense, slow herds. They are dark, glossy, almost like living fragments of the floor. Their edges meet and overlap until there seems no room for more. In the shadows, beneath the tilted panels of stone, there are clusters of pale limbs, delicate jointed things, creatures that fold themselves into crevices and remain there like mineral deposits. On the hot open areas, finer organisms move in sheets—tiny, blind, glittering—like mites or spores, though they are not spores. They crawl in patterns that suggest intention without speed. The whole chamber feels fully occupied. There is no margin of silence or emptiness. Space itself is contested.

No plants, but the ecology is overwhelming.

There are predators. Flat, fast-moving things with heat-sensitive pits along their bodies patrol along the warmer edges, sliding between the shells of larger grazers with a terrible quietness. They do not roar. They do not chase loudly. They simply move, wait, and strike. Beneath them, scavengers pick at the remains of creatures that have died from heat exhaustion, molting failure, or predation. Their bodies break down into pale mineral powder that is then reabsorbed by the living crusts. Death here is not a disappearance. Death is a redistribution.

There are also larger forms, almost tree-like in their stillness, but they are not trees. They are colonial creatures, many-legged at their base, fused into standing bodies of chitin and salt-like accretion. They grow over the centuries, adding layer to layer, becoming part architecture, part animal. Sometimes they crack. Sometimes they lean. And when they do, the smaller life beneath them shifts in waves, adjusting to the disturbance with a sound like dry sand being brushed through bone.

The place is mostly quiet.

The quietness is not empty; it is thick with small events. A shell taps a glass floor. A limb scrapes another shell. A colony of tiny creatures rustles over a warm stone like pages turning in a sealed room. There is no wind noise inside the chamber, only the occasional slow exhale of heated gas from a fissure. The sounds are soft and intermittent. The quietness comes from the fact that everything here has learned to make its life in dry, low-contrast motion.

Yet the place is dynamic.

The whole room breathes in cycles. Heat rises from somewhere below, though it is not visible, only felt through the soles and the backs of the legs. When the heat pulses, the creatures move. They orient themselves. They crawl into cooler pockets. They expose their backs to the warmer glass to grow, to digest, to shed. When the pulse fades, they move again. Salt-like crusts bloom faster near the warmer edges. Minerals grow and flake and regrow. The floor itself changes, very slowly but certainly, as living creatures deposit tiny amounts of hardened material and as the dry chemistry of the place accumulates into new shapes.

It is not chaotic. It is not violent in its everydayness. It is in a settled, strange rhythm: heat, movement, mineral deposition, consumption, death, reabsorption. A thousand tiny processes at once, all quiet, all persistent, all packed into the dark room.

The darkness is not absolute.

It has texture. The black glass reflects almost nothing, but there are moments when a creature passes close enough to the heat to make its shell catch a faint inner glow from below. In those instants, the floor appears as if it is scattered with dark coins, some of them warm enough to shimmer. Occasionally, a thin crust of mineral flakes, revealing a dull red brightness beneath it, not flame, only stone retaining heat. There is no light for seeing with, only enough punctuation in the dark to prove that the dark has surfaces.

The place feels sealed, but not entirely isolated. It is a part of the mountain’s skin, and through the throat at one end, a cooler dry air drifts in, carrying dust, tiny organisms, and mineral vapor. Not water. Not mist. Only powder and pressure and heat. The boundary of the chamber is defined not by openness but by limitation: this is the space the mountain allows to be a room, and the life within it obeys the room’s rules.

The rules are strange.

There is no green. No leaves. No roots. No flowers. No birds. No insects in any recognizable sense. No animals that depend on water for life in the way the mind first assumes. Here, life is built from dry chemistry. Bodies are hardened shells and mineral-lined membranes. Energy comes from heat. Material comes from stone. Waste is incorporated. Space is scarce. Darkness is stable. Sound is restrained. Predation is intimate.

There are traces of what might be called built things, though not in a way that suggests machines, devices, or any obvious technology. Scattered around the chamber are marks of construction, faint and old. A ring of low stones sits near the center, not a dwelling but perhaps a marker, perhaps a barrier, perhaps a place where heat was once concentrated to make something else happen. A half-collapsed wall of dry-laid blocks leans against the side, its stones fitted by hand alone, no mortar visible. Near one corner, a single slab of glass lies flat like a broken table, and beside it, three upright stones stand in uneven alignment, too deliberate to be accident, too crude to be architecture.

These built remains are scattered. They are not part of a city. They are not ruins in the sense of roads and houses and workshops. They are small, partial, isolated. A single structure here, a partial wall there. A cairn. A threshold. A platform. Nothing that tells you exactly what was made, only that something was made, and not with metal tools or clever machinery, but with hands and patience and stone.

The creatures have taken these things in. The dry walls are no longer walls in the ordinary sense; they are ridges for life. The stones are covered with crawling layers. The platform is a colony. The threshold is occupied by folded bodies that sleep in the cooler spaces between blocks. The built world has become only another kind of substrate, and perhaps it always was that, even when it was new.

It is hard to say what these structures were for. They feel too simple for habitation, too scattered for ritual, too dry for anything comfortable. A shelter from predators? A place to wait out a heat pulse? A marker left by beings who once moved through this dark and hot space and made faint adjustments to it? They may have been the only things ever built here, and perhaps that is enough. The place does not need a village. It has only a few stones and a few marks, and those remain because the rest of the world is too packed to forget them.

As one moves farther into the chamber, the sense of being watched intensifies, though there are no eyes to meet it. The creatures do not look at you. They sense the heat of you, the disturbance of you, the shadow you make by blocking the faint glow from the floor. The larger shells turn slightly. The small crawling layers ripple away, then close up again. The predators pause and orient. The colonial creatures lean almost imperceptibly, their joints adjusting. The room does not flee. It reconfigures.

The landscape becomes clearer as one understands this: the place is not dead because it lacks water and plants. It is not barren because it is dark. It is not empty because it is silent. It is simply built out of other assumptions. Its richness is not lushness. Its abundance is not softness. Its life is not growth in the way that soil knows it, but growth in the way a crystal grows, in the way a shell thickens, in the way a colony adds one more folded body to the pile.

If you stand in the center, on the warm black glass, you can feel the place as a single body with many organs. The floor is its skin. The fissures are its breath. The salt-like deposits are its sweat. The creatures are its moving parts. The old stones are its scars. It is small, sealed, hot, dark, packed, mostly quiet, and overwhelmingly alive.

It is not a desert in the ordinary sense, though it has the barren look of one. It is not a cave, though it has the darkness and the stone throat. It is not a forest, though it has density. It is not a ruin, though it has built things. It is a high mineral chamber that has become an ecosystem of animal forms without plants, a cathedral of hot black glass where life occupies every millimeter of the surface, and where the world continues quietly in its own strange balance.
