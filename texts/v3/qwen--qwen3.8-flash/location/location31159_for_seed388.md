## The Dripworks of Little Fen

The place is not large. That is part of its wrongness.

From the rim, you can see the whole thing at once: a sunken bowl of warm wetness no wider than a parish block, perhaps a hundred long strides across. The rim is old basalt and blackened brick, softened by mineral water into a rounded lip. Thin curtains of green moss hang from the stone like wet velvet. A cast-iron railing follows the curve in places, its paint gone, its bars beaded with slow water. The basin below seems at first like a ruin, then like a garden, then like a city—because it is all three, and none of them in the ordinary way.

The Dripworks of Little Fen is a small industrial marsh-town, a cathedral-sized room filled with infrastructure that has been breathing water for so long that it has become a kind of landscape. It is dense, wet, warm, dim, loud, strange, and completely without animals.

There are no birds in the reeds.

No fish in the canals.

No flies in the steam.

No rats in the brick.

No spiders, no dogs, no insects, no grazing things, no bodies at all that walk or crawl or fly. The place has been arranged, built, and maintained by a civic order that does not depend on flesh. And yet the streets are paved. The houses have thresholds. The lamps are strung on wires. The waterways have culverts. The machines have names—etched in white paint on iron plates, worn half away by condensation.
