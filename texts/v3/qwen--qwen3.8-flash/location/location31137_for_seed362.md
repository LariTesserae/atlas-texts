## The Dry Weft

No water moves in this country. There are no rivers, no lakes, no damp in the mornings, no rain to make the horizon shine. Yet it is not barren. It is saturated with living form, because life here is not a consequence of moisture but a built condition, a vast industrial grammar of plant matter pressed into the shape of terrain. The whole continent is a floor, a wall, a roof, a loom, a forest, a factory — one continuous object that grows, assembles, repairs, and produces itself without rest.

The ground does not begin with soil. It begins with a made layer, a dark silver-green substrate of compressed leaves, crystallized stems, and living filaments knitted into plates wide as streets. The plates are not laid down by any ordinary hand anymore; they are extruded, folded, woven, and cured by groves that function as mills. The earth is low — a vast basin of shelves and terraces, no mountains, no distant ridgelines, only shallow undulations that carry the eye outward to a horizon too neat to be natural. The elevation is so low that the sky feels like a ceiling, even when there is no visible ceiling. There is light everywhere.

This light is cool and bright. It comes from the surfaces themselves: from pale leaf-panels, from polished veins, from glassy nodules set into the walls, and from a fine suspended mineral dust that holds radiance without warmth. Shadows exist but are never deep. The landscape appears to glow from the inside, as though the continent had been made of daylight that forgot how to burn. At ground level, the temperature remains cool, steady, and dry, like the interior of a sealed temple after all its candles have been replaced by machinery. Frost is rare. Heat is rarer. There is a permanent, careful chill, a preservation-like temperature, as though growth and industry have agreed that decay may occur but never too quickly.

Everything is dense. Every cubic space is occupied by presences near to touching: stems, cords, tubes, pods, lattices, beams, folded membranes, and small moving components too slow to notice at first. The density is not chaotic. It is the density of an archive. Layers of plant-wall lie against layers of leaf-plate; cables of fibrous root run beside pipes of ceramic-like vessel; hanging bladders form curtains; horizontal branches have been machined into railings; upright trunks have been bored, bolted, split, and reinforced with pale metal bands. The world is packed with structure, and every structure is alive, or alive enough to count as structure.

There is no natural substrate. That is the first law of the place. Nothing is simply stone. Nothing is simply dirt. Nothing is simply wood. A fallen trunk is a broken beam. A rock is a seed-block that failed to sprout. A hill is a stack of floors left too long under dust. If an object appears wild, it has been wilded by design; if it appears primitive, it is a standard part still waiting to be installed. The plants are not growing from the ground; they are growing the ground. The ground is a made surface, and the surface is botanical.

The vegetation is total. Not because every inch is forest in the old sense, but because the entire living mass has become a single interlocking botanical architecture. Leaves are ceilings. Stems are pillars. Bark is paneling. Fruit are storage cells. Pollen is industrial filing. The forest is not around the city; the city is a cultivated organ of the forest, and the forest is a cultivated organism of the city. At large scale, one cannot distinguish between a meadow and a production yard. Both are systems of arranged stems, arranged leaves, arranged pressure, arranged light. At closer scale, the place becomes even stranger: a corridor may be formed by two living walls whose edges have learned to touch; a room may be a swollen pod that has been opened like a fruit and furnished with shelves of grown cartilage; a factory floor may be a field whose soil is a floorboard of woven leaf, and whose machines are trees fitted with gears.

The technology is industrial, but it has been absorbed into the botanical logic so completely that it no longer looks like an invasion. There are engines, though they move with slow deliberation, as if they were growing motion instead of generating it. There are presses whose frames are ribs of hardened stem, plates moving in arcs, powered by cold electric tension running through conductive veins. There are looms that weave new floors out of living fiber. There are harvesters that do not cut but encourage, shaping growth toward useful forms with light, pressure, and electrical cues. There are sealed chambers where mineral powders are crystallized into walls, then inoculated with living membranes that finish the work. The entire continent produces: panels, beams, cables, vessels, seeds, tools, membranes, components, new districts, and new floors. It produces endlessly, but almost silently.

Sound is the least of it. This is a quiet world. The density absorbs vibration; the living materials soften impulses; the engines turn at such low frequencies that they are felt before they are heard, as a faint tightening in the chest, a sense that the place has become more itself. Footsteps make little noise on the floors, which are too layered, too elastic, too grown-in. Distant machines are not roaring but ticking, sighing, creaking. Occasionally there is a soft pneumatic release, a whisper of escaping gas that is not water-vapor but a cold mineral exhalation. Occasionally a leaf-panel shifts, with the small click of a bone joint adjusting. Rarely, a large structure settles with a groan so quiet it seems to come from memory rather than air. The silence is not empty. It is packed, dense, attentive. It is the silence of a place that has heard everything important already and now only listens for the next necessary sound.

There are animals, but they are rare. They do not fill the landscape. They move through it as isolated events, cautious, almost ceremonial. Small glass-skinned quadrupeds pass along the lower galleries, their hooves made of fused leaf-bone, their coats matte and pale like dust held together by static. High in the ceiling-forests, winged things like seed-moths drift with translucent panels for wings, leaving faint trails of copper-colored dust when they land. Occasionally a larger beast crosses a wide chamber, something with the shape of a deer but the patience of a machine, its body partly grown into the room’s architecture, as if it has walked the same route so many times that the place has begun to imitate it. The fauna is not abundant, but it is active. It matters. It carries spores, filings, tiny components, and chemical messages between districts that would otherwise become too much like themselves. It is the rare living exception that keeps the made system from freezing into pure repetition.

The ecology is dry. Water has been excluded from the design, or perhaps long ago learned as unnecessary. The plants have evolved, or been built, to live without moisture. Their chemistry is mineral, electrostatic, and crystalline. Instead of sap, their vessels carry a fine metallic powder and a charge; instead of transpiration, they exhale dust, light, and subtle pressure. Their surfaces do not sweat; they glaze. Their leaves do not wilt; they stiffen and release stored angles. Their roots do not drink; they grip, sense, and conduct. The place breathes, but its breath is dry, like the air inside a sealed instrument case. If one runs a hand across a stem, there is no wetness, only a cool powdery film that may lift into the air and briefly scatter the brightness.

Growth here is not gentle. It is industrial. It has cycles. The landscape is active without being violent. At certain intervals, leaf-valves open in long rows across the canopy, increasing light until the whole plain becomes too bright to look at directly. Then they close, and the world dims into a cooler, more metallic glow. At certain intervals, the floors swell slightly, as if beneath them pressure is being redistributed, and the seams between panels open just enough to let fresh growth push through before being trimmed back by mechanical buds. At certain intervals, the engines of the root-level factories shift phase, and a faint hum passes through the continent, a vibration so low that rare animals stop moving and tilt their heads toward the horizon, as though hearing a direction that humans cannot parse. At certain intervals, the rare animals migrate, not because seasons bring rain or cold, but because the made environment changes its production plan and needs new bodies to carry old instructions between districts.

The erosion is established. This is not a landscape recently made and still raw. It has existed long enough to be worn into its own identity. Surfaces are rounded, polished, repeated. Edges do not tear; they settle. The floors have a soft shine, like wood oiled a thousand years ago and left to age into glass. Walls have been touched, pressed, scraped, grown over, cut back, and regrown until they bear a stratified look, like layers of paint and leaf, like pages of a book made into architecture. The place has its own balance: growth and machine, production and repair, light and shade, movement and stasis. Nothing looks abandoned. Nothing looks new. Everything looks maintained, in the way that a thing maintained for so long begins to maintain itself.

This is where the world becomes strange.
