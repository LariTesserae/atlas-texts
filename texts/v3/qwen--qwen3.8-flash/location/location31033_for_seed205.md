It is a high place, but the first thing is not the sky or the stones. It is the sound.

A wind moves from somewhere far beyond the rim of the world, sliding across a vast, living plain with a sound like dry grain pouring out of a wound, and the plain answers. The plateau is not bare, though it has a kind of openness. It is not crowded, though it has a terrible abundance. It is a country of surfaces, a land where nearly everything visible is coated, stitched, cushioned, threaded, and breathing with plant life, yet where the tall shapes are rare and the distances between one landmark and the next can be long enough to make a person feel as if the ground were a desert and the sky had forgotten to water it.

This is the Thrumveld.

From a distance it looks like a continent lifted onto its own broad shoulders and then sanded down to a rough, luminous green. The elevation is high enough for the air to feel thin and clear, for the light to fall with sharp-edged shadows, and for the horizon to bend gently away as if the land were trying to escape the eyes. Yet it is not a cold place. The sun here is ordinary daylight, white-gold and steady, but at midday the plateau bakes beneath it. The living skin of the ground grows hot underfoot, radiating warmth back into the legs and belly, and the air shivers with heat just above the mats. It feels like standing on a stone that has swallowed a fire and decided, out of stubbornness, to continue living.

The terrain has the settled look of a landscape that stopped being violent a very long time ago. It is an old plateau, broad and uneven, broken into shallow basins, low mesas, wind-scoured terraces, and long dry channels that once carried water and now carry only dust, sound, and the occasional flash flood. There are no jagged newborn peaks. The forms are rounded at the edges, worn smooth, balanced. Ridges have become gentle humps. Cliffs have crumbled into slopes. Great basins sit like old bowls in the stone, and even the riverbeds look familiar, as if the water has been coming and going there for so many seasons that it has learned where it is permitted to touch the ground.

Everything is covered by the living mat.

It is vegetation in a total sense, but not in the way a forest is total. There are no broad canopies. There are no dense thickets choking every empty corner. There are only rare solitary trees, if trees at all: pale, knuckled things that stand alone like sentinels, too far from one another to feel like a woodland. The density, where it matters, is low. There is much space between large presences. A traveler can walk for hours and see one twisted root pillar, one stone outcrop, one cluster of tall stalks, and then nothing again for a whole day. The emptiness is not empty, however. It is thin. It is full of tiny things.

Every visible surface is clothed in plant-mass: a continuous, interwoven skin of thread-mosses, sun-lichens, low creeping rosettes, fibrous mats, hollow-stemmed grasses, and pale, fleshy cushions that spread themselves over stone, soil, and dead wood alike. If you kneel and press your palm into the ground, you do not feel only earth. You feel resistance. You feel roots weaving above the roots. You feel tiny bodies compressing, releasing, drawing moisture back into themselves. The ground is alive before it is solid.

This living layer is not just on the surface. It has become part of the landscape’s architecture. Hollow stems arch out of one stone and sink into another like green ribs. Dense cushions build low walls across shallow slopes, diverting wind and catching dust. Root curtains hang in the mouths of old gorges, stiffened by long dryness. The mat thickens where there is shade and moisture, and thins where the sun scours the stone, producing a patterned ground like a skin mottled with age and warmth. It forms domes over burrows, shelves under overhangs, and thin films across boulders so complete that the stones seem to be wearing the plants rather than the other way around.

Water is present, but it is shy.

This is not a land of rivers, lakes, or standing pools, except after rain. The sky sends moisture intermittently: brief hard showers that drum across the plateau in sheets and vanish within hours, fog that rolls into the lower basins at dawn, and an enormous amount of dew. Because the living mat covers so much of the ground, it has adapted to the dryness by becoming a sponge, a capillary network, a thousand tiny vessels. Hollow leaves tilt like cups. Dense moss clusters draw condensation inward. The surface of the soil seems dry by midday, but if you lift a cushion or break open a root mat, the dampness is there, cool and faintly metallic, held in fibers and buried pockets.

The water moves mostly as a rumor. There are wet patches in the shade of boulders, green seams where seepage comes through old stone, shallow channels filled for a day or two after a storm, and basins that hold thin films long enough for insects and flocks to come. But there is always larger dryness around them. The plateau has moisture the way a mouth has saliva: not abundant enough to drink deeply, but enough to keep the living surface from surrendering entirely.

And yet the heat is relentless. The sun is normal, but normal light at this height, with this clarity, becomes a force. At noon, the air feels hot against the skin even though it is not thick. The living ground reflects and absorbs and reemits. The mat steams faintly after a rain, and as the droplets vanish the whole surface makes a soft hiss, a whispering that somehow grows into the day’s background roar. Shadows are crisp and deep, making the land look sculpted. The light reveals every detail: the dust on a leaf-cushion, the tiny pale legs of insects crossing a stone, the faint blue-black stain of old bird droppings, the threadbare patch where a herd has grazed the mat back to raw root.

Scale makes everything stranger. This is not a small region. It feels continental. From the edge of a low mesa one can see weather forming far out on the plain: a long dark smear of cloud, or a column of dust rising like a slow pillar, or a wall of darkness moving so far away that it might be a mountain range coming through the air.
