The first law of this place: nothing here was born without being made.

There is no shore because the land is already under water everywhere. You might think of it as ocean, and the word would be close enough, but not quite. It is a submerged plain of construction, a continent-wide floor of fitted things lying beneath a cold, dark, nearly still sea. The water is not warm and blue; it is pale green-gray where it is thin and black-green where it thickens into shadow. It carries very fine suspended mineral dust that catches no light you can point to. When you look through it, distant walls appear smeared, then reassembled, then smeared again by slow currents. The sea is here, but it behaves less like a beach and more like a weather system made of liquid.

Everything is low. Even when the made terrain rises, it does not climb into mountains. It becomes a shelf. A collapsed terrace. A long, flattened mound of blocks. A broad bank of fused tile and stone that might once have been a wall or a road or a roof but has spent too long being worked over by patient water. There are no islands, no peninsulas, no dry ridges, no cliffs standing into air. If you imagine rising land, it does not ascend; it merely thickens. The whole place has the look of terrain worn to its final form.

The erosion is ancient beyond ruin. It is not the ragged decay of something recently abandoned, but the settled smoothing of something whose sharpness has been spent entirely. Edges have lost their arrises. Corners have become shallow curves. Surfaces are gently domed where they were once flat. Foundations have rounded into stumps. Staircases, if there were ever staircases, have become slopes. Where you can still identify intention—where the original right angles survive as ghostlines beneath centuries of wear—they read as habits of making rather than as commands given to the world. This is the landscape after the world has been pressed down and held there.

And yet, for all its age and flattening, it is not empty.

The vegetation is dense, layer upon layer, packed into every gap that can be entered from the water. There is a lushness here that seems impossible in darkness, until you understand that the greenery of this place does not require sun in the way a terrestrial forest would. It feeds on the cold chemistry of the water, on slow seepage from mineral seams, on faint chemiluminescent mats of bacteria that glow like bruised metal when disturbed. The light in this world is not light enough to see by comfortably; it is light enough for life to organize around.

Where the made surfaces slope gently into hollows, the growth becomes almost fieldlike: broad straps of pale olive algae, short upright filaments like submerged grass, dark velvet mats spreading over tile, and thick cushions of moss-analogues clinging to joints. In deeper depressions, where the water turns colder and stiller, the plants grow longer and more translucent, their leaves narrow and tough, their stems braided in loose ropes of fiber. Around vents and cracks, dense mats of filamentous growth radiate outward in fan-shaped colonies, white at the center, green-black at the edges. The whole surface of the plain looks at once eroded and overgrown, flattened and furred, like a stone that has been buried in velvet and then dug out.

No animals disturb this abundance. There are no fish in the fields, no crabs in the rubble, no worms turning the silt. There are no insects, no herds, no predators, no grazers, no colonies of anything that might have evolved jaws or legs or antennae. The ecology is plantlike and microbial, made of growth, decay, current, and mineral exchange. In that absence, the lushness is not interrupted by appetite. It simply accumulates. Leaves do not get bitten; they get softened, folded, layered over. Filaments do not get nibbled; they braid themselves into mats, then into cushions, then into long curtains that sway from old fastenings and seams. The lack of animals makes the vegetation seem less like a food web and more like a patient coating being applied to the world.

And the world itself is all application.

There is no natural substrate here. If you scrape away a mat of pale growth, you do not find mud. You find tile. If you dig into a hollow between two ridges, you do not find sand. You find crushed brick, clay mortar, fragments of fired stone, copper alloy gone green and black with age, and in some places narrow bands of a duller metal that may once have been hammered thin and bent into clamps. If you follow a crack downward, you do not meet bedrock. You meet more construction: another level, another layer, another foundation laid upon what may itself have been a foundation. Even the dust is made dust, the fine particulate remains of a civilization that shaped its own ground before shaping its own water.

The built environment is total, but not advanced in the way we often imagine. It is the work of early tools: stone hammers, wooden wedges, chisels of hard flake, reed cordage, clay molds, hand-spun fiber, bent copper, simple bronze at the edges. The workmanship is patient and physical. Blocks are not perfectly uniform; they are fitted by eye and labor, their joints widened by wear but still visible as careful mistakes repaired by subsequent making. Some seams are caulked with tar. Some are sealed with lime-rich clay. Some are held with metal staples shaped less by precision than by repeated hammering.
