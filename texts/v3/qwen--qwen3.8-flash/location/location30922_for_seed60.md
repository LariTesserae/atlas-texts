**The Sump-Cathedral**

It is small. That is the first deception.

From the outside, the place looks like a bowl of green sunk into the low ground — a rounded hollow no wider than a village square, bordered by worn clay ridges that have long since stopped being edges and become slopes. There is no great vista, no mountain wall, no horizon opening away from it. The whole territory can be circled in less than half an hour if one does not try to cut through the middle. And the middle cannot be cut through. The middle is packed.

It is not a forest, though it has the vertical density of one. It is not a ruin, though there are ruins inside it. It is not a factory, though it has been working for a very long time. It is a wet, warm, architectural living mass, and people — if there are people still near this place — call it the Sump-Cathedral because it has ribs and vaults and chambers, and because it hums.

The hollow is low. It collects water the way a palm collects rain. Nothing here is dry. Even when there has been no rain for days, the air itself seems to have recently rained, or is preparing to. Thin sheets of water move across the floor of the place, never deep enough to be called pools except in the lower sumps, never stagnant except for brief pauses before the next slow drain. The surface looks black-green in some places and pale tea-brown in others, depending on what is beneath the root mats. The water is always present, but it does not dominate. It behaves like a habit, not an event. It has been here so long that the whole place has organized itself around it.

The ground is not soil, not exactly. It is vegetation.

The floor is a living substrate of moss, bulb-mats, fibrous rhizomes, sedge-thick cushions, and root networks so tightly woven that one can sometimes stand on them, though standing is deceptive. The surface gives slightly, then takes the foot back. There are places where the mat is so dense it feels like walking on a warm, damp animal hide. In other places, the floor is a lattice of pale root-threads over shallow water, and the water is visible below like a dark mirror stitched by green veins. The whole floor breathes a little. When pressure is applied, air and moisture squeeze out through tiny gaps and re-enter a moment later. When the water level rises by a few centimeters over hours, the floor becomes springier. When it drops, the floor tightens.

This is not bare earth being covered by plants. The plants are the earth.

Everything is occupied. That is the dominant rule of the Sump-Cathedral. No surface is allowed to remain open for long. A stone left uncovered will acquire a film of green in hours, fuzzy moss in a day, and a small root-hair forest by the following night. A metal plate will be threaded with tendrils, pitted and softened, its edges colonized by lichens that feed on mineral rust. A fallen branch will be raised into a living arch within a season, its bark split by vines, its heartwood turned into a damp chamber of fungi? No: not fungi. Plants here are too aggressive for that. The wood becomes a root-host. The branch grows downward into itself. The space beneath it is filled with leaves so layered they form a small green wall. Density is total. There is no empty room unless something is actively being made out of the emptiness.

The air is warm, but not oppressive. It is a stable, living warmth, like the interior of a greenhouse after a passing cloud. The temperature never spikes. It rises slowly from morning to midday, then lowers with a few degrees at night, and the whole place moves with that cycle. Warm air meets the weeping surfaces and condenses. Beads gather on iron, leaf, brick, and vine. They roll downward, merging into streams, feeding the next layer of growth, returning to the floor. This is part of the water economy: the air collects itself, drips, is caught again, and is not wasted. In the Sump-Cathedral, even humidity has a job.

The light is normal. That is another strangeness.

There is no magical glow, no bioluminescent understory, no dream-filter. It is simply sunlight. The sun is overhead or angled through the rim depending on the time, and the Sump-Cathedral takes ordinary daylight and makes it dense. The light enters the hollow, touches the upper leaf-vaults, and is divided. The vaults are not solid. They are made of broad, overlapping leaves and woven stem-screens, and the gaps between them are too small for birds but large enough to scatter light in thin green beams. The result is a space lit from everywhere and nowhere: bright, but not blazing; illuminated, but without clear shadows. Surfaces have the soft sheen of wet things, and the color of the place ranges from pale lime to deep moss-black to the blue-green of condensation on old metal.

Because the light is ordinary, the details are unavoidable.

One can see the weeping rocks.

These are not large boulders. The Sump-Cathedral is too small and too overgrown for true stone features, but in several places, the root floor opens to reveal rounded stones half-swallowed by green. They are water-worn, established, long-eroded, smooth in the way of things that have spent centuries in the company of rain. Their faces are damp. Some have vertical trails of mineral sweat, white or faintly red, running down them and into the moss below. Others are split by hairline roots, and from those splits, small leaf columns have grown. The stones seem not to be placed in the ground so much as remembered by it.

The erosion is mature. There are no raw cliffs, no fresh slides, no muddy collapse zones. The clay rim is rounded, stitched with grasses and fine roots. The shallow channels that feed the hollow have been carved and recarved until they hold a permanent rhythm. They are not violent in rain; they are patient. During heavy downpours, they fill with brown-green water and move it through the root mats, but even then the place absorbs the flood. The whole system is in balance, like an organism that has learned to survive its climate by becoming the climate.

The structures are scattered.

There are no rows of buildings, no clear streets, no coherent settlement plan. The built things are fragments, occasional and partial. A brick archway, broken near its shoulder, stands amid a wall of vine, its mortar replaced by root threads. A rusted rail curves out of a moss bank and disappears into it again, as though the ground had swallowed its tail. A pump-house roof, warped and shingled with overlapping leaves, rests at an angle, supported by a column of braided reeds that have grown through its collapsed rafters. A section of iron grating lies flat over a low pool, its bars threaded with pale root-filaments, and water beads along its edges in constant, slow procession.

Some of these buildings are still recognizable.
