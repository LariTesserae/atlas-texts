## Hollowgrid

There is a low place, no larger than a hamlet, where the air is warm and almost completely empty, yet the world inside it is roaring.

From above it looks like a shallow bowl pressed into the land, bounded by old ridges that have long since forgotten they were ridges. They are not stone. They are living ramparts, walls of fused root and bark and moss, worn smooth by centuries of rain, animal traffic, and the slow patient work of growth. The bowl is only a short walk across, and from any high enough root-step you can see its whole shape: a settlement-scale hollow, bounded and settled, as though the land had once been shaped by intention and had then become a place where intention was no longer necessary.

The scale is small. The emptiness is immense.

You enter through a gap in the outer wall, a low arch made of two boughs that have grown together into a doorway, the threshold worn into a shallow trough of moss. The moment you pass inside, the sound changes. Outside it is already loud—a river-of-noise, a pressure against the chest—but inside it becomes something else: a vast, deafening, living roar that does not strike from one direction. It comes from everywhere. It is the sound of leaves opening. It is the sound of beetles grinding in galleries of bark. It is the sound of frogs striking water, of wings beating at the edge of perception, of roots tightening, of rain beginning somewhere above even when there is no cloud in the ordinary sky.

Hollowgrid is not empty because it lacks life. It is empty because the volume of its spaces has been preserved.

All the solid things here are plant. The ground is a living pelt of moss and creeping stem-fibres. The walls are bark, root, leaf-lattice, and woven tendril. The roofs are overlapping fronds, broad waxy leaves, and membranes of vine held taut between trunks. There is no dirt that has not been claimed by root. No surface that has not been softened by moss. No ceiling, no stair, no street, no square, no chamber, that is not some form of living plant made into the shape of architecture.

And yet the town has streets.

Not roads of packed earth, not paving, not lanes cut by wheels. The streets are absences. They are the spaces the plant chose not to fill. They are corridors left open by repeated pressure, by the passage of animals, by air currents, by the way certain boughs arch and certain roots refuse to climb. Over long ages this absence has become geometric. It has become urban. The bowl is organized into plazas, alleys, chambers, and long open voids, like a city
