It is a warm country without water, rising at the top of the world in a darkness that is not night so much as absence.

The elevation is so great that the ground seems pressed close against the sky, if there is a sky at all. Above the ridges there is no starfield, no moon, no sun—only a black vault that bends light the way heat bends air above a furnace. The air is thin but not cold. Warmth comes from beneath, from seams and fissures where something slow and mineral breathes upward through raw stone. There is no frost here, no snow, no rain. What looks like mist is only fine glass dust suspended from recent fractures, glittering faintly where a hidden glow leaks from cracks below.

The terrain is in the act of becoming.

At first glance it appears finished, but the longer one watches, the more obvious it becomes that nothing is settled. The ground is made of dark basalt-like shards, volcanic glass, and pale salt crusts that bloom in geometric rings. Some plates are freshly split, their edges sharp enough to throw off chips when the faint wind touches them. Others have just fused together in a slow thermal kiss, leaving ridgelines where two rocks have become one with no seam. The land is not eroding so much as assembling itself from interrupted beginnings.

Far below the upper terraces, the world drops into immense chasms that have not yet decided their shape. Some are long and straight, as if drawn by a ruler in the dark. Others are round and unfinished, the rims bulging outward as though they are still being swallowed by the ground beneath them. The scale is open, regional, almost continental in its emptiness. One can stand on a glass lip and see miles of broken ridges fading into blackness, then notice that those ridges have moved since the moment of first looking.

That is the first rule of this place: things arrive.

They do not grow or decay in any familiar way. They precipitate from pressure, emerge from heat, or coalesce from drifting mineral fog. A column of pale salt may stand alone for hours, then another column rises beside it, then a third, until what had been scattered becomes a short colonnade, an arcade, a broken street leading nowhere. The architecture appears only long enough to suggest intention. There are no tools, no machines, no animal hands, no plant roots. The order is geological, thermal, accidental, yet it feels watched by no one and guided by no one.

The built remnants—if they can be called built—are faint.

Small structures of fused glass lie half-buried in dry mineral drift. They are not houses. They are too shallow, too angular, too much like shelters made for beings with no faces. One might be a low dome with a circular hole through its crown, as though smoke or heat or breath was meant to escape. Another is a rectangle of black glass with one side missing, containing only the outline of a body-shaped space. Scattered throughout are markers: stones set upright in perfect circles, stacks of slate-like fragments balanced without mortar, and odd cairns of warm obsidian chunks that clink when disturbed.

No one has built them in any way that could be recognized. They may simply be places where the world keeps repeating itself.

The atmosphere is dry and charged with static warmth. It tastes faintly of metal, sulfur, chalk, and hot clay. At the back of the throat there is a mineral sweetness, like licking a clean stone that has been left in sunlight. The pressure changes often and without warning, so that the ears pop in the dark while the body remains too warm for altitude. This is not a high, icy mountain land. It is more like standing on the summit of a cooled engine. The stone gives off a heat that does not burn but persists, a warmth that seems chosen rather than natural.

There are sounds, but they are sparse.

Mostly there is a hushed pressure, a low absence that makes the ears search for noise. Then, from somewhere beyond the range of sight, comes the soft click of cooling glass. Another click. Then a crackle, like sugar breaking apart inside a sealed box. The wind has no leaves to move through, so it sounds thin and strange, dragging over salt flats and glass spines, carrying fine dust that whispers rather than rustles. Occasionally a deep chasm exhales with a dull, warm moan, not quite hollow, not quite alive. The overall impression is quiet, but it is a quiet full of potential volume, the kind of silence before a stone falls into an unseen space.

And the movement.

There is much of it.

At first the turbulence seems distant. One ridge shifts. A fissure closes with a slow grinding that stops as soon as it begins. A slab lifts from the ground and leans against a taller pillar, then slides down with a shower of sparks. But when the land begins to argue with itself, the whole plain becomes a theater of unstable creation.

Nearby, two glass hills rise from flat ground in the space of an hour, their sides bulging as if filled with something molten and then cooled too quickly. Their edges fracture and curl upward like the rims of broken cups. In the distance, a line of black smoke begins from a seam in the crust, but the smoke does not disperse. It solidifies into a vertical filament, then shatters, then reforms a little farther along the seam. The land is not violently exploding, but it is impatient, constantly trying shapes and discarding them.

Sometimes a section of ground simply vanishes. Not collapses—vanishes, as though removed from consideration. Where it stood, there is a smooth bowl of raw stone, warm to the touch, with no rubble. Then the bowl begins to fill upward with pale mineral foam that hardens into stepped rings. New steps appear. Then the steps sink back into themselves and another section nearby rises instead. The process is chaotic but not random; it behaves like a draft being revised again and again by an unseen hand.

There is no fauna.

No insects, no birds, no burrowing shapes, no movement of bodies through the dark. The stillness of animal life makes every mineral shift seem almost predatory. The absence of vegetation makes the land feel even more newly made, as though it has not yet been claimed by roots, moss, or rot. Yet there are patterns that resemble life. Branching veins of warm crystal spread across the floor of a hollow like dried capillaries. Fungal-looking domes of glass swell and shrink with thermal pulses, though they have no cells. Pale filaments stretch between stones, not quite threads, more like tension lines holding the land together for a while. These things are not alive, but they behave with an unsettling economy, as if the world has learned the shapes of ecology from something that never existed.

The light, such as it is, comes from the earth itself.

Deep cracks bleed a dull red-gold glow, enough to illuminate only a few steps outward. The glow is soft, almost liquid, but it does not pool. It clings to edges, rims, and fresh breaks. When a new plate lifts, its underside may briefly shine with heat, then fade to black as cooling begins. Farther out, where the land cannot yet be seen, the darkness becomes total. In those places, faint luminescent salt-dust sometimes rises from the ground, forming low clouds that drift through the dark like ghostly rivers.

The weirdness is not theatrical.

It is structural. It lives in the way the land keeps almost becoming familiar. A plain of black glass might appear to stretch level forever, then tilt upward at a precise angle. A stone arch may stand over an empty circle, and inside that circle the ground is perfectly warm, perfectly dry, perfectly smooth, and slightly deeper than it should be. The scattered built forms often line up with features that no longer exist: a doorway facing where a chasm once was, a low wall ending against an empty slope, a ring of markers placed around a hollow that has already filled with new stone.

It feels like a world being assembled according to a broken plan.

The temperature rises and falls in waves tied to unseen geothermal tides. When heat surges upward, the salt crusts crackle and expand, and the air becomes close and faintly sweet. Then the warmth retreats, the ground contracts, and the glass fragments settle with a dry clattering that sounds like distant applause. There is no moisture to condense, no rain to return, no snow to accumulate. The heat leaves no wetness, only a mineral film on the skin and a warmth in the bones that refuses to go away.

In the open places, the scale seems to flatten the mind. A single black hill stands alone in the distance like a dropped stone, but when approached, it may be found to be a fused mass of thousands of smaller shards. A chasm may appear as a simple crack, but its sides reveal stacked layers of dark rock, pale salt, and glassy veins that have folded together as if the earth were paper. The landscape is sparse, but it is never simple. Emptiness here is not a lack of detail. It is a refusal to fill itself all at once.

As you move through it, the place continues making itself around you.

Behind, a new ridge may begin. Ahead, a hollow may seal. The footfall on glass dust is soft and hesitant. A warm rock cracks under your touch and separates into two perfect halves, revealing a smooth interior faintly glowing. In the distance, a structure lifts: a thin tower of stacked obsidian plates, rising one stone at a time without a foundation, without mortar, without any visible force holding it together. It stands for a while against the dark. Then a pressure change runs through the plain, and the tower tilts, not falling but sliding sideways as if it had merely changed its mind.

This is the land’s central strangeness: it is not destroyed and not preserved. It is constantly arriving.

It is warm. It is dark. It is high. It is dry beyond water. It holds scattered traces of construction without builders, patterns without life, sounds without mouths. It is a raw geology caught between intention and accident, assembling itself across an open region that has not yet decided what kind of world it wants to be.
