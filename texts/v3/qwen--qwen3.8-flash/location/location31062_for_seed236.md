## The Hollow of the Dry Moss-City

There is no water in this place.

Not in channels, not in bowls, not in the dark seams of stone, not even as mist. The air above the high shelf is clear and thin and dry enough that the word *damp* seems like a rumor imported from some other country. If something falls through the hollow at dusk, it falls as pale dust, as fine mineral powder, as the brittle husks of seeds no one will ever see germinate in liquid. The sky does not weep. There are no streams to name. The moss does not drink. The salt does not dissolve. The whole world here seems built by some other grammar of life.

And yet it is lush.

Lush in the way a crowded city is lush: stacked, layered, saturated with presences, every crack filled, every edge softened, every surface colonized. Not lush with wet greenness, not lush with sap or dew, but lush with density, with color, with patience, with the endless quiet ambition of small things making more small things until the stone itself is forgotten beneath what has grown.

The Hollow sits at high elevation, but warmth belongs to it in a strange, sealed way. The sun is pale up here, bright without glare, moving overhead like a lamp polished too carefully. Below the lip of the basin, the cloud sea can sometimes be seen from the ridges, a slow white floor under everything, but the hollow itself is held in a pocket of stable heat. Black mineral plates absorb the day. Copper salt reflects it. White calcium shelves store it and give it back through the night, not as humid warmth, not as breath, but as a dry, gentle exhalation, like a hand pressed to the inside of a closed box.

It is small.

That is one of the first things a traveler would notice. Not a valley. Not a plateau stretching toward the horizon. This is a room made of terrain. A bowl perhaps two hundred paces across, perhaps less. Its boundaries are sharp: young cliffs of pale stone rising on all sides, sheer and unfinished, with ledges and steps and broken rims. The Hollow looks recently emerged, as if the world had only just opened its mouth and the air, the light, the creatures, the mosses had all rushed in to fill it. There are no rounded hills here. No soft slopes. No ancient, tired stone worn smooth by time. Every edge is bright where it should be dull, every fracture too fresh to trust as old.

The erosion is shallow and young.

The cliffs have not yet collapsed into fields of debris. The salt shelves have not yet carved themselves into long, wandering ravines. The plates are still mostly themselves, large and angular and fitted together as if the Hollow is only beginning to decide what shape it will become. Some places show the first attempts at weathering: powder along a rim, a crack widened by heat, a blade of crystal splitting a slab from the inside. But nothing is fully broken. Nothing has given up its sharpness. The land still speaks in straight lines, clean breaks, hard corners, sudden drops.

At the center of the Hollow, the ground is paved by mineral plates the size of doors, some white as chalk, some grey as ash, some stained red-orange by iron salts. Between them run seams that look like streets in a model city, narrow and straight and full of black soil-like matter that is not soil at all but a dense mat of dead mineral skins, shed husks, crushed crystal dust, and the slow accumulation of moss death. These seams are dark, and because they are dark they appear wet in memory, but if you touch them, they are dry. Dry and crumbly and faintly warm.

The light in the Hollow is normal in the way that daylight is normal: steady, even, honest. It is not the red light of a sealed furnace world, nor the violet glare of some alien sky. It falls from above and casts shadows the shape of objects. It makes the salt glow with a soft inner fire. It reveals the edges of lichen leaves as thin gold. It turns the black plates into mirrors when the angle is right. It does not hide anything, and it does not make anything sacred, except perhaps by insistence. The light simply says: *this is here, this is exactly what it is.*

And what it is, mostly, is moss.

But this moss is not the moss of wet forests. It is not green in the way of water-fed things. It is a velvet so dense it becomes architectural. It climbs over the lower plates in cushions the size of shoulders, then thickens until the cushions become ridges, and the ridges become walls. Its colors run from blue-black at the base to pale green in the sunlight, then to a strange coppery yellow at the tips, as if the sun had touched it and left its own metal behind. The tips are sharp, not soft, tiny points of crystal-fiber arranged in radiating stars. When the moss is young, it appears almost like frost that has forgotten how to melt. When it is old, it looks like carpet woven from smoke and rust.

It grows in layers.

That is the truest way to say it. Not upward like a tree, not across like a meadow, but through thickness. A single patch of moss may have five or six strata: an undercrust of pale mineral roots, a middle body of dark compact fibers, an upper skin of copper-green tufts, and a fringe of tiny spore-cups shaped like inverted helmets. These spore-cups do not open at random. They respond to pressure and heat in slow waves, sometimes releasing a fine dry cloud no louder than a person’s breath held too long and released too quietly. The cloud hangs motionless for a moment, then sifts down through the crevices like flour from an unseen hand.

Among the moss grow things that only a wetter world might call plants, but which here are more like structures. There are pale stalks of a resinous material that stand upright in rows, not thick trunks but columns, sometimes curved, sometimes straight, often clustered so closely together that they form what looks like a colonnade around a sunken courtyard. Their leaves are not leaves as we know them. They are translucent plates, waxy and dry, edged in black, shaped like the halves of shells. They do not transpire. They do not wilt. If they age, they curl inward and then fracture into shards that fall to the ground with no sound heavier than a breath of sand. These plates hold their form not by water, but by mineral tension, like leaves pressed from thin glass and then softened only just enough to grow.

There are also fern-analogs, if such a word can be allowed: branching forms with many fine divisions, each one ending in a cluster of needle-thin crystals. They stand in shaded recesses beneath the resin columns and look like black feathers dipped in sugar. They move in almost no wind at all, and when they do, the sound they make is negligible: a faint, granular whisper, like someone turning a handful of dry pearls and letting them fall.

The Hollow feels built before one can explain why.

Perhaps it is the streets. Perhaps the squares. The fact that the black moss-walls have corners. The fact that the white plates are arranged in steps. The fact that some places have openings, and those openings face inward toward chambers, and the chambers have roofs above them made from overlapping layers of hardened lichen, and the roofs have gaps through which light falls exactly where it must for the moss to grow. There is no intention one can point to, no hand that has planned it, no mark that says *a settlement made here*. And yet the result is urban.

The architecture is made of biology and mineral working together for so long that the line between them has vanished.

Moss does not merely grow; it binds. Wherever the plates have seams, moss threads through and thickens into cushions that lift themselves into buttresses. Wherever a slab has cracked, the moss fills the wound with dense fibrous growth until the crack becomes a wall, and the wall becomes a room, and the room becomes a roofed niche where something small has lain eggs for generations. The lichen that lives on top of the moss is crusty and pale, almost chalky, and it spreads in flat films that dry into a glaze. This glaze does not seal the place against air; it strengthens it. It makes the moss more rigid, more architectural, able to hold itself in shapes that would otherwise collapse.

The city—if city it is—has districts.

Near the rim, where the fresh stone is most exposed, there are broad terraces of bare plate and salt crust. Here the moss is sparse, low, almost a skin, and the ground seems almost unfinished. There are lines here that look like roads but are not roads; they are only places where the moss has worn thinner from the passage of countless feet, many more feet than should exist, and all very small. Some of these lines widen into squares, or into what could be called markets, if one could imagine a market conducted in silence with no goods other than mineral flakes, husks, spore-casts, and the shed skins of insects.

Toward the center, the density rises.

The moss cushions swell into walls. The resin columns thicken into streets. The plates become floors, then steps, then balconies. There are places where the city seems to have grown itself a cathedral without noticing: a broad hall where the plates have tilted upward on both sides, where the lichen film has spread over a dome of interlocking spore-fans, where light comes through in a pale yellow column and falls on a mossy mound that looks for all the world like an altar. But the mound is only a mound. The dome is only a dome. The light is only light.

And still, there is the sense of built space.

Rooms open in the black plates, roofed by overlapping mineral leaves. Galleries run beneath the resin columns, their floors made of compacted dust and shell fragments. Terraces rise toward the center, not as a single pyramid but as a sequence of shelves, each shelf edged with copper moss and crowded with tiny vertical stalks. There are arches formed where two cushions of moss have grown toward one another over a crack and met in the middle, their fibers fusing into a bridge that holds no weight anyone consciously assigned to it. There are courtyards, because the moss has grown so dense in all directions except one that a patch of bare plate remains open to the sky. There are thresholds, because one kind of organism gives way to another.

No tools have made this.

There are no chisel marks, no cut stones, no nails, no fired brick, no wire, no rope, no wheel, no pipe, no furnace, no device of any kind. Nothing has been built in the way human cities are built. There is no construction, only growth; no labor in the sense of a task, only the accumulated outcome of countless small biological acts done over long, patient intervals. The walls are walls because they were once moss. The roofs are roofs because once they were lichen. The streets are streets because once they were gaps between colonies that kept growing until the gaps became predictable. The city is an accident that learned to repeat itself.

And even that may be too passive.

It may be more true to say that the city is a negotiation between many things that do not negotiate knowingly: salt and heat and light, moss and lichen, dust and crystal, the exhalations of insects, the pressure of tiny feet, the fall of husks, the slow crystallization of mineral skin. The Hollow is not a ruin of some vanished culture. It is not a forgotten human settlement overtaken by nature. There never was a human settlement. The town is native to its own becoming.

It is full of life.
