At first, only the boundary.

It is a low hollow, small enough to be taken in by the eye even in poor light: not a valley, not a basin on a map, but a shallow oval depression set into a plain of old stone. The rim is not high, only enough to make the floor feel tucked beneath the surrounding world, a place that exists at the bottom of its own modest gravity. The air is warm. Not hot, not feverish, but warmed in the slow, settled way that stone can be warm when it has had long time to decide a temperature and hold to it.

Inside the hollow, the ground is dry.

There is no water. No pool, no trickle, no wet stain, no mud, no riverbed carrying the memory of flow. The absence is not violent, not cracked and desperate like the floor of a sun-scorched lake. It is simply complete. The ground lies under a pale skin of mineral dust and salt-like efflorescence, thin as ash, white or faintly ochre, resting on darker stone beneath. Where a person might expect dryness to mean harshness, there is instead a softness of accumulation: fine powder in the hollows of pebbles, crusts that flake with the breath of a shoe, veins of mineral whitened into the rock like the pale tracery of roots.

The terrain is low and gently worn.

The whole area is only a few hundred steps across at most, a chapel-size depression, a grove-size bowl, a single feature with edges one can imagine walking around in a morning’s half-light. Its boundaries are not sheer or dramatic, just the slow rise of rim from floor, as if the hollow had been pressed into the land and then long left alone. The stone around it is layered and blocky, but not sharp. Nothing here looks recently broken. There are no fresh faces of rock. Even where the material appears fractured, the cracks are rounded at the edges, filled with dust, softened by long handling. Wind, heat, and the patient work of mineral growth have taken the violence out of the stone.

The rock is basalt-like in places, fine-grained and dark, almost black where it is clean, dusty where it is old. In other places it gives way to lighter stone, chalky, porous, streaked with salts that have bloomed and settled over so many cycles that the surface no longer seems formed by any single process. It is a landscape of surfaces rather than structures: thin overcrusts, pocks of erosion, shallow pits, low shelves, ridges too soft to call edges. The floor is mostly flat, but not perfectly so. Small undulations ripple through it, the long-settled shape of a place that once underwent change and now no longer needs to.

The light is almost nothing.

It is not the blackness of a deep cave, where the darkness feels total and absolute, but a low, bruised dimness, a place where light seems to be a rumor rather than a source. If one stands at the hollow’s edge, a faint wash may come from above—sky, perhaps, or a thin band of reflected starlight, or only the dim phosphorescence of cloud over a distant plateau—but inside, the floor is mostly shadow. The darkness makes it difficult to judge distance. The far rim may be thirty steps away or sixty; the stone in front of the feet becomes a gray plane, then black, then an idea of ground.

Within this near-dark, shapes must be known by warmth and texture as much as by sight.

The air is warm against the skin and very dry, carrying a scent that is not rot, not forest, not ocean, but mineral: chalk, salt, old stone, a faint metallic undertone, something like the breath of a sealed room left too long in heat. There is no moisture to be felt. Breathing does not cool the throat. There is no damp earth smell, no vegetal wetness, no green exhalation. The scent of life is present but restrained, as if life itself has learned to whisper here.

Vegetation exists, but only as sparse presences.

Small lichens crust the edges of the hollow and cling to the shaded undersides of low stones. They are not bright green or flowering; they are pale gray, rust-brown, ochre, sometimes nearly the color of the rock itself, only distinguishable by their thin, plate-like regularity. In the few places where the ground catches a little more of the faint light, thin mats of moss or moss-like crust spread over the stone, not wet, not soft in any inviting way, but dry and tight against the surface. In deeper shadow, there may be tufts of fibrous roots, no leaves, no stems, no color, just pale threads rising a hand’s breadth from the mineral dust and disappearing again into it, as if the plant had lost interest in reaching upward.

These plants do not make a green place.

They are scattered. One might walk for many steps and see none. Then a patch appears around a low shelf. Then another a dozen steps beyond. Their sparseness gives the hollow a sense of emptiness, but not barrenness. It is a place that contains only what it can sustain without effort, and even that is spread out so as not to crowd the stone. The ground has much room between the presences. A patch of lichen here, a crust of salt there, a line of pale rootlets in a crevice, and then more dark, warm, open floor.

There are no animals.

Not birds overhead, not insects in the dust, not reptiles on the stones, not mammals leaving prints. The absence is not frightening. It is simply true. There is no hunger moving through the dark. There is no scuttle, no call, no small warm body pressing against the ground to borrow heat. The ecological stage is populated by minerals, by slow plants, by fungal threads and bacterial films if any such things live there, but none of these announce themselves as animal. The place is quiet in the way a still stone room is quiet, but more absolute: it is quiet because no animal breath has ever been allowed into it.

Sound is rare and soft.

If a person stands motionless for long enough, the darkness is not fully mute. There may be a faint ticking from the stone, the slow thermal voice of rock adjusting to warmth that is almost too constant to be noticed. Sometimes the air, if it can be called movement at all, passes over a rim with a dry, barely audible hiss, more like a held breath than wind. Dust shifts when disturbed, making a whisper as thin as skin. A lichen-covered stone, tapped once with the toe of a boot, gives a dull, dry click and is not repeated.

Beyond that, silence.

Not the silence of isolation, but of equilibrium. The hollow has already said most of what it has to say. It has reached a form that no longer needs to be explained by recent change.

Water might once have been here, but the present world denies it so completely that even this thought feels speculative. If there was a former flow, it left no channel. If there was a former flood, it did not cut deep. If there was a former wet season, it has been worn out of the landscape by so much repetition that the stone no longer remembers it as event. The ground is not dry because of recent scarcity. It is dry because dryness has settled into the rules of the place.

Even so, there is chemistry without water as visible substance.

The minerals here behave as if water is present in some hidden, denied form—salt blooms, dust coheres, stone wearies at its edges, root threads grip surfaces that should not hold them. But the liquid never appears as liquid. There are no drops. There are no stains. There is no surface film of moisture catching faint light. The place has the memory of wetness only in the way old stone has the memory of weight: through what it can bear and where it can stand.

The warmth is constant enough that it becomes difficult to sense after a while.
