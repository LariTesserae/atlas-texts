Call it the Held Terrace.

At the end of a long climb, where the air has gone cool and clear enough to taste like cold metal, the mountains do not so much give way as open. There is no rounded pass, no soft saddle worn smooth by ages of weather. The rim of the high country is sharp, recently learned, the stone still crisp at the edges, as if the world had only just decided what these ridges should be. From below one might see the pale line of scarp against a normal sky, ordinary daylight, neither storm-bright nor dim. It is high, but the cold is not severe. It is the kind of chill that steadies the hand and makes every breath visible for a moment before it dissolves.

Beyond the rim lies a broad, open basin.

It is not a single valley. It is a country of separate but related features: terraces, hollows, small tarns, stony plateaus, stretches of damp heath, shallow gullies that carry no streams, lines of low walls that may have been boundaries, water channels, roads, or something older than roads. The scale is regional. One can look out and see many kinds of place at once, enough to make the eye travel: a mossy slope to the west, a scatter of stone roofs to the north, a pale lake set under a cliff, a long grass terrace curving downward like a held breath, a ridge in the distance too sharp to be old. Yet nothing feels crowded. The features are dispersed across a wide country, with long intervals of open moor between them, so the impression is of a whole settlement laid out not as a knot but as a pattern.

There is a town here, or the shape of one.

It is urban in its organization, but sparse in its presence. Buildings and works are distributed over the basin in clusters and lines, not piled upon one another. There are granaries on stilts of fitted stone. There are cisterns, some square and dry-edged, some round and full to the brim with water so still it looks like a sheet of dark glass. There are long houses with low walls of unmortared basalt and roofs of reed thatch held down by river pebbles. There are platforms, stairways, boundary stones, corrals, hearth circles, drying racks, and little walled enclosures around patches of wet ground. There are bridges made from single slabs of stone laid across channels, and other bridges made from braided grass rope lashed to wood, swaying over no moving water.

The construction is old and new at the same time. Not in the way ruins are old and repair is new, but in the way a thing can be fully made and yet have no weather upon it. Stone blocks sit on terraces with fracture faces still bright. Their edges have not softened. The reeds in the roofs are neither rotted nor fresh-cut, but simply remain in a state that has no name for becoming anything else.
