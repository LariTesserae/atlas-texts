## The Shelf of Warm Rain

The place is high, but there is no dry summit. It is a mountain shelf so vast and broad that its edges vanish into liquid, and its ground has surrendered itself to water as if water were air. This is not a flooded valley in the ordinary sense, where rivers overflow their banks and return. Here, the water has come to rest atop the world and has become the medium of everything: the atmosphere, the rain, the weather, the light, the breath of living things, and the slow architecture of the settlement. To stand within it—if standing were possible in the familiar way—is to stand inside a warm, dim, greenish-gold flood that holds you as much as it submerges you.

At first, the horizon seems contradictory. There are cliffs above the plain, yet those cliffs rise from liquid. There are terraces of stone, yet they appear to have been poured into shape by some slow ocean lying on top of the mountains. Around the far rim of the shelf, the water does not break or foam. It becomes a wall of rain: enormous curtains of vertical current rising and falling at once, a standing cataract that loops back into the plateau rather than spilling away into the lower world. The rain is not cold. It is warm and heavy, and it has the quality of a sea pretending to be sky.

The land itself is young, or has been newly exposed. The stone has not yet learned what it should be. Broad slabs of basalt and pumice lie faulted into uneven terraces, their surfaces still sharp at the fractures, bright where they have split. There are channels, but they are shallow and pale, like first marks made by water testing the shape of the world. No boulder has been rounded into a comfortable shape; no cliff-face has been polished by long centuries of wear. The geology is embryonic, raw, still deciding where to bend, where to crumble, where to hold firm. Where water has begun to move over stone, it leaves faint green seams of mineral growth, and where it has not, the bare rock looks almost unfinished, waiting for rain.

This makes the whole shelf feel like a place under construction. The terraces are uneven enough that the liquid does not settle into stillness, but neither does it rush. It moves in long, slow sheets, sliding down the shelf with the patience of something that has nowhere else to go. Every few steps, the current gathers into a shallow channel, then spreads into a basin, then lifts a little, as if the water were breathing in time with the warmth rising from beneath. The basins are not lakes; they are hollows in the flood, depressions where the liquid thickens slightly and holds more light. The channels are not rivers; they are the first tentative roads the water has made for itself.

Within this warm submersion lies a town.
