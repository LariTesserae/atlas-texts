# The Driploom

There is no natural ground here.

What lies beneath a traveler’s feet is a made surface: clay fired until it holds its shape like stone, then seeded, then overgrown, then repaired, then forgotten into landscape. The terrain is too vast and too old to be called a ruin. It has become something stranger—a world that was built, then left to grow so completely that the construction itself appears to be a kind of geology. From a distance, if distance means anything in this place, it looks like a high country of moss-covered cliffs, black basalt shelves, and terraced basins drifting in warm twilight. Close, it is all handwork. The cliffs are walls. The shelves are floors. The basins are vessels. The “rocks” are ceramic nodes, weeping thin films of moisture from pores that were once made, not born.

The Driploom sits at elevation enough that the wind should feel sharp, but the air is warm and humid, thick with green breath and mineral damp. It lies in a vast middle height, above whatever lower world might exist, below whatever sky continues into blackness. There is no mountain beneath it in the ordinary sense. The Driploom is its own mountain, a continental shelf of made shells, woven supports, and living architecture. Its height comes not from rising out of a valley, but from the long, patient accumulation of terraces built over void, then grown into, then weathered into stillness.

It is so large that its weather does not come from elsewhere. Clouds begin over one section of the loom and end over another. Rain may be falling on a distant terrace while the nearest basin is dry and silver with condensation. Storms do not travel across the Driploom from beyond the horizon; they are generated by the place itself, as if the whole constructed country has become a single organism with lungs made of vapor.

## Terrain

The land is not continuous in the way a plain or plateau is continuous. It is layered, interrupted, perforated, and spaced. The terrain has a rhythm: platform, gap, tower, basin, stair, void, platform again. Between these presences lie enormous intervals of empty air. The place is dense in the sense that every standing surface is alive, but it is empty in the larger sense of spacing. What has been made stands apart from what else has been made, and the distances between them are often vast enough to swallow scale.

Terraces extend outward like the seats of a colossal theater facing no stage. Some are broad and shallow, curved like bowls. Others are narrow ledges strung between root-wrapped pillars. Stairways rise where there should be no stairway, their steps too evenly worn to be new but too irregular to be precise. The steps are not polished to smoothness; they are polished into themselves. Every edge has been shaped by centuries of foot, water, vine, and weather until the form seems inevitable. One does not look at a stair and think someone built it. One looks at it and thinks it has always been going up.

There are basins large enough to hold clouds, and basins small enough to hold only three drops. Some basins are dry except for a sheen of moisture. Others are shallow marshes, where the water is dark and still until a moss-bead falls and rings through the silence. The marsh edges are not muddy in the ordinary way. They are felted, spongy, and layered with roots that have grown through fired clay, binding the artificial substrate into something that behaves like soil. When you step near the water’s edge, the surface resists gently, then exhales a cool damp breath.

Some regions are stair-like without being vertical. A valley may slope in a sequence of descending platforms, each one wider than the last, until the lowest terrace becomes a lakebed of black ceramic tiles covered in green slime and floating leaves. The lakebed may be dry. The “lake” may be a mist that pools in the same shape for hours and then withdraws into the surrounding plants. Water here does not always decide whether it is a substance, a vapor, or a promise.

There are walls that curve upward into a sky threaded with fibers. From a great distance these fibers look like the veins of some translucent ceiling. Closer, they reveal themselves as old support cables, now overgrown with pale lichen and hanging mosses. They catch water, then drip it in long, slow curtains into the void below. The walls are not fortifications. They are not retaining walls, though they retain. They are more like the sides of a loom, though nothing in this world is obviously being woven from one direction.

The terrain is established, balanced, settled into a form. This is not a landscape in the early chaos of creation. It is not collapsing, exploding, or being violently rebuilt. The rain has already found where to go. The plants have already learned how to stand. The animals have already chosen their routes. The made surfaces have already accepted the roles water and growth gave them. If anything has been broken, it has been broken so long that repair has become part of the terrain’s grammar.

## Materials

Everything is made, but nothing is sophisticated.

That is the central contradiction and the quiet miracle of the Driploom: it is a planet-scale environment shaped by simple tools. There are no engines here, no alloy beams cut by lasers, no foundations poured by machines. The materials are those of hands, fire, patience, and fiber: fired clay, sun-dried plaster, hammered copper, chipped stone, woven bark, lashed wood, braided vines, bone pins, resin, felted moss, ceramic shards, black glass-like nodules made by repeated heating and cooling. The surfaces bear the memory of primitive making: a thumb-print in clay, a nick from a flint edge, a groove where a copper awl was pulled too far, a row of small holes where a cord once passed and has since become a root.

The “stone” of the Driploom is often baked earth hardened by fire, then sealed with sap, then colonized by life. Some ceramic faces are smooth as bone, blackened by smoke or stained red with iron-rich washes. Some are rough, pitted with bubbles from firing. Some have split in old ways, and those splits are now channels for water and moss. The cracks are not signs of failure. They are seams. They are how the place breathes.

Metal is present but scarce. Copper is the most common. It appears in thin plates riveted to corners, in needles long enough to pierce a hand-span, in rings that bind climbing stems, in pins set into wall crevices like fossilized stars. The copper is not shiny. It has gone green-black from damp, or has bloomed into soft turquoise where the air has held it too long. Some copper shapes have grown so thoroughly over that they appear to be leaves with metallic veins. When touched, they are cold for only a moment before the warm moss covers them again.

The plant materials are not secondary. They are structural. Wood, where it appears, has often been shaped into stakes, beams, or pegs before being overtaken by growth. Many supports are alive: stems thick enough to bear a platform, trunks trained into arches, vines woven through ceramic grids and then allowed to fatten. The vegetation has been cultivated so long that it functions as architecture. A wall may be a row of interlocked trees. A roof may be a stretched membrane of broad leaves and resin. A bridge may be a vine that has learned to grow taut where it is meant to bear weight.

There is no bare substrate. Even when a surface looks like earth, it is layered compost and root-felt laid over made tiles. Even when a surface looks like rock, it is fired clay shaped and then softened by growth. Even when a surface looks like water, it is pooling in a vessel that was once hollowed by hand. The Driploom has no unworked face. It is a fully constructed ecosystem, or a fully grown construction, depending on which part of the day one trusts.
