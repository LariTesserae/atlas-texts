The plateau has the look of a place that finished eroding long before anyone learned to count the years.

It is high, though not sharp: the mountains around it have been reduced to broad, weathered shoulders, worn down by epochs to near-final shapes. What remains of their stone is mostly hidden, buried beneath a continuous living skin. The world here does not have soil so much as a body. The ground is moss as thick as felt, lichen in pale plates, fibrous root-mats, tubers swollen with stored water, and pale stems that rise like architecture. If you kneel and dig, you do not find earth at first. You find another layer of living tissue—white veins, brown nodes, damp threads, tiny ferns folded closed like letters.

The whole basin is vegetated in a total way. Not “many plants grow here,” but plant-life as substrate, as material, as structure. Trees and shrubs and creeping herbs have not merely colonized the land; they have been disciplined into civic form. There are no bare roads of crushed rock. Roads are hardened root-lattices, woven and polished by footsteps and wheels. There are no quarried walls. Walls are grown—layers of lignin and bark folded against the wind. There are no dead buildings. Even the houses breathe, though slowly, and at night they contract a little when the cool air thickens with dew.

And yet it is a city.

It is urban in the sense that there are districts, streets, warehouses, markets, workshops, and planned clearings. It is organized construction, though the construction is botanical rather than mineral. Settlements cluster in broad, rounded platforms where the root-mass has been trained to bear weight. From a distance the town looks sparse, almost accidental—each cluster separated from the next by long stretches of living green emptiness. But the spacing is deliberate. There are open terraces between neighborhoods where no structure is allowed to grow, kept clear by cutting back the moss and binding the surface down with cables. The town extends across a valley-sized region, not a cramped quarter of a square mile, but a whole shelf of land with multiple features: reservoirs in hollows, mill-grounds beside low ridges, orchards that have been made into public squares, factory-nodes rising from the turf like blunt knots.

The architecture is mostly low, rounded, and darkly green-brown, as if the buildings were fruits that had ripened too far. Many dwellings are grown from bulbous plant-masses—seed-chambers, pod-houses, tuber-halls, and domes of woven stem-fibers. Others are more mechanical: brass bands around living wood, copper pipes threaded through bark, glass panels set into membranes, steam-valves protruding from side-roots like metal flowers. The built world is layered. Newer plant-work grows over older plant-work; old foundations are swallowed into the living mass. A cellar may become a hollow trunk. A factory may begin as a stone-walled engine-room and end as a swollen burl that still runs gears.

The technology is industrial, but not of iron and coal alone. Engines are everywhere. Some are small and domestic, housed in the pith of a living wall, turning looms or pumps or grinding discs. Others are public machinery: condensing towers that weep water down their sides, hydraulic rams driven by pressure from the plant-body below, cable-ways running between tall seed-poles, and steam-factories set into broad hollows where exhaust rises into mist and drifts across the plateau. Electricity exists here as a secondary current—the plant itself carries weak voltages through its moisture, and engineers have learned to tap it. Copper combs are pressed into wet bark. Iron needles pierce the vascular tissue of great stems. Wires run along the surface of the living city like ivy made of thought.

The temperature is cool. High air. Long shadows. The sun is normal, bright but not hot; it gives light without warmth enough to dry the plateau. Breath fogs at morning around the mouths of engine-halls. Condenser pipes are cold to the touch. Frost forms on brass fittings when the wind drops, then melts into beaded water when the machines start again. The chill is not bitter; it is persistent, like a clean blade held near the skin.

Water is present, but reluctantly. This is not a drowned place, nor a lush river-country. It is damp in an intermittent way. There are seasons when the moss holds a heavy dew for days, and the whole city seems to be breathing out. Then there are drier periods, when the root-mat hardens and the hollows between ridges become cracked basins. Rain comes often enough but not constantly: short, cold showers, sometimes sideways with the plateau wind, sometimes fine and silent. In those times the water is caught everywhere—cups of leaf, channels in stem, stoneless basins lined with waterproofed lichen, brass gutters threaded through living walls. Old riverbeds are visible as faint depressions, but only occasional streams run in them, often for a day or two after rain, then vanish into the moss again.

There are pools too: still, dark water collected where the plant-body has dipped into a bowl-shape. These are not open lakes. They are pockets of water in larger dryness, like cups set upon a table. Sometimes they are fed from beneath by condensation; sometimes they are filled by a single cloud and then slowly taken back into the city’s body by root-wells and steam-pipes. Near the factory districts these pools collect oily films of plant-resin, and they shimmer oddly under the light, as if someone had poured a little weather into each one.

The sounds are large.

The plateau is not loud with voices alone. It is loud with machinery, movement, and tension. Engines thud through root-chambers. Hydraulics hiss. Cables groan under wind load. The living wood expands and contracts with changes of moisture, so the city creaks as if all of it is settling. Metal strikes stoneless earth and makes a strange dull ring. Steam valves snap shut at regular intervals. Somewhere always a hammer is beating, though sometimes you cannot tell whether it is a worker, a machine, or a growth-node splitting into two. Rain on stretched leaf-canvas sounds like drums. Wind through perforated stems makes low flutes. And when many machines start together, the whole district vibrates at a pitch you feel in the teeth.

Yet despite the noise, fauna is rare.

There are few animals. Not none, but very few, and they move like rumors. Pale deer with oversized ears appear on the high rim of the basin, watching the city from a distance before dissolving into the moss. In the reservoir hollows there are rare herons, almost the color of old paper, standing perfectly still beside the pockets of water. In the engine-tunnels, small white creatures—perhaps mice, perhaps something older and stranger—feed on spilled starches and warm oil. Above the factory roofs at noon, a single kind of moth with broad copper wings drifts from heat source to heat source, never many at once. Birds are scarce. Most living things in this place do not look at you. They are elsewhere, or they are only signs of elsewhere: a feather caught in wire, a print in wet moss that vanishes after one step, a bone in a root-pit with grass already growing through it.

The absence of many animals gives the city a different kind of life. It feels as if the environment itself is enough. The living mass has taken the ecological roles that fauna might otherwise occupy. Plants eat light, drink moisture, host machinery, shelter workers, and perhaps, in their slow vegetable way, remember too much.

That is where reality begins to thin.

Not everywhere, not dramatically, but enough to disturb certainty. Streets sometimes behave as though they are interior rooms. A road can enter an arch and, after a stretch, the moss on the ground grows in the shape of a ceiling pattern instead of a floor pattern, and you realize the city is deciding what counts as inside. Buildings occasionally shift position by inches overnight, not visibly, not with sound, but enough that a familiar door no longer lines up with the window across the square. People learn to adjust; the city expects it. Factories are fed not only with wood and water and resin, but sometimes with something less physical: old weather patterns, captured in condenser-coils, or with the memory of a previous season’s harvest stored in tuber-vats.

Water is strange here too. It sometimes remembers. A pool may hold rain for a week, clear as glass, and then, when disturbed, give off the scent of a river from a different year. In the reservoir hollows, reflections can lag a moment behind the things reflected, so that when a worker steps away, the image in the water still stands where he was. Steam rising from vents can form letters for an instant—old industrial labels, district signs, names of machines no longer installed. These things are not magic in a decorative sense. They are simply part of the rules. The plateau is too old, too worn, too saturated with life and labor for ordinary matter to remain stable. The world has been ground down until its surface became alive, and living surface has started to think.

At dawn, the light is normal and cool, almost pale silver. Long shafts pass through gaps in the city’s upper canopy, striking moss platforms and making them glow. The town begins to wake not with bells but with hydraulics. Somewhere, a cableway creaks into motion. In another district, an engine coughs, spitting white breath from a brass snout. Condensation gathers in the cups of leaf-roof and drips into collection channels. Workers walk along root-roadways, boots tapping against the living path. They wear thick wool, oilskin, and goggles. Many carry small cutting-tools or gauges; others carry nothing but the look of someone listening to the machines.

From high ground, the whole settlement is visible across the valley-sized terrace: scattered clusters of bulbous dwellings, factory knots, reservoir cups, drying frames, public squares kept bare by cutting back the moss. Beyond the clusters, the green emptiness goes on for long distances, soft and uneven like a breathing surface. In the distance, the worn mountains rise and fall, not sharply, as though they have already accepted defeat. Above them, the sky is open and ordinary, but it presses down on the plateau as if it too is part of the city’s construction.

The air smells of wet bark, metal oxide, steam, resin, cold stone, and something faintly sweet—like a fruit left too long in a locked room.

At noon, the sun is bright and the city is loud. Engines hammer. Condensers hiss. Wind moves through the upper stalks with a sound like a field of dry corn, except that the stalks are as large as masts. The streets are mostly empty, not because people are absent, but because the spaces between them are so wide and so carefully preserved. A single cart may cross a broad green plaza drawn by nothing more mechanical than a set of counterweighted cables and a steam drum. In the workshops, gears turn inside hollow trunks. In the markets, stalls are grown from curved stems and shaded with stitched leaf-cloth; goods lie arranged on slabs of hardened pith—bolts of fabric, coils of copper wire, jars of resin, dried fruits, machine parts, and small caged condensation units.

Evening comes without much drama. The sun stays normal, only lower, warming the metal on the rooftops without touching the moss at ground level. The air cools fast. Frost forms where the pipes sweat. The engines keep running, but their sound changes—less like work, more like a pulse. The living city settles. The roots tighten. The water pockets reflect the last light with an almost perfect symmetry, except that some of them show stars a minute before the stars are there.

This is not a pleasant place in the ordinary sense, but it is not cruel either. It is too worn for cruelty. It is a place that has passed through violence and exhaustion and emerged into a settled final shape. Its harshness is quiet and systemic: the chill, the damp, the long intervals between buildings, the constant mechanical groan, the rareness of animal life. Its beauty is equally quiet: the way moss holds the edge of a machine like a soft sealant, the way light falls through the city’s green vaults, the way the old mountains have become a gentle frame for an impossible town.

It is a place where a plant can be a house, and a house can be an engine, and an engine can be a rumor, and a rumor can be a street.

If you stand at the rim where the plateau begins to slope away into lower country, you can look back over the whole basin. It does not look ruined. It looks finalized. All the sharp edges are gone. The ridges are smooth. The structures are grown. The machinery is worn into the body of the place. The town seems sparse and open from a distance, almost empty, but the emptiness is not absence; it is spacing, the way a city makes room for air and water and movement. Between the clusters of dwellings, the moss swells and contracts very slightly with the passage of underground pressure, so that the whole landscape seems to be holding its breath while all its engines turn.

Then a factory-whistle sounds somewhere out of sight, long and bright, and the plateau trembles under it.

In that sound the city answers. Pipes open. Steam rises. Cableways swing. In a hollow near the center of the settlement, a new pool forms as rain that has not yet begun to fall collects first, perfectly still, catching the bright normal daylight like a mirror that refuses to explain itself.
