## The Hollow of Anhydrous Green

It begins not as a place beneath the surface, but as the absence of anything above it to press down.

Imagine a cavern so wide that distance is uncertain. The floor drops away in shallow bowls and dry terraces, all of it covered with plant life so completely that the stone no longer seems to exist. There is no lake, no stream, no river, no wet seam in the rock. There is no dew. There is no moss that gives underfoot, no lichen that holds moisture, no puddle of meltwater reflecting the strange bright vault. Water, here, is a rumor the body rejects. The air is cool and dry enough to draw moisture from the lips without seeming to threaten life at all. Everything that grows has learned to do without the soft liquid world, or has replaced liquid with something stranger: resin, dust, oil, crystalline vapor, the fine powders of mineral metabolism.

The ground is not bare earth but a living plate. It is a skin of root-felt and salted filaments, pale silver-green in the ordinary light, brittle where the air is thin and darker where it pools beneath hollows. This is not sparse undergrowth. This is vegetation as substrate: the city is built upon a plant. Roads are not paved over soil but grown through it, cleared and compressed by centuries of roots. Where a street runs, the root-mat has been stitched into firmness; where a plaza opens, the filaments thin into pale lace and expose dry mineral beds beneath. From above, if one were to see this cavern from some impossible ceiling, it would appear as though the floor had been covered with a vast textile, woven by a civilization of rootlets, with small clearings scattered across it like tears in cloth.

Light enters because it was invited.

There is no sun visible from within the Hollow, and yet the light is normal: bright enough to read by, steady enough to cast clean shadows, white with a faint greenish edge from passing through layers of filtered mineral dust. It comes from shafts so high they seem to hang in empty air. Mirrors of polished mica, copper, and grown glass are set along the vault, some of them ancient, some still being installed. They bend the daylight from the far surface into the cave, splitting it into long columns that fall through the city with dry brilliance. When the wind outside changes angle, the light columns tremble and crawl over the terraces. A person standing beneath one feels not warm, exactly, but noticed, as if the day has reached deep underground just to look.

The temperature is cool, stable, and slightly uneven. Near the upper shafts the air is clean and chilled, smelling of stone and light. Lower in the basins it warms faintly from industry and from the slow metabolic heat of the living substrate. There is no breath of wet weather here, no humid rot, no swampy exhale. The coolness is mineral, dry, close to the temperature of a cellar or a sealed storage vault, but softened by plant.

Erosion has not finished its work.

The Hollow is still coming into being. Not in a ruined or collapsed sense, but in the sense of a place mid-sentence. New chambers are opening at its edges. Root masses split raw stone with patient force. Dry cracks widen slowly. In the unfinished districts, the architecture is only half grown: stone blocks have been placed, root-arches have been trained over them, cables of vine and iron have been strung, but the final living shape has not yet arrived. Here the city looks embryonic, as if it might one day have rooms that do not yet have walls, stairways that curve into missing upper floors, plazas bounded by trees whose trunks are still young and uncertain.

This is not decay. This is formation.

The land has not settled into the kind of mature erosion that carves smooth cave passages or leaves a stable landscape. It is being made. The roots are masons. The machinery is a nursery. The old quarrymen and the new city planners seem to have become the same profession.

Because there is a city.

It is urban, but not crowded. This is a settlement laid across a regional basin, spreading into adjacent shafts and lower bowls, with enough space between buildings that a person can walk from one district to another and still feel outside. It is town- or valley-sized, not a sprawling megalopolis. Structures are grouped in scattered clusters around shafts, around mineral terraces, around large clearings where the living root-mat is too thick or too strange to build upon. The density is low, but the pattern is deliberate. It feels less like a city that has consumed the landscape than like one that has learned to occupy it with polite reservations.

The buildings are not simply built beside plants. They are built through them.

The most ordinary structures are seed-boxes: squat buildings with walls of compressed rootboard and mineral skin, roofs curved like shells. Some are grown from trained trees whose trunks widen into load-bearing pillars and whose branches form lattices over the upper stories. Others are industrial buildings, more rigid, more mechanical, built of iron, stone, and baked root-timber. They have tall vents, cable towers, flywheels, chimneys that exhale dry vapor rather than smoke. Pipes do not carry liquid. They carry air, oil, seed-dust, powdered mineral, and fine resin. Where pipes are broken, they do not leak; they hiss.

Factories stand in the mid-distance, their forms blunt and functional. They are powered by engines that run on plant oil, compressed air, and thermal gradients. The Hollow is cool, but not uniformly so. Warm air rises through shafts; cold air sinks through side corridors. Engineers have built around this breath. Huge fans turn in vertical wells. Pistons operate with dry rhythm. Belts of woven bark and rubber-like root resin spin around wheels of iron. Dynamos produce electricity for lighting, signaling, and the few machines that need sparks, though most operations prefer pneumatics, gears, or slow hydraulic substitutes.

This is industry that has never built the wet world. It has grown into a dry one.

The city’s infrastructure is layered in the way of places that have been occupied for generations but not continuously expanded. Some streets are old root-roads, pale and polished by traffic. Some newer avenues are still soft with dust and fibrous with young tendrils. Staircases rise beside the trunks of engineered trees. Balconies project from living walls. Cableways swing between towers. Small rail lines carry carts of salt, mineral powder, harvested oil-seeds, and timber cut from trees that regrow as part of the city’s civic plan. There are warehouses, drying sheds, grinding rooms, fermentation vaults where the process is not liquid but catalytic, where plant chemistry is coaxed into new materials. There are reservoirs of air, tanks of oil, bins of crystallized salts, and storage towers for seed.

None of it feels like abandonment. It feels occupied by a different idea of habitation.

The ecology is total. Plant fills nearly every available surface, but animal life is abundant. Creatures move through the city and around it, adapted to the dry, cool, bright-underground environment. Birds with pale feathers and salt-crusted beaks roost on the upper cables, where the mirror-light is strongest. Their songs are not loud, but they carry. Insects click and rustle through the root-mat: beetles with shells like polished slate, moths with wings dusted in fine mineral powder, crickets or cricket-kin that pulse their calls in long intervals so the quiet is never fully broken. Small mammals live in the spaces under buildings and between root shelves: creatures with large ears, dry fur, and teeth adapted for grinding hard vegetation. Larger ones graze in the outer clearings, animals like deer but wrong in the mouth, their hooves splitting into soft pads for quiet movement, their coats pale from the cave light.

Fauna does not merely survive here; it participates.

Predators are uncommon but present. A slender, many-eyed cat-kin moves through the upper beams. Something with a dry, rasping cry hunts moths by listening to wingbeats. In the outer terraces, packs of salt-toothed scavengers follow the migratory drifts of seed-pods and fallen fruit. They are not savage because the Hollow is savage; they are simply part of the system. The city does not exclude the wild. It uses the wild. Beasts are herded in some districts, protected in others, fed by the factories’ byproducts, harvested for oil and leather and bone, and often allowed to enter the root-lane margins where they are mistaken for harmless residents.

The plants themselves are not ordinary.

They resemble trees, shrubs, grasses, fungi, and mosses only as architecture resembles anatomy. Some grow with dry, waxy leaves that ring faintly when struck. Others are pale and translucent, their internal channels filled not with sap but with a clear oil that refracts light. Some have crystalline thorns or mineralized tips, as if their bodies have begun turning themselves into building material. Others exhale fine dust at intervals, so the air in certain lanes seems perpetually glittering with tiny suspended motes. The dominant vegetation is not thirsty. It feeds on light, mineral salts, atmospheric compounds, and the slow decomposition of dry organic matter. Its “moisture” is stored in chemical bonds, not in pools or veins of liquid.

This gives the place a strange, almost sealed quality. It is not that the Hollow lacks life. It lacks the usual signs of life as humans recognize it. The roots do not weep. The stems do not swell. The fallen fruit does not soften and rot in the familiar wet way. Instead it dries, curls, splits, and becomes seed or dust. The smell of the place is not green dampness, but something more like chalk, oil, dried herbs, warm stone, metal, and faint resin.

The weirdness is present but restrained. The world has one clear alteration and many consequences.

Sometimes the plants seem to organize themselves with civic awareness. A district boundary is marked not by a wall but by a change in root architecture. Streets tend to widen slightly where traffic expects to turn. Trees grow around lamp-posts and cables with a patience too consistent to be accidental. The factories feed certain groves. Those groves return materials that fit the next factory’s needs. Predatory animals avoid the inhabited plazas not because they are frightened, but because the root-mat there emits a faint signal when stepped upon, and the animals have learned to associate that signal with human tools and mechanical noise. None of this is obviously supernatural. It is simply as if the ecosystem has been urbanized for so long that the city and the plant have become inseparable.

Sound is mostly quiet.

The Hollow is not silent. It is not dead. But it does not roar. The machinery is scaled and rhythmic rather than explosive. Engines click. Belts hum. Presses fall with soft thuds. Air moves through ducts in low susurrant breaths. Above, wind passes through the shafts and produces a tone too steady to be weather, too dry to be rain. Below, the root-mat creaks as temperature shifts. Birds settle into roosts with muted rustling. Insects pulse their calls like small dry sparks. People speak in ordinary voices, but the place seems to absorb sharpness. Footsteps muffle. Metal strikes are softened by dust and oil. Even distant machinery has a hushed, working quality, like a city that has learned to labor without announcing itself.

There is no water to splash, no river to drown sound, no rain to make the air thick with noise. What remains is a world of dry motion. The sounds are textures more than events: grain shifting in a hopper, cable flexing, wingbeats on a stone ledge, the quiet knock of fruit falling and breaking open without liquid.

Yet the place is active.

Dynamic processes move through the Hollow constantly. The light changes as the surface day turns and the mirrors adjust. The upper shafts warm and cool, producing diurnal winds. In the early “morning,” the mirrors bring broad columns down into the main basins; the dust lifts visibly; the city wakes with a faint smell of heated resin. During the “day,” root-trees open their pores and the air takes on a cleaner, almost metallic taste. In the “evening,” the columns thin and slide across the terraces, and creatures shift from higher roosts to lower feeding grounds. The factories slow. Fans change direction. A new tide of seed-pods detaches from the upper growths and drifts down through the shafts in dry spirals.

Predators hunt at dusk. Scavengers gather at the factory vents to eat what the machines discard. Pollen moves in invisible rivers through the cavern air. The living ground expands by millimeters, by seasons, by the patient logic of root pressure. New buildings germinate in nursery yards and are later lifted into place. Old ones are consumed slowly, their materials returning to the substrate. The city grows as much by biological accretion as by construction.

This is where the urban and the plant become the same process.

Some districts are called “rootwards” rather than neighborhoods. A rootward may contain houses, workshops, a cable-station, a market stall, a shrine of stone, and a grove that serves as the community’s foundation. The grove is not decoration. The buildings are anchored to it. The grove regulates air, temperature, and sometimes water—not liquid water, but humidity through chemical exchange. In the industrial districts, the same logic is exaggerated. Factories are built around enormous engineered plants, trees with trunks wider than houses, roots drilled into the stone like piles, sap channels tapped for oil, branches trained into supports. These are living infrastructure. They grow into their purpose.

There is a kind of beauty here that is dry and structural.

The open spaces are wide, and because the light is normal, the depth of the cavern does not feel suffocating. The horizon, where one exists, is formed by low terraces, cable towers, groves of pale trees, and long mirrors tilted like frozen waves. Buildings appear scattered because the land itself is a single organism. If every surface were bare, the city might seem crowded. But because the whole floor is alive, each structure becomes an island in a green, mineral, silent sea.

The scale is regional without being empty. One can see many features at once: shafts, plazas, root-forests, factories, grazing clearings, stairways descending into lower bowls. These are not far apart. They form a connected landscape, a town-sized valley underground, but the spaces between are generous. A person walking from one district to another will pass through dry clearings, beneath low vine arches, beside slow-moving belts of seed dust, and through groves where the light changes color. The journey feels continuous, not segmented.

The architecture is layered with age.

Some structures are clearly old: rounded root-stones polished by hands, beams darkened with oil, mirrors pitted by centuries. Others are new and raw, their plant fibers still pale, their joints not yet sealed with resin, their names still chalked onto boards. The city is not a ruin. It is not a finished utopia. It is a working place, a settlement under construction, its future growing into it like roots into stone.

There is a strange civic order to the wildness.

The plants are not tamed, exactly. They are negotiated with. Certain species are allowed only on outer slopes. Certain trees are forbidden from growing inside certain chambers because their roots crack the mirror-bases. Certain animals are considered harmless if they move only at night. The ecology has customs. Not laws, necessarily, but habits repeated long enough to look like etiquette. The predators stay to the periphery because the peripheral prey is abundant. The insects pollinate the upper orchards because those orchards drop seeds the machines collect. The machines vent warm air through root channels because the root channels use it to cycle mineral vapors. Nothing is accidental anymore, but nothing is wholly controlled either.

This is the essential feeling of the Hollow: a place where an industrial city has learned to live inside an ecosystem rather than on top of it.

It is not gentle. It is not pastoral. The factories are real, the engines are real, the workers’ boots leave dust in the root-felt, the predators eat, the trees can split stone, and some structures are still so unfinished they look like wounds waiting to grow shut. But the wildness is not outside the walls. The walls are wildness. The infrastructure is biology. The biology is infrastructure. The city has not conquered the green; it has married it to labor.

In the quietest moments, before the mirrors shift their light, the place seems almost contemplative.

The dry ground gives softly underfoot, not wet, not spongy, but firm and fibrous. The roots creak once in a while as the cavern cools. Somewhere above, a cable hums. A bird calls with a small, clear note. Dust moves in a shaft of ordinary light. A factory breathes out a warm current of mineral vapor. The city’s plant-life opens and closes by rhythms no one fully explains. And beneath all this, far below the lowest terrace, raw stone remains where neither root nor machine has yet reached, the unfinished heart of the Hollow still waiting to be made into a place.
