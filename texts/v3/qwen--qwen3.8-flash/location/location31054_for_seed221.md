The place is called, if names matter, the Condensary.

It is a seam in a high plate above the clouds, a lip of metal and frost where the wind is thin and hard as wire. There is no mountain in the natural sense—no bedrock, no soil, no sedimentary patience. The ridge is a construction: a plate of pale alloy laid over other plates, stitched together with rivets, sealed with resin, braced by struts so numerous they look like thorns. Somewhere in that plate is a hatch the width of a hand, rusted not by age but by the fixed look of things that are meant to seem ancient. When it is opened, the air that breathes out is cold enough to sting the eyes, and it carries the smell of a freezer after rain: wet metal, brine, chilled enamel, chalk-dust, and something faintly living.

From outside, it is a pocket. From inside, it is a world that refuses the idea of outside.

The space is small enough to touch in two or three strides, but density has folded it into something larger than distance allows. Walls are lined with terraces no taller than a foot. Floors rise in stamped ridges. Channels cut through sheets that were never poured by stone and never carved by slow water, yet they look exactly as though geology had just remembered how to begin. The place has the shape of a marsh-edge after a rain, but the marsh is made of machinery, and the rain has been caught forever in the act of arriving.

It is a tiny highland. Corrugated plates become ridges. Hollows become tarns. A low step becomes a cliff. A pipe becomes a riverbed. The scale is intimate, room-like, almost domestic, yet nothing in it feels settled. There are no trees. No soil crusts. No fallen logs. No open sky. What would be ground is always a manufactured surface: cast alloy, ceramic tile, rubberized mesh, poured glass-like resin, ribbed insulation skins, black membranes stretched over frames like frozen skin. Even the frost, when it comes, comes on made forms, tracing the edges of things that were built to be touched by weather but never born from it.

The wetness is the first absolute law.

There is no dry surface here. Water is not a river, not a lake, not a pond in any large sense. It is a condition of the air and the skin of the place. Every wall weeps. Every seam exhales fog. Cold plates collect condensation in beads that swell, slide, and fall into capillary channels too small to call streams but too precise to ignore. In the lower basins, water gathers in cups the size of hands, black and still. In the higher channels, it moves in films so thin they seem only like a gloss, as if the made terrain were permanently just past rain. Mist hangs in the low passages, pooling where the air is colder, and droplets form on wires, on valves, on the backs of creatures, on the tips of tiny metal grasses. If you stand long enough, your sleeves darken at the shoulders and wrists from a chill damp that has no visible source.

The cold is deep and clean, not harshly biting, because the place is too enclosed for weather to change. It feels like the interior of a mountain at altitude in the dead season, if the mountain had been hollowed and furnished by industry. Breath becomes a short cloud. Metal surfaces numb the fingertips. Water beads instead of soaking; the air is cold enough that moisture seems to pause before deciding whether to be fog or frost. In the darkest corners, tiny ice flowers form along rivet-heads, but they do not grow. They are there in the same configuration each time, perfect and frozen at the moment of their first crystallization.

Vegetation exists, but only as an almost apologetic exception.

In the sparsest hollows, where the capillary water slows, there are thread-mosses: pale, wirelike tufts with no true root, anchored to tiny pits and scratches in the plates. There are silver crusts around drains that might be lichen or might be some mineralized organism bred to survive on condensate and machine dust. A few reed-thin filaments stand in the shallowest tarns, stiff and colorless, their tips curled like the ends of old wire. They do not cluster into thickets. They do not shade anything. They are scattered, solitary, too few to change the atmosphere, and when present they only emphasize that this is not a green place, not a growing place, not a place that has learned how to live through plants.

Fauna is the true overgrowth.

At first you think the walls are patterned with scales, studs, chips of mica, beads, flakes of varnish. Then one of the patterns lifts a wing. Then another pattern turns to face you with eyes that are too polished, too regular, like little lenses dropped into metal. Then you understand that the room is covered in animals.

They are not merely numerous; they are saturating. The place has too many presences for its volume to feel empty even for a second. Moth-things rest shoulder to shoulder on the condensers, their wings made of a material like oiled paper or thin tinned foil, trembling with a faint electrical static. Beetle-creatures with black carapaces crawl along the channel floors, their backs studded with rivets or little glass eyes, moving in slow processions over water films. Pale, segmented eel-threads swim in the cup-sized pools, too small to be fish, too regular to be larvae. Crustacean forms with segmented legs hang beneath the ledges like living hinges. Birdlike shapes, no larger than hands, sit in crevices with beaks of stamped sheet, their throats opening in silence or near-silence. And everywhere there are smaller things: six-legged specks, jointed flakes, crawling dots of living texture.

None of them seem wild in any recognizable sense. They do not flee. They do not forage with hunger. They occupy their surfaces as though the surfaces were made for them, which may be true. The place is so dense that creatures touch without collision, forming overlapping layers of fur, wing, shell, and damp. Some cling to other creatures.
