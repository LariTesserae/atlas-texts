The place is a vast low plain of made ground, as though the world were paved one piece at a time and still not finished.

Everything underfoot is constructed. There is no natural bedrock beneath the surface, no accidental geology, no wild rock that was merely discovered and walked upon. The ground consists of fitted plates, rammed cinder, fired clay tiles, slabs of dense glass-like material, and wide pans of shallow metal-looking stone, all set in courses that run for hundreds of miles in directions that almost make sense but refuse, at the edges, to resolve. The horizon is empty and enormous. The plain does not rise much; it stays low, broad, slightly tilted, a continent-sized floor of bowls, ridges, ledges, and seams, with occasional raised platforms standing like hearth-stones or unfinished walls.

It is hot. Not the bright dry heat of sunlit desert, but a damp, heavy, radiant heat coming up through the plates and out through the gaps. The air feels close and thick, carrying the smell of wet iron, warm mineral dust, and a faint sweetness like scorched salt. Steam rises in thin, wandering columns where hidden water has found a crack. Droplets bead on the surfaces and roll slowly downhill, pooling in shallow made depressions that have just become basins. The landscape is not soaked like a swamp, but it is constantly wet at the touch: slick rims, condensation clinging to the tiles, dark stains spreading from seams, little channels of warm water trickling across newly laid stone.

There is no plant life. Nothing leafy, nothing mossy, nothing green. Yet there are growths: pale filaments of salt-white fuzz, crusts like crystalline mold, branching threads that seem neither mineral nor fungus nor anything that would have a name in the old biology. They spread only where heat and water meet, forming soft halos around wet seams. They are quiet, patient, almost mechanical in their branching. They do not grow upward as plants would, but outward as patterns, like frost on a heated window, except that the window is hot and the frost is made of something closer to thought than chemistry. There are no animals. No insects, no worms, no birds. The stillness of the world is not empty, but it is unbreathed.

The light is dim. There is no obvious sun, or perhaps the sun is present but hidden behind a low, permanent haze that has been warmed into a dull copper-red. The glow comes more from the ground than from above. The seams glow faintly, as if heat were visible leaking out of a closed furnace. Shadows are soft and difficult to locate. Some of the plates shine with a wet black gleam; others are pale as bone. The whole plain looks as though it were seen from the inside of a cooling oven. The darkness is not night-like; it is simply too thin and too red to call bright, enough to see shapes, not enough to feel sure of them.

This is a world under construction. Erosion has barely begun. There is no ancient polish, no wind-rounded stone, no long-memory of weather. The surfaces are raw, abrupt, still deciding what they are. Edges are sharp where they should be blunt. The water has not yet learned how to wear down the land; it only gathers, weeps, and moves in shallow uncertain lines across surfaces that are still being set into place. The ground does not look old. It looks newborn, made by hands, but not finished by hands. It looks like a place in the first century of its own becoming, where the rules have not yet settled enough to produce habit.

And yet the making is primitive. The tools remain scattered through the landscape in sparse clusters: stone-headed hammers with salt-fiber bindings, copper wedges set into cracks, flaked obsidian chisels lying on ledges, long clay rollers worn smooth, simple levers resting against raised tiles, shallow kiln-pits with no flame but still radiating warmth. These are not machines in any high sense. They are hand-things, early things, made for the wrist and the shoulder. The world seems to have been built by slow, manual labor, by pressing, striking, fitting, dragging, ramming, and firing, by a people or process that never invented wheels of bronze or gears of iron, only tools with a directness of purpose. The tools lie about as if the laborers had stepped away for a moment and then, somehow, forever.

The scale is enormous. The plain is large enough to have its own weather of a kind. Not rain, not storms with lightning and wind in the ordinary sense, but slow atmospheric movements of heat, steam, vapor, and dust. Vast fronts of condensation roll across the tiles, making whole regions glisten at once. Warm mists crawl along the surface, low enough to be called breaths. The heat rises, meets the cooler air, then falls back in a fine warm drizzle that never quite becomes rain. At distant horizons, the plain bends under haze and shimmer, and for a moment the eye begins to mistrust the distance. The emptiness is immense, sparse enough that one can look in any direction and see almost nothing: a single tile seam, a small wet basin, a pale salt-branch, a distant upright plate like a leaning wall, all set at enormous intervals.

The density is low. The world is not crowded with features. There is much space between presences, wide areas of plain, and then a slight interruption: a low platform, a raised border, a cluster of steam vents, a line of wedges, a patch of white mineral fuzz. The emptiness feels balanced, not abandoned but not full, as if the making had proceeded slowly and without hurry, leaving room for things that might come later. The place looks like a sketch of a landscape drawn in stone and heat, with too much space between the marks and just enough marks to suggest intention.

But the construction is turbulent. The land does not rest. It is being assembled and rearranged at a scale larger than the eye can follow. Plates rise from seams when the heat beneath them shifts. Wet basins appear in places that were dry an hour ago. Small ledges tilt, then settle back, as though testing a hinge. In some directions, the plain looks like a floor of overlapping cards, and some cards have just begun to lift. Steam vents open without warning, hiss for a long time, and then close. Water pools in newly made grooves, runs toward a new boundary, finds that the boundary has also moved, and returns the other way. The turbulence is not explosive, except rarely; it is more like the whole place breathing and adjusting, shifting its own weight, trying to fit itself into a plan that changes as it is being followed.

There are moments when the landscape becomes difficult to trust. Not because of hallucination, but because the physics seem local and provisional. Shadows sometimes fall from objects that are not there. Heat can pool in a place and become visible as a slow turning color, like something trying to decide whether to rise. A hammer might lie beside a wall with a fresh chisel mark, but the mark seems too old, too weathered, and yet the weathering is only emerging. Water in a basin may be clear and hot, and then a thin film of it may creep upward along the side without a wave. The air hums faintly with a pressure that has no source. The ground, so made and solid, gives the sensation that some part of it has not yet decided whether to remain ground or become something else.

This is the place as it becomes: low, hot, wet, dim, and almost entirely empty of life as one would recognize it. A vast built plain with no natural substrate, still rough and embryonic, with tools half-buried in its surface and steam rising from its seams like breath from a sleeping thing. It is a landscape of primitive construction at planetary scale, sparse and silent, where everything is made but almost nothing is finished.
