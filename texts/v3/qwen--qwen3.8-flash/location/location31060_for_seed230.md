A place such as this can only be found where the land has already finished arguing with itself.

It is a high, small basin set into the shoulder of a broad tableland: a shallow, worn bowl of stone, no larger than a cathedral’s footprint and only a little larger than a village square, with a rim that is low enough to step through and high enough to hold the place apart from the wind. From a distance it would look ordinary—a pale scar in the hillside, a dry patch among ridges. Up close it becomes clear that it is not dry in the way of empty deserts, nor is it empty. It is a place where moisture and openness have learned to share the same ground. The stone is the color of milk, of bone, of old parchment left near a fire. Its surfaces are not sharp. Everything has been rounded, hollowed, and smoothed by ages of patient abrasion until the basin has the look of a vessel that was shaped for use and then left exactly as intended.

The walls of the basin are made of layered limestone and fine-grained shale, both weathered into long, low shelves. There are no dramatic cliffs here, no unstable scree, no fresh fractures. The erosion has gone past violence and settled into a final grammar: each slope at the angle of rest, each curve where sediment belongs, each lip of stone where water once ran now holding a dark, permanent line of dampness like an old seam. The ground inside the bowl is mostly open: gravel flats, worn bedrock plates, shallow terraces, and pockets of finer dust that have been arranged into stable patterns. The emptiness is not vacant.
