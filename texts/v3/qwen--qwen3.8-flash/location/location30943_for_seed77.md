The place is a low, immense made plain, so broad that its own heat seems to make weather. It lies almost nowhere near the sky in any dramatic sense: no sharp escarpment cuts it, no long mountain wall closes the horizon. It is a continent-scale basin of surfaces, gently undulating shallow pans, slightly raised ridges of fused material, and vast stretches of ground that appear, from a distance, like a sheet of pale, broken glass laid over the world. The curvature of the land is subtle, almost deceptive. It can feel like a floor more than a landscape.

Everything here is assembled.

That is the first strange thing, though “strange” is perhaps too strong a word. The ground is not wild ground. It is not naked bedrock, not raw silt, not a simple soil crust. It is a built surface, and it has been built by biology without any visible technology. There are no tools, no machines, no engineered joints, no signs of human or animal labor. Yet there is also nothing here that seems accidental. The plates fit. The seams channel. The surfaces face the sun at angles that shed heat unevenly, creating local drafts. The openings in the floor are not random cracks; they are pores, mouths, junctions, and hinges. The entire visible surface is a made environment, but it is made by growth, secretion, compaction, precipitation, and patience.

The ground is composed of interlocking sheets of pale mineral plate. Some are the color of old bone, others of sun-bleached shell, others of greyish tan with faint green or rust undertones. They lie across the basin like a tessellation that never fully agreed on its pattern. The plates are often rounded at their corners, though the rounding is not the soft erosion of ancient hills. It is the first rounding, the very beginning. The land is in the process of learning how to wear down. Its edges are still too crisp, its fractures too fresh, its channels too shallow. This is a landscape under construction, or perhaps under its first weather.

Beneath the visible plates is a denser matrix of fused fibrous matter: fossilized and living root networks, mineralized stem skins, fungal threads, microbial mats, and fine layers of calcium carbonate and silica that have been laid down in thin films by organisms that no longer exist except as chemistry and structure. The plate surface is not a hard crust sitting on natural earth. It is a living shell, or at least it has been until recently. Some of it has died. Some of it is dormant. Some of it is still secreting, still repairing, still drawing water up from hidden seams and letting it evaporate in ways that build new mineral veneer. The dust that gathers in the hollows is not ordinary dust. It is powdered phytoliths, calcium flour, salt bloom, and tiny flakes of dead surface skin. Even the loose material has been produced, sorted, and redistributed by the processes of the place.

The temperature is high, but the heat behaves differently than one might expect over natural rock. The made floor has thermal mass and structure. During the day, the plates hold heat unevenly. Pale surfaces stay brighter and cooler near their tops while dark seams warm faster. The shallow depressions collect hot air in a way that makes the horizon shimmer, as though the entire plain is breathing upward. At the same time, the low elevation prevents any dramatic temperature reversal from altitude. The air is close, heavy, and dry for much of the day, tasting faintly of mineral salt and baked silica. When the sun climbs, the plain becomes an oven of quiet expansion. The plates creak. Tiny fragments shift against one another with soft clicks. The whole surface seems to be settling under its own warmth.

At night, or in the early morning before the sun fully arrives, the made ground cools faster than the air near its surface. Condensation forms. Dew is not merely a surface phenomenon here; it is part of the hydrology. The plates are too smooth in some places to catch rain well, but their seams catch everything. In the narrow junctions between mineral sheets, moisture gathers in beadlines and threads. These seams lead downward into capillary networks built by roots and fungal filaments, drawing water away from the open, sun-scoured surface. Thus the environment is damp without appearing wet. One may walk for miles across a surface that seems dry, while the structure beneath it holds pockets of cool moisture in hidden chambers. Small pools occasionally appear in the center of broad plates, where the made surface has dipped into a shallow bowl. These pools are temporary, but they are frequent enough to keep the basin from being truly arid. They are also the beginning of local water bodies, embryonic basins that may last a day, a week, or a season before salt crystallizes around their edges and the floor closes over them again.

There is no river system yet. Water arrives and leaves too quickly to carve mature channels. Rain comes in bursts, sometimes from short convective cells, sometimes from wide, heat-hazed cloud bands that gather over the vast plain. Because the surface is so extensive, the weather has its own logic. The heat of the made plates lifts air, creating pressure differences across the basin. Winds move in long, lazy strokes, but also in sudden, gusty corrections. A distant storm can draw wind through the seam-openings and cool the ground just enough to change the airflow pattern hundreds of kilometers away. This is a place with weather of its own scale: large, rhythmic, not entirely predictable by ordinary terrain because ordinary terrain has not yet fully formed.

The vegetation is sparse, but its sparseness is intentional in appearance, as though each plant occupies a position chosen for maximum utility. They are not scattered at random. They stand in loose clumps, in single specimens, in small colonies at the seams where water can be trapped, at the edges of plates where mineral buildup is thinner, or in the shallow pans where dust accumulates enough to support a root. The plants are mostly low and dry, built for heat and for the peculiar mineral substrate. There are reed-like stems, pale and ribbed, with thick sheathing bases that look almost like boot-shaped collars. Some of the reeds grow alone, casting long, thin shadows. Others form small rings around moisture traps, their bases fused to the plate edge by calcium-rich crusts. Succulents with waxy skins appear in the more sheltered dips, their leaves pale blue-green or yellowish-grey, their roots spreading wide just under the surface. Lichens cover some of the plates in thin, slow-moving maps, and there are patches of blackish algal film where water remains longer, especially in the hollows.

The most abundant life is invisible: microbial mats, fungal networks, mineral precipitating films, and microscopic organisms that turn moisture into cement. These are not merely inhabitants of the landscape. They are its workforce, though “workforce” implies organization it does not possess. They build by growing. Where a root touches plate surface, it may secrete organic acids that loosen mineral grains; later, the same root may trap silica or calcium carbonate. Where a fungal thread crosses a seam, it may bind dust and moisture into a weak but persistent skin. Where algal mats spread over a shallow pool, they capture sediment and precipitate carbonate, layer by layer, until the pool’s bottom rises and the pool becomes a plate. The land is constructed by the residue of organisms, not by any intelligence that plans it.

Because there is no animal life, the ecology has a different texture. Nothing grazes. Nothing burrows in search of warmth. Nothing flies from plant to plant carrying pollen in a purposeful way. Pollination is mostly by wind, or by rain splash, or by simple spore dispersal. Seeds and spores drift across the plain in long, lazy currents. The absence of animals makes the soundscape extremely quiet. There is no rustle of mammals, no birdcall, no insect buzz. The loudest sounds are weather and material: wind moving through hollow stems, rain striking the plate surfaces, the ticking of mineral flakes loosening under heat, the faint groan of plates expanding as the day warms them. When a gust moves across a field of dried reeds, it produces a low, papery rasp, not unlike the sound of a wide sheet of dry cloth being drawn over a table. When rain comes, it is brief and percussive on the mineral floor, then softer as the water runs into the seams. The sound fades quickly, leaving a silence that feels architectural rather than empty.

Light here is normal in quality but altered by the surface. The sun is ordinary enough: bright, high, casting hard shadows at midday and stretching them at dawn and dusk. The sky is usually pale blue or whitish, washed out by heat. Clouds are not rare; they form because of the thermal instability of the plain, but they tend to be thin, high, or short-lived. What is unusual is how the ground reflects light. The plates are not polished, but their mineral skins have sheens—some chalky, some vitreous, some faintly metallic where iron or manganese compounds stain them. At certain angles, the entire plain can look like a mosaic catching fire. At noon, the light seems to come from the ground as much as from above. Shadows are dark but not absolute, because the pale surfaces bounce enough light to soften them.

The erosion is emerging. The plain does not have the weathered maturity of an ancient landform. The channels are too short. The slopes are too gentle. The cracks are too sharp. There are no deep canyons, no rounded hills, no long-term sediment terraces. There are only first drafts of erosion. Salt flakes from the edges of plates in tiny rosettes. Wind has begun to etch faint horizontal lines into the exposed faces of the mineral sheets. Rain has started to form shallow runnels no wider than a finger, sometimes no more than a crease. In one place, a seam has widened into a groove because the living root inside it has swelled with moisture and pushed the plates apart. In another place, a plate has lifted at one corner because the fungal mat beneath has retained water and caused expansion. These are the beginnings of forms. The land is raw geology, but not in the sense of being untouched. It is raw in the sense that it has not yet been finished by wear.

There are large areas of emptiness. The density of presences is low. A plant cluster may be separated from its nearest neighbor by hundreds of meters, or by kilometers. Fungal shelves, when they appear, rise like small stone tables from the plate surface, sparse and solitary. The spaces between these presences are not void; they are occupied by wind, light, heat, dust, salt, and the long silence of the ground. But the emptiness is so extensive that it makes each living form seem isolated, almost monumental. A single reed stand, casting a lattice of thin shadows, can feel like the only object in a sea of pale stone.

The place obeys a few simple but strange rules.

The first rule is that there is no untouched substrate. Within sight, every surface is made. The ground is a constructed shell. The dust is processed. The water flows through built passages. Even the apparent cracks are not simple failures of natural rock; they are joints in an organic architecture that is still changing. The “natural” is absent, but not because something artificial has replaced it. The construction is biological, geological, and slow. It has no device, no artifact in the mechanical sense, no technology beyond the capacities of organisms and physics. It is a made environment because everything about it is assembled from living and post-living materials.

The second rule is that water is episodic but never absent. The land is hot, but it is not pure dryness. It is a system of short moistures: rain, dew, fog, seepage, capillary rise, hidden pools. The made plates store water in ways that natural bare rock would not. The seams act like veins. The root channels act like pores. The shallow pans act like temporary reservoirs. The landscape alternates between open dryness and concealed dampness. This rhythm defines all growth. Plants appear near the places where water can be trapped, but their roots often extend under the plates toward reservoirs not visible from the surface. The vegetation seems sparse because it is choosing the places where the built floor permits life.

The third rule is that the environment is active without visible motion. There is no animal movement, no machine movement, no dramatic biological frenzy. The landscape seems still, but it is constantly shifting on a slow scale. Plates expand and contract. Mineral films thicken. Roots tighten around seams. Salt blooms and falls away. Fungal networks dry, rehydrate, and bind dust. Microbial mats precipitate carbonate in layers too thin to see. The wind reworks the edges. The sun reworks the temperatures. Rain reworks the channels. Nothing here is static, yet nothing is hurried. It is a world in motion at the pace of mineral growth.

The fourth rule is that erosion and construction are the same process seen at different times. A plate cracks because of heat. The crack widens because of root pressure. The widened crack fills with water and dust. Algae and cyanobacteria grow there. Mineral precipitate cements the gap. A new seam forms. The new seam changes the angle of water flow. The water flow begins a shallow channel. The channel becomes a groove. The groove weakens the edge of the next plate. The plate breaks. The broken plate exposes fresh material to weather. Weather flakes it. Salt crystals pry it apart. Dust settles in the hollow. Fungi bind the dust. A new surface begins. Thus the land is never simply decaying. Its decay is also construction. Its construction is also decay.

The fifth rule is that scale alters behavior. Because the made plain is so vast, its surface properties become regional climate. The pale plates reflect much of the sun, but the dark seams absorb it. The thermal contrast creates local winds that do not appear on smaller terrain. The moisture trapped in the capillary floor adds humidity to the air above the plates, making dawns foggy and hot days oddly oppressive. The lack of animal life changes the ecology, but it also changes the tempo. Without grazing or predation, plant growth is not balanced by consumption. Instead, growth is limited by water, mineral availability, and the physical constraints of the surface. Plants expand slowly, then senesce slowly. Their bodies are not eaten; they are incorporated. A dead reed becomes a root mold, a calcium-lined channel, a silica cast, or a fragment of the next surface.

Visually, the place is one of broad horizontals. The eye travels across it without finding a strong vertical interruption except for isolated plants or fungal columns. At midday, the sky and ground can seem almost equally pale. The boundary between them is a band of shimmering heat haze. In the distance, the surface may appear as a single sheet of light, interrupted only by tiny dark specks that could be shadows or living forms. Close to the ground, the pattern resolves into plates, seams, ridges, and grains. The textures are fine but repetitive: flaked mineral skins, salt lace, tiny channels, dust drifts in hollows, and the porous edges of root-cements. If one places a hand on the ground, it is warm, sometimes hot, and almost never smooth. There is a subtle grit to it, but the grit is bound. The surface feels constructed under the fingers, like pressing against a wall whose mortar has not fully hardened.

Water changes the appearance most dramatically. A dry plate looks matte, dusty, and pale. The same plate after rain becomes translucent in places, its hidden color veins showing through the surface. The seams darken to brown or greenish black as they absorb moisture. The tiny hollows hold temporary beads of water, and when rain has passed, the ground steams gently in the heat. In the center of some pans, a thin white crust appears as salt crystallizes, making the water surface look like a cracked mirror. Later, as the salt crust thickens, it becomes a brittle skin over a hidden wetness. This is one of the place’s recurring transformations: moisture becomes structure, structure becomes dust, dust becomes surface again.

There are also places where the built environment has failed, though “failed” is too judgmental. Some sections of plate have lifted unevenly, revealing the root-lattice beneath. These openings are like trapdoors in the floor, exposing pale fibrous webs that dry and darken in the sun. The exposed edges become colonized by lichens and algal films, which grow in thin green rings around the wound. Wind carries sand into the opening, and over time the sand becomes cemented by secretions, slowly sealing the breach with a new kind of crust. These damaged spots are not scars alone; they are construction sites. They are places where the land is remaking itself under the immediate attention of weather.

The atmosphere smells of salt, warm mineral, and faint vegetal dryness. There is a subtle resinous odor from some of the plants, released when their outer skins bake in the sun. After rain, the smell shifts: damp dust, calcium, and the green-metallic scent of activated microbial films. The air is usually clear, though distant dust can soften the horizon into a haze. Because the ground is made of fine particles, wind can lift small clouds of pale powder, but they rarely last long. The heavier dust falls, and the lighter spores and pollen ride higher, settling where moisture allows them to grow.

The light cycle gives the place its rhythm. Dawn brings condensation. The plates are cold enough that dew beads on their surfaces, and the seams fill with a thin film. The first light turns the whole basin into a glittering sheet. As the sun rises, the dew vanishes into the pores, and the ground becomes dry-looking again. Heat builds through the morning.
