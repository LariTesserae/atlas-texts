## The Hollow of Nine Cisterns

It sits high enough that the air feels cleaned and thinned, as if one breathes the inside of a bell, and it is small enough to be comprehended from its rim in a few slow turns of the head: a shallow bowl, perhaps two hundred paces across, set into the upper slope of an old plateau. There are no towers, no gates, no streets that lead outward, yet the place has a town-plan in it. From the rim one can see broad terraces, sunken courts, long low ridges, shallow channels, and dark depressions where water collects without ever settling into a proper lake. It is a hollow that looks built until one notices that every wall is frost, every roof is lichen, every courtyard is the accident of a stone worn flat by time.

The mountain has been here so long that its hardness has become a habit. The bedrock is grey-green, almost blue in shadow, veined with quartz that glows faintly when the ordinary sun strikes it. Nothing is young. Even the boulders have been so worn by ancient erosion that they seem to have forgotten their own angles. Edges round into shoulders. Surfaces become matte. Great slabs lie one against another with the patience of furniture. The hollow itself may have begun as a fracture in the high shelf, then been widened by meltwater, softened by frost, and finally polished into near-flattened form, as though the world had sanded away all unnecessary violence and left only a basin for weather to rehearse its oldest sounds.

The cold is not dramatic. It is constant in a settled way, the chill of a room long emptied of warmth. At midday the sun can feel thin and harmless, a bright white presence that does not promise heat. In the shade, breath clouds faintly. Stone gives a faint metallic taste to the tongue. Ice does not dominate the place, but it waits in crevices, appears overnight along moss-lined gutters, and cracks in the afternoon with a sound like a dry stick snapping underfoot. The high air is dry enough to make the water feel precious, and damp enough to keep it always present.

For the hollow is not wet, exactly. Nor is it dry. It is intermittent. Moisture appears in pockets, in sudden announcements, in long quiet seasons. Snow may arrive in sideways gusts from nowhere and vanish by the following afternoon. Fine rain, if it comes, seems to fall only over certain parts of the basin, drifting out of thin cloud and then retreating as if embarrassed. Elsewhere the air may be dry. But condensation is never absent. At night, stones weep a thin sheen. Pools gather in the shallow pits that look like cisterns dug without tools. Some pools last only a few hours; some sit black and still through several days; some are dry and only a pale mineral stain shows where water once lay. There are no springs roaring forth, no rivers cutting channels, only a damp persistence, a capillary patience, a high place that sweats water and then gives it back to the air.

The vegetation seems improbable for such cold, yet it is lush in its own scattered way. Green spreads wherever moisture collects. Mosses line the stone streets in thick cushions, not merely covering the ground but modeling it into softer shapes. Lichens grow in crusts of pale silver, rust-orange, and sulphur-yellow, so finely etched into rock that they make the stone look written over. In the hollows, sedges and small grasses rise from dark soil trapped between boulders. Saxifrage opens tiny white stars on dry ledges. Dwarf willow leans in sheltered folds, its leaves small and glossy. There are patches where moss grows so deeply that it seems wet and spongy, almost alive like fur. Other stretches are bare. The greenery does not fill the place; it traces it. It follows channels, terraces, cracks, and the sheltered sides of stones, leaving vast empty spaces between its occurrences. From above, the hollow looks less like a meadow or forest than like a city seen through fog: streets outlined in emerald, plazas half-swallowed by growth, parks isolated in a sea of bare grey stone.

That illusion strengthens as one moves through the basin. The terrain forms organized patterns that are not random enough to ignore. There are straight lines where frost has split stone repeatedly along one seam. There are curves where meltwater has polished a shallow run into a channel. There are squares where pebbles have been sorted by wind and ice into flat, orderly pavements. There are low walls formed by roots and calcite deposits binding gravel into compacted ridges. The terraces descend in steps, each one ending in a lip where water once spilled and now leaves a dark stain. The depressions in the floor are too regular to be accidental: circular cisterns, oval basins, rectangular hollows, each filled with a little water or a little dry mineral powder. One begins to think in terms of infrastructure, of public works, of a place arranged for use, though there is no toolmark, no mortar, no evidence of hands. It is a city built by weather, moss, frost, time, and the slow rearrangement of small stones.

The scale is intimate. The hollow can be crossed quickly, yet its boundaries matter. Around it rises a low rim of rock, worn smooth on its upper edge, enough to hold the weather in a bowl. The far side is not more than a shout away. There is nowhere in the hollow where one cannot see, if not everything, then at least the outlines of everything. And still it feels empty, not because it is large, but because there is so little between the presences that do occupy it. A boulder may stand alone in a wide court. A patch of moss may sit far from any other green. A pool may hold no visible life. A stone bench may stretch for many yards with nothing upon it but light and chill. The emptiness is not barren; it is spacious. The place has room for absence, and the absence is part of its order.

Light comes normal and unadorned. It is daylight, not moonlight, not stormlight, not magical radiance. The sun is high or moving toward afternoon; the blue sky is clear enough to feel distant. Because the air is thin and cold, shadows are crisp, almost hard. A stone’s shadow can lie across a patch of moss like a blade. At the same time, everything seems to absorb a little silver from the high sky. The lichens look brighter than they should. The moss reflects faint green. Water in the cisterns appears black because the sky overhead is bright and the basin walls are dark, but if one bends close, the bottom is pale with mineral powder and tiny threads of algae. There is no perpetual gloom here. The hollow is simply too old for any light to seem new.

Sound is what the place cannot escape. Though nearly empty, it is loud—not crowded with noise, but resonant. The bowl shape acts like a stone cup held to the ear. A drip falling into a cistern strikes and returns: plink, plink, hollowly multiplied. The wind passing over the rim finds the long cracks and fissures in the rock and makes a low, reedy hum that changes with its direction. When the wind drops suddenly, the hum does not stop; it bends downward, almost like a voice clearing its throat. Pebbles, loosened by frost, slide a short distance and knock against other pebbles with a sharp clatter that carries farther than the movement itself. Ice cracks. A dry twig snaps in some hidden crevice and sounds like a small door opening. Rain, when it comes, is amplified on stone into a rapid tapping, then on moss into a muted hiss, and finally into a collective whisper from dozens of tiny drains. The hollow is loud in the way an abandoned room is loud when one breathes in it. The sound is not crowded; it is exposed.

This exposure makes the place strange. One cannot move through it without hearing oneself, and that hearing seems to alter the act. Footsteps are too distinct. A hand touching stone produces a faint mineral rasp. When water moves from one basin to another, it seems to travel through hidden pipes, though no pipes exist, only capillary channels through moss, thin films across rock, and tiny underground fissures. Sometimes, after a sudden thaw in the afternoon, certain stones appear to sweat, drawing up moisture that they have no business holding, beading it along their lower edges as if the rock itself were breathing out. When the sun warms the lichen-covered terraces, the air above them shimmers slightly, not with heat, but with movement: evaporation, release, exchange. In the early morning, mist does not drift from the rim but rises from the cisterns, thin veils that sit above the water without touching the ground. The hollow seems to manage its own weather. It draws dew down. It lifts mist up. It lets frost expand the cracks. It lets moss close them again.

There are animals, but rarely. No flock darkens the stone. No herds graze the terraces. The fauna is scarce, almost accidental, yet present enough to keep the ecology from being merely mineral. A pair of ravens may sit on the rim, glossy and black against the grey sky, and their calls arrive delayed and doubled as if two more ravens answered from inside the stone. A fox or stone marten may cross the basin at dusk, stepping between the cisterns, leaving narrow tracks in the thin soil between rocks. A hare might shelter in a fold of moss on the leeward side, so perfectly colored that one mistakes it for a stone until it turns an ear. Small birds—ptarmigan, perhaps, or alpine pipits—stay low, their lives conducted in the spaces between moss and lichen, where seeds and insects hide. In the water pools, midge larvae curl and twist. Springtails jump across the surface film like flecks of silver dust. A spider’s web may be strung across a crack so regular it resembles a doorway. These creatures are rare but active, their presence magnified by the emptiness. A raven’s wingbeat can sound enormous. A fox’s approach can make the whole basin seem watched. When a bird takes water from a cistern, the ripple spreads across the stone floor as if a small hand had struck a bell.

Predator and prey move through the hollow in a pattern as quiet and deliberate as the rest of the place. The stone marten follows the scent-line left by a bird, crossing bare stone where nothing should live. A fox waits beside a dry channel where water may return by morning. A hawk passes overhead but does not enter unless the wind changes, because the rim is high enough to make the hollow a trap for any flying thing foolish enough to descend without planning. Even the insects have their own predations: beetle larvae in the damp soil hunt smaller creatures among moss roots; spiders wait in the shadows of the cisterns. The ecology is not rich in numbers, but it is full of motion. It is not crowded; it is consequential. Each animal feels like an event.

The vegetation also behaves like an infrastructure. Moss absorbs moisture and holds it against the stone, then releases it slowly into the hollow, so that the dry season is never truly dry. Lichens etch the rock, loosening it grain by grain, creating the fine soil in which the sedges and saxifrages eventually root.
