An ordinary human multiverse traveler should prepare for the Old Level as if preparing for a high-altitude winter crossing through an ancient abandoned city, inside a landscape that has stopped being dramatic and started being geological. The danger here is not that the place is hostile. The danger is that it is complete. It no longer needs you. It does not care about your plans, your comfort, your sense of direction, or your belief that distance behaves normally. It is old enough that human scale has been worn out of it.

If I were visiting the Old Level, I would treat it as a place where three separate survival disciplines meet:

1. **High-altitude cold weather survival**  
   Thin air, intense sun, patient cold, sharp shadows, frost cycles, unreliable hydration.

2. **Ancient urban archaeology without inhabitants**  
   Functioning but empty settlements, stone tools, drainage systems, granaries, kilns, looms, terraces, sunken courtyards, spiraling staircases, walls that shift.

3. **Spatial and psychological endurance work**  
   Distance does not behave reliably. A town may take three days or appear under your feet at noon. A tower may be folded into its own shadow. Rain may fall only in one district while the next square mile stays dry. The silence is not empty; it is active, absorptive, and capable of making a human mind begin listening to itself too closely.

The Old Level is not frightening because it wants to hurt you. It is frightening because it has already passed through all the things that usually give travelers comfort: animals, noise, continuous water, predictable distance, visible life, social presence, urgency, and rescue.

## 1. First understand the character of the place

The Old Level is a plateau that has finished its rising. The mountains have not gone missing; they have become worn, broad, and settled. The land is not wild in the ordinary sense. It is mature. It is late. It has entered a state where erosion no longer produces spectacular collapse but subtle adjustment. The relief has almost reached equilibrium. What remains is rounded stone, shallow pans, frost creep, moss, polished basalt, and quiet water that appears and disappears according to local moisture instead of ordinary watershed rules.

The sun is bright but not warm. The cold is not violent but patient. The damp is not a river system but a series of small, stubborn returns: condensation, frost, brief rain, mineral-rimed pools, blue threads of ice, fog, and moisture moving through stone like thought.

There are no animals. Not insects, not birds, not rodents, not predators, not scavengers. There is no bone. Life here is vegetal, fungal, mineral, architectural. The ecology does the work of animals using spores, ice, frost-heave, ground pulses, and slow structural adjustment. The towns and cities also do not behave like ruins. They behave like dormant infrastructure that continues to function without people.

The land’s quietness is therefore not a simple absence. It is a condition. You should prepare to enter a world where nothing watches you back.

That is harder than preparing for a place that threatens you.

---

## 2. Assume no rescue, no witnesses, and no ordinary geography

Even if your multiverse access includes communication devices, emergency beacons, or a return gateway, treat the Old Level as a place where rescue may be delayed, misdirected, or spatially meaningless.

Do not rely on assumptions such as:

- “If I see the town, I can reach it by walking in a straight line.”
- “If I have been walking east for six hours, I must be six hours east.”
- “If the satellite says I am here, then I am here.”
- “If the return point was visible, it will remain where I left it.”
- “If I hear something, something else is alive.”
- “If there is no danger, I am safe.”

You may hear nothing at all and still be in trouble.

You may see a town on the horizon and arrive there in twelve steps.

You may walk toward a tower and discover that the tower is not ahead of you but folded into the space beside you, behind you, inside its own shadow, or occupying the ground as if the plain had rearranged its own agreements about near and far.

You may stand in rain while dry miles remain on both sides.

You may enter a doorway and find that it opens into both a room and an alley at the same time.

You may find that your compass, your watch, your pace count, and your eyes are all honest but disagreeing.

The Old Level is not hallucinatory in the usual sense. The stone cuts. Frost burns. Sun burns. Water wets. Moss grows. The rules are physical, but they are layered, and not all layers agree with the rules you are used to.

Your preparation should therefore be both physical and epistemic. You must survive the climate, but you must also preserve your ability to know where you are.

## 3. Physical preparation: train as a high-altitude traveler, not a lowland tourist

If you are an ordinary human from a lower elevation, the Old Level’s severe elevation will matter more than many of its strangenesses at first. The cold is patient, the air is thin, the sun is harsh, and the terrain is deceptively broad. A traveler who is unfit, unacclimatized, underfed, or poorly dressed may find that the Old Level does not need to attack them. It simply continues, and their body slowly fails.

### Before the trip

If multiverse travel allows staged access, do not insert yourself directly at full elevation. Acclimatize gradually.

A responsible plan would include:

- Several days of progressive altitude exposure if possible.
- Cardiovascular conditioning.
- Strength training, especially legs, back, core, and feet.
- Load-bearing practice with your full pack.
- Cold exposure practice: hands, feet, face, and whole-body sleep in cold conditions.
- Night and low-visibility movement practice.
- Map, compass, pace, bearing, and barometric pressure drills.
- First-aid training for hypothermia, frostbite, dehydration, sunburn, altitude illness, trauma, and falls.
- Balance and rock movement practice on mossy, wet, uneven surfaces.
- Training for sustained solitude, reduced speech, and reduced sensory input.

If you cannot acclimatize gradually, assume you are arriving with physiological risk. Bring oxygen, know the symptoms of acute altitude illness, and be willing to retreat early.

### Altitude sickness awareness

Thin air can produce:

- Headache
- Nausea
- Poor sleep
- Dizziness
- Reduced appetite
- Shortness of breath
- Confusion
- Cough or breathlessness at rest
- Loss of coordination
- Extreme fatigue
- Pink frothy sputum
- Severe drowsiness

Do not “push through” altitude sickness in a place with no witnesses and uncertain distance. The Old Level may not punish you loudly. It may simply allow you to make a navigation error at the moment your judgment fails.

Useful tools:

- Pulse oximeter
- Thermometer
- Weight scale or body-weight monitoring if you can track it
- Acetazolamide or another altitude medication if medically appropriate and prescribed
- Portable oxygen or emergency oxygen reserve
