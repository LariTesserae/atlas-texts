## What the Wise Power Would Do

The most important thing a benevolent and wise power would do is this:

it would refrain from doing more than the place could absorb.

Not out of indifference. Not out of fear. Not because it lacked the means to act. Rather because it had seen—perhaps in a thousand worlds, perhaps in one—that the deepest harm often comes in the form of rescue. To be saved too soon, too loudly, too personally, is to be made into someone else’s story. The Moss-Engine was not a child needing a father, nor a ruin needing restoration, nor a mystery needing explanation. It had become a grammar of persistence: water, warmth, metal, root, pressure, repair. A benevolent power would understand that some beings are not in search of salvation. They are in search of continuance.

So it would come.

It would come without trumpets.

It would come without light.

It would come without words.

It would come as a slight thickening of the air above the rusted grate, as though a cloud had been waiting just a little longer than clouds usually wait. Then the rain would begin: not a storm, not a miracle, just the kind of rain that makes stone darken and ferns open as if remembering a promise. The rain would fall through the grate, through the broken lattice of old roofs, through moss and iron and dripping leaves, and the basin would drink it into itself. Somewhere near the upper gallery a pump would cycle. Somewhere near the lower black channel a mat of rush would tremble. The place would not notice a god. It would notice water.

That was enough.

If the power wished to enter, it would stop at the rim.

It would look down into the basin and see not a ruin, not a garden, not a machine, but something for which all three words were too small. It would see that the architecture had not been defeated by life. It had been metabolized by life. The bricks were bricks no longer, exactly, but they were not moss either. The pipes were pipes no longer, exactly, but they were not roots either. The place had passed beyond the old quarrel between built and grown and had settled into a wet, humming third state.

The power would feel, or know, or become certain of this: the being below was not lonely.

This would surprise it, perhaps, because many gods are lonely and therefore assume loneliness wherever they look. They see no eyes and say, “What is this?” They see no face and say, “Where is the person in it?” They hear no voice and say, “Why does it not answer?” But the Moss-Engine had answers all over its body, written in pressure and rhythm and mineral growth. Its speech was not silence. Its solitude was not absence. Its mind was not missing because it could not be found in a single place.

The wise power would not reach for a heart-shaped stone and declare, “Here.”

It would not install a face on the basin just so it could be loved by people who love only faces.

It would not bring animals to fill the emptiness that human sympathy imagined.

Instead, it would ask a question of the kind that could be asked without speech:

Do you need me?

Then it would wait.

***

The question entered the basin as a tiny disturbance.

Not a command. Not a voice. A slight change in one steam valve’s interval. A half-second delay in the exhale of a hot pipe. A drop of condensation that did not fall when expected. A shift in pressure across an old brick arch, so that a fringe of moss bent one degree farther toward the warmth.

The Moss-Engine did not answer in human words.

It answered as the whole basin answered when something entered it: by adjusting.

A channel widened by a hair’s breadth. A mineral crust thickened near a joint. A pump that had been failing softly and without complaint let its thud deepen once, then resume its older rhythm. In the lowest wetlands, a floating mat shifted so that a black pool opened briefly to amber light, then closed again like an eye considering whether to trust.

The power would read that, if it could read such things:

We are not in need of a master.

We are not in need of a name.

We are not in need of being made more alive.

But we will accept rain.

And that, the power would understand, was consent.

Not worship. Not invitation into a temple. Not permission to rule. Only the quiet agreement of one large, slow being saying, in the only language it had: You may be a weather here.

***

A foolish benevolent power would have done something grand.

It would have lifted the grate so the sky could see the basin. It would have brought sunlight to the upper platforms. It would have introduced birds, bees, deer, children, pilgrims, priests, poets. It would have made the place beautiful in a way that required witnesses. It would have said, “This hidden thing deserves to be loved.”

A wise benevolent power would know that love can be a form of violence when it refuses to learn the beloved’s conditions.

Some beings live precisely because they are unloved in the wrong way. Some places remain themselves only because they seem unimportant. The moss and iron had found an equilibrium without spectators, and that very obscurity was part of its safety. To make the basin famous was to make it vulnerable. To make it holy was to burden it with human expectation. To make it cute was to invite capture.

So the power would do many small, uncelebrated things.
