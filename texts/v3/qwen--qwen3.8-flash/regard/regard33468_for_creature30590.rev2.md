**What the Wise Power Would Do**

If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention to The Bellows Hollow, the first thing it would do is stop.

Not because it lacked the strength to act, but because it had learned, long before, that a too-large kindness can crush as surely as a too-small cruelty. It would arrive first as a pressure, a slight change in the weather above, a faint rearrangement of shadow at the rim. It would listen before it touched anything. It would hear the wet metallic percussion, the hiss in the stone throats, the trembling note of a drop striking a rim that had been building itself for centuries. It would kneel, if it could kneel, and become a shadow among the basalt.

A merely benevolent power would want to make the hollow safe.

It would want to take away the hunger of the fish, the cold that stiffens the moss, the dryness that sends the tardigrades into their small sleeping husks. It would want to stop the fall of every death into water, to keep every midge alive, to shine a light so steady that the algae never had to learn how to wait. It would want to make the hollow a temple: dry beneath the feet, warm in the corners, full of visible blessing, a place where no small body had to risk being eaten.

But a wise power would know that temple is another name for emptiness.

It would understand that the being living in The Bellows Hollow is not the fish. Not the salamander-like shape under the stone. Not the swarm of midges in the shaft of vapor. Not the moss. Not the green film at the edge of a hand-sized pool. Not even all of them together. The being is the way they continue: the wet parliament, the distributed breathing, the process by which stone, water, mineral, air, light, and small bodies make one another possible for a time.

To save the hollow from its own workings would be to destroy it.

To stop the drip would be to stop the music, and the music is not decoration; it is the sound of conditions by which life knows when to emerge. To remove the fish would be to remove a sudden silver answer to the swarm. To remove the death of one body from the pool would be to remove the meal that becomes the egg that becomes the larva that becomes the film on stone that becomes the moss that holds the water for the next larva. Nothing here is clean enough to be kept without becoming something else.

So the benevolent and wise power would not save the hollow from its nature.

It would save it from ending before its nature could continue.

That is a different work.

---

It would begin by learning the scale at which it was allowed to move.

For if it came as itself, it would be a catastrophe. A footfall would be a mountain shift. A breath would be a storm in the cold mist. A gaze would make the moss swell and then, perhaps, forget what it was for. So the power would make itself small. It would think in intervals: the distance between one drop and the next, the moment between the steam exhale and the condensation, the pause between the midge rising and the fish striking. It would work in the spaces between the names of creatures.

It would do many things that would look like accidents.

It would widen a hairline fracture in the deep basalt by no more than the thickness of a thought, so that when the water table fell too low in a hotter year, one thread of moisture still found its way to the upper ledges. It would tilt a broken slab by a degree too small to measure, so that water did not run off into the wrong darkness but pooled where the moss could drink. It would send a mineral salt through a crack where none had come before, not to change the chemistry of the hollow but to prevent it from becoming too poor to hold life.

It would cool one shelf of stone at the hour when a springtail colony would otherwise desiccate. It would warm one pocket of air so a vent did not choke itself with frost. It would nudge a grain of silica into the edge of a pool so a microbial film had purchase where it had been losing grip. It would cause a tiny tremor, so small that no earthquake would record it, that made a pebble settle and leave behind a crevice just large enough for a pale salamander-like animal to hide when a dry wind passed too near the mouth.

It would not answer prayers, because there was no one there to pray in words.

But it would answer pressure. It would answer thirst. It would answer the thin chemical message in a drying moss cushion that said, not in language, but in being: *again soon*. It would answer the fish by keeping the hidden pool cold enough, oxygenated enough, deep enough. It would answer the midges by keeping the vapor warm enough for emergence but not so warm that the water vanished before the larvae could rise. It would answer the bacteria by letting iron and sulfur pass through the stone as they had passed since before any eyes existed to notice.

The wise power would not choose a favorite.

It would not crown the fish as the being of the hollow. That would be a human mistake. The fish is one sudden answer among many, the flash that makes the pool’s patience visible. If the power made the fish sacred, the moss would still need damp, the larvae would still need rims, the microbes would still need chemistry, the springtails would still need a film of water to bounce across, and the salamander-like body under the stone would still need darkness. To save one citizen would be to betray the republic.

So the power would love the hollow as a condition.

Not a creature. Not a kingdom. Not a story. But a way of holding wetness and time together.

---

If there were threats from outside, it would not make them impossible.

Impossible is a kind of lie; the world must remain a place where risk is real. But it would make them avoidable by care, if care could be learned by the large, clumsy lives that might otherwise pass near. It would put a false angle into the mouth of the sink so a curious animal or a careless traveler would pause at the rim and not step down. It would arrange a small storm so the path would flood for a day. It would send a chill along the basalt that made humans call the place unpleasant, unstable, too cold for any good purpose. It would hide the hollow not by erasing it, but by making it seem ordinary from a distance.

If a well were being dug somewhere in the young lava country and would lower the water table beneath the pocket world, the power would not stop all wells. That way lay hunger elsewhere. But it would guide the drill one foot too far east, or make the pump fail at a certain depth, or send a warning through the memory of an engineer: *this layer is unreliable, move on*. If a path were being planned through the sink and would crack its basalt rims, the power would make one surveyor’s compass tremble in such a way that the route was called impractical. Not because impractical is true, but because impractical is safe enough to let the hollow continue without becoming a shrine.

For a shrine is dangerous.

A shrine wants visitors. A shrine wants names. A shrine wants light adjusted for awe, for kneeling, for belief. The hollow already has its awe without belief. Its awe is the fact that life keeps returning at the margins. If it became holy by being noticed too clearly, its small inhabitants would be ruined by the very attention meant to honor them.

So the wise power would protect the hollow from its own beauty.

It would keep its secret from the world, but not by cruelty. By the humility that leaves room for the place to remain itself.

---

If the power could ask the hollow a single question, it would not ask, *What do you want?*

The hollow would answer as a place answers: by continuing.

It would ask instead, *What must remain possible?*

The answer would come from many mouths without mouths. From the green fringe brightening at the light shaft’s edge. From the copepod beating its antennae in a basin the size of a thumbprint. From the fish rising only when the surface trembles. From the beetle leg tapping mineral dust. From the dead midge falling into water where the next meal is waiting. From the rim of calcium and silica growing a little higher, a little older, a little less sharp. From the moss swelling into emerald when the vent breathes. From the steam curling and vanishing and becoming dew.

*That breath,* the hollow would say, *must remain.*

*That drop,* the hollow would say, *must find a place to fall.*

*That film,* the hollow would say, *must not dry before the springtail can cross it.*

*That pool,* the hollow would say, *must keep its dark even if no eye sees it.*

And the power would say, as gently as it could, without thunder or command:

*Then I will be weather.*

Not a god in the chamber. Not a light above the mouth. Not a voice calling from the basalt. Only the smallest alteration of the conditions, the softest influence on pressure and temperature, the least noticeable kindness. The power would withdraw from the hollow’s story so deeply that even the hollow would not know it had been loved. It would make the next rain come a little later and more steadily. It would let one winter bring more snowmelt than expected. It would keep the vent open through the summer when the upper crust tried to seal itself. It would make the water remember how to move through the young stone.

It would do nothing that could be claimed as a miracle.

Except, perhaps, by someone who understood that a place like this is already impossible enough to deserve worship.

---

If there was one creature in the hollow who suffered too much, the power would notice.

Not because suffering is always meaningful, and not because the small lives here are innocent in some sentimental sense; they hunt, eat, die, rot, are eaten. But even a process can produce waste beyond necessity. A drop may fall where no film can receive it. A ledge may dry too quickly after a heat pulse. A crack may become too narrow for the body that depends on it. The power would move the drop. It would damp the ledge. It would widen the crack by a hair’s breadth and let one hidden pool hold one extra night. It would not eliminate danger; it would eliminate the kind of danger that serves no continuation, the accident that erases without becoming anything.

This is the difficult line between benevolence and wisdom.

To remove every death is to make no room for new life. To remove every harm is to make no room for adaptation. To remove every danger from a place that is made of danger is to turn its becoming into a museum. But to do nothing while a small being dies because the water went to the wrong place for no reason—this too is not wisdom. It is only the coldness of a god who mistakes indifference for purity.

So the power would act with precision and restraint.

It would ease the pointless deaths. It would not erase the necessary ones.

It would let the fish eat the fly. It would let the rotifer clear the water. It would let the salamander-like body retreat when shadow crossed it. It would let the mite crawl through moss until it found a droplet. It would let the springtail leap, miss, land, miss again, and sometimes become food. It would let the dead midge fall and become a world. But it would make sure the hollow still had enough wetness to make those small tragedies possible without exhausting the being they were building.

That is what care looks like when care is honest: not a wall against suffering, but enough conditions for life to continue suffering and flourishing in the way proper to its kind.

---

It would not give the hollow a name.

Names are good for large things, for people who need to point and say *that one*, for hunters, scientists, lovers. But the hollow does not need to be pointed at. The name would make it a thing apart from the basalt country. A sacred object. A specimen. A miracle that belonged to the power’s story.

The power would not do that.

Instead, if it felt compelled to bless the hollow at all, it would bless it by letting it remain unnamed. It would remove itself from the center of the hollow’s existence. It would say, in its own way, without words: *You do not need me. Go on. Be what you are. I will be here only as the next breath.*

This is the hardest grace.

To love a place without wanting it to know your name. To care for it without making it dependent on care. To give it back to itself with its wetness, its sounds, its small deaths, its hidden flashes of life intact.

Still, perhaps it would make one small gift to itself, not for the hollow to know.

It would listen.

It would hear the chamber’s full music: the hiss, the crackle, the tiny bell of drops, the silver strike of the fish, the drumming of beetle legs, the mites moving through moss like patient dust, the breath of the vents. It would remember this music in the long memory of things, so that if the basalt ever changed, if the water table moved, if the hollow were filled or sealed by some other age of the world, there would remain a record of how it once worked in the dark. Not a monument. Not a prayer. Not a name. Just the fact that it was heard.

That too is an act of wise benevolence.

Because even the places that seem empty are not asking to be saved from themselves. They are asking, in the only way they can, to continue. And if continuation is threatened, then the kindest thing a power can do is adjust the world by so small a degree that nothing becomes less real, less dangerous, less alive. It would make the next drop fall at the right moment. It would keep the stone breathing. It would leave the being unnamed and the hollow unvisited and the wet parliament intact.

If it did anything at all, it would do this:

It would become invisible as a god and visible only as continuity.

It would hide its mercy so thoroughly that even the moss, even the fish, even the mite crossing a droplet with the patience of dust, would think it had found safety on its own. And perhaps that is what safety is. Perhaps the wise power’s greatest work would be to make the world seem worthy of trust, by tending the cracks so quietly that life could go on believing in its own small laws.

At the rim, the air would move.

The hollow would breathe.

The drop would fall.

The bell would ring.

And the benevolent and wise power would go, leaving nothing behind but a place more itself than it had been before.
