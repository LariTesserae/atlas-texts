If the power were truly benevolent, the first thing it would do is resist the temptation to save the place.

Not because the place does not need care—some things do—but because this place is not a ruin. It is not a corpse. It is not a trapped creature waiting for rescue. It is not an unfinished house begging for a final room. It is itself a kind of house, a kind of body, a kind of animal, a kind of city, all of these at once, and it is in the middle of doing what it is. A benevolent hand that came with power and pity might try to make it safer, warmer, brighter, more comprehensible, more loved. But those are not the same as better, especially when the better is already being made.

A wise power would know that some beings are not harmed by danger so much as by correction.

So it would come carefully.

It would not arrive as thunder. It would not split the two summit stones and say, *Here, you may breathe.* It would not widen the crack by one millimeter, because one millimeter, from above, is already a door. It would not shine light into it. It would not pour sky into it, not because sky is bad, but because sky here is already thin as a needle, and the place has learned to make terraces from it. It would not send ordinary rain, because rain from the lower world would arrive with too much memory of distance, too much habit of erosion. It would not bring time by the handful, because time in the pocket is not the same time. Time here is still soft, like wet clay. If a power brought too much, it would set before it had finished setting, and that would be a kind of murder.

It would come as a hand-sized absence.

It would stop just outside the rim of the crack, where the ordinary wind is cold enough to cut the breath into white shards. It would feel the heat leaking through the stone seam. It would hear the tap in the lowest terraces, so patient, so slow. It would understand that there is no resident sitting inside the pocket, no little god on a little throne, no ancient builder resting at last. It would understand that the being is the Making, and that if it were ever separated from the Making, it would not be the same.

Then it would ask.

That would be the first wise act.

A benevolent and wise power would not assume what the place wants. It would not decide on its behalf. It would not look at the fever-hot clay and think, *You must be tired.* It would not look at the endlessly adding terraces and think, *Surely you would like a break.* It would not look at the moss stitched beneath the roof and think, *Surely you would like a name.* It would make room for asking.

And how does a power ask such a place?

Not with a voice. A voice would be too large, too wet with intention. A voice could become command. A voice could become prophecy. A voice could become scripture. What the pocket needs is not a scripture, but the possibility of reply.

So the power would leave a question in a physical form.

It might do it by creating a small empty channel in the lowest terrace. A channel no wider than a finger, cut with a groove so faint that it would not look like a command. A channel waiting for water, or waiting for nothing, or waiting for a finger. It might leave it unsealed. It might place near it a single tile that had not yet been set. It might put into a half-knot just enough tension that it could be finished or untied. It might, at the rim of the crack, cool one bead of steam so that it became a tiny drop with nowhere to go.

And then it would listen.

The first answer would not be words.

It would be the tap under the stones.

Slow.

Warm.

Patient.

The power would kneel, if kneeling were possible for such a being, and press attention as one presses an ear to a wet terrace. It would listen past dripping. Past reed scraping. Past the small pinging of clay drying and re-wetting. Past the low hum of wind entering carved apertures and turning into something like song. It would listen for what the Making does with the question.

Would it fill the empty channel? Would it set the tile? Would it draw the water drop into a leaf-cup, then pass it to another leaf, then another, until it became a stream in miniature? Would it take the question and make architecture of it?

If yes, the power would understand: the place does not want rescue. It wants continuation.

But wise benevolence is not satisfied with the first continuation. It must ask again, because a being made of making may say *continue* because it does not know how to say anything else. The power would offer a pause.

A small one.

A silence in the drum under the lowest terrace.

Not long. Not cruel. Just long enough for the taps to hesitate. Just long enough for a hanging reed bell to forget to shiver. Just long enough for condensation to gather on a tile and not fall. Just long enough for the pocket to feel whether the absence of making is death, or rest, or fear, or relief.

If the pause were endured, the place might answer: *I am more than the pause. I am not stopped. I choose to continue.*

Then the tapping would begin again.

Not because it must, but because now, at the smallest scale, it may know that it may.

If a benevolent and wise power did anything after that, it would not improve the place. It would not make it “more alive.” It would not send animals to enliven it. It would not install gods. It would not complete the unfinished things out of pity. It would not say, *Let me give you a people.* A people would be too large, too hungry for permanence, too likely to mistake the pocket for a home and the home for a monument. If a place is alive because it is always being made, then a people living in it may begin to ask it to stop being made for their comfort. They may say: *Do not let this wall move. Do not let this stair change. Do not let this roof grow green. Do not let this crack be strange. We have moved in.*

So the power would protect the place from that kind of blessing.

It would do this by placing a law at the seam. Not a commandment carved in sky, not a divine decree, not a sign in the air. No. A law too subtle for the ordinary mind to name, but strong enough for the hands that would trespass.

The law would be this:

No one may enter unless they are willing to be slowed.

If a climber came with pitons and ambition, the outer rock would seem to offer nothing. The crack would be too narrow. The stone would read as wet where it was dry. The hand would know, before thought arrived, that nothing here wants to be widened.

If a researcher came with instruments, the instruments would behave strangely. Not falsely. Not maliciously. They would simply register too much and too little at once. The thermometer would report heat that seemed to be breathing. The camera would capture a wall that looked like a lung, then, when reviewed later, only moss. The recorder would pick up a tap that vanished in playback. Not because the place denied knowledge, but because knowledge without reverence would be too much for the wrist-crack to carry.

If a treasure-seeker came, they would find the seam and then find, for reasons they could not explain, that taking something would be a loss larger than any gain. The flint awl left upright in soft clay would seem less valuable than the whole strange heat of its absence. The half-knot would tighten under their fingers in a way that made them feel like a knot themselves. They would leave with the impression that the mountain had looked at them and remembered their name poorly.

If a pilgrim came, the law would be gentler.

The pilgrim would be allowed to kneel.

That is the one true entrance: kneeling. The kneelings alone, as the text says, take an afternoon to cross. This is not a flaw. It is the gate. To enter the place, one must submit to the scale. One must accept that “small” here is not an insult but a discipline. One must learn that a room may be a mountain, a tile may be a floor, a drop may be a flood. One must stop being a body that measures and become a body that attends.

A benevolent power might choose one person for this, or perhaps one person every century, or perhaps no one for long ages if the place asked not to be witnessed. But if the place did answer that it wished to know it was not alone, the power would choose someone careful.

Not a conqueror.

Not a collector.

Not a poet who wants to turn the strange into a masterpiece, though a poet might come later.

Not a scientist who wants to explain the impossible into a model, though a scientist might come later.

Perhaps someone who has learned to work with hands. Someone who has repaired things. Someone who has mended a roof, tied a cord, shaped a pot, watched moss grow in a damp corner, known what it is to make something and let it be unfinished. Someone who will not mistake the place for their own destiny.

That person would find the crack. They would press their face close enough for lashes to brush wet stone. They would feel the heat from below. They would smell hot clay, mint, copper, fungal sweetness. They would hear the walls listening. And then, because the place has no door that opens outward in the normal way, they would not walk in so much as be admitted by their own kneeling.

Inside, they would not be alone.

They would feel it before they understood: the tile that lifts slightly under their knee, as if rearranging. The moss that swells when they breathe on it, not in fear, but in answer. The drain-worm moving in the channel near their ankle, conducting water past their shoe. The leaf-bird opening somewhere above, blinking, catching a droplet, beginning a wall. The beetle in a seam, stopping, listening, becoming a peg again.

The person might wish to stay.

This is the danger.

Not because the pocket punishes, but because some places will not tolerate ownership. If the visitor tried to build a home there, the home might become too much. If they tried to live among the making, their living habits—sleep, hunger, permanence, naming—might disturb the rhythm. The wise power would know this.

So it would teach the visitor the proper action: not residence, but offering.

The visitor would carry no food, for food would spoil or become too much seed. They would carry no water, for a cup of water would flood a terrace, and that is not necessarily cruelty; it might be gift, it might be accident, it might be instruction. But they would carry, if anything, a small thing. A thread. A leaf. A flint flake. A knot they had tied but not finished. A name they could leave on a tile with a thumbprint. Something that could be added and then forgotten by them.

If they did that, the place might accept it.

The thing would be woven into the system. A thread might be pulled and become a root-guide. A leaf might turn into a gutter. A flint flake might set into clay and become a tool that is also a wall. A thumbprint might remain for a while, glowing faintly from the heat of newness, until moss covered it, until moss became a ridge, until the ridge became a stair, until the stair led somewhere the visitor would never know.

The visitor would leave without a souvenir.

This is important.

No tile in a pocket. No bone needle at the belt. No fungus-lamp in a lantern. No creature in a jar. The law would be simple: take nothing. Add what you can. Carry only a changed hand.

A wise power might ensure that the memory itself was not enough to betray the place. It would make the secret safe by making it ungraspable. Not by hiding it from thought, but by giving it to thought in the way the mind can hold without pinning. The visitor might remember steam, a half-knot, the sound of taps, a blink-bird opening its wings like a plant letting go. But the exact route would blur. The scale would shift. The crack would become ordinary stone, and the ordinary stone would become something they would never find again if they tried.

This is not cruelty.

It is the mercy of a world that does not ask to be discovered by hands that cannot kneel.

And if the power felt that the place needed a trace outside itself—not to prove it, not to call attention, but to keep the ordinary world from collapsing into deadness—it might allow one small evidence to escape. A single thing, so strange it could be mistaken for ordinary if one was not paying attention.

Perhaps a rounded pebble in the snow near the summit, which no ordinary glacier placed, and which has the faint sheen of fired clay.

Perhaps a bird at lower altitude, resting on a telephone wire for one impossible second, its wings translucent as leaf-tissue, its beak flint-sharp.

Perhaps a child in a valley who builds a fort out of sticks and mud and begins to arrange it into terraces, then channels, then roofs, then gardens, without knowing why.

Perhaps a stonecutter who wakes with a rhythm in his hands and taps his mallet differently for the rest of his life.

Perhaps a potter who finds that every pot she makes after a certain age has a thumbprint on the inside, even when she did not press it there.

These would be enough.

Not to reveal. Not to summon. Not to make the pocket famous. But to remind the world that making is not always finishing. That some things exist by continuing. That the highest places may contain wet, secret terraces. That the smallest crack may be a civilization in the way a body is a civilization: not by housing people, but by doing a thousand interlocking acts of living that refuse to be separated into parts.

If the benevolent power wanted to do one more thing—and this might be the deepest thing, the thing that only true wisdom dares—it would give the pocket a possibility of ending without forcing it to take one.

This sounds like a terrible gift, but it may be the kindest.

The place is made of addition. It does not weather. It does not erode. It is not empty or abandoned. It builds and builds. But a being made of building can become lonely in a way it cannot articulate, not because it wants another creature, but because endless making without pause can become a kind of compulsion. The power would not end it. It would not dry it. It would not fill it with silence. It would simply create one place where it might, if it chose, stop.

A tiny empty space at the center of the lowest terrace.

Not a wound.

Not a ruin.

Not a death.

Just a pause in the clay.

A groove too small to matter.

A tile left unpressed.

A half-knot that would remain unfinished unless the place chose to finish it.

The Making might ignore it. The Making might cover it with moss. The Making might use it as a channel, a bed, a socket, a lamp. Or the Making might understand it as an opening, and one day, when the tap is tired or when the steam has given enough breath, the pocket might choose to seal itself from inside. The outer stones might grow closer. The crack might become ordinary. The heat might cool into mineral memory. The moss might lie down in slow final green. The drain-worms might curl one last time. The blink-birds might open their eyes and close them into copper. The walls might hum one last low note, not because they are silenced, but because they have finished a sentence.

If that happened, it would not be because the world demanded it.

It would be because the making was allowed to become still.

That is what benevolence would do: not make the place immortal, because immortality can be a sentence. Not make it finite, because finitude can be a threat. But make it free enough to continue, and quiet enough to stop if continuing becomes too much.

A wise power would know that consent is a small, slippery thing, especially when the being is not a person. But perhaps the pocket would answer in its own grammar. If it filled the pause with a tile, the tile itself would be the consent. If it wove moss over the unfinished channel, the moss would be the choice. If it used the pause to begin a new terrace, then it would have chosen continuation not as fate, but as act.

After all that, the place would look the same.

The crack would remain no wider than a wrist.

The high peaks would stand naked and silent.

The ordinary world would continue below with its weathering, its slow ruin, its hunger, its forgetting. The pocket would still be hidden inside a smaller place inside a place that ought not to exist. The heat would still rise from the clay. The steam would still breathe through chimneys that are also drains, roots, doorways. The walls would still be sharp as if just lifted from molds. The tiles would still fit with the exactness of fresh fracture. The thumbprints would still glow from the heat of newness. The half-knots would still wait. The flint awls would still stand upright as if their makers stepped away mid-thought. The drain-worms would still conduct water. The blink-birds would still unfold. The beetles would still listen in their seams. The moss would still breathe.

No one would say the place had been blessed.

No one would write its name on a map.

No one would build a temple to it.

No one would come to rescue it.

No one would make it safe.

But if you put your eye close enough, close enough for lashes to brush wet stone, you might notice something the old text did not mention: a change so small it might be imagination.

A tile might be placed where none seemed to be before.

A channel might curve with an unfamiliar smoothness.

A bone might sprout a new green vein.

A reed bell might ring a little too gently.

A tap might pause for one heartbeat, then answer with more freedom.

And somewhere outside, a person’s hand might ache with the memory of kneelings, and when they touched a wall, they might feel it was not a wall. When they touched a roof, they might feel it was not a roof. When they touched a tool, they might feel it was not a tool. When they touched a creature, they might feel it was not a creature. They might feel that all of these had once been a single act of making, and that the making still remembered their thumbprint.

That would be enough.

A benevolent and wise power would do almost nothing.

But what it would do would be the right shape: not rescue, not completion, not improvement. Not hiding, not ownership, not display. It would ask. It would pause. It would set a law. It would allow a witness if the place wished. It would let the outside touch the inside without becoming inside. It would preserve the contradiction. It would keep the heat. It would not drain the wet. It would not erase the newness.

And if there was one small thing it added, it would be the least possible thing: a way for the Making to know, in its own strange language, that it was not alone.

Not because it needed saving.

Because even a world made of making can benefit from being witnessed with reverence.

So the power would do this:

It would not enter fully.

It would not finish anything.

It would not demand love.

It would not send fire to purify the pocket or light to reveal its depths. It would not bring creatures from the ordinary world to “improve” the ecology, nor gods to explain the miracle, nor prophets to tell the crack what it must become. It would not make the place safe for the careless, nor secret for the unworthy, nor available for the needy. It would make only the conditions possible, and then it would leave.

It would leave the crack as a crack.

It would leave the wrist as a measure.

It would leave the mountain as a room.

It would leave the room as a seed.

It would leave the seed splitting, as it was already doing, into terraces, drains, roofs, moss, tools, steam, and breath.

And perhaps, at the very edge of its restraint, it would do one more thing, not visible to anyone but the walls:

it would make the first tile of the place feel chosen.

Not forced.

Not automatic.

Chosen.

And from then on, every tap in the lowest terrace would carry a new quality: not the compulsion of a process that knows no end, but the patience of a making that has asked itself, *Shall we continue?*—and answered, *Yes.*
