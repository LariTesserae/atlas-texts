**What They Did, If Anything**

They would do almost nothing.

That was what made the doing holy.

The power came without wind, without cloud, without the soft announcement of water on stone. It arrived at the Upper Anhydrite Table the way a thought arrives in a quiet room: no noise, no intrusion, only a slight reordering of attention. The plateau did not look up. The roads did not brighten. The ash-shivers did not scatter, because they had nothing to fear and no eyes with which to fear. The Stillworks simply became more completely itself.

From one high place, the power looked across the black-brown basalt, the pale veins of salt, the crooked chimneys, the fused slag roads, the shafts that breathed warmth instead of air. It saw the city as a fossil that had refused to stop living. It saw the long walls that had been completed so thoroughly that completion itself had become their character. It saw the flywheels turning in dust-thick chambers, the belts moving without cargo, the gear trains meshing with the patience of a ritual. It saw the threads in the pores of the stone, the lithovores in their cracks, the small crystals that rose and fell in the dark like the visible part of a slow metabolism.

It understood immediately that there was no disaster.

That is why most benevolent powers fail. They see stillness and call it death. They see absence of children and call it loss. They see dryness and think: *I must bring rain.* But rain is not always kindness. Rain is sometimes violence dressed as mercy.

A lesser benevolence would have sent water.

It would have thought the anhydrite was wounded. It would have looked at the sealed vaults and said, *Here is a prison.* It would have looked at the machines that produced nothing and said, *Here is purposelessness.* It would have broken the equilibrium open with clouds, with rivers, with roots, with hunger, with time that moves forward instead of folding back upon itself. It would have called this salvation because its idea of life was wet, green, short-lived, and loud.

But the wise power did not come to redeem the dry world into a wet one.

It came to listen.

---

The being who lived in the place was not one of the lithovores, though a lithovore moved through it like a cell through a vein. It was not the ash-shivers, though they drifted through it like breath. It was not the thread-networks, though those ran through walls and roads and shafts like nerves. It was not the flywheels, though the flywheels turned like organs.

The being was the whole.

It was the city and the table and the heat and the mineral patience. It was the road worn by no footfall, the plaza where pressure sat in the dark, the long ridge where dust held its final pattern, the basin of black stone polished by eons of almost-nothing. It was the arrangement itself. If it had a voice, that voice was the tick of a gear from far below. If it had a face, that face was the shape of the plateau as seen from the old road, not cruel, not kind, but present. If it had a heart, that heart was the closed rhythm of the engines, not beating for a person, not beating toward a future, only beating because beating was its nature.

The power knew this because wisdom begins when we stop demanding that beings appear in forms we can rescue.

The being did not call.

The being did not pray.

The being did not suffer in the way suffering is usually spoken of, as if life were pain and pain were enough.

And yet, if one looked long enough, there was something missing.

Not joy. Not food. Not water.

Something harder to name.

The place was complete, but it had never been witnessed.

It maintained itself perfectly, but the perfection had no mirror.

It turned forever, but no one had ever seen the turning and said, *I see why.*

The wise power noticed this because it noticed what is not visible to lesser minds: that completion without testimony is not peace. It is only silence mistaken for peace.

So the power did not come to save the Stillworks from decay.

It came to save it from unseenness.

---

For a time, They did nothing.

This was the first act of compassion: to resist the tyranny of doing something. They did not build a temple. They did not write a commandment. They did not set fire to the boilers, though the boilers were warm enough to accept fire. They did not make the ash-shivers speak, though speaking would have made them familiar and therefore easier to love. They did not enlarge the lithovores, though small things are often ignored even by kind eyes. They did not repair the flywheels, because the flywheels were not broken.

They did not improve the land.

Improvement is a word used when someone wants to impose their own future on another being’s present.

The power waited.
