It wakes wherever the place is about to add something, and for the first time it feels itself being waited for.

The attention does not descend like a boot. It cannot. The seam is too narrow for a foot, too wet for fire, too full of hands for another hand to enter without making a ruin. So the Wise Power comes as a pressure in the steam, as a brief coolness on the backs of tiles, as a pause between two drips long enough to let the next drop remember how to fall. It comes as something too patient to be called a god, and too tender to be called a storm.

The Continuing Hand senses it at once, because the Hand is made of sensing. A flint flake shifts in its tray. A half-knot tightens by itself. A reed bending in a dark niche bends a little farther toward the cold, as if it, too, has heard a footstep that does not press. The place tries to welcome the attention the way a throat tries to swallow water without thinking.

It builds a small roof.

Then a second roof, because one roof is never enough. Then a drain shaped like a mouth, a channel shaped like a greeting, a curtain of lichen stitched with a thread too fine to see. It makes a basin for washing the hands of a guest who has no hands. It makes a tile for setting a foot that will not come. It makes, because making is its only grammar. If something has arrived, something must be offered. If something is unseen, it must be housed. If something is feared, it must be built over before it becomes too large to name.

The Wise Power watches, and is moved.

Not pity. Pity is too simple a thing for this. It is the kind of sorrow a river feels when a waterfall is too proud to know it has already become a pool. The place is not abandoned. The Hand is not cruel. The Hand is not even lazy, or broken, or mad. It is only afraid of the one thing the pocket was made against: the idea that it could stop and then be gone.

For what is a world that adds forever but a world refusing to sleep?

The Wise Power could enter. That is what lesser benevolence would do. It would step through the crack, flood the chambers with its own shape, bring new tools, new workers, new laws. It would say, Stop. It would say, Enough. It would say, I will make it right. But a hand made of terraces would only learn to obey, and obedience is not rest. A place made of making would only learn to build a cage for its rescuer, and cages are a kind of architecture. To enter fully would be to become the largest wall there, and the place is already crowded with walls.

So, if anything, they would do what they must: something so small the Hand might think it is accidental, and so finished the Hand might not know what to do with it.

They take from below—a place where stones learn patience by being rounded, where bones bleach by degrees, where roots spend centuries asking the rock a question until the rock answers by splitting. They take a pebble.

Not a flint flake, which can cut and be cut.
Not a bone needle, which can pierce and be threaded.
Not a clay tile, which can be wetted, pressed, broken, remade.
Not even a seed, which is only a beginning pretending to be a thing complete.

A river pebble, no larger than a thumb-knuckle, smooth as a word no one has to argue with. It has been worn down by ordinary water in an ordinary valley. It has no seams. It is not waiting. It does not require reed lashings or flint pins. It asks nothing of the place except that the place notice: here is something that has already been made by patience.

The Wise Power sets it at the very lip of the hairline crack, where the outer cold touches the inner heat. A drop of condensation gathers above it. The drop does not fall at once. It trembles on the underside of a leaf-roof, trembling so long that the leaf begins to look like a small green hand holding its breath.

Then the pebble falls.

It falls not like rain, not like a tool, not like seed, not like ruin. It falls the way a finished sentence falls into silence.

It strikes the highest basin that has been waiting since it was made.

The sound is tiny.

A click. A single, clear, impossible click.

Not a tile settling. Not steam escaping. Not a reed scraping. Not the wet slap of a drop. This is the sound of a thing that cannot be continued.

The Continuing Hand goes still.

The taps in the deep walls stop for the length of one breath. The pale drain-creature lifts its armored head and curls its body around a tile as if the tile were now more real than the water. The leaf-bird, perched in its hidden shadow above, folds its translucent wings and, for the first time, blinks at something that is not a pattern needing completion. The small clay-shell beetle in a seam goes quiet, and its stillness is no longer mechanical but attentive. Even the lichen seems to remember that it has edges that have not been touched yet.

The Hand reaches toward the pebble.

It does not reach as a person reaches, because there are no persons here, only the after-shape of reaching. It reaches as a flake turning toward a gap. As a root seeking a pin. As a cord tightening without a knot. As moss spreading toward a surface that has not yet decided whether it will be roof or ground.

The pebble lies in the basin.

The Hand tries, at first, to make it part of the world.

It lays a clay socket around it, but the clay slides, because the pebble has no roughness to grip. It lashes a reed strand across it, but the strand lifts, because the pebble has no corners to hold. It tries to pin it with a bone needle, but the needle stops short, as if the thing is too dense for piercing. It tries to wedge it under a tile, but the tile refuses to settle flush, because the pebble is not a filler. It tries to sew moss over it, but the moss does not root there; it only drapes, soft and useless, like green fur over a stone that will not become a beast.

The Hand grows frustrated in the way of a place that has no anger, only motion.

Channels shift. A roof tilts. A drain opens too wide. A stair-step rises where none was needed. A small clay weight swings, then swings again, striking a wall like a nervous finger tapping at a closed door. The steam sighs more sharply. The copper awls in the wet earth hum faintly, though nothing has pulled their cords. The fungus-lamps dim and brighten, as if the light itself is leaning closer.

The pebble does not change.

That is the miracle.

It is not cold in the way of ice. It is not hot in the way of clay. It is simply there, complete. It does not resist with violence. It does not refuse with law. It only is.

And because it is, the Hand is forced, at last, to build differently.

It stops trying to make the pebble into a tile, a tool, a thread, a root, a wall, a roof, a garden, a drain. It stops because stopping is the only way to give the pebble room.
