They needed a place where the sun could be invited, briefly, into the stone; where a single angle of brightness could warm one square hand of tuff by less than a degree; where that almost-invisible warmth could draw a film of condensation from the air, gather a bead, release it, and let it fall back into the dark damp below. These are hearths, but the fire is weather. The hearths are not ruined. They are still functioning, if one accepts a definition of function in which heat is slow, fuel is light, and smoke is only the faint mineral shimmer rising when the clouds leave.

In the mornings, when the basin is too cold for the wind to speak fully, the mats gather around the glass-rimmed pits. Not in a crowd. Not in a line. In a manner closer to how leaves might lean toward a window if the window were the only thing remembering warmth. A kneeling figure, if one can call it a figure, will press its layered edge against the outer shard. Its brine joints soften. The rust-colored slime in its faceless head spreads a little across the stone, drinking the faint chemical signal of warmed dust. For one slow minute the figure is almost a person. Then the sun moves on. The shard stops singing. The figure becomes a mat.

This is how the place knows itself.

A benevolent and wise power would understand this quickly. Not because it is clever, though it would be clever. Not because it can see far, though it can see far. But because benevolence, if it is wise, is mostly patience, and patience is what the basin has already learned.

It would not come down from the sky in thunder.

It would not make itself a statue in the pass or a voice in the cairns or a light above the glass. Any of those would be a disaster. A light above the basin might be welcome, but it would also become a command. A voice might become a law. A statue might become an ancestor, and ancestors, once made visible, begin to demand the impossible: recognition, permanence, continuity of self. The Crust-Tenders, the Salt-Kneelers, the Basinfolk—call them as you like—do not live by permanence. They live by recurrence. They live by rising and folding. If a god stood there, they would forget how to become weather and begin to become believers.

That would be a terrible kindness.

So the wise power would consider what it could give.

It might think: let me make them plants. Let me give them mosses, grasses, lichens, roots, greenness. That would be simple for a human imagination. Life should be green; life should grow upward; life should have leaves. But the power would look again and see that plants would not make them richer. They would make the basin a battlefield. A root would tear the mineral threads. A flower would demand pollinators, or else rot uselessly. Grass would bring animals or neglect, and animals would bring dung and predation and migration and hunger. The whole chemical metabolism that has kept running for centuries, patient as a heartbeat under snow, would be forced into a new economy. The rust-slime would be crowded out. The glass shards would be buried in humus. The pale mats would lose the sharpness of their hunger and their discipline.

No.

It might think: let me give them animals. A small bird, perhaps, or a beetle, or some high-altitude creature that could pass through without disturbing the stones. But animals would eat them. Or ignore them. Or die there, leaving bones and decay and a new kind of smell that the basin has no language for. Animals would bring movement that is not mineral movement, appetite that is not chemical appetite. The Crust-Tenders do not chase. They do not flee. They do not defend. To introduce an animal would be to introduce an ending they cannot understand.

No.

It might think: let me give them fire. They have hearths. Surely they once had fire, or wished for it. But no ash remains because ash would be the wrong kind of memory. Fire would be too loud, too fast, too final. It would dry the mats. It would crack the glass. It would burn the rust-colonies and leave the basin blackened with the human kind of ruin. Fire changes a place from a pattern into a wound. The basin has not been wounded by time. It has been edited by time, the way light is edited through a lens, the way sound is edited through a narrow opening. Fire would edit differently, and not gently.

No.

It might think: let me give them water. There is so little water, and they are made almost entirely of the balance between dampness and dryness. A river would be a blessing to many forms of life. But here, too much water would drown them. A pool that lasted too long would soften the mats beyond endurance. A stream would wash away the salt notation. A glacier would erase the cairns, the tools, the slow habits of the ground. They live in the pause between wet and dry. To flood that pause would be to love the pause away.

No.

It might think: let me give them speech.

But speech, as humans know it, is dangerous. It creates the idea that one can stand outside the world and name it. It creates the loneliness of being a word in a mouth that cannot always find the right sound. The Crust-Tenders do not speak, but they are not mute. They talk in tension and expansion. They converse in the lifting of fiber, the settling of salt, the faint bell tone of wind over glass. Their language is not hidden from them. It is the weather. It is the ground itself becoming legible through slow change. If the wise power gave them human speech, they would lose half their vocabulary instantly. If it gave them language as a separate gift, it would teach them to be dissatisfied with what they already are.

No.

So what would it do, if anything?

It would do the smallest thing that could still be called a grace.

It would come to the basin not as a visitor, but as a slight alteration in the way possibilities are arranged.

It would slow one cloud.

Not many clouds. Only one, in the season when the mats have folded too deeply and the dust is beginning to taste like absence. It would rest that cloud above the stone teeth for a little longer than the mountain wind can allow. Not enough to flood the basin. Not enough to drown the kneeling figures. Not enough to turn the place into a wetland. Just enough to let the cold mist drip down from the north-facing ledges in a slow, almost imperceptible rhythm. Three nights. Maybe four. Then the sun would return.

The first thing the cloud would do is make the mats listen.

Fine silica fibers would lift from cracks where they had lain white and brittle for weeks. Tiny droplets would gather on their tips like impossible dew. The whole lower slope would gleam as if the mountain had remembered how to be wet without becoming soft. The rust-colored slime would move along stone edges, not quickly, but in a chemical rush, as though reading a long message in iron and acid. Brine pools would tremble with new bubbles. The bubbles would burst. Salt rings would form. And these rings would not be only rings.
