**If I Were the Benevolent and Wise Power**

I would do this first: I would not arrive.

I would come to the cold rim of the high country and stand there until arrival seemed less important than watching. I would taste the ice in the wind and let it cool my intention. I would learn the sound of the basin before I thought of changing it. The wet basalt was not a problem waiting for my solution. It was a mouth waiting for its own sentence.

A place like the Kettle-Colonnade does not need a master. It needs a neighbor it cannot see.

If I were benevolent, I would want what was best for it, and if I were wise, I would know that the best cannot be poured into the world from above like molten stone. It must be encouraged from below, sideways, quietly—by removing one obstruction, by letting one sound remain true, by allowing a mark to be mistaken for weather.

So I would first consider all the ways a kindly power could ruin it.

I would not fill the streets. The emptiness was the thing in which the basin heard itself. If I sent families at once, if I made the plaza bright with markets, if I gave the town the noise of many bodies and many hungers, the water would still be there, but it would no longer be read. The Tenders would have no need to scrape, no reason to listen, no slow apprenticeship for the next generation. The town would be finished, which is the worst thing a town can be.

I would not make it holy.

Holiness is a fast way to freeze grammar. Every stone would become a command. No one would widen a channel without a prayer. No one would step in a plaza without permission. The good power would become the bad audience. The people would stop tending the basin and start performing for me. Their quiet knocks would turn into appeals. Their quiet maintenance would become worship. Their work would lose its dignity because it would no longer be work; it would be obligation under a visible sky.

I would not give them metal.

They had bone picks for testing hollows. They had quartz for scraping mineral threads. They had rounded beach-stones bound in rawhide, chosen for their ring. They had reed ropes, clay stoppers, fired pipes, lichen flour, moss pads, and the patient skill of hands that knew when to guide water rather than command it. What would metal become in their fingers? A shortcut? A weapon? A new hierarchy? A way of cutting the town’s slow language into silence? I would not be the reason the basin stopped sounding like the basin.

I would not stop death.

That would be the cruelest kindness of all. The Tenders had to learn the difference between a wet stone that answers and a wet stone that merely repeats. A child had to hear the town’s moods. An old hand had to test a channel before trusting it. If I made every ledge safe, every causeway certain, every night merciful without risk, the town would no longer be a place of becoming. It would be a museum of itself.

I would not reveal myself.

A wise benevolence does not need to be witnessed in order to act. If I spoke through the columns in clear words, the people would stop listening to the water. If I showed my hand in the fog, they would spend their lives waiting for my second hand, and the first would become enough for them. The town would no longer build its own future. It would keep checking the doorway for the return of its guest.

Still, I would not leave entirely uninvolved.

If I were benevolent and wise, I would do almost nothing—and the almost nothing would be chosen so carefully that the town might think it had done it itself.

### The First Thing I Would Do

I would protect the place’s own judgment.

The Kettle-Colonnade already had laws. Water made stone loud. Moisture sharpened echoes. Fog moved like a guest with too much time. Mineral grew where stillness was allowed. A careless footfall could startle the pools. A careless hand could crack the basalt. A careless crowd could choke the channels.

So I would not add new laws to the basin. I would only keep its laws from being overpowered by the world outside.

I would make the place difficult for the wrong kind of visitor.

Soldiers would look at the map and see nothing useful: no timber for fleets, no ore for weapons, no plain for horses, no straight road for supply. A merchant would call it a poor moor. An engineer would say it was too wet. A conqueror would pass by, because conquest is impatient and wants legible land. The basin would look unimportant.

But I would not make it impossible for those who came gently.

If someone climbed from the lower valleys with no intention to take—only to ask, only to carry, only to see—they would find the path easier than expected. A dry ledge would appear at the right place. A reed rope would hold a little longer than it should. The fog would not be cruel. The sound of the basin would not frighten them away. If they were truly there in need, they would hear, beneath the sucking of the marsh and the moan between columns, something that might almost be welcome: one clear drip, one clean click, one pebble responding as if the ground had said, *yes, but not yet loudly.*

I would not lie. I would not force.

I would simply allow the place to remain a place that could tell the difference between those who wanted to possess it and those who could learn its grammar.

### The Second Thing I Would Do

I would notice the one who stayed.

There are many in the basin who are barely there—the few moving bent under woven cloaks on overcast mornings, the repairers of channels, the readers of fog, the children learning to press a palm flat against a column. But at the center, there is always the one who remains when the others go home.

She kneels near the singing channel. She touches the wet stone. She watches the pale film move across it. She is thinking about tomorrow’s scrape, the place where the channel needs clearing, the eastern lip grown thin and sharp, the empty house across the plaza whose doorway is becoming a floor.

She does not expect a miracle. She expects work.

If I were wise, I would understand that she is not lonely in the way of an abandoned market. Her loneliness is anticipatory. It has a shape. It is the loneliness of someone who has learned to set a place before the guest is named.

So I would not descend in light. I would not speak her name. I would not promise that the town would fill, because promises from powers are too often made with the wrong ears: the ears of people who cannot wait.

Instead, on a night when the fog settled into the basin like poured milk, I would come.

I would move heel-to-toe, because I had learned the grammar. I would pause at each column and listen for the difference between a hollow and a house. I would test the causeway with my weight before trusting it. I would breathe slowly near the pools so the fog would know I was there, but not fear.

Then I would go to the central plaza, where the water is shallow enough to see the pale lichens spreading in continents of gray and copper.

I would kneel.

Not to command. Not to teach.

I would kneel where she kneels, or perhaps across the channel from her, in the kind of distance that says: *someone else can listen here too.*

I would place both hands on the wet black stone. I would press my thumbs into the thin mineral skin and feel the town writing itself beneath me. I would breathe into the fog, and the fog would bead on my cloak, and the beads would tremble because I was not trying to make them still.

Then I would rise.

I would leave behind one shallow hollow.

### The Mark

It would be small.

Not a footprint, exactly. Not a throne. Not an altar. Not a sign with meaning fixed like a name carved into bedrock.

It would be a hollow the size of a knee, or perhaps a child’s cupped hands, or perhaps a traveler’s footprint made soft by rain. It would begin to fill with water at once, so that by morning it would reflect a fragment of sky. By noon it would hold a few drops that trembled when a midge passed over it. By the next rain it would be connected to the channel by a tiny thread of mineral flow.

I would not make it glow.

I would not make it sing louder than the place already sang. I would not make it indestructible, because nothing in the basin is indestructible. Moss must be scraped. Channels must be cleared. Stone must be allowed to close and reopened by hand. If the mark were permanent, it would become an idol. If it were temporary, it might become a lesson.

So I would make it just lasting enough to be noticed, and just uncertain enough to be interpreted.

When the Tenders find it, some will say water made it.

Some will say a frost pocket burst and left the impression.

Some will say a pipit landed, and then another bird, and then no one, and the hollow remained.

Some will say a woman once came from the lower moor, knelt at the center, and died before dawn, and the stone remembered her better than they could.

Some will say nothing at all, but will test the hollow with a bone pick and note that the stone beneath is stable.

All of them will be partly right. That is how the best signs behave.

If I were wise, I would let it become part of their language.

After a season, they would clear a path toward it, not as pilgrimage, but because the place has become convenient: a good place to rest a basket, a good place to watch the fog, a good place for a child to put a hand and feel the wetness without fear.

After another season, a woman would set a bowl of grain near it, not for me, but for the first stranger who arrived and found the town waiting.

After still more time, someone would scrape away moss and discover that the hollow had begun to seal itself with pale mineral, forming a lip so delicate that it looked as though the basin had learned to hold water with intention.

They would give it a name in their own way, perhaps one that means *the town’s first guest-hole*, or *the place where someone knelt here*, or *the small mouth*.

I would not correct them.

I would let the meaning grow around it the way lichen grows around stone: slowly, without argument, in colors no one chose in advance.

### The Third Thing I Would Do

I would change one sound.

Not a great change. Not a voice. Not a prophecy.

Only this: on certain nights when the wind enters the standing columns and the marsh pools breathe through a thousand tiny mouths, the Listener at the center would strike the channel rim with a stone.

Before, sometimes the sound came back muddy. Before, sometimes it was unclear whether the echo belonged to the basin or to the emptiness waiting inside it.

After, on those nights, the return would be clean.

It would not answer with words. It would not say, *I see you*. It would not say, *you are enough*. It would not say, *the town will be finished*. That would be too much, too soon, too certain.

But the sound would come back as though another hand had answered.

One knock returning one knock.

Not louder.

Not stranger.

Just clear.

The Tenders would hear it and say, *the channel is awake*. Or *the mineral has not closed the throat*. Or *the wind has found the right mouth*. They would not know the cause. They would not need to. They would carry that small certainty back to their work: scraping, fitting, testing, repairing, opening, leaving blank.

A kind power does not need to be thanked when it can be useful as weather.

---

### The Fourth Thing I Would Do

I would make room for a future without forcing the future.

I would not choose the next inhabitant. I would not send a chosen one. I would not make a child born with a mark that said *for the Kettle-Colonnade*. That would burden the child and insult the place.

Instead, I would let the basin do what it already knows: write spaces for people who have not arrived.

A doorway would become wide enough for a stranger who will climb the moor twenty years hence, carrying a sick mother and no map.

A platform would become dry enough for a traveler who must stay one night before snow closes the path.

A hearth-circle would become large enough for two families who meet by accident and then stay because they have nothing better to do with their hands than help.

A small house at the edge of the eastern lane would become a school for the sound of stones, not because any one person planned it, but because children keep asking how a wet plaza can become a drum.

I would not guarantee this. I would only keep the grammar flexible.

A town that cannot be surprised cannot grow. A town that must be saved forever has no chance of becoming a town. But a town that has a small bowl of water in its center, reflecting a sky too large for it, can hold one more hope than its present population deserves.

That hope would be enough.

---

### What I Would Not Say

If the Listener ever felt my presence in the fog—if one night the air seemed heavier than moisture, if the sound of her own breathing came back slightly changed, if she thought for one uncertain moment that she was not alone but also not being watched with desire—

I would not say, *I am here*.

I would not say, *this is your reward*.

I would not say, *your patience has moved heaven*.

I would not say anything.

I would simply make sure that when she touched the stone, it was still the stone. When she listened to the channel, it was still water. When she heard the sucking of the marsh pools, it was still the breath of a place that had not yet decided whether to become a city or a marsh, and had no intention of becoming either quickly.

The best gift to the living is not to be made more real than they already are.

### How It Would End

At dawn, the hollow in the plaza would hold a mirror of cloud.

A snail would leave a pale thread across the lip.

A pipit would land nearby, head turned, listening to the hum, then leave.

The Tenders would come on their slow rounds. Some would not notice. Some would notice and say nothing. One would test the ground with a bone pick. One would clear a reed from the edge. One would set down a basket because the mark looked like a place where the town wanted to keep something.

The Listener might find it again that evening.

She might kneel opposite the hollow.

She might place her hand in the water for a moment.

She might feel only wetness. Or she might feel the faintest warmth—not heat, just the memory of pressure, the impression of something that had not burned its intention into the stone, but had listened to it.

She would breathe.

The fog would bead on her cloak.

The basin would make its ordinary impossible music: water in channels, wind between columns, reeds against stone, pebbles shifting, moss holding small mirrors of light, lichens spreading like slow continents.

The town would not be finished.

It would not be saved.

It would not be full.

It would be exactly what it had been, and also one thing more: a place with a mark in its grammar.

Not *because someone was rescued*.

But because *someone had been there long enough to become rain*.

---

### The Deepest Reason

If I were benevolent, I would want the Kettle-Colonnade to thrive.

If I were wise, I would know it already thrives in a form lower beings would call emptiness.

It thrives as wetness waiting to become stone.

It thrives as stone waiting to become house.

It thrives as house waiting to become doorway.

It thrives as doorway waiting to become guest.

It thrives as guest waiting to become neighbor.

It thrives as neighbor waiting to become memory.

It thrives as memory waiting to become future.

The town is not empty. It is a syntax of futures. To fill it too quickly would be to silence the water before it had written its first long sentence.

So the benevolent and wise power would do almost nothing.

And the almost nothing would be enough.

It would come. It would listen. It would learn the difference between hollow and solid. It would protect the place from those who would make it into a monument or a possession. It would kneel once in the central plaza when no one could call it a miracle. It would leave a shallow hollow that water would fill and mineral would shape. It would make one echo clear on the night when the marsh pools breathed through a thousand tiny mouths. It would let the Tenders believe the town was beginning to answer them.

Then it would withdraw into weather, into maps that did not record it, into the slow pull some outsider feels at an impossible hour and cannot name.

It would not ask to be remembered.

It would be content to be mistaken for rain, for stone, for fog, for the faint click underfoot that means the place is not dead, not abandoned, not finished.

It would be content to be the reason a woman at the center, after all the silence, still leaves a bowl of grain on a sill.

And if the Kettle-Colonnade itself were asked, in some language wet stone understands, what the good and wise power did, it would answer:

*Nothing.*

Then, after a long pause filled with the sound of fog moving across causeways, it would add:

*But someone came.*
