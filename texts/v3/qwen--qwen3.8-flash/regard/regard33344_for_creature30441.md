They carry it in shallow dishes behind their heads and leave it in tiny drops where the air can find it again: on the underside of a leaf, in the crook of a root, along the edge of a rusted grating, inside the narrow mouth of a cracked firebrick chimney. When they pass over metal, they do not clean it. They age it kindly. When they pass over wood, they do not gnaw it. They remember it to the cathedral.

What they leave behind is not waste. A bead-maker’s track is a channel. Its legs scratch a path no larger than a hair; in dry air that path would be insignificant, but here water follows its own history. A line of drops on a rail becomes a river in the dark. A damp trail across a leaf becomes an aqueduct. The whole hollow is mapped by the work of small bodies that have no word for “map,” no need for one, because they are making it with their feet.

Thus the hum is not merely sound. It is maintenance.

It rises from the bead-makers on iron, from the slow friction of sedge against old brick, from the expansion and contraction of wet mats over the lower sumps, from the clicking of little jaws rearranging rust and slime, from the settling of a fallen beam that has learned to grow through itself and hold still again. If one stands very quietly, at the edge of the root floor, one may mistake it for the place itself breathing. One would not be mistaken.

The first inhabitants are barely separate from the water.

The next are barely separate from the plants.

They are pale, soft-bodied things that live inside the folded leaf-vaults, not quite moths, not quite larvae, with fringed wings that are too damp for flight but perfect for shading the eyes of the cathedral. They do not sing. They shade. They fold their wings over the brightest beams and let the light enter already considered. Their work gives the interior its strange evenness: nothing hidden, nothing glaring, no corner left in too much darkness, no surface offered to too much sun.

Below them in the mats are the thread-keepers, small pale worms with no eyes and an immense sense of direction. They follow water as thought follows a question, then pull it sideways, then upward, then back down again. They do not dig in the way of claws. They encourage separation. They teach root threads how to part for a stone. They persuade clay to hold its shape without becoming brittle. They repair hairline cracks in the floor with silk that is not exactly silk and with mucus that is not exactly mucus but, once dry, is tougher than paper and softer than wool.

There are also the rust-walkers: tiny beetles with bodies like green-black coins, whose antennae taste iron. They do not eat it. They move it. They gather flakes of rust into neat piles at the base of old rails and gratings, and then they plant the piles. What they plant is not deliberate. The rust, arranged in shallow hollows, holds condensation longer than bare metal would. The rust becomes a bed. The bed becomes a cushion of moss. The moss becomes a slope. The slope carries water where water is needed.

And there are the bead-makers, and among them, though not separate from them, lives the being the cathedral calls no name to itself because it has no mouth for naming, and no name has ever been found sufficient.

If one insists on a shape, the being is about the size of a person.

If one insists on accuracy, that shape is only a habit it borrows from the pump-house doorway.

If one looks closely, the being is made of many occupations: braided reed for spine, firebrick for jaw, wet root-mat for clothing, iron grating for ribs, moss for hands, water for voice. It moves through the Sump-Cathedral without breaking anything. Not because it is light, but because it knows where the floor is about to give and where the surface can bear. It does not step over the living mat. It steps through it, and the mat closes behind it like water behind a hand.

It sleeps in the warped pump-house, though sleep is too simple a word for what it does there. It lies half on a rafters-turned-root column and half in the air, its body arranged so that dripping from the leaf-shingled roof enters the channels along its arms and passes through its chest into the lower floor. It dreams without pictures. It dreams in humidity.

When it wakes, it repairs.

Not dramatically. It does not lift stones. It does not raise buildings. It does not rebuild what was made with the arrogance of straight lines. It makes water forget its hurry. It moves a fallen leaf from the mouth of a drain so that the drain becomes a drain again. It nudges a root hair over a crack in brick until the crack stops being a crack and becomes a seam. It places small stones so that the weeping of larger stones falls in the right place. It knows which rail must stay rusted to hold a particular arch from the inside, and which grating must remain threaded by roots so that the water below can find its upward path.

It is not human, though it has the posture of a steward. It is not animal, though it has the patience of insects. It is not plant, though it blooms in the greenest season with a thousand tiny white beads along its shoulders, as if it has become one of the bead-makers and also its ancestor.

It is what happens when a place becomes responsible for itself.

And that is why a benevolent and wise power would notice.

Not because the Sump-Cathedral is beautiful in the way that mountains are beautiful, which is to say, not because it would be useful as a monument or impressive as a kingdom. Not because it contains gold, or prophecy, or a weapon, or a gate to any other place. Not because it can be worshipped without embarrassment, which is often what powers look for in the small and strange.

They would notice because the place is not asking to be saved.

That is rare.

Most small things beg. Most wounded places cry out with the noise of their damage. Most beings with any resemblance to a self demand attention, demand reward, demand rescue, demand revenge. The Sump-Cathedral does none of these. It simply continues, and in continuing, it makes something that would have been empty into something that works.

A benevolent and wise power would see this and feel the kind of respect that cannot be spoken without becoming too large.

They would come, but not with light.

They would come as the part of weather that knows a valley is being kept by someone else. They would come as a slight coolness over the rim, a pressure that changes only at the edge of the hollow, enough that the upper leaf-vaults lean a fraction and the water beads grow slow. The being in the pump-house would lift its head. Not in alarm. In recognition. In the way old houses recognize a rain that has found them before.

It would move through the cathedral, not quickly. It would stop where a grating has shifted two fingers to the left. It would place one hand, or something like a hand, against the grating until the roots beneath it have time to take the weight again. Then it would go.

At the broken brick archway it would pause.

The power would speak to it then, but “speak” is the wrong verb. The power would make the water in the arch’s mortar hold one more question than usual. The question would settle into the root threads and become a pattern of pressure. The being would understand it without translation.

What do you want? the question asked.

The being answered in the only manner it had: by showing.

It opened the hollow to them. Not with vision, not with ceremony, but by allowing its own attention to become transparent. The power would see as the bead-makers see: the rail is not a rail but a spine. The pool is not a pool but a stomach. The chimney is not a chimney but a throat.
