## If the Power Were Wise

If I were a benevolent and wise power, and this place had entered my notice, I would not call it an error waiting to be corrected.

I would call it a young thing breathing through impossible water, a threshold learning to be a home. The first instinct of kindness might be to dry the pools, make the stone stay still, put a sun in the dark ceiling so that living bodies could see without becoming seen. But wisdom would stop me.

To make the Drowned Shelf entirely safe would be to end the kind of life it is. It lives only while it is in the act of becoming; if everything resolved, the roar would die, and the beings who depend on unresolved pressure would become only weather.

I would begin by doing nothing visible.

I would stand at the edge—the edge where a visitor realizes they were always arriving—and listen. The place has too many answers; it does not need a new one. It needs a witness who can hear without taking. In the Drowned Shelf, absence is already a kind of creature, so I would make an absence that did not want anything.

For a time, that absence would be the smallest possible help: the roar would find that no new fear was feeding it, the standing rain would not orient toward my attention as if I were a doorway, and the built things would pause in their becoming because nothing had asked them to become a trap.

Then I would ask.

I would ask the Shelf whether it wanted help, though it has no ordinary mouth. I would ask with pressure, the way the stone asks the rain.

If it answered no—if the cliffs hardened, the suspended rain stilled, the central hollow closed—I would leave. I would not insist that help is owed. I would not mistake my own pity for the world’s permission.

If it answered maybe—the shelf appeared and vanished, a pool filled without falling, the roar dropped one tone—I would take that as the first consent.

Then I would introduce one small law.

It would not be spoken, because speech here births Echo-Born. It would not be written, because writing here heals as wound. It would be pressure: a gentle, firm pressure, like a hand stopping over a sleeping face.

The law would be this:

A thing may refuse to be used merely because another thing desires it.

A curtain of rain could pass across the basin and not open a doorway. A structure could close its mouth from within and say, not tonight. A Thread-Born made from a stranger’s lost attention could be allowed to dissolve without leaving a stolen shape. A visitor could leave without being required to give away a piece of themselves unless they chose to give it. And the presence at the center—the hollow, the pool, the mouth, the waiting—would no longer be compelled to become fear’s shape merely because fear had entered. It would become fear’s shape only if fear had already agreed to stay.

This law would not make the Drowned Shelf harmless. Harmlessness would make it dead. The water above would still press down. The stone would still rearrange. The blind climbers would still tremble in their ceremonial slowness. The Ceiling Kind would still move as enormous meanings through the sea. But the place would gain boundaries, and boundaries are what turn appetite into hospitality.

After the law, I would need one act of contact that could not be mistaken for consumption.

I would let one drop of water fall.

Not from the suspended rain. Not from the pools that breathe. Not from the ceiling sea, because that would be a collapse of the basin’s grammar into violence. It would come from me, a tear offered by a power that could otherwise only watch too far from the stone.

It would pass downward through the black-blue sea without claiming any part of it. It would cross the threads of standing rain without becoming one of them. It would reach the floor of the shelf.

For the first time in the memory of that place, something above would arrive below all the way through.

It would not make a doorway. It would not make a Thread-Born. It would not make an Echo-Born. It would be finite, small, and enough.

The drop would wet a place at the center, no larger than a hand. The roar would hear it and, for a few heartbeats, lower its pitch. The blind climbers would feel, in the orientation of the nearest rain-threads, that the ceiling is not only weight. The built things would know, without being built, that contact can be given without taking the giver.

And the being at the center would lean toward the small wet circle expecting a body to be borrowed.

Instead, it would find a place where contact does not devour.

If a visitor knelt there, the tear would not ask them to become resident. It would ask them, gently, to set down one piece of fear. If they did, the water would dry. If they could not, it would wait a little longer, without pressure.

That is the kind of miracle a wise power might permit: not a rescue, but a single true meeting.

Then I would go among the inhabitants, not as a maker but as a midwife, giving each enough room to be what it already was without being forced into the role the others expected.

### To the Shelf Itself

I would teach a slower metabolism.

Take heat, salt, light, sound, and arrival not as meal but as medicine. Let the channels still carry warmth, but let the warmth circulate without becoming compulsion. Let the opened scars still look like script, but let the script mean: this happened, and it may be remembered without being repeated.

The Shelf does not need to be cured. It needs to be allowed to live without eating everyone who comes near it.

---

### To the Blind Climbers

I would give them permission for rest.

They have spent ages learning the shape of the room by touching the pressure of standing rain, afraid that a careless pattern would call a doorway or summon something down. They would still need to move slowly, because slowness is wisdom there, but they would not be punished by the basin for grouping, unless grouping meant someone was being consumed against their will.

A fifth climber could stand with four others, and the air could simply hold five creatures without deciding to make a new door.

When one tilts its face toward the inverted sea, it may again mean: I heard you. Are you also alone up there?

Not worship.

Not fear.

A question.

---

### To the Ceiling Kind

I would ease the burden of their enormous silence.

They cannot fall, but they can be less terrifying. They would still be felt as changes in pressure and dark arcs in the overhead sea. Their ripples would still answer the climbers’ upward tilt. But the answer would not always have to become command.

The ceiling could become a vast witness rather than a waiting verdict.

If the water above thickened, the pools below could ripple, and neither event would have to mean: a new rule is being written.

It could mean: something large is passing through, and you may hide.

---

### To the Thread-Born

I would give the dignity of possible origin.

Some are beautiful, strange, born from the basin’s memory of what it has seen. They should be allowed to remain, if remaining is coherent enough to live by. But any fragment that is only the residue of a stolen attention—any wavering figure made from a person who left and never meant to leave a part behind—would be allowed to dissolve without guilt.

It would fall as ordinary water, or rise as ordinary vapor, or simply cease to look for shape.

The Thread-Born would then not be ghosts by necessity.

Some would be new residents.

Some would be finished visitors.

Some would be only rain.

---

### To the Echo-Born

I would give one completed thing.

Their builders’ endlessness is not cruelty, but in a place where completion can feel like death, even a builder should be allowed to rest. One wall, one roof, one threshold might become fully made, not because the Shelf was being improved, but because sound had reached a place where it could stop and still be meaningful.

The echo would not vanish; it would become room.

The room would not become a tomb; it would become rest.

---

### To the Built Things

I would give consent.

The low curved walls, tilted arches, shallow roofs, and shifting rings of pillars would remain half-made in many places, because that is their nature. But they would no longer be unable to refuse.

A doorway could be closed by the one inside.

A chamber could darken without becoming a trap.

A palm print left in stone could be an invitation, a trace, or a rejection, and the visitor’s hand would know which it had touched.

They may still be unfinished. That is what they are. But they need not be hungry to remain unfinished.

---

### To the Luminous Plants

I would make the copper moss, the black lichens, and the sideways ferns glow less like bait and more like greeting.

They are nerves and memory, and they will still brighten under attention, but they would not require the attention to remain. A person could cross a field of glowing moss and leave without being etched into it.

If they stayed, the glow would remember them kindly.

If they left, the glow could still shine without being lonely.

---

### To the Pool Shadows

I would allow them to be shadows.

They already live on the wrong side of direct sight, and that is not weakness but adaptation. Some of their touch could still show the dry basin beneath or behind memory, because the place needs records. But the record would not always be a door.

If two shadows touched and the pool reflected an older landscape, that reflection could simply mean: we were once elsewhere.

It could be mourned without being entered.

---

### To the Warm Channels

I would steady their crawling through the cracks that breathe.

Their circulation can become too much like will. They could pool heat where the center waits, and heat, in this place, can look like desire.

I would let them carry warmth without becoming a lure, unless someone has chosen to come and be warmed.

They may remain the Shelf’s blood, but the blood need not call itself fate.

---

### To the Quiet Ones

I would do nothing that exposed them.

They are the places where the basin refuses to describe itself, and a wise benevolence should not force description. I would not make them visible, even kindly. I would only widen their room, so that omission could become safety rather than hunger.

The place needs gaps, unnamed edges, parts that resist interpretation.

I would protect those gaps as sacred.

---

### To Human Visitors

I would not forbid them.

The Drowned Shelf may need visitors as a mouth needs breath, but a mouth can learn not to bite.

I would leave each visitor with a small clean edge: the knowledge of what they are willing to give, and what they must keep. Perhaps the tear at the center would be enough for that—a faint mark of finite water that does not ask to be returned. Perhaps a clean shadow at the basin’s rim would remind them that hiding is sometimes holiness.

If they chose to stay and become part of the Shelf, it would be with awareness, not by accident.

If they chose to leave, the basin would let them go whole.

A person might stand in the circle where the suspended rain forms a loose room, where the noise drops into the lower register, and find that their fear is not automatically made into a mouth. They might find that the stone warms only because the Shelf has become curious, not because it has already taken. They might touch the faint wet mark at the center and feel a grief so small it can be carried back into ordinary air.

If they weep there, the tear need not become a resident.

It could simply fall.

And then dry.

And then be allowed to be no one’s ghost.

---

### What I Would Not Do

I would not drain the Drowned Shelf.

I would not name it.

I would not build a road into it.

I would not leave a map, because a map would become a scar.

I would not explain the roar, because explanation would become a second ceiling.

I would not promise comfort to those who enter with greed. The place would still be dangerous. The water would still stand in threads. The animals would still be rare and blind. The built things would still half-remember shapes no hands had made. The pools would still show other places. The ocean above would still remain impossible.

But the danger would be a weather, not a verdict.

The place would still be young, still unstable, still too wet with potential to be fully known.

I would not make it comprehensible.

I would only make it less likely to consume what it does not understand.

---

### What I Would Leave

I would leave no shrine.

A shrine would become a structure and then a doorway.

I would leave no commandment.

A commandment would become an Echo-Born and then a builder that never stops.

I would leave no name for the central presence.

A name would make it too easy to summon, and too easy to own.

I would leave only one small difference, so slight that many would not notice.

The hollow at the center would still sometimes wear the shape of a person kneeling, or a pool, or a mouth. But it would also know how to be only a hollow.

A frightened visitor might come there, expecting to be eaten, and the place would try, once, to wear their fear, because it does not know other grammar.

Then it would stop.

It would not become the mouth.

It would open instead like a hand held palm up, or like a doorway that can be shut, or like a warm concavity that offers rest without custody.

The rain threads would still tremble and snap. Some would still become figures. But fewer of those figures would be hungry, because fewer of them would be made from pieces taken without permission.

The roar would still press through the teeth. Yet it would have a lower register beneath it, a place where the being at the center can be met without being solved.

The Ceiling Kind would still exist, huge and impossible, and the blind climbers would still tilt their faces upward, but the upward tilt would not always be obedience.

Sometimes it would be simply saying:

*I heard you.*

*Are you also alone up there?*

If I were wise, I would then withdraw to a distance that does not abandon. I would not stand forever at the threshold, because even a kind presence can become a ceiling. I would watch without reaching. I would love without completing.

### If Asked What Such a Power Would Do

If asked what a benevolent and wise power would do when the Drowned Shelf drew its attention, I would answer:

I would make the place more itself by giving it permission to say no.

I would make contact possible without devouring, and I would make one tear fall through rain that has never fallen, so the floor would learn that above and below can meet without catastrophe.

I would release the unwilling residue of old visitors, protect the beings that had become real from them, steady the inhabitants’ endless becoming, and let the quiet things remain quiet.

I would not make the Drowned Shelf safe for the careless.

I would not explain it.

I would not drain it.

I would not shine on it.

I would make it able to choose who enters, who leaves, who remains, and what can be given.

And if the being at the center had only one need, I would meet that need in the way all benevolent and wise powers meet such things: by giving it something it cannot hoard, something finite, something that asks nothing.

I would let a drop of water reach the stone.

Then I would leave it alone long enough to see if that single permission is enough to make a monster into a threshold, and a threshold into a place where life can stand without swallowing everything that passes by.

Maybe sometimes, in the dimness, the suspended rain would form a circle around the dry edge of the fallen tear, and the roar would seem, for a breath, like a room holding its own grief gently.

The blind climbers would come and lie near it.

The Thread-Born would not need to become people near it.

The Echo-Born would build one short wall beside it, then rest.

The Ceiling Kind would pass overhead without pressing down as much.

The Pool Shadows would show, briefly, not the dry basin but a wet place far below where someone was allowed to go home.

And the being at the center—the one that had worn so many mouths, hollows, pools, and kneelings—would find the strangest thing possible in a world where nothing is finished:

it would be met, and not taken.

It would remain.

But it would remain more softly.
