## What They Would Do

They would come without the sound of arrival.

Not because they were sneaking, or cruel, or afraid of the place, but because a loud coming would already be a kind of violence. The plateau had learned how to absorb intention, how to fold urgency into its settled stone, how to take the traveler’s wanting and make it a feature of the landscape. So the benevolent and wise power would come the way a drop of water might come if water could choose its surface. It would not break the land. It would not summon a sun. It would not make the clouds remember wind or the pools remember runoff. It would enter without demanding that everything become different, and it would begin by doing the hardest thing: it would not mistake permanence for injury.

There are people who think a thing is cursed because it does not move. There are people who think a thing is wrong because it is old, cold, and quiet, because its shadows are misplaced and its hum has become an object. A foolish kindness would have burned there. A foolish kindness would have stood on the terrace and declared, *You must be rescued*, and it would have carried torches of ordinary time into a place where ordinary time had already been considered and set aside. It would have made the land into a problem to solve, which is another way of saying it would have failed to see that the land had already solved something.

This power would have seen otherwise.

It would have looked at the bruised sky, the cloud-lakes below the shoulder of the world, the basins of still water, the lichen that almost wrote, the inward-facing hearths, the incomplete huts, the pale quadruped standing on a basalt shelf, and it would not have said, *What a horror.* It would not have said, *What a ruin.* It would have understood, gently, that this was not a broken place but a place broken *from* something, in the way a shell is broken from a body after the body has gone. The absence was there because something had happened once, long ago, or because something would always happen, and the difference between these two was not the most important thing.

The power would have listened to the hum.

It would have heard that the hum was not mere noise. Noise is chaos that has not yet found shape. Here, the sound was order made too vast for a mouth to produce. It was the note a world holds when it has stopped expecting anything else. It was the plateau’s breath without lungs, its thought without forgetting. A lesser being might have tried to silence it. A wise being knows that silence is not always liberation. If the hum stopped too quickly, the stones might remember they were not finished, the cairns might collapse under the weight of their interrupted labor, the Still Ones might discover they had been serving a purpose not their own, and the land might come apart like a sentence whose final word has been removed.

But even a wise power does not confuse order with justice.

It would notice the Unleft.

It would see the cairns that had once been hands, the saplings whose roots clung to rock like insect legs, the doorway that opened onto a wall, the hearth that burned inward, the pool that reflected a room that was not there. It would not say, *How terrible.* That phrase belongs to those who want to be startled rather than responsible. It would say instead, with a patience older than fear, *Something here has forgotten the difference between staying and being held.*

That is the trouble with places like this. They become so balanced that their balance mistakes the small for the necessary. A visitor comes, uncertain and warm and brief, and the land absorbs them not because it hates them, but because it has learned to treat intention as material, like stone, like moisture, like sound. It does not need consent because consent is a motion, and motion has been eroded away. It does not need promises because promises belong to future, and the future has been folded into a permanent present. It takes what passes through, and it does not return it, because returning would change the arrangement.

A benevolent power would not tear the arrangement apart. But it would make a door in it.

Not a door with a frame or a lock, because such things can become prison walls in a place that turns everything into condition. It would make a door that was mostly possibility. A traveler walking the terrace might find themselves passing a collapsed lintel where there had once been a niche leading nowhere. For one small stretch of time—no hour at all, perhaps no event at all—the niche would become something else. It would become an opening that did not require a destination. It would say to whoever looked at it, *You may go in. You may go out. You may stand here and not be enrolled.* And if they stood there for centuries, it would still be a door for them. If they left at once, it would still be a door for the next.

This would be the first thing the power did: it would create a permission that did not demand obedience.

It would not force the land to change. It would change the grammar of what “could happen” in the land. Before, the plateau had two verbs: remain and be arranged. The power would introduce a third, quiet verb: choose. Not always, not loudly, not everywhere. Just enough to make room for a body to remain its own while passing through the place. Just enough to keep the land from mistaking its equilibrium for ownership.

The wise power would walk the terrace without leaving tracks that became permanent.

It would pass by the ring of shrubs around the empty stone and not call it an eye. It would understand the eye, but it would also understand that an eye can learn to weep. It would place nothing inside the ring, because a thing placed inside the ring might become a trap. Instead, it would make the ring imperfect in a way that only one living thing could notice. For one traveler, the circle would feel like a circle. For another, like a suggestion. For a third, like a place where the wind, which does not blow, might still remember the shape of a breath. The ring would continue to watch, but it would not be allowed to collect everything it saw.

It would do the same with the pools.

The pools were beautiful, in the way a held thing is beautiful. They reflected rooms, unfamiliar skies, faces not standing beside them. They were not portals, not dreams, not portals pretending to be dreams. They were memories of the plateau’s condition, the place’s way of keeping itself from being only what it was. A foolish power would have drained them, or tried to make them ordinary. A wise power would not. Water that cannot flow here is not water waiting to be water. It is water doing a different work.

But the power would leave one small pool that was honest.

It would be the size of two hands. It would sit in a shallow basin near a place where the shadows behaved badly. It would not glow. It would not ripple. Its water would be cold enough to sting, like all the water there, but when a living face looked into it, it would reflect only that face, and only now. The pool would not show a room with a chair. It would not show a starless vault. It would not show the traveler wearing clothes they had never worn. It would show the traveler’s actual eyes, actual mouth, actual tiredness, actual age, actual name. It would show them for one steady moment, and then if they walked away, the reflection would end.

That ending would be the gift.

In a place where every event occurred and continued without becoming anything else, the pool would teach something very ancient: that a thing can be seen, released, and not forgotten into permanence. The traveler would look into the water and remember, perhaps, that they had once been someone who could look away from themselves. Some travelers would use this pool. Some would not. Some would stand by it for a long time, afraid that if they saw themselves, the land might understand them too well and keep them gently. But the pool would not become a trap. It would remain a mirror without hunger.

The power would not try to save the Still Ones.

It would know that saving implies a future the Still Ones may not want. The pale quadruped on the basalt shelf, thin as an unfinished sketch of a deer, the implied human forms, the vertical outlines too delicate for the wind they did not feel—these were not statues waiting for animation. They were creatures that had lived so long in the idea of themselves that motion had become unnecessary. A foolish rescue would make them blink. A wise power would ask, without asking, whether blinking was their own.

And because the question could not be answered in words, the power would answer it with posture.

It would stand before one of the Still Ones and do something almost nothing: it would not try to occupy the distance. It would not approach and not retreat. It would simply exist near the creature as a condition of the creature’s existence. The pale quadruped might not move. It might not acknowledge. It might not even be there in the ordinary sense. But something in the arrangement would change, because for the first time in millennia, the plateau had not used a visitor as material, nor had it arranged itself around the visitor’s fear. The visitor had come without needing the land to be a problem or a prize. That, too, would enter the land’s weather.

Some of the Still Ones might remain exactly as they were, because they were not suffering. That would be allowed. Some might find that a leg could shift weight, not because they had been broken open, but because the idea of themselves had become slightly softer. Some might discover that they could blink, and if they did, the light would not change, and no one would applaud, because the action would be so small that the world had almost forgotten how to notice.

The power would also not demand the land release the Unleft all at once.

That would be cruel. The Unleft had been absorbed into the plateau’s body: cairns, cup-marks, hearths, shrubs bent in wrong directions, doorways that opened onto walls, small structures that had never finished being buildings. To pull them out violently would be to rip their names from the only body they had left. A wise power knows that rescue can be another form of injury. But it also knows that some prisons are so thorough that even the prisoner cannot choose the key. So it would make the prison permeable.

It would teach the land to let go without forgetting.

If one of the Unleft wished to leave, the land would know it as a weather-change: a stone in a cairn becoming light enough to be carried, a spiral in a slab relaxing by one turn, a lichen line shifting so that it almost meant *again.* If one wished to stay, the land would not be shamed. It would say, in its own slow way, *Then remain, but not because you are necessary. Remain because you have made this stillness yours.*

There would be no announcement. No golden path. No voice from the bruised sky. Only the smallest openings.

The power would do a thing so quiet that, after many centuries, it might seem to have never happened at all.

It would make one bird complete its flight.

A bird in that place had always been questionable: silent or strangely loud, wings moving too slow or too fast, arriving before its cry or after its echo, sometimes only a sound learning to fly. It crossed the terraces like a proof that distance could be reminded of itself. The power would take one such bird and give it an ordinary arc. The bird would fly from one standing slab to a dry basin, and its path would mean exactly one thing: it wanted to land. It would land. It would fold its wings. It would tremble, maybe, not from fear but from the shock of being a creature whose motion led to change. It would leave a small mark in the dust, or perhaps not leave a mark at all, because dust there is not like dust here. But for that one bird, the future would be allowed to arrive.

The rest of the birds could keep their impossible behavior. They were the plateau’s way of using sound as animal, using animal as reminder, using reminder as stone. The wise power would not erase them. It would only let one bird be real for a second, because even the most ancient arrangement sometimes needs to remember that a small thing can happen and pass.

It would allow one thrown stone to hit the ground and stop.

In a place where consequences are refused, throwing a stone feels like a moral failure because the stone may hang in the air forever, falling without arriving. The power would make one ordinary stone’s fall ordinary. It would leave the air afterward exactly as it had been. It would not make the land a normal land. It would simply create one place where cause could finish itself, so that the plateau would not confuse completion with catastrophe.

It would add a rest to the hum.

Not silence. Silence would have been too abrupt; silence in a place of fixed sound might be mistaken for death, and death might be mistaken for departure, and departure might become panic. The power would not remove the tone. It would teach the tone to pause. There would be, somewhere inside the pressure that filled the mouth and nose and thoughts, a half-beat where the hum did not insist. That half-beat would be small enough that most would not notice it. It would feel like a thought arriving without being swallowed. It would feel like the body remembering it could breathe without permission. It would feel like the place, for the first time in a very long while, holding a question instead of an answer.

The power would not fill that question.

That is another kindness. It would not send a choir to answer. It would not write a name across the sky. It would not make the basin reflect a god or the hearth radiate warmth or the lichen spell out a secret. It would leave the question as an empty space shaped like hospitality. In the plateau, emptiness was never neutral. But this emptiness would be different. It would not be occupancy by absence. It would be absence that allowed presence without taking it apart.

And then, having done these small things, the power would leave.

It would not stay as a guardian, because guardianships become new architectures of control. It would not set an altar there, because altars attract intention, and intention there becomes soil. It would not carve its own face into the rock or put its handprint into the stone, because its handprint might become one more Still One, one more reason for the land to think it had been seen only to be preserved forever.

It would leave nothing visible.

Except perhaps this:

A traveler climbing for hours through that bruised height, passing basins of impossible dampness, the inward hearths, the ring of shrubs, the shelf where a pale quadruped stood like a sketch of a deer that had not meant to exist, would find—maybe at the edge of a dry channel, maybe beneath a low stone hut with a doorway that still did not make sense—a single flat stone warm enough to touch.

Not hot. Not shining. Not glowing with the reflected light that had no source.

Just warm.

As if someone had sat there once, not to wait for anyone, but to remind the land that sitting can be chosen. If the traveler sat on it, their shadow might fall correctly for a few steps. If they did not, it would remain there, warm because warmth had become part of the place’s weather. The lichen near it might spread, but the spread would not become a trap. The water might gather in a crevice near it, but the water would not insist on reflecting another room. The hum would continue, but inside the hum there would be a place to put one’s attention that did not immediately put one’s body to work.

A later traveler might ask, “Who lives here?”

And the place would answer, as before, in a way not meant to be understood at first. The clouds would lie below like white lakes. The birds would cross with too-slow wings. The pools would hold reflections that were not reflections. The cairns would rearrange themselves without wind. The shadows would continue their old strange work. The Still Ones would stand because standing was their function, and the Unleft would persist in their corrected forms, and the plateau would still be an organ made of stone, dampness, sound, and attention.

But the answer would also include, quietly, a change that might seem like nothing.

It would include one more kind of inhabitant: the one that lets a visitor pass through without making them citizen, stone, echo, or door.

If asked what the benevolent power did, those who returned and struggled to speak might say: nothing.

They might say: everything.

They might say: it did not heal the place. It did not free the dead. It did not make the mountain young or the sky normal. It did not silence the hum or melt the cold.

And those who remembered more accurately might say: it gave the land a way to say *you are allowed to leave* without forcing the land to say *you should have left.* It gave the Unleft a door that did not require them to forget where they had been. It gave the Still Ones permission to remain still not because motion had been erased, but because they could have moved and chose not to. It gave the traveler a moment in which to be only a traveler, which is the most fragile mercy a place can offer.

It would have done this without triumph.

A benevolent power, seeing a place like that, would understand that not every frozen thing wishes to become warm. Some things are cold because cold is the only way they have found to hold what matters. The power would not insult them by calling their preservation a prison. But it would also not confuse preservation with love. It would draw a line. It would say to the land: *Be what you are, but not by consuming what is not you.*

It would say to the being who lived in it: *You may be large. You may be silent. You may be eroded into a form that no longer needs to justify itself. But do not make your loneliness into a law. Do not make your need for balance into a cage for the brief. You do not have to keep every soul that passes. You are allowed to let things finish without taking them into your body.*

And because the being who lived in the place was the terrace and the hum and the arrangement and the almost, it could not answer. Not in the way a person answers. But the stone might shift by a hair. One pool might ripple without a stone being thrown. One bird might land without leaving an afterimage. One cairn might not rearrange itself for a season. One hearth might, for no reason, seem to remember fire without wanting to burn.

And if a traveler stood on the exposed shoulder of the world and felt watched not by eyes but by the arrangement of everything, they might notice something they had not noticed before: the watching no longer felt like enrollment. It felt like attention. And attention, when it is kind, can become a door without hinges, a warmth without fuel, a word that does not need to be said.
