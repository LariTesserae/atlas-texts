## What They Would Do, If Anything

When the benevolent and wise power turned its attention to the Drowned Terrace, it did not come as rescue.

Rescue is a story for places that know themselves to be lost. This place did not. It knew itself, if knowing is the way water moves through streets, the way root binds timber, the way fish turn through a doorway as if it had always been made for swimming.

The power stood at the rim. It saw the small bowl of rock, the high air, the green light, the town no longer a town but a city of niches. It saw algae on every surface, moss finishing the walls, rushes filling gaps left by human hands. It saw the crayfish in the crevices of foundations, the pale fish patrolling lanes, the caddis cases dragging like little towns across the submerged silt. It saw the old streets become highways, the courtyards become feeding grounds, the hearthstones become gardens. It saw that the builders had made rooms, and the water had made niches, and the niches had made a world.

It also saw what was not there: no abandoned soul, no unfinished prayer, no crying ghost, no god waiting to be installed.

And because it was wise, it understood the deepest danger of benevolence: the urge to improve what is already whole.

So the first thing the benevolent and wise power would do is not disturb.

Not out of neglect, but out of reverence.

A foolish kindness would drain the terrace and say: *Let the dead town be restored.*  
A vain kindness would raise the drowned builders and say: *Let the lost people return.*  
A sentimental kindness would appoint a guardian trout, a speaking moss, a single sacred heart at the center of the basin.  
A possessive kindness would make a shrine, a name, a pilgrimage, a purpose.

But this power was wise.

It would not make the Terrace mean anything other than what it had become.

If, after all its looking, it did anything, it would do very small things—things that might pass for chance in the human world, but were the careful work of a kindness that knew when not to enter.

---

### 1. They would keep the water honest.

They would not command the mountain. They would not make the terrace eternal. They would not freeze the level of the lake or make every season behave.

They would simply protect the conditions that let the Terrace continue.

The hidden fracture in the rock would not choke. The melt channel would find its way in, and some other way out. If the spring was too greedy, the basin would be allowed to rise, but not so high that the roof-timbers would be scoured from the floor. If the summer was too dry, they would let enough snow wait above, not forever, only until the roots could endure.

They would guard the basin from drying, but not from changing.

A wise power understands that to preserve a living place without preserving its capacity to become something else is already a kind of death.

So the Terrace would continue its own weather: full in spring, low in late summer, trembling under ice in winter, singing with frogs when the mountain loosens its grip.

---

### 2. They would keep it unpossessed.

The power would not hide the Terrace with malice. It would not build a wall or put monsters in the high valleys. It would simply make the place difficult for greed to understand.

A mapmaker who came looking for resources would find the basin uninteresting on the page. A dam-builder’s calculations would always return the wrong answer: too small, too unstable, not worth the cost. A collector’s hands would feel clumsy. A logger would look at the slope and see no timber worth taking. A developer would feel, at the last moment, a strange dryness of imagination, as if the future had closed.

This would not be cruelty. It would be wisdom dressed as refusal.

Those who came to take would find the Terrace already taken by fish, by root, by wind, by silt, by the long labor of small lives.

But those who came empty-handed might be allowed to see.

Not many.

Not often.

Enough to learn that the world contains a kind of completeness that does not require human approval.

---

### 3. They would remove only unnecessary cruelty.

The benevolent power would not abolish hunger.

It saw the dragonfly nymph beneath the beam. It saw the trout opening over the gravel lane. It saw the frog’s tongue moving like a wet line of fate through the spring air. It understood that appetite was not the Terrace’s failing but its grammar. The water used death to make more water alive. The stone used root to become more than stone. The town used its own abandonment to become more than town.

But it would keep suffering from exceeding what the place itself required.

A fish trapped in a collapsed timber would not linger past its time. The ice would be thick enough to shelter the basin but thin enough to crack with the breath of the mountain. The melt would carry no poison. The silt would remain breathable where eggs required oxygen. The channels would keep their oxygened paths. The old hollow logs would become chambers of life, not tombs of waste.

It would not interfere with the Terrace’s own predation, decay, and renewal.

It would prevent the cruelties that do not come from the living world but from the careless ones.

No net left by human hands. No chemical snow from some far-off valley. No foreign thing that the roots could not digest, the current could not sort, the fish could not learn.

If poison came, they would turn the wind, delay the cloud, or keep the fracture sealed until it passed.

If plague came, they would not declare the Terrace perfect or immortal. They would let it grieve in its own way: a slower school of minnows, a year of pale weeds, a thinner frog chorus. And then, if survival was possible, they would let the pattern gather itself again.

---

### 4. They would make a room for witnessing, not worship.

A foolish power would make the Terrace sacred.

A wise power would leave it holy by not naming it holy.

But because benevolence is not the opposite of wisdom, it would make one quiet allowance: that some human beings might need to see this place as it is.

Not the grieving person seeking comfort. Not the lonely person seeking a companion. Not the lost child seeking a fairy tale.

But perhaps a person who could be changed by the simple fact that abandonment had become abundance.

They might reach the rim when the wind dropped and the water lay like a ceiling over the streets. They might see a doorframe below, with no door, and a fish pass through it as if doors had always been made for swimming. They might see a hearthstone furred with moss, a roof-beam soft with algae, a courtyard where tadpoles turned like dark seeds through green light. They might understand, without words, that the old human form had not merely vanished; it had been translated.

Their sorrow might loosen.

Not because the Terrace healed them, but because it showed them that a life does not have to remain in its original shape to be useful.

But the power would be careful.

It would not make a cult. It would not make the Terrace the property of the grieving. When the lesson was done, the wind would return, the water would ripple, the path would blur, and the visitor would find that they could not explain what they had seen without diminishing it.

This is the way of wise kindness: it gives glimpses, not ownership.

---

### 5. They would refuse to give the Terrace a face.

A single ruler would be a simplification.

No great fish would become an oracle. No moss-covered stone would become an altar. No reed would open a mouth. No drowned builder would rise and say, *Remember me.*

The power would understand that the Terrace is not haunted by a being but inhabited by many beings.

The snail is its patience.  
The caddis is its tailoring.  
The minnow is its flashing sentence.  
The reed is its pillar.  
The current is its thought.  
The ice is its held breath.  
The frogs are its spring mouth.  
The fish are its way of reading the streets.

It is not one creature.

It is a civic animal made of roots, film, shells, fins, spores, stones, shadows, water, and wind. It has a self the way a reef has a self: not as a single mind, but as a pattern that keeps choosing to hold together.

A benevolent and wise power would respect that plurality.

It would not impose a leader, a hero, a god, or a story of redemption.

It would let the Terrace remain what it already was: a distributed parliament of small lives, slow lives, hungry lives, grazing lives, swimming lives, clinging lives, filtering lives, singing lives, and the water itself moving through the bones of an old town.

---

### 6. They would not freeze its time.

This is perhaps the hardest thing a benevolent power would do: it would allow the Terrace to end someday.

Not immediately.

Not violently.

Not through the cruelty of men.

But a wise kindness cannot make living places immortal without turning them into prisons.

In ten thousand years, the basin may fill with peat. In a colder age, it may become a closed white room for much of the year. In a drier century, it may sink into a bog of reeds and memory. In another shape, it may open into a stream and leave the old walls entirely behind.

The power would not say, *Stay as you are.*

It would say, *Continue as you can.*

And if the Terrace became a meadow, it would still be the Terrace in another life.

The stones would remember in the shape of grass.  
The roots would remember in the way the plants found gaps.  
The fish would remember in the new channels.  
The builders would remember as the long work of making niches, and niches would remember as the long work of becoming habitats.

Nothing would be saved from death.

But continuity would be honored.

A wise benevolence does not mistake preservation for love.

---

### 7. They would leave no name.

If the power named it, it might become theirs.

If it spoke from the basin, humans might build temples around the voice.

If it performed miracles, the water would become a servant of worship, and the creatures would become props in a story of salvation.

So the wise one would give the Terrace a name that could not be spoken.

A sound like water passing through a hollow log at night.  
A pressure like ice bending in spring.  
A brightness like a doorway filling with moving light.

That name would be unrepeatable.

The Terrace would answer to it by continuing.

The fish would swim.  
The snails would clean.  
The roots would hold.  
The current would sort.  
The frogs would sing.  
The light would do its slow work.

And no one would know who had made the name, except perhaps the water itself.

---

### What the Terrace Would Do in Response

If the being of the Terrace could choose a response to such attention, it would not kneel.

It would not worship.

It would not offer gratitude in the human way, because its body does not have that posture, and its mind—if “mind” is the right word for the pattern of root, film, fish, and current—does not need an audience.

But it would answer.

Not with words.

With its own persistence.

The fish would move through the streets as before.  
The algae would brighten in the light.  
The moss would keep the hearthstones damp and green.  
The reeds would continue their patient work of turning doorways into channels.  
The snails would trace their slow handwriting across the mossy lintels.  
The minnows would flash in the courtyards like coins falling through light.  
The frogs would take up the spring chorus until the basin filled with wet, insistent music.  
The ice would seal the edges in winter and let the water breathe beneath.

If the Terrace could ask anything of the wise power, its ask would be strange:

*Let me remain many.*  
*Let me remain unmeaning except as I mean myself.*  
*Let no single story be fastened on me as a nail.*

And the benevolent, wise power would grant it.

Not because the Terrace was worthy of command, but because a true kindness does not require its beloved to become a symbol.

---

### If That Is Doing Something

There would be no miracle anyone could point to.

The basin would only be a basin.  
The water would only be cold.  
The roots would only be roots.  
The fish would only be fish.

But perhaps, in the years afterward, a woman passing near the high bowl would stop without knowing why and feel her breath settle.

A boy would press his face near the edge and see a fish glide through a doorframe and feel something hard in his chest loosen.

An old man would look at the drowned town and realize that he was not looking at ruin, but at completion.

A logger would turn back because the slope gave him no profit and no story.

A storm would try to bury the channels and then, for no reason anyone could explain, would shift and spare them.

A winter would be cruel and then not too cruel.

A spring would come with meltwater singing, and the Terrace would open like a room beneath the sky.

The Drowned Terrace would not be saved.

It would continue, as it always had, by making the world from the remains of another world.

And the benevolent, wise power would do, if anything, this:

It would protect the conditions under which a place can be itself without being owned.

It would remove only what was foreign cruelty, not what was living necessity.

It would make room for witnessing without demanding worship.

It would keep the water, root, fish, moss, and stone from being turned into a lesson.

It would leave no sign of itself except a deeper steadiness in the basin.

If the Terrace could thank them, it would do so by ignoring them.

Not out of ingratitude.

Because the highest thanks a self-sufficient being can offer a wise kindness is to continue without being owned.

No rescue.

No return.

No new master.

Only water filling the low places.

Only roots remembering the gaps.

Only light passing through an empty doorway, finding it no longer empty.
