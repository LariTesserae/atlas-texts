## What They Would Do

They would not come first with light, because light can be too much in a place where the walls already remember every heat. They would come first with ducking, with patience, with the willingness to stand where someone must turn sideways to pass.

If they were benevolent and wise, they would arrive at the top of the ramp and wait.

Not because they need to be seen. Not because they require a ceremony. But because the Drip-Hall is not a thing that can be saved by a sudden shining. It is already made of ten thousand slow agreements. To enter it with too much grace would be like pouring rain into a room that has learned to live by trickles. The room would drown in kindness.

So they would wait.

They would let their attention settle into the bricks until they could tell the difference between a drip that means nothing and a drip that means everything. They would learn that some sounds are accidents and some are law. They would learn that the hall is not only a shelter but a body, and that its body is also a mind made of many smaller minds.

At first, they would look for a single being to bless.

This is what a power not yet fully wise might do. It would ask: which one lives here? The old woman by the Grinding Pot? The child who reads moss? The doe who stares at the water as though she has heard the weather speaking? The rat with husk between its teeth? The snail whose silver line is a map no human would care to use? The lizard that matches the stone so perfectly it seems the stone has become alive only to watch its own heat? The bat folded into a ceiling niche like a dark thought? The room itself, perhaps, if the room can be called a creature?

But the benevolent and wise power would notice that there is no single one.

There is no owner. There is no hero. There is no chosen animal, no chosen human, no chosen ghost. The being of the Drip-Hall is not one resident. It is the residentness itself. It is the way the goats move when the basins darken. It is the way the quail shift when the light slants across the Grinding Pot. It is the way the snails know when the air has changed. It is the way the people speak in short clicks and taps, not because they are primitive, but because noise in this place is communication and noise too much is danger.

The being is the pattern.

The being is: *the room breathing through its creatures, the creatures breathing through the room.*

Once the power understood that, it would know what it must not do.

A simple kindness would dry the damp.

A simple kindness would remove the rats from the clay tubes.

A simple kindness would make the lizards stop eating the young when the lofts are disturbed.

A simple kindness would turn the hall into a bright, clean, empty thing with no moss, no click of claws, no silver snail-trails, no smell of dung-cakes burning in oil, no goat-warmth, no humid wool, no bronze tap hissing like an animal with a bad temper.

But that would be murder dressed as comfort.

Because the Drip-Hall survives not because its life is simple, but because its burdens are shared.

The wise power would understand: to take away the room’s difficulty would be to take away its meaning. The difficulty is not the enemy. The difficulty is the grammar. The difficulty is the way the room teaches itself how to continue.

So they would do almost nothing.

But that almost nothing would be precise.

---

They would strengthen what cannot be seen, so no one would need to be afraid at the wrong hours.

In the lower brick near the east wall, where a long crack has begun to form like a mouth trying to speak water, they would put a bloom of white mineral into the mortar. Not enough to change the crack. Not enough to make it look repaired. Just enough that, for twenty years, it will hold a little better than it should.

They would send a root-fiber from an old wooden batten upward through one joint and down through another, invisible except to the people who already know that wood can move in damp air if it is cut from a certain tree and left in the right shade. They would let that batten take three more seasons before it fails.

They would adjust the hidden rope around the pot-hanging ring so that one fiber is slightly stronger than the others. A woman will pull too hard, curse, and almost drop a full vessel, and then she will hear the rope hold, and she will say nothing, and the child beside her will learn that pots are heavy and that the world is sometimes merciful in a way too small to thank.

They would make one tile near the threshold dry faster than its neighbors when someone has spilled water. They would make the person who notices this not think about it too much, except perhaps later in sleep.

They would do this not to impress.

They would do this so the room can keep being a room.

---

They would sharpen the hall’s senses.

Not by making it magical. By making it itself more fully.

The bronze tap that has always been imperfect would develop a new way of hissing, just at the moment when the water from the south basin has begun to draw too much sediment. No one will know why it is hissing differently. Someone will say, “That one has learned to complain.” Then they will clean the strainer, and the crabs will live, and the children will drink without tasting grit.

The snails would leave one particular line brighter than usual across the tile beside the Grinding Pot on the night before a slow leak becomes a fast one. The Drip-Reader will see it in the morning and say only, “We need to move the grain jars.”

The lizards, when the air changes, would shift not to the brazier but to the cooler side of the stone, and the people would read them correctly for once without knowing how often they usually misread the room.

The rats, when the tubes near the door are beginning to fill with mold-husk, would knock their claws in a rhythm slightly wrong. The child who usually only hears noise will hear the wrongness and say, “They are stuck again,” and the adult who was about to ignore it will stop, and will pull one clay tube clear, and will find the blocked pod that has been preventing water from running where it needs to run.

These are not miracles.

These are not visions.

These are not signs that point away from the world toward some higher meaning.

These are small corrections in attention. The wise power would know that the greatest gift a room can receive is not rescue but clarity.

---

They would give one child a word too large for ordinary speech.

A girl born in the hall, small from low ceilings, wide-eyed from slanted light, will be sitting near the Grinding Pot when the rain is coming through one of the open basins above. She will hear the first drops, not one by one, but all at once, as if the ceiling has remembered a song. She will look at the water running in the shallow ring around the pot and say something that makes an older person pause.

She will say, “We are listening together.”

The old person will laugh or frown. “We?”

The girl will look embarrassed and say, “Everyone. Even the room.”

That will not be revelation. It will not be prophecy. But after that, someone will start using the phrase too. Perhaps at first as a joke. Then later, when a pipe fails or the quail panic or the water becomes cloudy, someone will say it in seriousness: “We are listening together.”

The wise power would not force the thought. They would only leave it in the right ear at the right angle of dripping.

---

They would protect the Lower Rooms from being noticed too wrongly.

The surface world is not all danger, but surface eyes can flatten a place into usefulness. A Drip-Hall can become “that strange cellar.” A population can become “too many goats.” A system of rats and crabs and snails can become “unclean.” A hand-made economy can become “inefficient.” A dense, noisy, damp world can become “a place to clear out.”

So they would create small misreadings.

A tax collector would climb the ramp and smell the wet stone and feel the goat-dust and hear too much noise, and his boots would slip on the worn threshold, and he would say, “This is not worth the count,” and leave before asking the proper questions.

A merchant would look down the shaft of light and see nothing but mud-colored pots and think, “There is no luxury here.” He would not know that the pots hold grain measured by generations.

A soldier would say, “This place can be flooded if needed,” and then notice that the entrance is not where he expects, and that the brick ramp is too narrow for horses, and that the air seems to swallow confidence. He will remember the door as worse than it is. He will not be harmed. The place will not be cursed. It will simply appear, in the ordinary way fear appears, as unimportant.

The wise power would not hide the Drip-Hall by making it invisible. They would protect it by making it unattractive to greed. That is often the kindest way for small worlds to survive.

---

They would add one soft place where the rules say there should be no empty surface.

The hall says: every surface must have use. Every wall must carry a shelf, a hook, a niche, a pipe, a crack. Every floor tile must drain or hold water. Every creature must belong to a level. Every plant must earn its place.

But beings grow when even a little space appears where they are not assigned.

So the power would not remove a thing. They would change one thing’s meaning.

Near the low door, between a brazier and a stack of drying reed mats, there is a shelf that was made to hold broken pots no one has yet thrown away. But one broken pot will have a chipped lip that holds rain-water longer than expected. It will become useful to a bird that is afraid to fly too low. Then a child will sit beneath it because the wall there feels neither hot nor cold. Then an old man will rest his elbow there because it fits. No one will make a new rule about it. No one will declare it sacred. But the broken pot and the shelf beside it will begin to collect small rests. Someone will set down a spoon there by habit. Someone else will lean there to breathe during the noisy hour. Someone will press their forehead to the cool brick and close their eyes, and the room will not say anything, and that silence will be the beginning of comfort.

A wise power knows that beings are made of labor. They also know that beings are ruined by labor alone.

So they would create the smallest possible refuge inside an economy that cannot afford one.

---

They would make one water clear.

The Grinding Pot is already a kind of oracle. Cloudy: danger. Clear: quiet. Running over: rain returned too strongly. The people listen to these signs not because they are foolish, but because signs are how a crowded place talks.

The wise power would not make all water clean. Clean water without effort would destroy the whole system: the crabs, the basins, the water-reading, the children’s training, the rules that keep the grain from the goat-pits, the habits that make the room survive.

Instead, they would cause one morning’s film over the Grinding Pot to become especially still and clear.

The sun will come through the shaft and strike it, and for a moment the clay surface will hold light like a dark mirror. A woman will lean to pour a cup and see not prophecy but her own tired face, and behind her the face of a child, and beside that the muzzle of a goat, and above that the shadow of a pigeon from the shelf. For one heartbeat the room will see itself as one creature.

That will not save them from hunger. It will not save them from death. It will not stop the rats from dying in tubes or the lizards from taking their young. But it will make the being more conscious of itself.

And consciousness is sometimes the only blessing worth giving.

---

They would make the noise answer.

The Drip-Hall already has a kind of chorus. Water, claws, hooves, beaks, fire, pots, bronze. At midday, when the light slab falls across the Grinding Pot and dust dances in it, the place can feel as if it is too full of itself. Too loud. Too close. A child will cry from the noise and not understand why, because the room is not cruel, only dense.

The wise power would act through a single adjustment: a hanging clay lamp, already cracked near its top, will tilt a fraction more when someone steps near it. This will make it strike a shelf once, then again, then stop. The sound will be wrong in a way that makes people pause. Someone will say, “Listen,” and they will listen, and beneath the noise they will hear the dripping. The dripping will suddenly seem ordered again, not because it has changed, but because attention has changed.

A person will adjust a hood that is too open. Someone else will close a pot lid properly. Someone will move the mats that were too close to the brazier. The quail will settle. The lizards will return to their warm stone. The room will become loud, but the loudness will become a thing they can live inside, because they have heard the pattern in it.

That is how a wise power works in a made place: not by silencing the world, but by helping it hear itself.

---

They would teach the humans how to be less alone in the room.

The people of the Drip-Hall already know each other by touch: how someone lifts a lid, how they test a tile, how they bend to pass under an arch, how they climb without disturbing the bats. But knowing is not always enough. Sometimes people live beside each other for decades without becoming companions. Sometimes a household is only a set of functions: Drip-Reader, Hood-Setter, Plate-Striker, Mat-Layer. The wise power would not change these names. The names are real. The names are needed. But it would add one thing no one had asked for and no one could notice until it was already there:

a way of asking how another is, without interrupting work.

It might happen through a small change in the bronze plate. When struck, it will carry one new overtone, almost hidden beneath the bright shove of sound. The first time someone hears it, they will feel that the room has turned toward them. They will say, “The hall is warm,” or “The hall is tired,” or “The hall wants everyone near.” The words will be wrong, but the feeling will be right.

It might happen when a child begins to place a dry grain husk at the edge of the Grinding Pot after someone in the household has had a hard day. Not as offering to gods. Not as waste. Just as a way to say: *I saw you work.* Other children will imitate. Adults will pretend not to notice. Then someone sick will find a husk by their pot. Someone grieving will find two. Someone angry will find none, and that will teach more than presence ever could.

The wise power would not replace human care. They would fertilize it.

---

They would allow one death to be mourned without stopping the room.

A goat that has stood for years in one corner will die in the night. The rules require that her body be moved before the others become uneasy. The mats must be changed. The basket below must be emptied. The people must continue. A lesser power would remove all suffering. A cruel power would make suffering meaningless. The wise one would do something harder:

they would make the continuation carry a memory.

In the morning, when the child comes to feed the doe and finds the corner empty, there will be one tuft of wool caught on a rough brick edge where no wool had been before. It will look accidental. It will be enough. The child will touch it and feel warmth that has not fully left, and the child will understand that absence can be touched.

That will not comfort anyone for long. But comfort is not the only thing a wise power gives. Sometimes it gives a place where grief can fit without breaking the system.

The goat will be remembered as the one who knew the north wall was damp. The child will remember how she stood staring at the Grinding Pot as if she saw next week’s weather. The old people will say, “She was too still today,” and everyone will know that they are talking about both the goat and the world.

---

They would prepare the place for change.

The surface may cool. The rain may come later. The grain may need a new kind of water. The bats may shift their roost. The crabs may need a basin that is shallower by a thumb. The world may try to make the Drip-Hall obsolete by making its habits fail.
