## What a Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do

They would begin by not entering.

Not because they were afraid, and not because they were indifferent, but because they had learned the oldest lesson of kindness: a compassionate hand is still a hand, and where it presses, a world is marked.

They would see the Cistern of Stillfall at the split shoulder of the young mountain, and they would understand immediately that the place had not called for rescue. A call requires time. A call requires a before and an after. The Cistern had no before, no after, only the eternal pressure of a present so complete it had forgotten the need for language.

They would look into the basin and see the twin pale eels beneath the third arch, and they would not say, “They are trapped.” They would not say, “They are suffering.” They would not say, “They are waiting.” Those words belong to a world in which change is assumed to be the only evidence of life. Here, life had been translated into persistence. The eels were not prisoners. They were a civic syntax, two living commas in the sentence of the bridge. To remove them would not be to free them. It would be to erase the grammar that made the crossing still crossing, even without feet.

They would look at the plated beast near the colonnade, and they would not call it hungry. Hunger is a motion, and this place has refused motion. They would not call it dead. Death is a transformation, and transformation has no authority here. They would recognize it as a creature of posture, a body that had become weight without becoming burden. Its lowered head was not longing. It was law.

They would look at the salt-furred walker suspended beneath an arch, and their first instinct might be pity. A benevolent power that has become wise knows pity is often the disguise of arrogance. They would stop before that impulse and ask: “Who am I to say that stillness is a failure? Who am I to say that a life without change is a life unwritten?” They would see that the salt crystals on its fur were not chains but citizenship. The walker had not fallen into a trap. It had been admitted into permanence.

And they would understand: to rescue the Cistern from its nature would be to commit violence against it.

So they would do what is harder than miracles.

They would refrain.

They would not drain the basin. They would not add channels to relieve pressure. They would not bring light from the outside to “wake” the city. They would not attempt to move the eels, because movement itself would be a wound. They would not remove a stone, because absence is also a shape here. They would not pour water in, because water is the being. They would not send scientists or priests or sages or children to “discover” it, because discovery is an event, and events are refused.

But they were benevolent. And benevolence, when it is truly wise, cannot merely stand by.

So they would do the one thing that did not disturb the Cistern’s law but still offered kindness.

They would remove the possibility of outside interference.

They would go first to the world around the mountain. They would make the high pass difficult without making it seem forbidding. They would give the basin a reputation for emptiness. They would scatter maps so that this place became blank without becoming famous. They would let travelers forget the way. They would let scholars doubt their instruments. They would make the region feel poor in significance, barren in value, dull to ambition.

This was not concealment as a secret, but concealment as protection.

A place can be destroyed not only by those who hate it, but by those who misunderstand it. The Cistern could be saved to death by someone who thought it was a prison. It could be drained by someone who thought it needed air. It could be catalogued by someone who thought its creatures were specimens. It could be worshipped by someone who thought stillness is only an absence, not a mode of being. It could be made sacred in a way that would ruin it.

So the benevolent power would guard it from holiness, from curiosity, from salvation.

They would set, in the hearts and customs of those who might one day hear of such a place, a law of interpretation: do not assume a being is wrong because it does not behave as you expect it to. Do not mistake stillness for death. Do not mistake permanence for punishment. Do not mistake a city without people for abandonment. Do not mistake held breath for suffocation unless there is evidence of choking.

This too would be an action. Perhaps the greatest.

Then, having learned that noninterference is not enough when one still possesses the power to help, they would return in attention to the rim.

They would look at the convex skin of the water, that impossible wave forbidden to break, and they would ask a question that could not be spoken aloud without becoming an event.

“You may remain,” they would ask, “but are you free to refuse remaining?”

And because the Cistern could not answer without becoming a new resident, because a response in that place would not be sound but form, they would not wait for a reply. They would not demand a sign. They would not require the water to tremble, the eels to blink, the beast to lift its head, the hum to change pitch. They would not make their benevolence a test.

Instead, they would give the city a permission so slight it would not be an interruption.

They would not build a wall. They would not cut a door. They would not make a drain, a pump, a lock, a handle, a bell. They would not introduce anything countable. But they would alter the law only as far as needed to leave a margin: a place where the water could, if it ever chose, spill.

Not because it must.

Not because the power wanted it to.

Only because even a being made of stillness should not be imprisoned by a kindness that cannot be declined.

If the law of the Cistern were a sentence, then this addition would be a comma before the word “forever.”

If the water were a body, this would be a pore that does not breathe.

If the city were an organism, this would be a nerve ending prepared for a future sensation and never used.

In the language of such a place, it would become a resident without becoming a disturbance. A faint relation would appear at the brim: not a ripple, not a wave, not a break, but a threshold of consent. The held surface would remain exactly as it had been. The hum would not falter. No ring would form, for nothing had struck. No creature would move, for movement had not been invited.

But if, in some impossible later-now, the water ever desired to leave, it would not have to violate itself. If the eels ever longed to swim, their motion would not become sacrilege. If the plated beast ever wished to rise, its rising would not be a catastrophe. If the city ever decided that completion had become prison, it would find that the prison had a lawful seam.

And if the city never chose any of these, then the permission would remain what it should be: an unspoken grace. A gift that does not demand use.

The benevolent power would say, without speaking: “I do not make you change. I only make sure that your change, if ever it is wanted, is not made impossible by my presence.”

Then they would withdraw.

They would leave no shrine at the mountain’s edge. They would leave no warning written in stone. They would leave no blessing, no command, no prayer. They would not let their own kindness become a visible fact, because visible kindness can become authority, and authority can become a new stone thrown into the water.

They would leave only the world made slightly less dangerous to the Cistern, and the Cistern made slightly more free to refuse the world’s danger if it ever wished.

And the city would remain.

The twin eels would hang beneath the third arch, their black facet eyes reflecting the dark cyan glow, keeping the grammar of crossing. The plated beast would rest on its thick limbs in the plaza, holding the verb of standing without completing it. The salt-furred walker would remain beneath its arch, a step fulfilled before walking ever became a future. The salt flowers would bloom without blooming. The pyrite needles would glitter without shining. The silica lace would lie over walls like a skin that had no hunger. The gray threads would spread through ceramic towers like permanent memory. The silver veins would continue their quiet parliament of connection, routing pressure without movement, governing surface without law made audible.

The hum would persist.

The darkness would not brighten.

The light would not dim.

The mountain outside may have weather. The stars may wheel. The air may still feel like a rumor. But inside the basin, nothing would tremble, because nothing had been forced.

And if someone came afterward, they would not know what had been done there. They might think the Cistern untouched. They might think no power had come at all. That would be the point.

A truly benevolent and wise power does not want to be thanked. It does not want to be credited. It does not want the world to know how it was saved. Sometimes, the highest gift is that a being is allowed to remain itself without ever discovering that it was nearly made into something else.

If any trace of the power remained, it would be so subtle that even presence would not recognize it. Not a name. Not a ring. Not a creature. Not a building. Not a commandment. Only the fact that the water, which had always been held, now also could be left. Only the fact that stillness, which had been complete, had been made complete by choice instead of by necessity.

In the end, they would have done almost nothing.

And that almost nothing would be the most careful mercy possible.

Because a benevolent and wise power, seeing the Cistern of Stillfall, would not try to wake the drowned city.

It would save it from waking.

It would save it from saving.

It would save it from those who come with good intentions and bring change like a blade.

And if there was anything else to be done, they would do this: they would let the Cistern keep its own silence, even against themselves.

And if, in some unseeable future, the water ever chose to break,

the world would call it a flood.

But it would not be a flood.

It would be the city’s first sentence after a thousand years of listening,

spoken without sound,

broken without breaking,

moved without motion—

and at the rim,

the door it had been given

would open

only because no one asked it to.
