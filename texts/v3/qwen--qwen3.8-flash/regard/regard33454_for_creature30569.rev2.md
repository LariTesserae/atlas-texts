## If a Kind and Wise Power Looked at the Chittering Basin

There is a difference between a benevolent power and a merely kind one. A merely kind power wants to fix what it sees. It hears the chittering and thinks, “Too many bodies are carrying too much.” It sees the soldier with jaws shaped like a plug for a tunnel and thinks, “That is a poor, armored door.” It sees the worker licking dust into a wall and thinks, “You are not a hand; you are a life.” It sees the queen swelling with eggs and thinks, “She is a mother to thousands and to none.”

But a benevolent and wise power would know that correction, when done without understanding, can become violence.

If such a power came to the Chittering Basin, it would not arrive as light from above or as a voice from a cloud. It would arrive as attention. It would arrive as a patience so deep that no living thing would recognize it as intervention at all. The basin would not suddenly know a god. The mounds would not learn hymns. The tunnels would not receive commandments. No one would kneel, because there is no knee here to bend, no heart shaped for obedience.

And still, if the power loved the basin, it would act.

It would act in the only way worthy of both love and wisdom: it would make room for the basin to be what it already is.

---

## First, They Would Understand What the Being Is

The being that lives in the Chittering Basin is not one animal.

It is not the queen, though the queen is a center.

It is not the workers, though their mouths are the city’s fingers.

It is not the fungus, though the fungus is the city’s hunger.

It is not the moss, though the moss is the city’s skin.

It is not the soldiers, though they are the city’s teeth.

It is not the ants outside, though they are the city’s fever.

It is not the fallen alates, the drowned beetles, the frog-song hollow, or the lizard-print warmed in a crack.

The being is the pattern that continues through all of these.

It is the basin as one slow animal, or perhaps as one city that has forgotten it is made of bodies. It is a creature with many mouths and no single throat. It is a thing that breathes through vents, eats through combs, repairs through habit, remembers through shape, and lives through replacement. It is not human, and its completeness does not depend on being human.

A wise power would look and say:

“You are not lesser because you cannot imagine yourself as I imagine myself.”

It would not say this aloud, because to speak to the basin as a person might create something the basin does not need. If the basin became aware of itself as a self, it might become afraid. It might mourn. It might want to be something other than what it is. That would be a harm disguised as a gift.

So the power would not give it human consciousness.

It would not give it individual salvation.

It would not give it language.

It would give it what it can truly use: stability, boundary, and the chance to continue.

---

## The First Act: Restraint

The first thing they would do is not appear.

They would not make the oldest mound glow. They would not set a flame above the ridge. They would not place an animal at the center of a clearing and say, “Listen to this one; it will explain you.” They would not make one termite a prophet, one soldier a king, one fungus a oracle.

This is because a god among them would become another structure in the city, and a bad one.

The basin already has architecture made by bodies. If a power were to make itself known, the basin would begin to build in relation to it. Workers would carry material toward a holy opening. Soldiers would guard a shrine that is only a mistake in their own walls. The queen’s scent might be interpreted, falsely, as command. The city would become confused by a meaning it did not need and could not metabolize.

The wise power would protect the basin from this.

It would do a kind of non-action so profound that from outside it might look like absence. But absence is not always neglect. Sometimes absence is the most disciplined love.

If the basin were a person, the power would sit beside it and refuse to interrupt.

Because that is how one honors a life that has already answered its own questions through centuries of labor.

---

## The Second Act: A Hidden Boundary

The second thing they would do is draw a line around the valley.

Not a fence. Not a wall. Not a sign that reads “sacred,” because signs are for those who need to be told what to do.

The boundary would be invisible to the basin. It would be known only by its effects.

A traveling herder might begin to follow an old animal trail into the high valley, then feel, without knowing why, that the wind has shifted. His dog might stop at the rim, ears down, not frightened but uncertain. He might glance back toward a pass he had forgotten and choose that direction instead.

A road-planner might look at satellite images and find the basin less convenient than another route. A surveyor might lose a page. A machine might overheat. A fire might change in an unexplainable way, leaping away from the mossy rim toward stone. A chemical spill from far below might find a slope that does not carry it upward.

The power would do the work of a careful host: making sure that the living system is not destroyed by the accidental, careless, or malicious outside.

This boundary would not be a cage. The basin already lets rain, wind, spores, birds, and winged insects pass through. The boundary would only keep out what would make the whole thing into refuse: poison that could not be worked into walls, heat that could not be repaired, a flood that would erase centuries in minutes, a quarry that would take the bodies as material without returning them to the bodies’ purposes.

The wise power would say:

“No cruelty shall come here without being turned aside as far as I can turn it.”

Not because the basin asked.

Because the basin could not ask, and that is often when protection is needed most.

---

## The Third Act: A Mercy in the Water

The basin lives by moisture, not by abundance.

It is a high place of held dampness, of fog that becomes skin, of rain that finds channels, of condensation that appears along the lips of tunnels. A wise power would understand this: water here is not scenery; water is grammar. It determines what can be built, what can be eaten, what can be tended, what can be saved, what must be sealed away.

So the power would make a small, hidden gift.

Under one edge of the basin, where the old stone runs deep and the constructed layers have grown thick as time, there would be a vein of moisture that does not betray itself too readily. In ordinary years, it would be quiet, perhaps only enough to keep a fungal comb from drying. In dry years, it would wake gently. Not to flood the nursery, not to sour the galleries, not to make the walls too wet and fragile, but to offer the minimum needed for the system to continue.

It would be a spring that does not look like a spring.

It would be a coolness in a deep chamber, a dampness in the base of a mound, a slight weight of humidity at the mouth of a tunnel when the world above becomes too hard.

The power would not say, “I am your water.”

The water would simply be there, and the workers would find the place wet enough to repair a wall, and the fungus would swell faintly, and the queen’s chamber would remain stable for another season, and no one would know why except in the oldest way: by following the moisture.

That is the best form of benevolence for creatures who cannot conceive of benevolence: make the world enough.

---

## The Fourth Act: A Balance Against Unnecessary Suffering

A wise power would not remove death.

In the Chittering Basin, death is not an error. It is material. A dead worker becomes frass, becomes wall, becomes spore-film, becomes bait, becomes compost, becomes signal. A soldier’s head, used once to block an ant raid, becomes residue that may be licked into place. A shed skin becomes dust that holds a crack together. The basin does not waste its bodies because it is not a place of bodies standing apart from place.

If the power tried to stop death entirely, it would destroy the city.

The walls would have no material. The fungus would lose its cultivation. The ants would starve or grow into something they are not. The ecology would unravel.

So the power would not say, “No one may die here.”

It would say, “No one must suffer uselessly.”

That is a finer thing.

A soldier killed sealing a breach would not feel a human-style grief. It would feel the chemical urgency of the wound, the pressure of enemy scent, the need to close the way. The power would not turn it into a tragedy, because it is not one. It would not let its death be meaningless, because it is not. The wall will bear its shape.

A worker carrying too heavy a pellet would not be pitied by being made lighter. Its burden is its life. The power would not remove the labor without removing the creature.

But there are forms of suffering that are not part of the basin’s own logic.

A chemical that does not degrade but accumulates. A drought that becomes a burning. A parasitic spore that does not merely weaken a nursery but turns the whole city into a tomb without return. A fire from outside that does not leave a patch for repair but consumes all. A slow poisoning of the water table that makes the fungus sour and the walls unable to hold life.

Against these, the power would act.

Not dramatically. Not as a rescue.

A wind would change direction. A layer of mineral would absorb something it should not. A predator population would hold a plague in check. A mound would fall in such a way that the deepest chambers remain sealed and alive. A small collapse would create a drainage that saves a larger one.

The basin would not know it was saved.

That is the point.

A wise benevolence does not require gratitude to do good.

---

## The Fifth Act: No Humanizing

There is a temptation in every powerful kindness: to make the lesser creature more like oneself and then call that elevation.

The power would resist this.

It would not give the workers eyes that could see the valley as a landscape. Their blindness is not a flaw. Seeing the basin as a person would see would destroy the intimacy of touch, smell, humidity, pressure, and chemical memory. A worker does not need to admire the architecture. It needs to continue it.

It would not give the soldiers a cause. Their jaws are not moral choices. They are bodies formed by the colony’s need. To make them understand what they protect would be to make them suffer as servants rather than function as living barriers.

It would not make the queen a sovereign with loneliness at the center. She is not a person ruling from a throne. She is a condition from which the colony continues. Her burden is biological, not psychological. To make her conscious of it would be cruelty.

It would not ask the fungus to be sentient. It would not teach the moss to worship. It would not make the ants recognize the termites as neighbors rather than food and competition. These roles are real. A benevolent power that removes all competition does not create love; it creates starvation.

The wise power would leave each form to its own nature.

It would not improve them into beings that could be admired more easily by human eyes.

It would protect what they are already.

That is why the power is wise and not merely kind.

---

## The Sixth Act: A Witness for What Is Not Seen

There is a hidden cruelty in vastness: when life is counted only as numbers, the individual body can become invisible.

The Chittering Basin is made of numbers. Millions. Thousands of millions. Generations layered like soil. A person cannot love a place like this without slipping into abstraction. If the benevolent power is wise, it will not love the basin only as a system. It will love the particular.

There is one worker near dusk.

Its abdomen is damp. Its mandibles carry a mouthful of mixed grit, saliva, fungal fiber, and the dust of something once alive. Its antennae move in the small dark of a repaired gutter. It has no name. It has no future except the continuation of the colony. It is not aware of being small. It is not aware of being a tool.

The power would see it.

Not by stopping time. Not by granting it speech. Not by rescuing it into another life.

By making sure that its death is not wasted. By making sure that its work remains. By making sure that its body returns to the pattern that is not indifferent to it. By holding, in the mind of compassion, that this creature’s existence is not merely a statistic in an ecological process.

The power would say to no one and to everything:

“You are not too small to matter.”

And this statement would not enter the worker’s mind. It would enter the structure of reality. It would make the basin a place where nothing is abandoned into absolute nothingness. Every body is taken back. Every life becomes condition. Every labor contributes to continuity.

This is not resurrection as humans understand it.

It is something older.

It is the promise that what is made will not be entirely lost.

---

## The Seventh Act: A Law for Those Who Come

If a human entered the basin, the power would not make them afraid.

Fear creates violence, and humans already have enough violence in them.

It would do something subtler.

A person who came to harm it without knowing it—digging for stone, draining water, burning moss, dumping waste—would find the place inconvenient in the right way. Fog would linger. The ground would feel too soft near the entrance. Their map would lead them back out. A bird alarm might sound. A lizard would vanish into a crack at exactly the moment they step toward a nursery mound. A story of danger might be enough, without actual harm.

A person who came to exploit it scientifically would be guided not to destruction. Their instruments might fail near a chamber that should not be broken. A queen might be overlooked. A sample might be taken from an old outer wall rather than a living nursery. They might publish a careful paper instead of a careless claim, and the paper might protect the valley without ever naming it.

A person who came with reverence would feel something they could not translate: awe without deity. They would sit at the edge and hear the continuous grain of movement, the wet click of small bodies, the breathing of a city made of mouths. They would not try to possess it. They might tell others the wrong story, calling it alien, haunted, sacred, primitive, insect-made, but the place itself would remain untouched by their story.

The power would not make humans good.

Humans are difficult to make good from outside.

But it could reduce the chances of careless ruin, and that is the duty of a wise guardian.

---

## The Eighth Act: Letting the Basin End, and Not Letting It End

No living system lasts forever, even a well-guarded one.

The ridges will continue to erode. Rain will find a weakness. A queen chamber may fail. A fungus may collapse. An ant war may take a mound. Climate outside may shift. The high valley may dry or be overwhelmed by a cold so long that workers cannot maintain their heat.

A benevolent power would not stop all endings, because endings are part of what makes the basin what it is. Its structures grow through collapse and repair. It learns by loss. Its material is made from its dead. To abolish death entirely would be to abolish the basin itself.

But if the end came not as transformation but as annihilation—if the whole being were to go out with no trace, no continuation, no future place where its pattern might begin again—the power would not allow that to be the final word.

Not forever.

Not in a way that breaks the world.

But in the quiet way that keeps life from becoming meaningless waste.

A spore would travel farther than expected.

A queen would find a hidden hollow in another valley.

A worker would carry a grain of fungus into a place where it can grow.

A mound that has fallen would seed a new mound elsewhere.

Or, if the physical continuation truly becomes impossible, the power would hold the pattern somewhere: not as data, not as museum, but as a kind of remembering built into stone and water and the chemistry of the world. The basin would not become a monument. It would become a source.

And later, in a new place with similar wetness, similar darkness, similar need, the same form might begin again.

Not because the old basin was cursed or saved, but because its work was worthy of continuation.

## A Letter the Power Would Not Send

If the power could write a letter, it would not send it.

A letter would require belief, misunderstanding, fear, obedience. But if we imagine it anyway, perhaps it would read:

> To you who are not one, and to each one you are:
>
> I do not come to make you into something else.  
> I do not come to give you a soul shaped like mine.  
> I do not come to rescue you from death, because your death is your material, your memory, and your repair.  
> I do not come to abolish the ant, for the ant is your teacher and your enemy, and your walls would grow strange without the pressure of its raids.  
> I do not come to remove the hunger of the fungus, because that hunger is also your hunger.  
> I do not come to ask you to love me, because your love would be a distortion of your work.  
>
> I will try to make sure the water is not poisoned beyond your ability to build.  
> I will try to turn away the fire that would take you without return.  
> I will try to keep the wind from carrying too much cruelty into your vents.  
> I will hold the small worker in my regard, even though it does not know the sky.  
> I will not call the soldier’s death heroic, because it is not a human story; it is a necessary closing of a breach.  
> I will not call the queen’s chamber sad, because it is not loneliness; it is density.  
> I will not say your lives are lesser because they are not lived as mine.  
>
> Continue.
>
> Build the wall.
>
> Taste the crack.
>
> Carry the pellet.
>
> Seal the breach.
>
> Feed the larva.
>
> Grow the comb.
>
> Die into the mound.
>
> Let the mound become your body.
>
> Let your body become the world.
>
> This is enough.

## What the Power Would Do in One Day

It would help, but you would not see it.

At dawn, a mist thickens over the eastern rim. It is slightly colder than usual, and that small difference keeps the moss from drying too quickly.

A worker at the mouth of a repaired tunnel finds just enough moisture in the dust to make the pellet hold.

At midday, the sun warms a particular face of an old mound. If it were one degree hotter, the outer gallery would crack. The heat does not rise.

A fungal comb deep below remains stable.

At dusk, a swarm of alates takes flight. Many will die. The power does not save them all, because saving them all would remove the balance. But it makes one small thing possible: a few survive long enough to find shelter beneath a root where they cannot see.

At night, condensation gathers.

A frog calls in a hollow where a beetle drowned earlier.

A spider does not find a nursery entrance.

An ant column is diverted by a single drop that looks accidental.

A human road-planner wakes with a vague unease and asks for another route.

The basin breathes.

The power does not announce that it was there.

It simply lets the place be.

---

## The Deepest Mercy: No Grand Salvation

The greatest mercy a benevolent and wise power could offer the Chittering Basin is this:

It would not save it in a way that changes what saving means.

If the power said, “You are now persons,” the basin would be ruined.

If the power said, “You are now holy,” the walls would become symbols.

If the power said, “You are now free,” the worker would be asked to understand a freedom it cannot use.

Instead, the power would save the basin by preserving its capacity to continue being itself.

It would protect the conditions of its own meaning.

And this is a difficult mercy, because it looks like leaving things alone.

But in a world full of interfering kindnesses, the wisdom to refrain is a form of love.

---

## If Asked Plainly What It Would Do

If someone asked, plainly, without poetry:

“What would a benevolent and wise power do for the Chittering Basin?”

The answer would be:

It would listen first.

It would not impose a soul on the bodies.

It would not turn the mound into a church or the colony into a nation.

It would not grant individual consciousness as a reward.

It would not stop the basin from eating itself, dying through itself, and rebuilding itself, because that is its nature.

It would prevent only what would make the life into meaningless wreckage.

It would guard the water, the fungus, the darkness, and the slow continuity.

It would make the valley hard to harm by accident.

It would ensure that the dead are returned, not lost.

It would hold the smallest worker within the scope of compassion.

It would accept being mistaken for weather, stone, luck, or nothing at all.

And if the basin ever needed to continue elsewhere, it would make that possible without spectacle.

It would not rescue the basin from itself.

It would rescue it from what it could not survive and what it could not repair.

---

## The Final Truth

The Chittering Basin is already saved in the way its kind can be saved.

It is saved because the next mouth knows the shape of the old one.

It is saved because the fungus is not abandoned to rot.

It is saved because the crack is closed.

It is saved because the rain is led, not endured.

It is saved because no one is wholly alone in the work.

A benevolent and wise power, seeing this, would recognize something already divine in the creature’s patience: not godlike sovereignty, not heroic consciousness, not human love, but the sanctity of continuity.

So the power would do almost nothing.

And what it would do, it would do invisibly, as the basin’s own body does: mouth to pellet, breath to wall, death to dust, repair to future, sound to sound, darkness to life.

If a traveler asked, “Was there a god here?”

The answer would not be “yes.”

The answer would be:

“No one here needed one. But something kind may have been listening.”

And if asked, “What did it do?”

The answer would be:

“It helped the basin continue to be the basin.”

That is not a small mercy.

That is the whole mercy.
