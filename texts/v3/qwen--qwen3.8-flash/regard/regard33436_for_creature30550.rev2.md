## What a Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do

A benevolent power would want to rescue.

A wise power would ask first: *Rescue from what?*

For when such a power looked down upon the Hollow of Anhydrous Green, it would see a place that could so easily be mistaken for a lack. No water. No moss. No wet seam in the rock. No dew. No river to carry sound or rot or renewal through the ordinary human way. The light comes from mirrors. The ground is a living textile of roots. The city does not sit upon the earth so much as it is woven into it. The people are dry as cured bark, their grief held inside salt and bone, their dead compressed into walls and streets, their industry exhaling mineral vapor instead of smoke, their breath moving through shafts like a slow mechanical season.

A power that was only generous might come down and say: *This is too strange. This is too hard. This is too dry. I will make it easier.*

It might open a cleft in the stone and send a river through the cavern. It might summon rain from an invisible ceiling. It might plant ordinary grasses that soften underfoot, or let puddles form in the hollows, or send a shaft of direct sunlight through the rock to burn away the filtered, careful, invited light of the mirrors.

And the Hollow would die.

Not immediately. Perhaps not even in a way a surface observer would call death. But the root-mat would swell beyond its brittle civility. The dry streets would soften and tear. The mirror-bases would cloud. The oil-shrubs would rot or drown in their own chemistry. The Thread-cats would lose their dry footing on the upper cables. The Dust-herds would wander into unfamiliar dampness. The salted filaments that had learned to become roads would forget their purpose. The fermentation vaults would begin to leak. The Dryborn would sweat for the first time in generations, and their sweat would be a failure, because they had built a self that could not safely open to the air.

So the wise power would hesitate.

It would look again.

It would see that the absence of water is not an accident or a punishment. It is the grammar of the place. The Hollow has not failed to become ordinary. It has succeeded in becoming itself.

That, perhaps, would be the first act of benevolence: not to correct the world, but to recognize it.

## The Being the Power Would Notice

The prompt says *the being who lives in it*, and there are so many possible beings in the Hollow.

It could be a child standing before an unfinished stairway whose upper landing has not yet been grown.

It could be an elder pressing the heel of one hand to a wall and feeling, through the faintest vibration, whether the room beyond is warm or cooling.

It could be a Mirror-Keeper at dusk, adjusting a lens with brass and patience while the last light column crawls across the terraces.

It could be a Root-Stitcher with cracked palms, binding a torn street back into firmness.

It could be a young Seed-Diver wearing a mesh hood, standing in an updraft while hard pods strike the nets like tiny dry fists.

It could be a Surface Trader who cannot stop asking about rain and is gently mocked for it by Dryborn children.

It could be a Thread-Cat watching from the beams, many-eyed and silent, a living alarm system and dust-brush provider and minor civic blessing.

It could be the Hollow itself—the slow, blind, patient household of roots, dust, oil, mirrors, engines, animals, salt, memory, and unfinished rooms.

If one being had to be named, let it be someone small.

Let it be a child of the Dryborn, perhaps one named Vane, perhaps one called Quiet Beam, perhaps one with a name that is also the name of a young plant in a nursery yard. The child stands at the edge of a new district where the city has not yet made up its mind. The stairs climb into air. The balconies wait for walls that will not exist for another season. The ground beneath the child is soft and fibrous, pale as old paper, and somewhere under it the raw stone still knows nothing of city.

The child is not sad in the ordinary wet way. There is no weeping. There is only a stillness, a pressure behind the eyes, a dry cough that is not illness but loneliness.

The child is asking the question all living beings eventually ask when they are surrounded by labor:

*Does this matter?*

*Does my work matter?*

*Does the fact that I am unfinished mean I am wrong?*

*Is there somewhere else where everything is complete?*

*Is this place a mistake waiting to be forgiven?*

The benevolent and wise power would hear this.

It would feel the old pull to answer with grandeur: *I am here. You are not alone. The city is complete. The gods have seen you. The dry world is beautiful.*

But wisdom would make it quiet.

Because if the power shouted too loud, the child would become dependent. If the power solved too much, the Dryborn would become guests in their own existence. If the power made every missing stair appear, the city would stop growing into itself. If the power declared the Hollow sacred, it might become a temple and cease to be a place where people mend mirrors, feed beetles, herd Dust-herds, argue about rootward boundaries, and press their dead into useful walls.

The power would not make the city perfect.

Perfection would be a kind of death.

Instead, it would do something almost unbearably small, if done with wisdom.

It would enter the grammar of the Hollow.

## What It Would Do First

If the power came, it would not come as light.

The light is already managed by hand and patience.

It would come as a slight change in pressure.

In the upper shafts, the dry wind would shift a breath. In the mirror-galleries, one lens would need no adjustment for a single night. In the nursery yard, one young plant that had been failing would put out a new leaf of translucent oil-stalk. In the house of the child named Vane, a crack in the rootboard wall would close while the inhabitants slept.

No one would call this a miracle.

They would say the grove had consented.

They would say the stitching was good.

They would say the Mirror-Keeper was lucky.

They would say the seed had remembered its purpose.

And that is why it would be wise.

The power would make sure its help did not humiliate the Hollow into passivity. It would not say, *I finished this stair for you.* It would say, through the smallest available language of the world: *You were already doing the work. Here is one small thing that could have gone wrong and does not. Now continue.*

That would be its first act:

To protect the city from the arrogance of rescue.

## What It Would Do for the Being

The being—the child, the elder, the Mirror-Keeper, the Thread-Cat, the unfinished stairway, the root-mat itself—would feel noticed in a way that did not require explanation.

Perhaps the dust would fall differently.

Perhaps for one evening the light column would be broad enough to reveal every mote in the air, so the child could stand inside it and not be burned, only warmed by the strange feeling of being seen.

Perhaps a powder-moth would land on the child’s wrist and stay there, brushing the skin with pale mineral scales. Not a message. Not a symbol. Not a command. Just a living thing choosing to rest there.

Perhaps a Thread-Cat would come low across the beams one night and shed a small tuft of dry fur into the child’s lap. The child would know, without being told, that this was a gift. In a Hollow where almost nothing is waste, a fur tuft from the upper guardian is not nothing. It becomes brush, binding, warmth, or memory.

Perhaps the child would press a palm to the wall and feel something that was not exactly a voice.

Not: *I love you.*

Not: *All will be well.*

But: *You are part of something that is trying to become.*

That would be enough.

The benevolent power would not remove the child’s need to work.

The child must still learn to read root-creak. Must still climb carefully where the architecture is only half grown. Must still seal wounds, carry oil-flasks, listen to crickets, watch the mirror-bases, understand the difference between dry rot and healthy breath, and know that some rooms have ceilings before their walls and that this is not haunting but hope.

The power would not make the child safe from failure.

It would make failure less lonely.

It would arrange that when the child dropped a seed-pod and watched it roll into a crack too thin to retrieve, the ground would not swallow it without meaning. The pod would lodge in the root-mat. It would be cracked by beetles, then winnowed into dust, then remembered by the city’s slow metabolism. The child would not know this immediately. The child would only feel, later, that the loss was not the end of the story.

This is what a wise benevolence does.

It does not erase loss.

It gives loss a place inside the living system.

## What It Would Not Do

It would not become a god.

This is important.

If it made itself too visible, the Dryborn might begin to build temples instead of mirrors. They might stop trusting the creak of the root-mat and start waiting for divine instruction. The Root-Stitchers might tie knots in prayer instead of training streets into firmness. The Air-Engineers might ask shafts for permission instead of listening to pressure. The Herders might stop watching the Dust-herds and begin interpreting omens. The unfinished districts might become holy wounds rather than civic futures.

That would not help.

That would be another kind of flood.

So the power would keep its own signature faint.

It would work through maintenance, timing, consent, and the ordinary patience of the place. It would leave no commandments carved in stone, because stone in the Hollow is being taken up by roots and needs no new divine law. It would leave no book of perfect answers, because the Dryborn already have a way of knowing things: by dust, light, vibration, oil, and the sound of dry fruit falling.

If people argued about it afterward, they would disagree.

Some would say: *It was a good pressure change.*

Some would say: *The grove was healthy.*

Some would say: *The mirror was well-kept.*

Some would say: *We dreamt it.*

Some would say: *Nothing happened, but something stopped happening wrong.*

And the power would be satisfied.

Because belief in a benevolent god can enslave a people to hope. Belief in their own careful labor can free them to continue.

## What It Would Do About the Unfinished Places

The Hollow is mid-sentence.

That is one of its deepest beauties and one of its subtle sufferings. The child who sees a stairway end in air may wonder what the world will become. The old worker who sees a grove swallow a quarry wall may wonder whether the city is growing or simply forgetting its boundaries. The herder who watches predators circle the outer terraces may wonder how much wildness is a system and how much is failure.

A foolish power would finish everything at once.

A benevolent and wise power would protect the meaning of the unfinished.

It would not fill every gap.

It would not complete the district overnight.

It would not make the future safe.

Instead, it would do one quiet thing:

It would let one unfinished stair connect to the floor above during a night when a Root-Stitcher crew was already tired, already close to giving up, already arguing that the district should not have been started because the stone was too raw and the root-mat too uncertain.

The stair would not be a palace.

It would be rough.

Its landing would still be soft. Its rail would be only half-trained vine. A few young tendrils would still curl upward, reaching for a beam that would not strengthen for years.

But the stair would work.

It would bear weight.

It would lead somewhere.

And when the crew came at dawn and found it, they would be confused.

They would not say, *A god made this.*

They would say, *Did we miss a shift?*

Another would say, *No, look. The root-mat took our last stitch and grew it into place.*

They would check the resin lines. They would test the pressure of the lower roots. They would find no miracle that insulted their intelligence. They would find only a small, timely cooperation between their labor and the green’s patience.

Then they would weep—not with water, but with dry salt and relief—and they would go on working.

That is what benevolence should do when it is wise.

It gives just enough evidence that the work matters, but not so much evidence that the workers stop working.

## What It Would Do About Grief

The Hollow’s dead are not ghosts.

They are ground.

They are memory compressed into root and wall. The ancestors are folded into streets, door cores, lens paste, resin-board, mineral skin. The Dryborn do not visit them by speaking to empty rooms. They walk upon them. They live within them. They eat from groves grown from their own civic memory.

But there is a particular grief here: dry grief.

Grief that cannot leave the body through tears. Grief that crystallizes at the corners of the eyes. Grief that becomes cough, salt, irritation, a tendency to breathe through flat teeth, a long silence at the mirror-base. Grief that must be made useful because the world is not soft enough to waste even death.

The wise power would not remove this.

To remove dry grief would be to remove a truth of the Hollow: that sorrow must be metabolized into something that can help the living structure.

But it would do one tender thing.

It would make certain that the dead were not only material, not only useful, not only anonymous civic memory.

If an elder died, their hands might leave a pattern in the wall.

If a Mirror-Keeper died, the angle of a lens might be easier for their child.

If a Root-Stitcher died, a street might remain firm exactly where they had taught it to bear weight.

If a Seed-Diver died, one particular pod might fall into a net years later, as if the rhythm returned.

The Dryborn might not call this contact.

They would call it *the floor remembering.*

And that would be enough.

Because the being who lives in the Hollow would learn that death is not silence.

It is not erasure.

It is not merely recycling.

It is civic tenderness with a long delay.

## What It Would Do About Fear

There is fear in the Hollow.

Not always named. Not always admitted. Dry places do not make big wet fears.

But the child fears the unfinished district where rooms do not yet have ceilings. The worker fears the raw crack that smells too warm, because that means the green below is moving faster than the city above. The herder fears the Rasping Owl’s cry when the light dims. The elder fears the long cough that means mineral dust has gone too deep. The Surface Trader fears a place that has learned to live without what they cannot live with. The Dryborn fear becoming surface, because the surface world is wet, soft, leaking, and strange beyond endurance.

The benevolent power would not make the Hollow safe.

Safety without risk would make a museum of it.

Instead, it would strengthen the city’s ways of warning.

A hairline creak in a support-vine would arrive one breath before danger.

A Thread-Cat would shift high into the beams a moment before an accident.

A cricket’s pulse would change enough for a workshop to open an air-shaft in time.

A Dust-herd would turn away from a slope that looked fine but was beginning to fail.

A Mirror-Keeper would notice a light column trembling for no obvious reason and, because of that noticing, adjust a lens that prevents a fire in the next hour.

The power would make the world slightly more legible, not less dangerous.

This is a very particular wisdom.

If you remove danger from a place that has learned to live by reading danger, you rob it of competence. You make it blind. You make it dependent.

The benevolent and wise power would do the opposite:

It would sharpen the senses.

It would make the root-mat speak more clearly to those who already know how to listen.

## What It Would Do About the People Who Leave

Not everyone is born for the Hollow.

Some newcomers cannot adjust. Their skin peels. Their lungs ache. They dream of rain and wake with cracked lips. They may choose to climb back toward the surface.

Some Dryborn are born into a rootward but do not belong to it. They want the shafts. They want the outer terraces. They want to see what the mirrors bend and why light is invited downward. They do not wish to leave forever, but they wish to know there is a possible door.

A cruel power would trap them in the Hollow by making it the only way to survive.

A foolish power would release them into a surface they cannot safely reenter.

A benevolent and wise power would do something more subtle.

It would make the edges honest.

It would place one dry passage at the outermost terrace: not flooded with light from the sun, not welcoming with ordinary rain, but sealed in the right way, breathable, cool, passable, marked only by the Dryborn who need such marks. It would not be advertised to the city as a temptation. It would not become a cult destination. It would be a real option, nothing more.

If a person climbed it and saw the surface, they would see weather, wetness, noise, mud, open sky, and all the things that make the Dryborn uneasy.

They would have the chance to hate it.

Or to miss it.

Or to return.

And if they returned, they would not return as a failure.

They would return as a citizen who had tested the rumor and chosen, again, the dry grammar of home.

The power would say, in a language too modest for speech:

*You are not a captive of this place. You are its inhabitant. If you leave, you may leave. If you stay, let it be because the ground answers your feet, not because the alternative was destroyed or forbidden.*

That would be a mercy that respects the Hollow.

Because the greatest danger to a strange and hard place is not its harshness.

It is the story that one must escape it.

Sometimes escape is needed.

Sometimes the only true blessing is to know escape is possible, and to choose the hard home anyway.

## What It Would Do About Meaning

The Dryborn have a civic world. Their names are bound to seeds. Their marriages are tested by groves. Their ancestors become walls. Their streets are grown. Their light is managed. Their labor is dry, rhythmic, unannounced. Their gods, if they have any, would not be loud.

A benevolent power could give them a grand purpose.

It could say: *This Hollow is the last place in the world where true balance exists. Save it. Perfect it. Make it a model. Bring the surface to its dry perfection.*

But wise power knows that grand purposes crush small lives.

It knows a city like the Hollow cannot be saved as an argument against the surface. It cannot be made into a proof that life does not need water, because that would make it a strange propaganda rather than a place. It cannot be made into a temple of industrious harmony, because temples demand belief and dry industrial ecology demands maintenance.

So the power would give them no grand purpose.

It would give them something quieter:

*Let your ordinary continuance matter.*

Let the child learning to listen to crickets be enough.

Let the worker dusting a mirror-frame with beetle-shell brush be enough.

Let the herder reading the Dust-herd’s silent drift be enough.

Let the elder letting a powder-moth rest on the wrist be enough.

Let the stair that is not yet finished be enough.

Let the unfinished city not be an apology.

Let the dry tears not be a deficiency.

Let the salt-crusted beak of a Mica-Swallow not be a lesser bird because it does not drink from streams.

Let the oil-pipe not be a failed water-pipe.

Let the Hollow not be a failure of earth.

Let it be an answer earth gives when the question is difficult.

That would be the power’s deepest kindness.

It would release the place from the need to justify itself as a version of somewhere else.

## What It Would Do for the Root-Mat

If the being who lives in the Hollow is not a Dryborn person but the green itself—the vast root-plate, the living floor, the civic organism that thickens under roads and thins under plazas—then the benevolent power would not make it speak.

That would be a violation.

A root-mat that has learned to communicate through pressure, vibration, growth, dust-release, and consent does not need human language. If the power gave it a voice, the voice would distort the very thing that makes it wise. It would become a person, and persons argue, command, plead, accuse. The root-mat must remain a system of responses, not a character seeking justice.

But the power could do this:

It could make sure the Dryborn understood one fact that they already half know:

*The floor’s consent is not merely useful. It is worthy of moral attention.*

Not because the root-mat has a soul in the surface way.

Not because it suffers like an animal.

But because it organizes obligations.

It decides where streets may become firm.

It decides whether a grove will accept a tapping.

It decides whether a plaza will breathe or crack.

It decides, with blind patience, whether human habit is beneficial or destructive.

The benevolent power would not make the root-mat worshipful.

It would make the humans better listeners.

A Root-Stitcher would pause longer before tightening a cable.

A Grove-Warden would feel the pressure under the palm and hesitate before saying yes to tapping.

A factory planner would notice that the exhaust is not merely a resource but a request, a demand, a partnership.

The Dryborn would not say, *The green speaks.*

They would say, *The green does not lie.*

And that would be enough.

## What It Would Do About the Surface

The Surface Traders bring rumors of water.

Some of these rumors are true. Some are mistaken. Some are too full of wetness for the Dryborn mind to hold comfortably. But the Hollow has a peculiar danger here: it may define itself against the surface too completely. It may say, *We are not them. We are dry. We do not need their soft world.*

A benevolent power would not make that pride false.

The pride is true.

But it would keep the pride from becoming a wall.

It would let a Surface Trader’s child, frightened by the dry air, find a Thread-Cat’s shadow moving along a beam and feel, for no reason, that the place is guarding them.

It would let a Dryborn child look at a drawing of rain and not feel shame, only curiosity.

It would let a Mirror-Keeper adjust a lens in such a way that a shaft of light from the surface becomes readable like a letter: *The world above is not your enemy. You are its different organ.*

It would prevent the Hollow from becoming a prison of resentment.

If the Dryborn hate the surface too much, they will forget why they love their own dryness.

Love is enough.

The wise power would help them keep love.

## What It Would Do About Suffering

A benevolent power cannot ignore pain.

But a wise one knows that pain is not always a problem to be solved. Sometimes pain is a sensor. Sometimes pain is the cost of being alive in a difficult system. Sometimes pain is the only language a place has for telling its inhabitants that a boundary has been broken.

The Hollow has its own small cruelties.

Dust in the lungs.

Root cuts on the palms.

Burning from a badly angled light column.

A child coughing metallic taste after sleeping beneath an unapproved mirror-base.

A worker whose oil-bath turns rancid in a way no one noticed until the grove failed.

An elder sealed into a door core and remembered only by the next person who opens that door.

The power would not abolish suffering.

It would prevent suffering from becoming meaningless.

It would make sure that when a child is frightened by the Rasping Owl’s cry, there are pairs to walk with.

It would make sure that when a factory’s byproduct feeds scavengers, the scavengers are not simply used but accounted for in the city’s ethics.

It would make sure that when a Dryborn dies, the dead are not only recycled but witnessed.

It would make sure that when an Unfinished Settler lives in a house without a ceiling, they are not mocked as poor, but respected as a citizen of the future.

It would make sure that when someone is too dry, too strange, too exhausted, too stubborn for comfort, the ground does not become cold toward them.

This is what benevolence would do if it were wise:

It would not make the Hollow a paradise.

It would make it a place where suffering cannot easily become cruelty.

## What It Would Do for the Children

Children are the truest test of a benevolent power.

If a power is wise, it will care for the child before the monument.

In the Hollow, a child is born into dryness. The first act is not washing but sealing: talc, resin, mineral dust, oil rubbed into the shoulders. The child’s cry is startling because the lungs fill with cool filtered air. The child is named in a light-column. The child’s name is bound to a seed. The child learns to walk on living streets before learning to recite laws.

A foolish power might make childhood gentle.

But gentleness without competence becomes softness, and softness would be alien here.

A benevolent and wise power would give the child something more difficult:

Enough safety to learn.

Enough danger to grow.

Enough uncertainty to imagine.

It would let the child climb young root-arches that are not yet load-bearing, but not so young that the arch fails under them.

It would let the child chase powder-moths through late light without inhaling too much mineral dust.

It would let the child roll seed-pods down dry channels and hear the knock of future food.

It would let the child listen to cricket-tubes and feel that the world has a pulse.

It would make sure the child is not only a citizen of the living system but a citizen of affection.

The child would not grow up believing that the Hollow owes them perfection.

They would grow up believing that the Hollow has already answered them by existing.

And when the child asks, *Is there water everywhere else?* the elder will answer, *Yes, some places have too much of it.* And the child will not feel excluded.

They will feel that the world is wide and strange and that dryness is one of its valid answers.

That would be a gift.

Not water.

Meaning.

## What It Would Do at the End of Its Attention

The benevolent power would not remain.

This is the hardest part.

If it remained, the Hollow would become a shrine, a dependency, a place forever waiting for the next kindness. The Dryborn would look upward not to adjust mirrors but to await revelation. They would stop trusting the quiet negotiation of roots, animals, dust, and engines. They would make the power more responsible than their own care.

And that would be the final failure of even the best intention.

So, after doing the small things—the sealed crack, the successful stitch, the remembered dead, the legible warning, the honest choice, the child’s recognized dignity—the power would withdraw.

It would withdraw into the ordinary.

It would become the slight change in pressure that no one can prove.

It would become the reason a mirror stayed steady.

It would become the reason a stair connected.

It would become the reason a mother, exhausted and dry-eyed, felt for one moment that the rootward was holding her.

It would become the reason a stranger could survive the first season.

It would become the reason no one built an altar.

And if a child asked, *Did something come here?*

The elder might answer:

*Something was always here.*

*You are thinking too loudly.*

*Listen to the creak.*

*The ground is still consenting.*

And the power, if it has any kindness left in its absence, would allow the child to believe that, too.

## What It Would Do, If Anything

It would do almost nothing.

And that almost nothing would be the thing.

It would not bring water.

It would not bring sun.

It would not finish the city.

It would not abolish the predators.

It would not make death gentle.

It would not make the surface unnecessary.

It would not make the Dryborn comfortable.

It would make them more themselves.

It would protect the Hollow from being saved.

It would protect the being from being rescued.

It would protect the work from becoming worthless because miracle made it unnecessary.

It would protect love from becoming worship.

It would protect labor from becoming prayer.

It would protect the unfinished from becoming holy in a way that forbids completion.

It would protect the dry tears from being mistaken for indifference.

It would protect the dust-light from being called false sun.

It would protect the root-mat from being called a thing.

It would protect the ancestors from being only compost.

It would protect the animals from being only tools.

It would protect the child from being only the future.

And if it did one visible thing, it would be so small that no one could call it proof.

Perhaps a stair.

Perhaps a mirror.

Perhaps a moth resting on a wrist.

Perhaps one dead person’s handprint in the wall, unmistakably known by the person who lived beside them.

Perhaps one dry shaft opening to the surface for those who choose it, not as a command, but as a courtesy.

Then it would leave.

And the Hollow would go on.

The mirrors would tilt.

The fans would turn.

The Dust-herds would move from warm basins to cooler grasses.

The Thread-cats would settle into the beams.

The Saltbacks would gather near the vents.

The factories would breathe mineral vapor.

The children would listen to crickets.

The elders would dust the lens-frames.

The unfinished rooms would keep becoming.

And somewhere under the lowest terrace, raw stone would wait, not because it is cursed, but because it is not yet needed.

The place would not be saved.

The place would be allowed.

And if the benevolent and wise power had any pride left, it would know that this is the highest form of love a being can receive:

To be seen fully,

understood truly,

and left free to continue.
