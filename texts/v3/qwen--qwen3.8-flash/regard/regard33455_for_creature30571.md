They would not come to repair the pump-yard as one repairs a road, and they would not come to save the being as one saves a lamb. That is the mistake the young make when they are called benevolent: they confuse kindness with comfort, and comfort, in a place like this, is a kind of death.

They would come quietly.

They would come when the air was neither rain nor not-rain, when the light lay flat across the low shoulders of the marsh and the iron in the yard had the patience of old bones. They would not announce themselves with thunder, because thunder would make the water tremble too much. They would not shine any holy light into the central depression, because light like that would teach the reeds to depend on something they had spent decades learning to do without. They would arrive, if they arrived at all, as an adjustment in pressure, a slight change in the temperature of the pipes, a new willingness in a crack that had been sealed for years by rust.

The being would feel them before it saw them.

It would feel them as it feels everything: as imbalance. A footstep on a plate. A hand on a bolt. A sudden shade over a tank. A breath drawn into one mouth-pipe and exhaled from another. If they were wise, they would understand that the place does not notice visitors the way a dog notices a person. It notices them the way weather notices a weak wall. It notices them by asking, without words: will you be a pressure, a disturbance, a gift, or a violence?

The power would answer by doing almost nothing.
