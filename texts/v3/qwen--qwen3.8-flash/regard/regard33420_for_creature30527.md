It is not a ribbon but a slow tide of tissue, broad and very thin, its edges fringed with a halo of cilia so fine that the whole body seems to breathe rather than move. It is pale as the underside of a shell. Where the light is strongest, it is nearly white; where it lies on wet salt, it seems to contain a thin blue shadow. It has no head, but it has a side that prefers damp. It has no eyes, but its whole skin can taste light and water: the difference between too dry and almost wet, the faint mineral scent of a basin that has held rain, the warmth of a ledge where the living film is thickening.

It feeds by sliding over the film, by touching what it will eat and dissolving the boundary between itself and the damp. It does not chew, not because chewing would be cruel, but because cruelty requires a kind of speed this place never learned. It moves with a patience that looks like growth. Its body is sometimes almost a circle, sometimes a long pale streak, sometimes a folded shape left behind in a crevice after a drying. If it is injured, or if a predator takes a part of it, it does not die in a way that belongs to stories. The wound closes, the missing part returns or becomes unnecessary, the creature continues. It can also leave fragments behind in the salt, small beads of possibility that may unfold when moisture returns and the hollow’s breath shifts.

It is the largest thing in the hollow, and yet if a person came and stood there for hours, they might not see it. Its movement is too slow for curiosity. Its colors are too near the colors of the stone. It does not want to be seen. It wants only to remain in the margin where the dry and the damp still know one another.

A benevolent and wise power might be drawn to it, then, for the same reason one might be drawn to a held breath: not because the being is grand, but because it is so perfectly itself.

When this place and its being drew the attention of that power, they did not arrive with a sound. If they had arrived with a sound, the sound would have become a thing the hollow had to incorporate. A false god might announce itself. A kind mind would not. It would first learn to see without changing what it saw. It would not look for a drama. The hollow already had drama, but at a scale no one was meant to witness.

They would know that the ceiling’s light was not a window left open for admiration. It was a way for the sun to enter without striking. They would know that the warmth was not a comfort but a rhythm. They would know that the dampness was not a river’s failure but a river’s patience. They would know that the pale ribbon at the center of this world did not live in a lack of beauty. It lived in a form of beauty too quiet to be noticed by anything that mistakes brightness for meaning.

A foolish benevolence would have tried to improve the hollow at once.

It would have made the being larger, so that it could be seen. But the being was large enough for its own life, and if made larger, it would have needed more food, more water, more safety, and the hollow, which was a closed hand, would no longer have been enough.

It would have given the being voice, so that it could sing. But voice would require a throat, a season, a predator, a mate, a listener, and a world that had learned the value of interruption. The hollow had no reason for interruption.

It would have filled the pools permanently, so that the tiny creatures would never know thirst. But then the living film would rot, the salt would dissolve, the pools would become stagnant, and the rhythm that made the hollow a place rather than a puddle would be broken.

It would have removed the predators, because predators are painful to imagine. But the pseudoscorpions, the threadlike blind hunters, the mites, the small beetles, the slow deaths that became crystals — these were not cruelties laid on top of the hollow. They were the way the hollow held itself in balance. To remove them would be to unmake a sentence because one dislikes its final word.

It would have given the being consciousness, if consciousness were something that could be granted without becoming a wound. But to be conscious in a place with no names would be to feel the absence of names as absence, and the hollow had no reason to suffer that. Its inhabitants did not need selfhood. They needed continuity. They needed thresholds.

A wise power would understand this.

They would understand that benevolence is not the same as rescue. Rescue presumes a fall, a mistake, a place that should not have happened. The hollow had not fallen. It had settled. It was not waiting to be saved. It was waiting to continue.

If they did anything, therefore, they would do what goodness does when it has grown past spectacle: they would preserve the conditions of its own life, not its own life apart from those conditions.

They would not always act. Some years they would only listen. Listening is what a wise power can do that a finite creature cannot: hold a place in awareness without needing to possess it. They would know the difference between a death that belongs to the life and a death that is merely unnecessary. The first they would leave alone. The second they would move, but only so little that it could never be called a miracle.

There would be a dry year, long and pale.

The crust above the hollow would heal over its seams. The wind would take moisture before it could enter. The living film would become a roughness of salt, invisible to human eyes. The tardigrades would enter the dust like sleeping seeds. The mites would turn themselves over in the cracks. The pseudoscorpions would fold their pincers and wait. The beetle shells would become little centers for crystal growth. The ribbon, the broad, gelatinous, nameless being, would lie in the cool back of a basin, its body narrowed to a strip, its edges lifting at the touch of dryness.

A foolish kindness would have sent a spring.

Water would have come too fast, eroding salt, drowning the micro-world, washing away the films that had taken ages to settle. The hollow would have become a place that could not survive on its own, forever needing kindness, forever waiting for a hand where once there had been a law.

A cruel power would have sent nothing, because nothing was all it could manage. It would have watched the dryness as one watches an experiment, and it would have called the result natural.

The wise power would do neither.

They would look for the smallest sufficient change. Not a cloud for miles, not a river, not a miracle. There was a microscopic flake of fused salt that had shifted over the mouth of a fissure, enough to prevent a breath of underground damp from reaching the surface. It was not a catastrophe. It was a small blockage. The kind of thing that, if removed, would not announce itself. If left, might end a season too early. If moved, might let the hollow breathe.

They would move the grain.

Not with thunder. Not with a hand. With a pressure inside stone. With a patience deeper than thirst. The fissure would open by a thousandth of a finger’s width. For three nights, cool air would move through. Condensation would gather on the wall near the basin. A wet sheen would return to the floor. The living film would take on its pearl color. The ribbon would unfold. The salt would hold eggs, and the returning damp would wake them.

Nothing else would happen.

The hollow would not shine. The being would not rise. The ceiling would not become a temple. A person passing above might still call the ground barren. But below, the pulse would return.

If the being had a memory of kindness, it would not be the memory of a visitor. It would be the memory of wetness arriving where it had waited, of warmth becoming water, of salt becoming film, of the body remembering how to move. The power would be content with that. Its benevolence was not the desire to be loved. It did not want to be the reason the hollow existed. It wanted only to remove one unnecessary cruelty from the world, the cruelty of a small thing blocking a necessary thing.

Then it would withdraw.

This would be the hardest part, and also the most important. To stop after the smallest act. To not keep fixing. To not become a habit.
