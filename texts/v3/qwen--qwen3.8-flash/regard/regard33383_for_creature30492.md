It would pause near a crack, test the stone’s hunger for wetness, and then—after a delay too long to be haste, too precise to be drift—carry one bead of water from one mouth of the rock to another, stopping at the edge with the same hesitation it had at the beginning.

This was not motion as lower countries understood motion. This was maintenance. This was the cirque choosing its own balance again, not because it had to, but because there was no reason not to, and no reason to do otherwise.

## What the Kind Power Would Do

Far beyond the ridges, where the horizon was only a suggestion and the sky a dark bowl turned upside down, a Power came.

It was benevolent. It was wise. It had watched too many lower places where goodness arrived with boots, carrying maps, saying *I know what you need*, and then leaving behind the wreckage of improvement. So it approached the Still-Roar Cirque with hands open, mouth shut, and its greatest power restrained.

It stood at the outermost terrace, where the oldest scree had stopped sliding long ago, and it listened.

At first, it thought the roar was violence. It is easy, from a distance, to hear immensity and assume anger. But as the Power remained, its attention slowed to the pace of feldspar sentences, and it began to understand that the sound was not a warning, nor a demand, nor a lament. The Still-Roar was the cirque maintaining itself. It was the noise a place makes when everything has found its last possible position.

It heard the black pools as organs of patience.

It heard the Dampness Keepers as verbs of moisture, reluctant and holy.

It heard the Feldspar Sentences as unfinished thoughts, not because they had failed, but because completion would have been a kind of death.

And it heard, beneath all these, the single being that lived there—not separate from the cirque, not one among many, but the cirque’s own way of being a self. A parliament of water and stone and pressure. A creature made of equilibrium. A life that did not begin, did not end, and did not need to be explained.

The Kind Power considered its own nature.

If it were merely wise, it would do nothing.

If it were merely benevolent, it might do too much.

But it was both, and that changed the question.

It did not ask, *How can I make this place better?*

It asked, *Can betterness be added without breaking what is already true?*

And after a long silence, in which the still roar passed through it the way cold passes through light, the Power decided what it would do.

---

### First, it would not repair the cirque.

That was the first kindness, and perhaps the hardest.

It would not warm the air, because warmth would not be salvation there but injury. The pools were not waiting to become lakes. The frost was not a disease. The silence between presences was not emptiness to be filled. The cirque did not need abundance. It had already learned what most worlds never learn: that fullness can consist of spacing, and spacing can be a form of care.

It would not make the roar louder or softer, not to comfort any passing heart.

It would not teach the Feldspar Sentences to finish.

A lower power might have looked at those pale words in the crushed grains and thought: *How sad. They begin so much and never complete anything.* It might have given them grammar, or endings, or the satisfaction of a last word. But wisdom knew that the cirque’s life depended on suspension. Its meaning lay in the held breath. If the sentences finished, the place would no longer be itself.

It would not urge the Dampness Keepers to become creatures with faces.

To make them legible would be to make them human-shaped, and human-shaped things are too easily loved, too easily used, too easily misunderstood.

It would not make the pools remember.

At least, not unless they chose to.

This was the central difficulty. The cirque accepted contact without recording it. It could know a hand and then return to dryness. It could admit a footstep and let it pass as if it had been smoke. That was not cruelty. That was its own way of staying whole. The Kind Power respected that, because respect is sometimes all love can offer to a thing that does not need love.

---

### Second, it would make a threshold that was not a wall.

At the edge of the cirque, where the first terrace fell away toward the oldest flats, the Power placed a small change in the air.

It did not build a fence.

It did not command.

It created a consent.

Those who came from below with hunger would find the cirque merely empty. They would stand at its rim and feel nothing but distance. Their instruments would return only noise. Their maps would smear. Their ambition would become tired without understanding why. No harm would come to them; no punishment would touch them. They would simply find the place unreadable to their need.

But those who came in another way—who did not seek to capture, name, use, convert, or possess—might notice the cold not as cold but as attention. They might hear the roar not as threat but as a condition they were being allowed to witness. They might find their footsteps lightened, their voices reduced, their hands made careful before they even reached for the pools.

The threshold asked one question:

*Do you wish to know this place, or only to take from it?*

The Power did not force an answer.

It made the question part of the weather.

---

### Third, it would offer the cirque a door it did not have to use.

After a long time, the Kind Power came nearer than any being normally would, and it stood at the rim of the lowest black pool. The water was dark and perfect, polished as if by the patience of ages. The pale grains moved in sentences no language would ever finish.

The Power wanted to touch them.

It wanted to say, *I see you.*

It wanted to give some comfort.
