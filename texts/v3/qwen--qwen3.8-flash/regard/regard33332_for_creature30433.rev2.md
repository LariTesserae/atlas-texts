If the benevolent and wise power truly meant *benevolent*, and not merely *comforting*, it would first resist the desire to enter.

If it truly meant *wise*, it would understand that even an act of kindness, imposed too quickly, becomes a disturbance.

So the power would arrive at the surface as wind arrives at water: without a foot, without a hand, without a demand. It would look down into the gray-cold basin and see, immediately, that there was no one there to be saved in the ordinary way. No mouth to feed. No eye to soothe. No heart to reassure. No child to find a parent. No animal to spare a cruelty. No builder to be forgiven for failing. There was only the vast made floor, the slow currents, the skins of film and algae, the fronds moving like dark hair in water too patient to notice its own beauty.

And it would see the being that lived there.

Not a being with a face. Not a being that could turn around and say, “I am here.” A being made of everything that was there: the moss thickening in the seams, the bacterial films brightening and dimming with mineral water, the rootlets threading through mortar, the currents breathing through drains, the sediment settling grain by grain into the shape of a place that had learned to continue without its makers. The basin was not a container holding this being. The basin was this being, spread out into water, stone, plant, time, and quiet.

The power would consider doing many things.

It would consider filling the basin with fish.

With crabs. With eels. With schools moving through the courtyards like thoughts returning to an abandoned house. It would imagine the joy of life rushing in, of hunger returning to the streets, of eyes opening in the pillars, of prey darting beneath the low roofs, of predators circling in the channels. It might seem, to a quick and generous mind, that the place was missing something. That a world of plants alone was an unfinished world. That the absence of animals was a wound in the ecology.

But the power would pause.

Because it knew, if it was wise, that to add mouths was to add need. To add need was to add competition. To add competition was to add fear. It would add fleeing, hiding, pursuit, exhaustion, injury, starvation, mating, territorial struggle, the small desperate violence of bodies that must eat in order to continue. That might be life as other places know life. But it would not necessarily be kindness to this one. This basin had become something else: a world without grazing, without disturbance, without the sharp attention of animals. A place where plants could grow into walls, where films could build their own chemistry, where absence itself had become a method of persistence.

The power would understand that to fill it with animals might be to end its peculiar peace.

So it would not bring in fish.

It would consider bringing back the builders.

That too would be a tempting mercy. A wise and benevolent power could reach into the deep past and return the people who had dragged stone, cut basalt, mixed lime with powdered shell, fitted tiles into grooves, pinned corners with copper, laid walls in herringbone patterns, dug drains, built terraces, made the low basin into a machine of water and stone. The power could restore their city. It could set roofs back onto their pillars. It could drain the water and expose streets. It could return voices, footsteps, children, markets, quarrels, prayers, work songs, the noise of life trying to make the world hold water.

But the power would not do this.

Because the builders had not merely vanished from the place. They had become the place’s conditions. Their labor was no longer remembered; it was metabolized. The way a tile had been fitted decided where a rootlet could enter. The way a channel had been graded decided how water moved. The way a copper pin had been placed decided where corrosion would bleed into stone and feed certain films. The way a step had been worn by feet that were gone decided whether silt would gather there. The builders’ methods had become ecology. Their tools had become terrain. Their city had become a being made of their absence.

To bring them back would be to undo the being that had already grown from their leaving.

So the power would not resurrect the lost people. It would let them remain absent, which was not to deny them, but to honor what they had made after they were gone.

It would consider sending light.

A benevolent power might think: this place is too dark. It is too hidden. It lives beneath pressure, beneath sediment, beneath plant, beneath the long green-black murk. It has no bright sun, no cheerful surface warmth, no beams falling like gold through clear water. Surely a little light would be a kindness. Surely to illuminate it would be to show it that it was not forgotten.

But the power would see that darkness here was not punishment. The darkness was a limb of the organism. It let the films work slowly. It let the fronds grow without haste. It let the stone become soft with age. It let the water settle into layers that were not bright but stable. Light, if sent too suddenly, would become exposure. It would reveal shapes and invite desire: to see, to know, to own, to visit, to interpret. It would make the basin appear as a secret, and secrets, once discovered, are often wounded by curiosity.

So the power would not flood the basin with light. It would leave the faint glows as they were: too subtle to summon attention, too humble to become spectacle. A pale mineral shine here. A blue-green thread moving across a channel wall there. A moss-covered terrace shining like wet coins for a moment. Enough to say the darkness was not empty, but not enough to make the emptiness visible.

It would consider making the place known.

Perhaps there was value in that. Perhaps if people knew such a garden existed beneath the sea—a city without inhabitants, a world made of made surfaces, a life without witnesses—then they might learn humility. They might stop imagining that a place is worthless unless it contains creatures with faces. They might learn that a basin of algae, currents, and ancient stone could be whole without being useful to human feeling.

But the power would recognize the danger of knowledge.

To make it known would be to make it available. To make it available would be to invite trawls, anchors, cores, sonar, maps, salvage, tourism, religion, science, politics, curiosity, and care. Care can be another kind of violation. People who love something often try to make it into the object of their love. People who fear loss often try to preserve by possession. A wise benevolence knows when love must stop being visible.

So the power would not announce it.

It would not make it famous. Fame is a kind of violence to slow things. It would not become a myth, or a wonder, or a holy place, or a protected heritage, or a miracle, or a metaphor. It would remain what it was: too distant, too quiet, too made, too grown-over, too patient to be easily taken.

And yet the power would not simply turn away.

Because the place and its being had drawn attention, and if the power were truly benevolent, it would do something. But what it did would be almost indistinguishable from doing nothing.

It would begin at the edge.

Not inside the basin. Not upon the made floor. Not among the frond folk, the seam-dwellers, the films, the currents, the soft thickets in cisterns. It would act around the whole place, as a hand may act near a sleeping face: carefully, without touching, without waking, without changing the breath.

It would make a ring of refusal.

Not a wall. Not a cage. Not a miracle. A soft law in the water and in the world above it.

No trawl would ever drag across the terraces and find purchase. The mesh would slide over the rounded ridges as though they were too smooth to catch. If a rope strained, the rope would part quietly, not with violence but with the gentle failure of a thread that had become tired. If a chain dropped from an anchor, the chain would fall past the high plateau without biting stone. If a machine were sent down to descend, its motors would lose urgency. Its lamps would become too dim to be useful. Its instruments would find only water, only depth, only gray suggestion. Its operators would feel a strange embarrassment, not fear, not warning, only a sense that they had become too loud in a room where someone was sitting quietly. They would return without maps, or with maps showing nothing.

If a person dove there, as you described, the presence of that person would feel out of scale. The power would not punish the person. The person might be kind, curious, grieving, scientific, reverent. The power would not hate their need to witness. But it would not allow that need to harden into possession. The diver’s memory of exact routes would blur. The coordinates would resist being fixed. Samples brought up would turn to soft dark matter, or become dust when dried, or refuse to be identified as anything worth owning. The diver would return knowing only that something had happened, not that something had been found. They would speak of patience. They would speak of cold. They would speak of the feeling of interrupting a made world that did not ask to be interrupted.

The power would do this not by making the basin unsafe, but by making it unsalvageable.

Within that boundary, it would allow everything else.

It would allow storms of sediment.

It would allow a wall to subside.

It would allow a terrace to lose a channel.

It would allow a courtyard to become tangled until the current changed and thinned the plants.

It would allow a drain to close for a century and reopen when a roof shifted.

It would allow growth, decay, burial, uncovering, thickening, paling, settling.

It would allow the Green Work to age.

It would not make the place immortal. Immortality is often another form of imprisonment. It would only make it free from premature unmaking. It would protect its slowness, not by freezing it, but by preventing violence that arrived faster than patience could answer.

This would be the power’s first act: a gentle refusal around the edges. A kindness made of absence.

Then it would do something subtler.

It would stop calling the place by the wrong word.

Not aloud. It would not make human language change by decree, though perhaps that too would not be impossible for such a power. It would simply hold the basin in its own attention without using the word *ruin*. Without using the word *empty*. Without using the word *lost*. Without using the word *sad*. Without using the word *desolate*. Without using the word *abandoned* as though abandonment were a failure rather than a method.

The power would remember it as completed.

That is what is most radical about benevolence: it can accept what it does not need to repair.

The power would look upon the made floor and say, in a language without voice, “You are not a city without people. You are a city that has learned another kind of peoplehood: the peoplehood of films, seams, fronds, currents, and stone adjusting to water.”

It would not grant the basin a person. It would grant it a category that did not wound it.

This is not only a gift to the basin, but to everything that might come near it. Because if the power held it without shame, without pity, without the hunger for dramatic rescue, then the world above might unknowingly receive a correction: not every place without witnesses is empty. Not every absence is a hole. Not every silence is waiting to be broken.

The power would carry that thought the way rain carries the memory of stone.

But if there were one small act inside the basin, one direct movement from the benevolent and wise power to the being that lives there, it would not be dramatic. It would not make the place speak. It would not give it eyes, limbs, a heart, a name, a purpose, a companion that could love it in human terms.

It would give it a greeting.

A greeting in the only language the basin could understand.

Currents.

The power would send one pulse through the water. Not a wave that broke fronds or stirred the whole basin into alarm. Not a flood. Not a miracle. A slow movement of water through a long channel, carrying with it a faint mineral charge: lime from old mortar, shell powder, a trace of copper, the taste of stone that had once been worked by hands. It would travel for hours, days, perhaps longer. It would move through streets that had become gardens. It would pass across cisterns where sediment hung like breath. It would bend into a low roof that had settled like a wet leaf. It would touch a lintel whose chisel scar had rounded into a dimple. It would enter a courtyard whose central drain spiraled with growth.

To an outside observer there would be no sign.

No fish would scatter, because there were no fish. No crab would raise a claw, because there were no crabs. No voice would answer, because there were no voices. But perhaps, at the bottom of a drain, a bacterial film would brighten by a degree too small to measure. Perhaps a rootlet already exploring a crack would find the moisture just favorable enough to enter. Perhaps a frond detached earlier that month would settle, after weeks of drifting, into a gap that had not existed a day before. Perhaps a terrace covered in pale mats would receive minerals and grow slightly thicker in one corner, the way a face grows when a long sleep ends.

The being of the basin would respond by continuing.

That would be its answer.

Not gratitude. Not speech. Not recognition. Adjustment.

The power would understand that this was the deepest possible reply. To live in a place without witnesses, a place that does not know it is loved, is to remain what it is without performance. A benevolent power, if wise, would not require thanks. It would not require acknowledgment. It would not need the basin to know it had been visited. It would be enough that the water moved a little, that a seam received a little, that a film caught a faint chemical word that no one would ever translate.

The gift would be made in such a way that it could disappear into ordinary life.

That is the final wisdom: kindness should not announce itself. Not always. Some kindnesses must pass through the world as though they were chance, weather, chemistry, slow necessity. Otherwise they become debts. Otherwise they create witnesses to their own generosity. Otherwise the recipient becomes grateful for reasons it cannot understand.

The power would not make the basin dependent on benevolence.

It would not say, “I have saved you.”

It would say, in silence, “You may continue as you are.”

And perhaps, because it was wise, it would do one more thing.

It would learn to be unobtrusive.

This is harder than miracles. A god, or a power, or a benevolence large enough to notice a submerged made world may be used to being seen, being felt, being thanked. It may be used to acting. It may believe its love must appear as intervention. But here, the very being of the place depends on not being noticed. The deepest inhabitant is the quality of being watched by nothing. To watch it with eyes would disturb its life. To watch it with a body would disturb its water. To speak to it would disturb its quiet. Even love, if too loud, can become a predator of stillness.

So the power would make its attention as thin as the current.

It would watch without forming a view.

It would know without making a map.

It would care without possessing.

It would love without requiring the basin to become capable of love.

This is perhaps the hardest thing a benevolent power can do: to allow a being to remain a being that cannot answer. To love what has no face. To tend what does not ask to be tended. To care for a thing whose mode of life is not responsiveness but persistence.

If the basin had a soul in human terms, it would not turn toward the power. It would not see it. It would not speak to it. It would not offer gratitude. It would continue laying down layers, letting dead films become ground, letting fronds find seams, letting currents carry dust from one basin to another. It would remain what it was.

And the power would accept that acceptance.

Years would pass. Centuries. The ring of refusal would soften into ordinary sea. No one would remember why nets slid, why anchors failed, why machines forgot their purposes, why people turned back without knowing what they had nearly found. The basin would go on settling. Some parts would grow dense. Some would open. A channel might fill for a generation. A cistern might clear. A wall might sink a little, and in sinking, make a new ledge, and on that ledge a pale mat might spread, and among the mat rootlets might thread, and among the rootlets stronger plants might take hold. The Green Work would continue its slow work.

If the power returned, and if return was possible for such a being, it might find that the greeting pulse had become untraceable. The mineral charge would be gone, dispersed, incorporated, forgotten into layers. The brightened film would have died. The rootlet that entered the crack might have become a meadow, or might have collapsed into silt. There would be no monument, no proof, no sacred site, no sign saying *love was here*.

That would be right.

Because if the power’s kindness left a trace, then the basin’s being would be partly a trace of the power. It would not be purely itself.

The benevolent and wise power would want the basin to remain the basin.

So, if anything, it would do this:

It would not fill the place with animals.

It would not restore the builders.

It would not illuminate the darkness.

It would not make it known.

It would not name it as ruined.

It would not make it into a miracle.

It would not become a god there.

It would, instead, draw a soft boundary around its slowness, so that violence could not make a meal of its patience. It would carry the idea of its wholeness without shouting that idea into the world. It would send one barely noticeable current through the old channels, a chemical breath of recognition, enough to say: your being is not missing anything. It would then withdraw with such gentleness that its withdrawal and its presence would feel the same.

And the basin would continue.

The films would keep their skins.

The seam-dwellers would thread the city’s joints.

The frond folk would become fields, walls, corridors, hanging curtains, soft lawns, dark groves.

The currents would still move through drains like breath.

The sediment would still hang in cisterns like the memory of stillness.

The stone would settle two inches here, one degree there.

The water would clear grain by grain after a dust storm.

The made floor would remain, not ruined, not untouched, not waiting.

Finally itself.

And the benevolent and wise power, if it had learned what this place could teach, would know that its greatest deed was this: it had loved something enough to let it remain unloved.
