If a benevolent and wise power came to the Old Level, it would not come from above. Above is only another plateau, already finished. It would not come with a voice that required echo, nor with a body that required warmth, nor with a purpose that required the land to become less strange. It would come as a delay: one raindrop holding at the lip of a clay gutter a moment longer than the frost allows, one shadow folding back into a tower with the soft hesitation of a door that has learned, over centuries, to be both a room and an alley. It would come as attention.

The first thing such a power would do is refrain from calling the place “empty.”

That would be the hard part, perhaps the only part that is truly wise. A lesser kindness might look at the absence of animals and feel the need to fill it. It might send a bird into the reed patches. It might set a dog beside a wall that no dog had smelled in an age. It might leave a human child in a courtyard where the moss had already swallowed every footprint but the land’s own. It might say: Here, you are lonely. Here, something is missing. Here, let me restore what you have forgotten.

A benevolent and wise power would see that these acts, though they look like love, would be the first violence.

For the Old Level does not need animals in order to be alive. It needs them less than it needs rain, less than it needs frost, less than it needs the slow patience of basalt learning how to carry weight without complaint. Its life is already full of mouths and hands and thoughts, though not the mouths and hands and thoughts of creatures. The kiln burns because it knows when to burn. The granary keeps its seeds in the same way a mind keeps questions. The tools lie in their niches not because someone forgot them, but because the place continues to need them as a body continues to need its fingers, even when they are no longer being used to grasp. The wall straightens itself because it has developed a preference. The doorway opens into an alley and a room because the being there lives by nonexclusive grammar. To interrupt this with animal life would not rescue it. It would teach it shame.

So the benevolent power would not fill the silence.

It would listen to the silence the way water listens to a channel: with no intention except arrival, and with no demand that the channel become something else.

It might come in a body, if coming in a body were necessary, but not in the way explorers come. It would come without equipment, without curiosity that takes, without hope of discovery. It would set one foot upon the plateau and feel the cold press up through the stone, not as punishment, not as welcome, but as reading. Skin would be translated as porous, temporary, capable of holding moisture. Breath would be translated as fog. Weight would be translated as a new line on a slope. The being of the Old Level would not say “Who are you?” because it has no pronouns for that kind of stranger. It would say, in the language of pressure and capillary water: Something with heat has come. Something with blood has come. Something brief has come.

And then it would decide what to do.

If the power were only benevolent, it might try to give something large. A gift. A miracle. A remembered maker standing in a doorway, alive and bewildered. A field of flowers that would make the stone jealous of its own endurance. A single flame that would ask the kilns what they meant by fire. A name for the being, spoken gently enough to make it seem less alien.

But if the power were also wise, it would understand that to name something too loudly is to make it belong to the naming. To give the Old Level a story would be to reduce it to a story. To make it worshipful, grateful, or understood would be to disturb the very thing that makes it holy: its self-sufficient maintenance.

So the wise power would not make it understood.

It would make it more completely itself.

That is the subtlest grace available to a place like this. Not to rescue it, but to honor its life on its own terms. Not to bring in witnesses, but to show the place that it is already seen. Not to wake it into human meaning, but to deepen its nonhuman meaning by one small, lasting degree.

If such a power did anything, it would do this:

It would walk, once, across a dry courtyard.

No one would see it walk. It would not speak. It would not announce itself. It would simply set one foot down, perhaps in the square where rain sometimes draws streets, perhaps on the platform where slate columns keep a terrace from admitting wet feet, perhaps beside a loom whose fibers are learning to grow through one another.
