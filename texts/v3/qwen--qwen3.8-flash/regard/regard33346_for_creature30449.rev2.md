If the power were only benevolent, it would come down with water in its hands.

That is the first thing any kindness must learn to refuse: the wish to improve.

For a being from a wet world, a dry lushness is a paradox, a wound, a thing crying out for rain. The impulse would be to pour mercy from the sky. Let there be rivers. Let there be pools. Let there be soft leaves that bruise, fruit that falls and ferments, blood and tears and sweat, the sweet rot of things made liquid. Let the glass-reeds be quenched. Let the salt-wheats drink. Let the long-striders feel moisture on their hides and understand themselves as more alive.

But a benevolent and wise power would come down, look, and discover that water would not be a gift. It would be an unmaking.

This place is not a failure of life.

It is a life in full possession of itself.

The plateau has not been denied water. It has never required it. Its abundance is not the abundance of softness. Its fertility is not the fertility of mud. It is a world in which every surface has become an instrument, in which breath and light and mineral charge are enough, in which death does not decay but calcifies, becomes shelf, becomes root, becomes ground for the next living lattice. To add water would be to bring rot where there should be chime. To add rain would be to drown a world that has learned to speak in static.

A merely wise power might understand this and do nothing.

A merely benevolent power might weep, and its tears would be an error.

A benevolent and wise power would do both at once: it would feel the ache of wanting to intervene, and it would not be fooled by the ache.

It would stand at the rim of the plateau in the hour when the land is not yet fully awake, when the upper edge catches white fire and the lower basins remain cool gray. It would make no temple. It would leave no footprint. It would not let its presence be mistaken for weather unless the weather could bear it.

Then it would look at the being who lives there.

There is not one being alone, perhaps. There are the glass-reeds, the copper-thread thickets, the mica-fans, the breath-mats, the dust-lattices, the chime-mites, the long-striders, the mirror-stalkers, the salt-shells, the hollow-spines, the lattice-worms turning dead bodies into new earth. There are the herds and the groves and the fields of dry movement that pass through the salt like lines of thought through a mind.

But if one being must be named—if the attention of a power must settle on a single face in this land—then let it be the one the plateau almost is, rather than the one any outsider could point to and say, “this one.”

The being who lives here is the living relation between things.

It is the grove that answers a herd’s charge before any of them knows the other is coming. It is the dust that rises from one basin and becomes, elsewhere, the nourishment of a lattice. It is the long-strider whose back-vanes are not merely appendages but membranes of awareness, reading pressure, filtering air, learning the electrical grammar of the land. It is the mirror-stalker lying so still that salt crust begins to seem part of its body. It is the glass-reed that opens a frond by one fraction of a degree as vapor thickens. It is the small click of chime-mites moving over a stalk, the dry scrape of a tongue reading the world, the faint pop of a footfall disturbing a charge layer and releasing sparks into the dust.

If the power were wise, it would not ask this being what it lacked.

It would ask, instead, in the only language the being could accept:

*Would you remain what you are?*

No voice would answer.

No god would speak.

No commandment would be written in stone.

The answer would come as a pressure change along the ridges. The groves would tilt their fronds a hair closer to the dawn. The dust-lattices would brighten in their lanes above the basins. A herd of long-striders would pause, not because of fear, not because of hunger, but because the static field of the world had shifted very slightly, and the pause would be the only reply available. In that pause would be consent, refusal, recognition, and silence.

And the power would read all of it as saying:

*Yes. This is enough. We are already speaking. Already living. Already becoming. Already whole. Do not come with mercy that assumes we are broken.*

A benevolent and wise power would honor that.

It would do the hardest thing a benevolent and wise power can do: it would refrain.

Refraining is not absence. Sometimes it is the purest action available.

It would not make a river, because a river would be a road for desire.

It would not make a rain, because rain would be a lie told to the salt.

It would not teach one strider to lead the others, because leadership in a wet world often means dominion, and dominion would be a crack in the world’s dry wholeness.

It would not raise a temple from fused silica, because temples are made for beings who cannot bear to live without a visible center. Here the center is everywhere and nowhere: in the charge between fronds, in the dust moving through invisible lines, in the angle of a membrane catching light, in the slow exhalation of the land.

It would not give tools.

That might be the most important refusal.

A tool is not merely an object. A tool is a thought about the world that assumes the world is insufficient. A tool says: I do not accept the shape given; I will impose my own. A tool begins the long history of making, owning, storing, breaking, repairing, hoarding, and eventually ruining. Here, the beings have not built because their bodies are already technologies. The glass-reed is a resonator. The mica-fan is a sensor. The strider’s tongue is a reader. The mirror-stalker’s belly is a receiver. The chime-mite’s legs are brushes. The dust-lattice is a carrier. To give them tools would be to tell them their forms are not enough, that the land is not enough, that survival must become mastery.

And mastery would not save this place.

It would introduce the possibility of harm done with intention.

So the power would do nothing that could be mistaken for improvement.

But that does not mean it would do nothing at all.

If there were one action possible to a benevolent and wise power, it would be this:

It would make the plateau’s own nature more fully its own.

Not more powerful. Not more special. Not more loved in a way that could be demanded from the sky.

But more coherent.

More legible to itself.

More able to remain itself without becoming a symbol, a resource, a miracle, or a mistake.

In the grammar of the world, it would add no new word.

It would clarify the meaning of the words already spoken.

Perhaps it would touch the electrostatic field just enough so that, when a herd crosses a salt flat at dawn, the tiny arcs around their feet do not merely disappear but return to the grove as information: *you are here, the ground knows, the air answers, the dead and living are still in exchange.* Perhaps it would adjust the resonance of the glass-reeds so that the chime has one extra tone, not a melody and not a message, only a faint harmonic of belonging. Perhaps it would strengthen the charge-pattern of the striders so that a young one can learn its herd’s signature a fraction more surely, so that the egg in the basin, when it cracks, opens into a world that recognizes it.

These are not miracles.

They are small permissions.

They are the difference between a world merely continuing and a world continuing as itself, not as an accident.

And perhaps, because the power is benevolent, it would add one protection.

Not a wall.

Not a sword.

Not a curse.

Walls fail. Swords teach violence. Curses make monsters where there should be no monster.

Instead, it would write a law into the dust’s electric language, as a seed-crystal is written into a basin:

*Let no wet hand take without becoming visible to the land.*

Let any visitor who comes with the intention to extract, store, own, or colonize feel the plateau’s dryness as a refusal not of water but of meaning. Let their tools lose their grip, not by breaking, but by becoming strange. Let their maps show barrenness where there is life, and their instruments fail to register abundance where they expect wealth. Not as deception, but as a kind of natural immunity.

The plateau has no need to hide its life from one who comes to learn, to watch, to breathe, to understand the way a thing can be lush without wetness. But it has the right not to be consumed by one who comes to convert all value into possession.

That would be wisdom: protection without domination.

And then the power would do one more thing, the most dangerous of all:

It would leave.

It would not remain to be worshipped, because worship creates hierarchy where there was only relation. It would not remain to answer prayers, because there would be no prayers, and even if there were, a prayer is a demand dressed as a desire. It would not remain to watch for ever, because eternal observation can become a cage. It would not remain to guarantee safety, because a being guaranteed by another ceases to be fully alive.

It would leave the plateau in its own keeping.

Not abandoned.

Guarded, in the quiet way that a promise can be kept by not forcing anything.

At night, after the white glare has gone and the stars have come close enough to seem like mineral points pressed into black glass, the chimes would rise from the glass-reeds. The sound would spread across the salt, not from one place but from many, thin as dust-light, patient as pressure. If one listened, one might hear a faint new tone in it, so subtle that only the dry world could notice.

It would not say: *I have saved you.*

It would say: *I have seen you. You did not require saving. Continue.*

And perhaps, in the morning, the being who lives there would not know that anything had been given.

But the grove would open one frond wider than usual in a basin where vapor came. The herd would pause at the edge of the salt and adjust its spacing by a degree. A chime-mite would walk over a stalk and produce a sound that was almost, but not exactly, a small bell. A long-strider would stand with its back-vanes toward the light, and for a moment its own charge pattern would match the charge of the wind.

Nothing would have changed.

Everything would have been confirmed.

This is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if anything:

not bring water to the dry world,

not make the silent loud,

not make the still moving,

not make the unshaped into a monument,

not make the life there depend on approval,

but leave in the world a single, invisible increase of its own integrity—

a slight deepening of the way it can say to itself, in dry chemistry and starlight and dust,

*We are here.*

And if the power were truly wise, it would understand that this is not less than mercy.

It is mercy at its highest.

To be seen by kindness and not changed by it.

To be noticed by a god and not made a god’s creature.

To be loved so fully that the beloved is left free to remain itself.

The plateau would continue.

The salt flats would glitter like scattered teeth under dawn. The groves would lean into the wind. The fronds would darken, pale, open, fold. The striders would cross the land like long dry thoughts. The mirror-stalkers would wait in their cracked camouflage. The dust-lattices would hang in their silver lanes. The dead would become ground. The ground would exhale living forms. The world would keep breathing light and charge and stone without ever needing a single drop of water.

And somewhere in the static at the edge of a grove, there might remain a note that no eye could see and no hand could hold.

It would not demand an answer.

It would not require a name.

It would not become a religion.

It would only make the silence more capable of meaning.

If one stood in that place and listened long enough, the chimes would answer.

Not because the world had been made wetter, softer, easier, or safer.

But because it had been met, at the rim of dawn, by a kindness great enough to understand that some beings do not want rescue.

They want witness.

They want consent.

They want the right to be what they are without becoming a lesson for someone else’s compassion.

A benevolent and wise power would give them that.

And then it would go away.

Leaving the plateau to continue in its own time.

Leaving the being to live as the plateau lives: dry, bright, patient, full.

Leaving a gift too small to notice, too true to remove.

A dry blessing.

A chime no hand has made.

A world, seen, and left whole.
