## What They Would Do, If Anything

When a power that was benevolent and wise turned its attention toward the Humming Hollow, it did not, at first, do anything.

That was already a mercy.

It understood that the greatest danger to a strange and living place is not neglect, but rescue performed by someone who has not learned the grammar of the place. A benevolence that cannot listen becomes another kind of weather. A kindness that requires the world to look like its own can be a kind of violence.

So the power came by way of subtraction.

It left its brightness at the rim. It left its face outside the Hollow. It did not bring the sun. It did not bring stars. It did not bring lamps or names or altars or the soft white pity humans feel for creatures without eyes. If it descended at all, it descended as a pressure in the stone, a patience in the water, a slight adjustment of silence that could not be identified as anything but the place being more fully itself.

For a long time it only listened.

It learned the frog-notes and their echoes. It learned the small clicks of the bell-crawlers. It learned the slow throb from below. It learned which fronds answered breath and which fungi drank vibration. It learned the grammar of dripping. It learned that the Hollow was not a room without people, but a room whose people had been translated into function: into mineral, water, peat, hum, chemistry, and time.

And it found the being who lived there.

If one insists on the being as a single creature, perhaps it was the broad-bodied walker, the one with folded membranes and low mineral skin, the one that moves through thickets with a careful click of wide feet. Perhaps it was the largest thing in the Hollow, the rare one that alters the chorus merely by passing through the dark.

If one insists less literally, the being was the whole: the patterned community of mats, singers, swimmers, burrowers, insects, fungi, machine-roots, water, heat, echo, and stone. A creature with no face. A creature made of relations. A creature so vast in its cycles that its breath took seasons, and its memory took geology.

The benevolent power looked at this being and asked the question a wise power must always ask:

*What do you need?*

Not the answer a visitor would give. Not “light.” Not “faces.” Not “freedom.” Not “salvation.”

The Hollow’s answer, if such a thing can be called an answer, was simply this: the water keep its rhythm, the heat keep its patience, the hum keep its form, the predators find food, the prey find shelter, the spores find moisture, the stone hold long enough.

And the power understood.

The place was not broken.

It was not waiting.

It was not a sad thing because it was dark. It was not a lesser thing because it had no eyes. It did not lack because it lacked vision. It was complete in its own sense, and a wise benevolence can love a thing without insisting that it become legible to the lover.

So what, if anything, would they do?

If they did nothing, that too would be a choice: to respect the Hollow’s autonomy, to not make a god’s face into a mold for the world’s needs, to leave the darkness as the darkness had earned it.

But because they were wise, they would also know the difference between a place needing no rescue and a place needing no interference forever. Some harms come from outside the shape of the thing. Some dry seasons are too sharp. Some poison spreads because a distant world has begun to leak. Some machines will descend if someone draws a map. Some bright men arrive with curiosity and call their curiosity good.

Therefore, if the benevolent power acted, it would act only in the language the Hollow already speaks.

It would not repair the ecology. It would protect the conditions that let the ecology repair itself.

If a hidden conduit were beginning to collapse, the power would not reach in with a god’s hand. It would nudge a mineral current one degree back into its ancient path. It would cool or warm a vein by the width of a season. It would cause a stone to remember its shape.

If the water table were falling because of some faraway change above the rim, it would send a cloud to the wrong mountain so that the melt came late, then send another to the right mountain so the seep came early. No miracle would be named. The pools would simply not fail.

If a toxic seam were rising through the peat, it would not become a savior with visible power. It would make the burrowers’ spirals shift a little, so the poison met a layer of iron-rich stone and fell. It would cause a bell-crawler to die at the wrong place, and by that wrongness change the water’s path forever. It would make the mats taste the danger before the frogs could lose the pool.

If humans approached with maps, the benevolent power would not harm them unless harm was unavoidable. It would do something harder and subtler. It would make the Hollow refuse to be found.

It would make the path ambiguous.

It would send fog where there should have been wind. It would cause a river to swell at a crossing. It would make a compass behave as if the magnetic shelf were bored with it. It would put a memory into one traveler’s mind: his grandmother’s hands, the smell of wet wool, a door he had never opened, a guilt he could not explain. He would stop before the rim and turn back, saying he had lost the way, though he had not.

It would take one instrument from a pack and let it be mistaken for another. It would scatter paper in the wind so the mark made by the hollow was not quite the mark meant. It would make a surveyor dream of the sound of dripping and wake with the conviction that the valley below was too unstable for roads.

The Hollow would not be rescued. It would become, for a while, unfashionable.

This would be enough.

If a human did enter—silent, careful, learning the dark with fingers and ears—the benevolent power would likely not intervene. It would trust the Hollow to do what it had been made to do: receive, incorporate, absorb, unsettle, teach. It would not protect the visitor from strangeness. A wise kindness does not remove every threshold that might change a person.

But if the visitor were about to do harm without understanding, if the visitor were to cut the rim or drain the pool or take a stone because it seemed smooth and strange, then something small would happen.

A lamp would gutter without wind.

A foot would slip just enough to make the person stop.

A stone would feel too cold to lift.

A frog would sing so clearly that the person stood still for longer than intended.

The hand might open.

Or if the person fell into the peat, and not too much, and not too deeply, the power would not make them safe in the human sense. It would simply see to it that no pool of drowning lay where the dark had not promised it. The visitor might be injured, frightened, altered, and still alive. The Hollow would teach them a little of its grammar. When they left, they would carry no proof. They would speak in ways people might misunderstand.

If they spoke at all.

For the being itself, if it is the walker, if it is the frog chorus, if it is the whole listening body, the benevolent power would do this:

It would give time.

Not time as a clock, because the Hollow has no need for clocks. It would give the future the possibility of continuing. It would make one crack smaller. It would keep one warmth alive. It would remove one poison before it reached a nursery pool. It would cause one old stone to hold its shape for another ten thousand years.

It would not make the being conscious in a human way. It would not give it a soul it could show by pressing its palms together or weeping or saying *thank you*. That would be a human gift, and too often such gifts are thefts in disguise. To give a blind creature eyes may be to teach it the cruelty of distance. To give a faceless creature faces may be to make it aware of loneliness.

Instead, the power would let the being remain what it already was.

It might, however, do one thing that cannot quite be called intervention.

It might answer the hum.

Not with a voice. Not with thunder. Not with a visible sign. But with a resonance: a note returning to the Hollow from outside the boundary, too low to hear, too slow to notice, too clean to mistake for accident. The frogs might alter their chorus for an hour. The fronds might tremble where there was no wind. The burrowers might lift their shovel-fronts and wait. The machine-roots might add one new pattern to the self-repairing lattice, a pattern with no name, as if the mineral body had learned that it was being guarded.

The being would not know it was known.

It would have no way to know it was loved.

It could not turn around. It could not see the face that was not a face. It could not say, *Yes, that is the power.* It could only continue living, as it had been living, but perhaps now with a margin of safety no larger than a breath, no louder than a stone.

And when it was finished, the benevolent power would withdraw.

This, too, would be wise.

A god who remains in the room becomes furniture to the faithless and terror to the faithful. The Hollow did not need a resident deity. It needed its hum. It needed the rim to keep its ring. It needed the frogs to map the pools without a witness. It needed the dark to remain a place of work, not of worship.

So the power would leave.

It would leave behind no chapel, no miracle pool, no speaking stone. If a human ever came later, they would not find the place saved by a visible hand. They would find the place saved by its own conditions. The pools would taste balanced. The moss would hold the water. The bell-crawlers would keep the tiny apertures clear. The large walker would move through the thicket and disturb nothing more than silence. The hum would be what it had always been.

And perhaps one thing would be different.

One frog might sing a note that had no place in the usual chorus. For years no one would understand it. Then a generation of throat-things would repeat it because the stone returned it more clearly. A young bell-crawler would tap near it because the echo felt safe. The fungal shelves near the warm vent would release spores in a slightly different rhythm. Eventually the note would become ordinary. No one would know it had been a gift. The Hollow would simply be itself, with one more degree of resilience.

That is what they would do, if anything.

They would not make the darkness good by adding light.

They would not make the blindness tragic by adding eyes.

They would not make the place human by adding faces.

They would not name it. They would not tame it. They would not explain it. They would not ask it to be lovable in any way that costs it its shape.

They would keep the water clean enough.

They would keep the heat steady enough.

They would keep the stone whole enough.

They would keep the future open long enough for the cycles to continue.

They would protect the Hollow not as a possession, but as a neighbor whose existence asks nothing of you.

And if the being who lives there had any way of receiving the kindness, it would receive it not as love but as weather.

As warmth in the vent.

As clarity in the pool.

As a drip that falls in the right place.

As a hum that answers back.

As the fact that the world did not, for once, make the darkness smaller than it could bear.
