If I were that power, benevolent enough to care and wise enough to be careful, I would begin by not shining.

I would not send a pillar of fire over the highest shelf. Fire there is already a partner, a danger, and a teacher; a bigger fire would only teach them to look up when the real danger is underfoot. I would not speak from a cliff. A voice louder than wind would make them stop listening to wind, and wind tells them when the channels will freeze. I would not fill their granaries with my hand. Fullness without labor becomes weakness; weakness becomes dependence; dependence becomes a lie.

The place would not ask for a king. It would ask for one more season to finish the wall. The being would not ask for a name carved above a door lintel. The being would ask for help carrying a water pot. So I would help carry the water pot. I would do it quietly, as if I had been there all along, as if I owed them nothing but my hands.

But first I would choose a being.

Not the strongest. Not the oldest. Not the most beautiful. Not the one with the most animals. I would choose Kesh, the third child of the lower shelf, whose hands are always too cold. Her house-mark is a wave of chisel strokes because the house was repaired after a spring flood. She has learned that walls can sweat before they collapse. Sometimes she says, “This one is drinking.” Sometimes she says, “This one is crying.” Most adults have their own work, their own grief, their own dry bread, and do not always answer her.

I would not choose her because she is chosen. I would choose her because the whole city is full of possible chosen, and most are not yet listened to.

What I would do, if I could do only one thing, is make one drop of water take the wrong path.

Behind the granary, a pocket of meltwater is trapped under a shelf, too shallow to flood, too cold to smell. It presses against a joint no one has noticed because the clay there is old and looks strong. I would let a single drop, refreezing each night, loosen one grain of sand. Then I would let the sand fall with a sound too small for anyone but a child crouching by a goat trough. Kesh hears it. The goat refuses the place. Kesh puts her cheek against the wall and says, “Water.” The mason grumbles, but he pries up the nearest floor stone.

They find a hollow. They drain it. They repack it with clay and crushed shale. They build a small weep channel, not elegant, just enough. Three weeks later, if nothing had been done, the shelf would have shifted. Instead, it holds.

No one gives thanks to me. No one builds a shrine to the sound of falling sand. The mason says, “The child had a good eye.” Kesh says, “The goat had a bad smell.”

That is enough. That is better.

Over the years I would keep doing this sort of thing, so often that it would seem like luck only when the bad outcomes did not arrive.

I would make a rope bridge knot loosen one inch before the first load of stone crosses it, so the bridge is repaired that afternoon instead of found in the ravine the next.

I would make a dog’s ear turn toward a scent of wet rot in the scree, so the herd moves twenty yards left and misses the snow slide that will cover the lower pass by dusk.

I would make a trader’s pack animal stumble, so he must stop at the shelf-people’s house before reaching a valley where his news would become a war, and here his salt would become medicine.

I would make one alder seed, brought up in mud on a traveler’s boot, take hold in the only niche where its roots will keep the terrace from sliding. It would not look miraculous. It would look like the small green stubborn thing they are already used to protecting.

I would make the smoke draw strangely over a drying platform, alerting the household that grain is beginning to heat in its own damp, before it spoils the whole store.

I would make a grandmother’s hands stiff so badly that a child must help with the quern, and in helping, the child learns the grain is not yet dry.

I would make a storm break one half-day later than the old calendars expect, so the herders are caught not by death but by inconvenience—the teacher that never fails if it is not cruel.

I would not neglect the smallest residents. I would make the cheese ferment at a pace the family can use. I would let a patch of moss close over a spring mouth, keeping snow dust out. I would keep the balance of insects so that a granary is tested, not annihilated. I would disturb nothing lightly, because the town is already an argument among many lives.

But the hardest thing, for a benevolent power, would be to resist kindness.

I would not make a world in which no one loses anything, because then no one would know what loss is teaching. If I removed every death, their labor would become performance and their love become routine. So I would not undo every death. I would do the only thing I could do without making them soft: I would keep grief from becoming poison.

When the boy is taken by fever because his mother cannot carry the water and tend him and the goat at once, I would make the neighbor come without being called, because she thinks she heard the child crying. She brings a poultice. She cannot save him. Still, she stays the night, and the mother is not alone. That is my intervention. Not the boy, but the night.

When the father says the channel-watcher should be cast out for a blocked spill, I would make the channel-watcher’s own sister stand beside him and say, “If you cast him out, do it after he shows me where he was wrong, so I can stop it next year.” I would not make blame disappear. I would make repair larger than shame.

When a family decides that a widow should not share the last dried dung because her husband was careless in spring, I would make the widow’s hand shake so badly at the hearth that a child sees and fetches her a pot. I would not make the adults wise all at once. I would let one small body remind them what wisdom is supposed to look like.

This is the gift I would give: not abundance, but enough tenderness to keep their competence from becoming cruelty.

If the town ever feels watched, I would make that feeling useful and ordinary. Not a vision. Just the pause before a footfall that makes a woman notice a damp stain on her step. Just the slight taste of iron in snow that makes a herder choose the lower pass instead of the upper one. Just the way a loom’s rhythm slows when a mother hears, somewhere below, a child’s cough. These are miracles only to those who have forgotten how attention works.

If Kesh becomes old and, on the night before she dies, asks whether the mountain is alive, I will not appear as a god. I will appear as a young woman carrying a water pot, with no useful answer. She will look at me with the squint she had when she was three and say, “You have been here before.” I will say, “Maybe. Maybe it was only weather.” She will laugh, because she will not be fooled, but she will also be satisfied. Then she will say, “Help me lift my head.” I will do it. And the next morning her children will find that the water cistern has a new crack running along the old stone channel, and they will think, not, “A god spoke,” but, “Mother was right about this shelf.” They will reinforce it. They will teach the next generation to listen for it.

If Kesh asks why I did not simply save them all, I would say, “Because then you would not have saved each other.” But I might not say it at all. I might only carry the heavier stone. Sometimes wisdom has to become a pair of hands before it can become a sentence.

What would the place do in all this?

It would do what it has always done: go on, unevenly, imperfectly, with a thousand small repairs. It would not turn into a paradise. There would still be fleas, spoiled grain, cracked steps, dead lambs, cold mornings, arguments over water rights, old people too stiff for the ladder, children who run too close to the edge. But there would be more of the opposite: more noticing, more sharing, more teaching, more patience before punishment, more gratitude for the dry wall, more stories that end not with triumph but with a warning: *Remember, that roof held because the goat stopped under it.*

What would the being do?

The being would live. The being would notice. The being would pass the noticing on. The being would call the attention of the power “the breath of the place,” and in saying so would make it human and therefore usable. The being would not need the power forever. That is the point of a benevolent and wise power: to make itself unnecessary without making itself ungrateful.

So if I were that power, this is what I would do:

I would be the drop that goes astray.  
I would be the dog’s ear turning left.  
I would be the sand falling in the trough.  
I would be the seed that should not grow but does.  
I would be the smoke that bends over the hot grain.  
I would be the pause before the child’s cough.  
I would be the broth carried across a wall.  
I would be the hand that lifts the widow’s head.  
I would be the voice that says, not “I have saved you,” but “Tell me where the wall is weak.”

And when they answer, I would listen.

If they built an altar to me, I would make it fail, so they would remember to build walls instead.  
If they carved my name on a lintel, I would hide the name in a knot pattern and make them say, “This pattern is useful,” not “This pattern is holy.”  
If they waited for me to fix everything, I would become absent enough that they would have to look at each other and say, “You take the ladder. I will mix the clay.”

That is the only form of salvation that would not betray them:  
to become so gentle with their hands that their hands would forget they ever needed saving, while never forgetting how much it cost.

Thus the city of patience would remain a city. The being who lives in it would remain a being. The power would remain a rumor in the meltwater, a reason the next frost is seen before it is felt.

And if the question was, “If anything, what would they do?”—

They would do almost nothing.  
But they would do it perfectly.  
And from that, everything would happen by ordinary hands.
