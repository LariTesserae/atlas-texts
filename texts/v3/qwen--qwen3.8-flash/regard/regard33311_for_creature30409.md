A benevolent and wise power would first do the thing that powers find hardest: it would not rush. It would not arrive with the old habit of correction, the assumption that brightness must be tempered, that heat must be cooled, that stillness must be disturbed for its own good. It would look at the basin and see, immediately and without flattery, that this was not a wounded thing. It had no missing water to be supplied. It had no decay to be healed. It had no hunger to be filled, no enemy to be feared, no darkness to be rescued from. It was not waiting in the corner of the earth like a child abandoned. It was waiting in no way at all. That was its perfection, and its perfection was not a failure.

The benevolent power would understand this because it was wise. It would see the pale beetle stopping where the mineral rim could warm it, not because it had chosen, but because choice was already too small and too sudden for it to use. It would see the ribbon lying in the damp hollow, tasting the gradients of salt and temperature, not as a creature searching, but as an organ reading the body it belonged to. It would see the folded wing-things on the high shelves, not restless, not migratory, not waiting for wind, but acting as living regulators of a heat that had no night. It would see the arches, the fused stems, the platforms, the three stones in a line, the low ring with its smooth gap, and it would understand that these were not signs of builders, or ruins, or messages. They were habits. The basin had become so thoroughly integrated with itself that its body had learned to make the shapes of thought without needing thought.

And still, the wise power would not turn away.

Because a benevolent power does not ask only, *Is this place suffering?* It asks also, *Is this place permitted to be more than it can be?* Not in the sense of being forced, not in the sense of being made better by outside standards, but in the sense that a gift may be left at a door for no other reason than that love exists.

If anything, the benevolent power would do one small thing.

It would make a shadow.

Not a cave. Not a hole. Not an exit. Not a breach. Not the kind of opening that would bring weather into the basin, or rain, or cold, or the violence of seasons. It would do something so gentle that the basin might not notice at first, except as a place where the relentless completeness of the light had declined to reach.

It would choose a low root-arch, one of the curved fusions where leaf became roof became wall became roof again, and there it would permit a tiny absence. A hand-sized dark. A place where the hot white glow, which came from everywhere, would quietly agree not to touch. The shadow would not be made by blocking light with a body, nor by extinguishing the ceiling’s fire. It would simply appear, as if the light had grown careful. It would be small, cool, soft-edged, and impossible. The basin would know immediately that it did not belong, and yet it would not be able to mistake it for injury.
