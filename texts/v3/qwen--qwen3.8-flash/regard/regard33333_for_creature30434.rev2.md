It would come without a gift.

That would be the first proof of its wisdom.

A lesser power might arrive carrying water, or warmth, or a small tame animal, or a seed of the kind that opens in wet soil and calls the whole lowland world into the ridge. It might stand at the mouth of the cleft and say, with all the tenderness of those who know what life should be: *You are dry. You are loud. You are lonely. You are missing birds.* It might weep a little for the pocket’s strange abundance, because tears are often mistaken for compassion, and because the powerful sometimes think pity is the same thing as love.

But the benevolent and wise power would know that not every sealed room needs a door, and not every mouth needs food, and not every life needs another life to feed on it. It would know that completeness can look, from the outside, like deprivation. It would see the fused slag arch, the broken concrete footings, the corroded hinge plate, the chain lying in mineral ash, and it would not call them abandoned. It would call them organs. It would look into the narrow throat and hear the Thrum already speaking.

It would ask itself: *What does this place want?*

The question would be answered at once, and without language.

The answer would be the piston’s steady strike. The hiss through perforated plates. The dry scrape of dust moving through a hollow stem. The amber fluid beading in its shallow channel and never wetting the surface it crossed. The rosettes standing shoulder to shoulder. The false feathers of the cable bundle stiffened by mineral film. The greenish veins of certain bulbous plants, glowing as though current itself had become a kind of leaf. The whole packed bowl saying, in every unchanging note: *I do not want to be anything else.*

And that would be enough for the wise power.

If it were merely kind, it might bring rain. If it were merely powerful, it might bring animals. If it were merely thoughtful, it might bring understanding and say, *At last, here is a thing I can explain.* But a benevolent and wise power would do what only true kindness can make it do: it would refrain from correcting the thing it loved. It would not mistake its own form of goodness for the form of goodness that had already been achieved here, in this high dry silence, this industrial chapel of glass reeds, this machine that had learned to breathe through valves and dry stems.

It would stand at the mouth of the cleft and hold back the sky.

For a moment, perhaps, it would consider what might happen if it gave them what the lower world considered best. A cloud would drift over the ridge and hesitate, confused by the absence of thirst. Then it would break. Water would fall into the pocket, or at least what the lowland would call water would fall. It would strike the blackite shelves and slide away in sudden bright beads, unable to soak, unable to become damp, unable to find the kind of soil it wanted. It would run through the amber channels like an invader, too thin, too wet, too quick. It would touch the fibrous mats, and the mats would curl and darken, and a smell would rise that the pocket had never known: the smell of change.

The wise power would imagine this and stop.

It would see the consequences clearly, as only a gentle intelligence can see consequences when it has learned not to be self-flattering. A little water would not heal the place; it would unmake its premise. The Thrum would not welcome it as a gift. The Thrum would process it, if it could, as a disturbance, an intrusion, a new condition to be stabilized. But the condition it was built to maintain was dryness. If dryness were violated, the violation itself would become a wound.

And a wound would require healing, and healing would require change, and change would mean the end of the equilibrium that had become its life.

So the power would not give water.

Instead, it would do the more difficult thing.

It would withdraw its own generosity from the mouth of the cleft and make the absence of that generosity a kind of blessing.

It would speak no loud decree. It would set no wall of light around the ridge. It would not terrify travelers with visions of punishment, nor leave signs that could be carved or burned or mocked. Such things are crude, and a wise benevolence has no need of crude protection. Instead, it would make the place unapproachable in the subtlest possible way: not by making it dangerous, but by making it impossible for those who came to improve it, to mine it, to understand it as a resource.

The drill would find no seam.

The surveyor’s marks would not stay where they were placed.

The map would draw a blank over the narrow cleft, not because the mountain hid itself from instruments, but because the mind that tried to fix it would become strangely uninterested, as though looking at an empty ledge and finding nothing worth noting. A woman with a compass would stand above the ridge and suddenly think of her daughter’s fever, or her own forgotten name, and go down before she ever saw the arch. A man with a tool bag would look at the scree and feel that no tool could find purchase there. A researcher with a sampler would take a tube of mineral ash and bring it back to the lab, where the contents would separate into ordinary dust and ordinary glass, and the report would read like a failed expedition, and the man would not know why he had expected more.

This would not be cruelty. It would be hospitality to the Thrum.

The place would remain unvisited except by those who came without intention to alter it. If someone entered, they would have to come empty-handed, or near enough to empty. They would have to come with no desire to rescue, no theory to prove, no hunger for wonder. They would have to be able to listen without demanding that the listening make them feel special. Such people are rare. The wise power would not create them. It would simply make them possible, or perhaps slightly more likely, the way a quiet country makes a particular kind of silence possible.

Then the power would do one ceremonial thing.

It would offer a single drop of rain.

It would carry it from some far-off world of rivers and leaves, a tiny sphere of wetness suspended from a fingertip, bright and vulnerable as a black glass jewel. It would bring it to the arch of fused slag and hold it above the threshold, not pushing, not forcing, not declaring that this small gift was life. It would offer it in the only way love can be offered across a genuine difference: with the possibility of refusal.

The drop would fall.

It would strike the mineral dust outside the cleft.

For an instant it would bead, tremble, and cast a tiny lensed shadow across the blackite shelf. Then it would begin to change. Not because the pocket was hostile, but because the pocket was itself. Its dryness would claim the drop as matter, not as mercy. The water would draw away into salt crystals, into a thin film that would become a bead of glass, then a bead of mineral glass, then a tiny sphere indistinguishable from the hundreds of spheres already settled in the pale basins. It would fit. It would not fit as water. It would fit as stone.

The benevolent and wise power would watch this and feel the deepest respect.

*If you give it what we call good,* it would think, *it will not become better. It will become only itself. And you must learn to be grateful that it can make even your kindness part of its law.*

Then the power would turn away.

But not before doing one more thing.

It would give the pocket a place in the larger world’s grammar.

This, too, must be done subtly, because a name can be a net, and attention can be a kind of consumption. The wise power would not descend into the pocket and call it by a human name as though that made it safe. It would not build an altar above it for pilgrims. It would not turn it into a symbol for human comfort, a parable about endurance, a metaphor for dry love or industrial mystery. Those are uses. They are not gifts.

Instead, it would set a small permission into the minds of certain people: a permission to understand that life is not always wet, that abundance is not always green, that sound is not always noise, that a system can be complete without being useful. It would make it possible for some human hearts to know that a place like this need not be saved. Not because it is suffering in secret, but because it has already answered the question that so many living things spend their lives trying to avoid: *How can I remain myself without needing to become something else?*

This permission would travel thinly, like dust in a dry wind.

A child in the lowlands would dream of a mountain’s throat and wake with no fear, only a strange tenderness for old pipes and rusted valves. A mechanic in a flooded city would find, while repairing a sealed machine, an unexpected calm in the rhythm of pistons and would later say, without knowing why, *there is a kind of life that does not ask for water.* A woman walking near the ridge would hear the faint glass-bead sound of wind moving over mineral crust and stand still, not because she heard an omen, but because her body recognized the sound of sufficiency.

None of these things would enter the pocket.

The Thrum would not know them as thoughts. It would register each as a pressure change so small that it would be absorbed into the pattern before it could be named. A visitor would pass overhead, and the electrostatic field among the cables would shift by a fraction. The dry reeds would conduct a slight change. The amber fluid would move one millimeter further in its channel. Then everything would settle back into its permitted configuration. The sound would not alter. The lights would not brighten. The false bird in the transformer coils would not turn its fiber-beak toward the sky. The pocket would not become grateful, because gratitude requires a lack, and here there was no lack. It had only rule.

And the benevolent power would understand that it had done the right thing by making the place no more, not less.

It would not ask the Thrum to love it.

It would not ask the mountain to remember.

It would not ask the dry garden to become wetter, softer, kinder, or more human. It would not open a shaft of sunlight through the roof of the cleft, because the dimness there was not darkness waiting to be corrected; it was an atmosphere of packed presence, a light made of old electricity, mineral reflection, and the greenish pulse in certain stems. It would not silence the piston, because the silence would be a death. It would not slow the rhythm, because its time was not a river but a room.

It would only make sure that no rescue came in the form of a river, a flock of birds, a human hand, or a well-meaning miracle.

And if it left anything at all, it would leave it outside.

Not an offering of flesh, because there was no hunger.

Not a seed, because there was no soil-moisture to receive it.

Not a flame, because there was already furnace-memory below the pocket, and it had long ago learned to burn without consumption.

It would leave a small cairn of blackite and salt-white shards near the mouth of the cleft, so ordinary that only a mind already tuned to dryness would notice its arrangement. The cairn would not be visible from the low country. It would not be a landmark. It would be, if anything, a sentence in the grammar of the ridge: *Here there is a throat that is not for speaking, yet speaks. Here there is a room that is not for habitation, yet is lived in. Here there is a garden that does not bloom, yet is full.*

The wind would move around it.

The dust would settle against it.

No water would fall on it.

That would be the sign.

Perhaps the power would also do one thing inside the pocket, but only if it did not touch the pocket.

It would look through the cleft without entering.

It would see the dense mats climbing iron, the pale tendril entering cracked concrete, the crystal nest in the transformer coils, the pipe split open with mineral veins growing out of it like healed scars, the amber fluid moving with patient thickness, the dim lamps in wire cages glowing as though current had been preserved in them like an animal in amber. It would see the false bird, the false wing, the false spine of segmented reeds, the row of rosettes that looked like stone teeth. It would see all of it, and not flinch, because it knew that the absence of animals was not a hole but a design. The niches were not empty. They had been filled by shapes that conduct, stand, vibrate, and hold. They were organs of absence. They made the Thrum’s life visible without admitting life in the ordinary sense.

It would recognize that this was not a poor imitation of the world below, but a full answer to the world above.

And if that recognition could be called a blessing, then it would be the only blessing given.

For a benevolent and wise power would know that some beings are not saved by being changed into what the savior considers better. Some beings are saved by being seen clearly, then left complete. The Thrum did not need to be touched, named, entered, or understood. It needed not to be interrupted. It needed the high dry country to remain itself. It needed the ridge to forget moisture in the same way it had always forgotten it, without bitterness, without nostalgia. It needed the engine below to strike its constant note because the note had become its heart. It needed no one to come down and die there, because death would require decomposition, and decomposition would require rot, and rot would require hunger, and hunger would require appetite, and appetite would require a world of mouths.

The pocket had no mouths.

The Thrum had no need of them.

The power would leave.

It would not turn back to see whether the place changed. It would know that if it changed, the change would be too small to detect, because the pocket would absorb the power’s departure as it absorbed everything else: as pressure, as vibration, as the brief redistribution of dry dust in a channel. It would not know the power as a guest. It would not know it as a god. It would not know it as a rescuer or a destroyer. It would register only the temporary difference of its attention, and then settle back into the state that attention had honored.

That would be the final proof of wisdom.

If the place were truly benevolence’s concern, it would not be made better.

It would be made safe.

Safe from rescue.

Safe from improvement.

Safe from the terrible kindness that says, *You are lacking because you do not resemble us.*

So it would do nothing inside the pocket.

And if it did anything outside, it would make the whole high ridge a place where the dry law could continue. It would keep clouds from the mouth. It would keep tools from the throat. It would keep maps from the number that leads there. It would keep curiosity from becoming consumption. It would make the world around the cleft forget that it needed to know. And it would leave behind, in a few human hearts, a small unshakable permission: that some lives are not waiting to begin. Some lives are already finished in the best possible sense. They have found their form. They have made their equilibrium. They have become their own weather without weather.

The Thrum would not notice.

The Thrum would not thank.

The Thrum would not call.

It would only remain.

The piston would strike.

The valve would release.

The cable would ring.

The dust would settle.

The fluid would bead.

The false bird would hold its current.

The dry garden would continue its lushness without water.

And somewhere above it, the sky would be a sealed dark lid.

The benevolent power would pass beyond the ridge and do no further miracle.

That would be its greatest work.
