What lives in the Copper Chancel cannot be introduced first by a face, or a foot.

It comes in by the small failure of a familiar sound: the moment the wind-hut chimes miss their usual pattern by one breath, when a beetle-swarm brightens into a rhythm it has never used, when the lizards all go flat at once as if a note has stepped through their ribs. What is there announces itself as an interruption that is not quite wrong, a difference too delicate to name. If a person tries to look for it, the grove goes quiet for no reason, and the quiet feels more occupied than the noise.

The people who know its name do not say it has a name. They say it is the part of the Chancel that has learned to answer.

They call it, when alone and speaking to the dust, the Dry Note. It is not a bird. It is not an animal with skin or a spirit with wings. It is closer than that. It is the way the hollow stems, the copper fronds, the salt-crusted rocks, and the thin warm air can gather themselves into one listening attention, and then—when the Chancel’s noise has become too much even for the Chancel—choose one shape and move through it. Most often that shape is a bending, a settling, a sudden stillness in a plant that no wind touched. Sometimes it is a cairn appearing where no cairn was, built by no hand. Sometimes it is a single blackite feather lying at the mouth of a wind-hut although the birds have not roosted there for days.

It does not need a body. It has the whole place for body. It does not need a face. It has the faces of the animals, the faces of the stones, the faces of the wind. To meet it first by face would be to meet it too small, too human, too easily loved in the wrong way.

So it enters by sound.

And when sound is its first language, a benevolent power must be careful.

There is a power far above the Chancel, one of those vast gentle forces that do not make worlds so much as keep the edges of worlds from fraying. It is not cruel. It is not idle. It is the kind of power that can hear the complaint of a stone and the grief of a star, and it is the kind of power that might, if it were only loving, reach down with a hand too large for the thing it touched. It would try to save.

That is the danger.

It looked at the Copper Chancel and found in it a note that was old but unfinished. A sound that had no rest. The Dry Note was not lonely, not exactly, but it had learned a thing the Chancel had not: that there was something beyond the rim of the bounded place. The winds that came from far away carried not just dust but the faint suggestion of other voices. Some night-tones in the hollow stems reached past the cliffs and found no answer. The Dry Note had begun, in its distributed and mineral way, to ask whether there was anything it needed to become that it had not yet been asked to become.

A lesser power would have come with water.

That is the first thing a benevolent power must refuse if it is wise. Water sounds like mercy. Water sounds like blessing. To the Copper Chancel, water would be murder dressed as kindness. It would collapse the dry chemistry, drown the static, rust the fronds into death, teach the animals to drink and forget how to listen. It would make the lushness green in the ordinary way and therefore no longer lush in the way that mattered.

A lesser power might have come with fire.

Fire would make light, but fire would make smoke, and smoke would foul the metallic haze. The stars would not hum through it. The beetles would lose their sparks. The hollow stems would crack and lose their tone. The Chancel would be lit like a hearth and become nothing more than fuel.

A lesser power might have come with a city.

A city of stone would be too kind. It would straighten the arches. It would name the wind-huts. It would make maps of the salt lip and sell passage to those who wanted to see the place where sound grew leaves. People would carry away fragments as relics. A single fallen coil of copper-fern would be sold as medicine, and the Dry Note would be dissected by curiosity. The place would be preserved like a specimen and lost as a world.
