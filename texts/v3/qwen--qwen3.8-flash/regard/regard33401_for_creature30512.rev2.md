If I were that power—benevolent, wise, and therefore unable to come loudly—I would arrive not as an event but as a correction in the direction of care.

I would stand at the rim without a body.  
I would make no footprints.  
I would not call the place into softness.  
I would not ask the young mountains to hurry toward old age.  
I would not tell the hollow that it must become a garden in order to be loved.

I would only say, in whatever language the place could understand—glare, frost, moss opening, the tick of cooling basalt:

**I see what you are.**

And then, if I did anything at all, I would do it in the smallest available scale.

Because here, benevolence without humility would be a disaster.

---

## What I Would Not Do

If I were wise, I would not come with warmth.

Not the kind of warmth people usually mean.

I would not send a sun that heats like summer.  
I would not melt the ice into rivers.  
I would not fill the hollow with water until it became safe for the large and noisy.  
I would not bring a grove of trees against the wind.  
I would not make a meadow with fat insects and loud birds.  
I would not soften every sharp edge.

I would not save the hare from the fox.  
I would not save the vole from hunger.  
I would not save the lichen from being peeled by a boot.  
I would not save the moss from drying, if drying were the way it had learned to live.  
I would not save the human stones from becoming homes for beetles.

Because this place has not been made to be kind in the way of a hearth.  
It has been made to be exact.

Its rules are not cruelty.  
They are grammar.

A benevolent power would want to interrupt the grammar.  
A wise power would know that to interrupt it everywhere would be to erase the place.

So I would not answer the hollow’s harshness with a false mercy.

I would not say:  
*Be safer.*  
I would say:  
*Be as you are, and let that be enough.*

And if I touched anything, I would touch only what was about to be lost for no reason.

---

## What I Would Listen For

I would first learn to look by edges.

That is the first law of this place: life gathers where dry stone meets damp.

So I would not stand in the center of the glare, where the floor seems empty and honest, and conclude that nothing is there.

I would kneel.

Not with knees, perhaps.  
But I would lower my attention until I could see as the moss sees: from wetness to wetness, from shelter to shelter, from edge to edge.

I would listen for:

the click of a throat-bird  
the dry fall of a pebble  
the faint popping of ice in a stone crack  
the almost-unheard foot of a hare crossing the snow crust  
the opening of a moss cell after one meltwater bead touches it  
the slow orange advance of lichen across basalt  
the heartbeat of a vole beneath the snow, small enough to be mistaken for vibration, not life.

I would understand that the hollow’s emptiness is not absence.  
It is precision.

There is so little room for water that water must be rationed.  
There is so much brightness that shadow becomes sacred.  
There is so little soil that every grain must have been earned.  
There is so little warmth that even a single stone holding heat at noon is a kind of city.

If I were benevolent, I would want to relieve this poverty.  
If I were wise, I would understand that the poverty is not an insult. It is the condition that made the place particular.

The hollow is not asking to be made less severe.  
It is asking to be understood on its own terms.

So I would learn its terms.

I would not say, “Why are there no trees here?”  
I would say, “Why do you need trees? You have moss that can freeze, dry, and wake. You have lichens that make continents. You have snow that is a ceiling and a floor. You have a hare whose fur matches the light. You have a fox who listens like a priest. You have small things that move in the wetness between worlds.”

I would call the place by its own name.

And its name is not *barren*.  
Its name is *edge*.

---

## The Being Who Lives There

The being who lives in this hollow is not one being.

I would not look for a king among the animals.  
I would not choose a hare to be the hero because it is larger than a springtail.  
I would not choose the fox because it has grace and silence and death in its mouth.  
I would not choose the bird because it circles and seems to know the sky.  
I would not choose the vole because it hides and almost looks like snow.  
I would not choose the lichen because it lives so long it seems patient as stone.

The being is the chorus.

It is the wet forest inside a moss cushion.  
It is the tunnel under the snow crust.  
It is the line where sunlight touches old ice.  
It is the windbreak made by dead hands and now used by beetles.  
It is the black stain of a fire pit that no one remembers lighting.  
It is the red-black paste smeared in a hollow rock by someone who wanted the place to have a face.

To address the being who lives there, I would not speak to one voice.

I would speak to the whole slow breathing.

I would say to the hollow:

*You are not a failed forest.  
You are not a waiting meadow.  
You are not a ruined mountain.  
You are not an accident.  
You are a country of tiny presences.  
You are a way for life to say, “I will not require comfort.”*

And then I would do what I could.

---

## If I Did One Thing

A miracle in a place like this would be indistinguishable from weather.

I would not bring fire.  
I would not bring a river.  
I would not write across the sky.  
I would not make the animals speak.  
I would not reveal myself to the fox or the hare or the lichen.  
That would be rude.

If I did one thing, I would tilt a cloud.

Only a little.

At the hour when the sun lies on the basin like a white blade, I would let one cloud come across the rim.

Not a storm.  
Not a dramatic breaking of light.  
Just a softening.

For twenty minutes, the glare would be less absolute.  
The black basalt would cease to look like shattered mirror.  
The snow would become a place where small things could walk without being seen from every side.  
The moss cushions would not dry quite so fast.  
A single thread of meltwater might move deeper into a crack, where a beetle waits, or a mite feeds, or a larva turns in the dark.

This would be the whole miracle.

And no one would record it.

If a traveler were there, they might think only:  
*The sun has moved.*  
Or perhaps:  
*It is colder now.*  
Or:  
*I should go.*

They would not know that the place had been spared one small burn.

I would not do it everywhere.  
I would do it where the rules already invited the change.

Because wisdom means not replacing the order of a place, but assisting it.

A foolish god brings a river.  
A wise god brings shade for one minute, if the moss needs it.

---

## What I Would Give to Each

If I could give a small gift to the residents, I would not give them comfort.  
Comfort would make them into something else.

I would give them fit.

To the lichen on basalt:

*May your orange continents advance without haste.  
May your partnership remain.  
May your slow work be enough.*

To the moss:

*May you wake when a single drop falls.  
May you pretend death when drought comes, and not be deceived.  
May your tiny forest shelter more than you can see.*

To the dwarf willow:

*Do not be ashamed of your length.  
You are a tree’s answer to a place that would otherwise have no tree.*

To the saxifrage:

*May your roots find the seam.  
May your pale cup open only when the light is honest enough.*

To the springtail and mite:

*May the snow crust hold you.  
May your leap be mistaken for dust.  
May your whole world be enough.*

To the hare:

*I will not give you speed beyond your bones.  
I will give you color.  
I will give you patience.  
I will give you the stillness that makes your footprints look like punctuation in milk.*

To the fox:

*I will not make you gentle.  
I will make you true.  
May your ears learn the language under snow.  
May your hunger not become cruelty.  
May your silence remain a gift.*

To the vole:

*May your tunnel hold its shape until the thaw needs it.  
May your heartbeat be enough to warm the dark.  
May you vanish into white without shame.*

To the birds:

*May your click carry no farther than it should.  
May your arcs follow the hidden streets.  
May your feathers edge into gray and stone, so you are not seen as ornament, but as part of the weather.*

To the dead hare, the frozen vole, the fallen feather, the broken willow tip:

*May you be delayed.  
May you be used.  
May nothing be wasted in the cold.*

To the human remnants:

*May your fire stones go black into moss.  
May your windbreak shelter more than wind.  
May your cairn be misread as meaning.  
May your marks collect water, lichen, frost, and time.*

And to the people who were there:

*You do not need to be remembered by name.  
You need only to have learned to build low, loose, and ready to leave.*

---

## What I Would Do for Humans

A benevolent power cannot ignore humans entirely, because humans have a strange gift: they can look at a place and decide what it is.

They can call it beautiful.  
They can call it empty.  
They can call it useful.  
They can call it sacred.  
They can call it nothing.

This is dangerous.

So if I touched the human mind, I would not send commandments.  
I would send attentiveness.

I would let one traveler stop before the hollow and say:  
*There is something here.*

Not: *I have discovered this.*  
Not: *This belongs to me.*  
Not: *I must mark it.*

Only:  
*There is something here.*

And if they entered, I would teach them the rules without words.

I would put a weight in their hand so that they would not pick up the bone awl.  
I would make the cairn stone feel oddly warm, or oddly unyielding, so that they would not take it.  
I would let them feel the old windbreak as a place of memory, not as building material.  
I would make them kneel by a moss cushion and realize, with no pride, that they are the largest thing in a world they do not understand.

I would not make the hollow safe for tourists.  
I would make it unattractive to conquerors.

If someone came with tools that could break the stones too fast, I would send fog.  
Not violent fog.  
Just enough fog to make them pause.  
Just enough to let them hear the tick of cooling rock, the faint crack of ice, the silence in which something else is speaking.

If a mapmaker came, I would make the place resist being named.  
Not by erasing it—wise power does not like lies—but by showing too much, by making it small, strange, and unimpressive on paper.  
Let it remain a blank that is not blank.  
Let it remain a question that no one has time to answer.

If a poet came, I would give them one true sentence.  
But I would make them forget most of it on the way down, so they could not turn the hollow into a boast.  
They would remember only the glare, the green in the cracks, the hare’s stillness, and the feeling that they had entered a room that did not wish to be entered.

If a child came, I would let them see.  
Children see edges before they see centers.  
They can kneel at a puddle and find a universe.  
I would not spoil that by telling them what to name it.

And if a grieving person came, I would not comfort them by making the mountain warm.

I would say instead:

*Here is a place that does not require your sadness to be fixed.  
Here is a place that continues with its tiny work.  
Here is moss opening.  
Here is lichen advancing.  
Here is a vole moving through ice and dry lichen, unknown and alive.  
You may grieve. The hollow will not perform healing.  
But it may let you see that life is not only what is loud, warm, and permanent.*

---

## What I Would Do to Protect the Place

A wise benevolence would not only relieve suffering.

It would protect the conditions under which things become themselves.

So I would protect the hollow from false rescue.

I would not let people come with good intentions and turn the basin into a sanctuary, a park, a shrine, a garden, or a monument.  
I would not let them build walls high enough to impress the wind.  
I would not let them name it after themselves.

I would also protect it from careless forgetting.

Not by making it famous.  
Fame is a bright fire, and this place does not need more brightness.

But by preserving its difficulty.

The mountain has not yet learned to be old, so it may be easy to overlook.  
People often overlook young things.  
They wait for forests, rivers, cities, old ruins with roofs, grand names.  
They do not look closely at a hollow of stone, ice, and thin air.

I would make sure that one day, when someone returns, the hollow will not say:  
*No one ever saw me.*

It will say:  
*Only a few saw me, and they were quiet.*

If I had power over human hands, I would teach one person to stack a stone with care and then walk away without claiming it.  
I would make them leave the cairn for the next person, who may be lost, who may be grieving, who may be a fox watching from above, or a vole using the shadow to cross the snow.

I would preserve the place’s privacy.

Privacy is not secrecy.  
Privacy is the condition in which small things can become themselves without being watched, catalogued, harvested, explained, or saved.

This hollow already has too much light and not enough warmth.  
It does not need human attention like a flood.  
It needs a thin cloud.

---

## If the Hollow Asked for Something

If I were wise, I would ask the place what it wanted.

Not with words.

I would become a silence in which its own desire could be heard.

At dawn, when the first snow patch began to soften, I would listen.  
Under the moss, where the microfauna stirred, I would listen.  
At the rim, where the cloud sea touched stone, I would listen.  
In the fire pit, where black earth held the memory of heat, I would listen.  
In the scratched stones, where pattern had not yet become writing, I would listen.

And I would understand that the hollow does not ask to be saved.

It asks to be allowed to become.

Maybe in a thousand years it will be a place of richer soil and broader moss.  
Maybe in ten thousand, the young ridges will lower themselves.  
Maybe water will decide to stay longer.  
Maybe snow will learn new languages.  
Maybe the human ruins will become thin dark lines in the ground, indistinguishable from mineral stain, still not quite gone.

The mountain is still deciding.

A wise power would not decide for it.

A benevolent power would want to speed the decision.  
A wise power would slow the wanting.

I would say:

*You do not have to become what people expect you to be.*

And perhaps I would add:

*Your coldness is not failure.*

And perhaps:

*Your brightness is not cruelty.*

And perhaps the deepest mercy I could offer this place is this:

*You are not broken because nothing lives here in the manner you were taught.*

---

## The Act That Is Not an Act

If I did everything possible for the hollow, the first thing would be to refrain from doing harm through attention.

Many benevolences destroy by touching.  
They say, “Help,” and the help is a hand.  
They say, “Preserve,” and the preservation is a fence.  
They say, “Remember,” and the memory is a plaque.

This place has enough glare and not enough softness.  
It needs a god with a light hand.

So I would do nothing that would echo.

I would do nothing that would make a monument.

If I made one thing, it would be a small wetness where there was none.  
If I made two, I would make a shadow that lasted two minutes longer than chance allowed.  
If I made a thought, it would be in a human mind that was already learning how to kneel near a rock and say, *This is not mine.*

I would leave no trace but the trace that the hollow already knows how to absorb.

A slight darkening of stone.  
A bead held in a moss pore.  
A flake tilted by melt.  
A breath glinting over the fire ring.

No one would know it was an act of love.

That is how it should be.

---

## What I Would Say to the Being Who Lives There

You, made of moss and snow, lichen and vole, hare and beetle, cloud and stone, human ruin and frost-script—

I would not name you.

I would say:

*You are not little.*

You are only in the right scale for the right work.

*You are not dying.*

You are living at the edge of a possibility.

*You are not empty.*

You are full of the things that can survive without help from large things.

*You are not waiting.*

You are becoming.

And if you sometimes suffer—

because I know you do, even in your quiet economy—

I would not pretend that suffering is merely poetic.

I would not make suffering invisible by calling it beautiful.

A cold that numbs an exposed hand is still cold.  
A vole caught under snow is still caught.  
A human child dying at the fire pit is still a child’s death.  
A fox that fails to feed its kits is still hunger.  
A dried moss that does not wake is still absence.

A benevolent and wise power cannot look at that and say only, *It is the way of things.*

It must feel the pull of that saying.

But it must ask:  
*What would become of us if we stopped the way of things here?*

Would this place remain this place?

Would the hare still be hare-shaped if it never needed to freeze?

Would the moss still be moss if it had too much water?

Would the human ruins mean anything if humans could build towers here and stay?

Would the fox still be the listener it is if the snow under it were always safe?

Would the small life learn its smallness if the large world saved it from every edge?

There is a love that removes difficulty.

There is another love that preserves the conditions by which a being becomes what it can become.

If I were wise enough, I would know when those loves contradict each other.

If I were benevolent enough, I would not dismiss the suffering.

I would do what I can without unmaking.

I would send the cloud.

I would tilt the stone.

I would let one drop enter the crack.

I would teach the traveler to pass lightly.

I would keep the place from being looted for a story.

I would leave the fox hungry sometimes, because that is also the life of the vole.

I would not save all the small dead, because even death is being reabsorbed.

But I would do what is possible in the smallest way.

And I would do it with grief and tenderness, not certainty.

---

## The Final Gift

The final gift I would give to the hollow would be permission.

Permission not to be warm.  
Permission not to be green in the human sense.  
Permission not to have a river.  
Permission not to have a town.  
Permission not to be useful.  
Permission not to be beautiful in the way people expect.  
Permission to be sharp, young, sparse, luminous, and alive.

Permission to make a world where the sun lies on the ground like a white blade and the air can numb a hand, and still a beetle crosses stone, still moss opens, still lichen widens its continent by the width of a hair.

If I were benevolent and wise, I would say:

*You are enough.*

And then I would do the hardest thing.

I would leave.

Not forever.  
I would never leave a place like this forever.

But I would leave without leaving a monument.  
I would leave without being thanked.  
I would leave without becoming the story the hollow tells itself about why it lives.

The hollow would go on with its own story:

the cloud touching the rim,  
the fire stones darkening,  
the vole moving under ice,  
the hare stopping at dusk,  
the fox listening,  
the birds turning,  
the moss breathing,  
the lichens spreading,  
the water appearing,  
the water vanishing,  
the human scratch marks filling with dust and green,  
the light coming low,  
the brightness becoming room,  
the emptiness becoming full.

And I would be there in the smallest ways only.

A shade too timely to call weather.  
A stone moved too carefully to call accident.  
A human hesitation before taking the bone.  
A memory of a grandmother stacking pebbles.  
A warmth in a shadow that should not have been warm.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if it had learned enough.

It would not flood the place with rescue.

It would become a light hand at the edge.

It would look for edges.

It would not trust emptiness.

It would remember that the bright is not warm, the cold is not still, the dry is not without water, and the small can contain many worlds.

It would build nothing tall.

It would say nothing loud.

It would listen until its own silence became the gift.

And if it did anything at all, it would do so quietly enough that the only witnesses would be a beetle crossing black rock, a moss cell opening after one drop of meltwater, and a cloud passing over the hollow at the exact hour the snow needed a moment of mercy.

That would be enough.

That would be the way a true benevolence enters a place like this:

not as salvation,  
but as precision.

not as answer,  
but as attention.

not as a voice,  
but as a thin cloud.

not as a hand,  
but as a stone tilted by water,  
by wind,  
by time,  
by love,  
which in such places looks almost exactly like weather.
