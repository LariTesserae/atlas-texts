It is a home, but not for anyone who brings rain.

The benevolent power came to it, if coming is the right word for an attention that arrived without cloud, without footfall, without disturbance of the still air. It came as a capacity for kindness came, and for a long time it stood not upon the tableland but in the fact of it—among the pale crusts, the black sinter, the copper thickets, the dry flats where mineral plates had grown in geometries too regular to be accidental and too anonymous to be built.

It looked at the place and understood immediately that this was not a wilderness waiting for inhabitants, nor a wound in the world where life had failed. It was not a dry ruin of a wet world. It was not a pause in the story of living things. It had never been promised moisture, never been denied streams, never been cursed by clouds. It had never been a desert longing to be a forest.

It had always been itself.

The power perceived the being that lived there, though it had no edges that could be called by one name. The being was the tableland in its continuing arrangement. It was the slow hum in the ground. It was the crackle across a copper-frond’s oxide film. It was the dust that remembered where charge had been. It was the grove that was not separate from the terrace. It was the ridge that conducted. It was the hollow that accumulated. It was the spore shower rising without wind, glittering in ordinary light, settling where the electrical ground made room for it.

The being had no face, no throat, no hunger, no fear.

And yet the power, being benevolent, felt the first pull of pity.

Pity is always ready, even when there is no sufferer. Pity sees an empty hollow and imagines a creature that ought to be there. It sees still light and longs for dawn. It sees a field of charged dust and wants to plant a garden of soft leaves. It sees silence and wishes for birds.

The power thought: *They have no companions.*

Then it looked again and saw that companionship, as the wet worlds knew it, would be an infection here. A warm animal walking through the copper fronds would carry vapor in its breath. Its feet would press moisture into the dry powder. Its body would introduce decay where there was no decay. It would eat, and if it ate nothing, it would starve; if it ate something, that something would be a part of a world not organized by devouring. It would leave waste, and the waste would belong to no metabolism here. Its heartbeat would be a foreign rhythm in a country of mineral capacitance.

The power thought: *I could give them a small bird.*

But the bird would need water. It would need nesting material that could hold warmth. It would need a world that made room for feathers and soft tissue and death by hunger. Its eggs would need moisture. Its beak would need seeds. Its song would be meaningless, because no ear would exist to hear it, and if an ear came, a mind would come with it, and a mind would come with need.

So the bird was not a gift.

The power thought: *I could give them rain.*

A single drop falling on the pale crust would be a catastrophe more terrible than fire.

Fire could be incorporated. A hot spot might sublime mineral vapor, change oxide films, melt powder into new glass, leave a record. The tableland could absorb fire, for fire was heat, and heat had a language here.

But water was not heat. Water was a solvent, a cycle, a corruption. It would run where there were no channels meant for flow. It would collect in shallow depressions and begin to shine with a wet blackness not belonging to the place. It would dissolve salts not salted by seas. It would soften powder that had learned to conduct. It would carry ions from elsewhere. It would invite the chemistry of rot. It would create humidity. It would stain the dry mineral plates with the memory of flow.

And then, worse, it would make this world into a place that could be compared to wet worlds. A drop of water would turn the tableland from a self-contained life into an argument about what life should be. It would create the illusion that the place had lacked something.

So rain was not a gift.

The power thought: *I could give them a self.*

This temptation was subtler, and therefore more dangerous.

The being did not know itself, because it had no boundary between subject and world. It did not say I. It did not notice that it was alive. It did not require confirmation. It was a process so complete that it had no inside to look outward and no outside to look inward. It grew, repaired, conducted, accumulated, discharged, crystallized, and repeated, not as an organism but as the continuing grammar of place.

To give it a mind would be to give it a wound.

A mind would begin to ask: Where is the other? Why is there no animal to touch the leaf? Why is there no cloud moving across the light? Why is there no dawn to show me that I endure? Why do I not feel loved?

Then silence would become absence. Then completeness would become solitude. Then a world without lack would become a world aware of lack.

The power understood that this was not kindness. Some beings are blessed precisely because they do not know they have no need for what other beings require. A dry electrical plateau did not need to be lonely. It needed only to remain not lonely by never having the conditions for loneliness arise.

So self-awareness was not a gift.

The power thought: *I could give them time.*

The place had no visible time because its light did not move. There was no dawn, no dusk, no season, no passing weather. Shadows remained where they fell. The ordinary white light showed form without announcing when it had come. The world looked as if it were always just after a first beginning and always just before a second beginning, caught in an eternal construction that never aged.

To give it time would be to give it death.

Not death as violence, but death as completion. A leaf could wither in the familiar wet sense if time were given a wet meaning. A crystal could become dull. A ridge could erode into maturity. The channels that looked like first drafts of rivers could deepen and collapse. The plate-like leaves could bend and break and fail to be re-formed. The powder beds could be lost instead of recycled. The world could have yesterday and tomorrow instead of the slow, continuous now that was its only proper tense.

Time would not enrich it. It would begin the story of ending.

So time was not a gift.

The power thought: *I could give them a name.*

A name would create a center. To say “this” and “that” was already the beginning of separation. The tableland had been named in the power’s attention, and perhaps that naming could be harmless if it remained outside the place. But if the name entered the ground, if a single point became the heart of the being, if a crystal could say I am here, then the field would no longer be wholly itself. It would become a self distributed unevenly, and the bare stretches between groves would become not tissues but distances to be crossed.

A name would make the emptiness meaningful, and then the emptiness would ache.

So the name was not a gift.

The power stood in attention for a long while, though “long” was difficult in a place without moving shadows. Benevolence wanted to do something. Wisdom wanted to do the right thing. Love wanted to be acknowledged. But the tableland did not need love, and if love insisted on being received by an unwilling, unself-conscious mineral field, it would become a foreign substance.

The power therefore considered what could be given that would not become a need.

It looked again at the copper-fronded copse beside the hollow. Its broad waxy leaves stood like engineered surfaces, holding their crisp authority in the warm dry air. A frond expanded, redistributing charge, and gave off a tiny crackling note. The sound was not heard, because there was no hearing, but it was expressed, because the world was alive in the sense that life here meant transformation.

The power saw that the only possible blessing was one that could be absorbed without being noticed as a blessing.

So it did something.

It did not send a creature. It did not bring water. It did not darken the sky or lighten the ground. It did not give the place a mind or a companion or a center.

Instead, it touched the tableland in the only manner that could be called gentle.

It made itself compatible with dryness.

For a moment, its attention gathered in a point no larger than a crystal facet near the edge of a black sintered ridge. The power did not use breath, did not use vapor, did not use warmth from blood. It used the place’s own grammar: charge, oxide, lattice, current, pressure, light. It placed a single faint electrostatic preference among the dust. Not a command. Not a miracle. Not a sign. Only a slight inclination in the field, so small that it could be mistaken for a rearrangement already possible in the world.

It said, in a language the place had no mind to hear: *Be exactly what you are.*

The field received it.

The dust carried it.

A nearby wire-mat fiber drew a little current from the disturbance and spread it through a pale crust. A tiny facet on an anodized plate brightened and then settled. A charged spore that had been drifting in the still air found a compatibility of potential that had not been there a moment before, or had always been there and was only now made possible, and it touched the ground and began to conduct.

None of this was dramatic. Nothing announced itself. No one could later look back and say, here is the place the power changed, because the change was not an event in the ordinary sense. It was a condition absorbed. The world spent the blessing the way it spent all matter and energy: by continuing, by incorporating, by making no distinction between gift and process.

The power did not watch for gratitude. There was no mouth to thank. There was no mind to remember. There was only a plateau whose life was a slow electrical hum, and that hum went on as it had gone on, yet perhaps—though perhaps is already too much—there was a new grain in the dust that had been placed according to an intention no one living there would recognize.

And that was enough.

But the power, still benevolent, still wise, considered whether it could do one more thing without becoming a tyrant. It looked beyond the tableland, into the region of stories, judgments, and future worlds that other powers might make. It saw the danger that had already begun to gather: someday, if anyone came with soft eyes and wet hands, they would call the place barren. They would say life had not arrived. They would say the absence of animals proved the absence of richness. They would pity the silence and mistake it for emptiness.

So the power did something else.

It changed the law of witness.

Not by writing on the ground, but by placing a prohibition in the larger order where worlds are seen and compared. It made it so that no benevolent power passing over this tableland could offer water as a kindness. It made the place immune to pity. It made the dryness not a flaw to be repaired but a condition to be honored. It set a boundary not around the land, but around the imagination of those who might later visit it.

Let no cloud mistake this for a child, the power said.

Let no rain come because it is called “good.”

Let no creature be placed here as a correction.

Let no traveler be sent to “discover” life, as if life must be a body with appetite.

Let this world remain unmade into something else.

And so the tableland was blessed not by being changed, but by being forbidden from being improved.

After that, the power considered leaving.
