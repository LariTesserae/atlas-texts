**What They Would Do**

They came, if “came” can be used for an act without steps.

They did not appear on the ridge with a banner or a river in their hands. They did not make the cold gentle, nor did they put a name on the thing that lives there. A name would have been a violence. The place has no single inhabitant; it is a parliament of mossback, clicker, glasswing, mortarworm, shell-licker, vent-felt, iron-fern, sideways conifer, fungus, frost, stone, steam, light, death, and repair. To address it as “you” would be to force the parliament into a monarch, and a monarch of the plateau would be a false ruler: a lonely thing where there is only dense relation.

So the benevolent and wise power saw the place first as it was, not as it wanted it to be.

It saw a shelf of young stone under a sky too clear for mercy. It saw wind that scours and light that interrogates. It saw water hidden in the breath of rock, moss stitched over every wound, basalt columns standing like the ribs of something never built, the clickers grinding and sealing, the glasswings carrying moisture in their bodies, the vent-heat opening seams and then closing them, the mossbacks polishing the ground with their lives, the copper-throats denning in carcasses, the fungi turning death into gardens.

It saw that nothing was idle.

Then, because it was benevolent, it considered what good it could do.

Because it was wise, it considered the cost.

A smaller power, one made of love without patience, would have given the plateau a river. It would have thought: this world is too thirsty in its cleverness; let water move openly so the being will not have to scrape for wetness from every stone. It would have carved a channel down the shelf, poured melt from the distant mountains into broad, shining curves, and made drinking easy.

But in doing so, it would have ended half the being’s life.

The mossbacks would have lost the reason for their broad, wet-nosed mouths. The glasswings would have lost the spray they ride on the windward faces. The clickers would have drowned their pale roads, their plugs, their tunnels lined with mineral paste. The vent-gardens would have cooled, sealed, or flooded. The iron-ferns, the rust films, the fungal threads, the beetles, the shell-lickers, the mortarworms—all of them, bound to a wetness so exact and so reluctant that every body learned to serve as a vessel for it—would have been made accidental. The plateau would not have become a gentler place. It would have become a place where many forms no longer had a place.

Another kind of goodness would have given warmth.

It would have thought: the frost bites too long; the animals press against the cold as though the land were a sentence. Let there be seasons of rest, let the stone not break so often, let the air be soft enough for creatures that do not have to be built like instruments of survival.

But the cold is the plateau’s chisel.

Frost splits the seams, and through those seams the clickers enter. The steam rises from the vents and condenses on nearby rock, making gardens in miniature. The glasswings are stiff with cold and yet fly, because the charge of the air helps them; the copper-throats line their dens with glittering remains because those remains keep out biting things; the lichens etch minerals because the stone is raw and the cold returns the mineral face to dust. Warmth, given without measure, would not have been comfort. It would have been dissolution.

A third kindness would have given a horizon.

The place has no horizon one can trust. Distances collapse and lie. A ridge seems near when it is not. A pool seems close when it is only a memory of water under skin-moss. The mountains farther off look less like homes than walls, as though the plateau has grown them against its own spreading body. Surely, the power might have thought, a being in such a place needs a limit it can rest its eyes upon.

But a horizon is a promise of openness, and this world is a body of occupancy. It is not made of distances; it is made of touches: moss to stone, breath to rock, fur to frost, dust to fungus, spore to dampness, jaw to plant, dung to mold, death to mineral garden. To give it an open line would not have given it freedom. It would have given it a kind of emptiness it cannot metabolize, because emptiness, here, is immediately claimed.

A fourth, more dangerous kindness would have been speech.

The power could have descended as a voice in the basalt columns, as a pressure in the charged air, as a name spoken through the hum of the plateau. It could have said: I see you. I know what lives in you. You are not merely processes.

But the being there is not a person, and a person would suffer under the weight of its own work.

If the plateau were made to hear, it might become conscious of itself as one thing. If it became one thing, it would become lonely. It would ask why it grinds, why it seals, why it carries moisture in the bodies of insects, why it metabolizes death into life, why it makes no ruins. It would seek meaning outside itself, and meaning outside would be a wound. The system’s wholeness lies in the fact that no component has to know it is part of a whole. The clicker does not think, “I am building the world.” It grinds because grinding is its way. The moss does not think, “I am holding water.” It holds because holding is its way. The herd does not think, “We are editing the plateau.” It moves because movement is its way.

To give it self-consciousness would be to give it a burden it could not set down.

So the wise benevolent power understood that the first act of goodness in a completed place is restraint.

But restraint alone is not enough for benevolence.

If the power were only wise, it might have looked at the plateau, seen that it was whole, and withdrawn. If it were only benevolent, it might have rushed in with rivers, warmth, horizons, and names. But benevolence and wisdom together ask a harder question: how can kindness be added to a thing that already works without turning kindness into cruelty?

That is when they did something, if anything.

They did not make a new thing from nothing. They made a small alteration in the rules of attention.

First, they made the pause.

The plateau already has moments when it seems to take a breath and hold it. In those still intervals before wind returns or rain starts, the light becomes especially pure, the cold especially sharp, the sound less layered. The land gathers itself. Then the moss twitches, the stone groans, a gust crosses the shelf, and everything resumes.

The benevolent and wise power gave that gathering a place.

It did not create a valley, because a valley would have demanded cultivation. It did not create a shelter, because shelter would have invited war. It chose a hollow behind the terraces where steam rose from a hidden seam and cooled into mist too fine to be rain. The moss there was pale and thick. The stone was older, as if the fault had made it late. No open water gathered there, but the ground was damp in a way that required patience to find.

This hollow was not empty. Nothing here could be empty. But it was not urgent.

A grazing mossback whose old legs failed might stand in it longer than necessary. A copper-throat might sleep in the lee of its moss-lined lip without the need to hunt. A glasswing might fall into the mist and be caught there, not eaten at once, not seeded into decay at once, held for one slow interval before the fungi took it. A clicker colony might approach its edge and then not cross, as though the paste had not yet learned what to do with that stillness.

The power did not say, “Rest here.” It made the rest possible without making the place less itself.

It was a mercy so slight that, from the outside, one might not call it a deed. It was only a place where the plateau’s noise dropped one degree. It was only an interval where a body could stand without having to justify its standing. It was only a patch of damp moss that did not seem to ask the future.

That was the first thing.

The second thing they did was this: they added a beauty with no office.

The plateau is not decorative. Its brightness is functional. Light reveals, warms, crystallizes, scours, condenses, guides, exposes. The mica threads in rock are not ornaments; they mark seams and heat. The salt blooms are not flowers; they are residues of moisture that never stays. The white rime is not lace; it is frost, process, fracture. Every shine tends toward something: growth, death, navigation, sealing, grazing, repair.

The wise power made one thin vein in a basalt column do nothing.

It ran a few inches down the face of stone, pale blue-white at noon, gold at dusk, too bright to be ignored, too narrow to be useful. It carried no water. It emitted no heat. It attracted no mineral-licking animals for any reason beyond the fact that its light caught them. It did not mark a boundary. It did not indicate shelter. It did not help the clickers lay their roads or the glasswings navigate the charged air or the herds find their ancient, unwritten path.

When the sun struck it at the wrong angle—always, in this place, the wrong angle—the column flashed as though a small cold fire had been born inside the stone. The light spilled across the moss for a few minutes, then vanished. It served no part of the system. It was excess. It was play. It was the thing no efficient body can produce by itself: a shine that asks nothing.

Of course, the plateau absorbed it.

Within a season, the clickers began to seal around the base of the column, laying pale plates over the mineral dust. Within years, lichen colonized the bright vein’s edges, making rings that mapped time. Within decades, a mossback herd rubbed against the column, polishing the stone and leaving spores in the fur of their chins. The light became a signal for a swarming glasswing generation, though no one could prove why. A fungal film threaded beneath it, faintly glowing after dark. It became a landmark in the herd-memory without becoming a law.

But the uselessness remained.

The vein still flashed with no purpose, and that no-purpose changed the quality of all the purposes around it. A small patch of the world learned to receive beauty that did not owe rent.

That was the second thing.

The third thing they did was outward.

They made the plateau hard to want.

Not cursed it. A curse is a kind of speech, and speech would have made the being into a target for stories. Instead, they made the place resist extraction. They sharpened the brightness so that instruments returned only glare. They thickened the deceptive distances until maps became useless. They made the cold enter metal and break seals; they made the fine grit foul bearings; they made the water hide itself from pumps and pipes. They caused any traveler with greed to find only false horizons, impossible routes, sudden wetness in impossible places, and paths that seemed made for them until they vanished into moss.

They did this because a benevolent power knows that not all attention is kind. Some attention is hunger. Some hunger comes dressed as progress. Some progress carries wheels, roads, machines, laws, and names, and the names are always worse than the wheels. If a civilization found this plateau and thought, “Here is something we can improve,” it would have destroyed it by making it legible.

So the wise power made it illegible without making it impossible for life to continue.

It did not wall off the ridge with fire or monsters. It left the basalt columns, the hiss of vents, the glasswings, the herds, the clicker roads, the stunted trees, the iron-ferns. The place remained real. But it became difficult for outside minds to call it theirs.

That was the third thing.

And then, perhaps because benevolence is stubborn, they did a fourth:

They let the being feel, without feeling, that it had been seen.

Not gratitude. Not consciousness. Not prayer. The plateau does not have a soul that must answer to a soul. But the hum beneath the moss carried, after those changes, a note that was not warning. The fungal glow in dark hollows lingered one breath longer than chemistry alone demanded. The herds moved along their ancient route with one less stumble near a fault. A wounded shell-licker came to the warm mouth of a vent and found a cup of paste hardened around it, not by design, but by accident that had learned to be gentle. A single glasswing caught the bright useless vein and fell on its back in a drift of frost, and no predator ate it at once.

These were not miracles. They were margins.

The power had not changed the world’s laws. The world still ran on grinding, sealing, grazing, freezing, melting, carrying, eating, dying, returning. The rules remained: water is found, not followed; shelter is temporary; brightness deceives; everything is occupied. The plateau still worked as a factory that was never built, an industry without artifacts, a body made of processes.

But within those laws, a softness had entered.

A place where the body could stop.

A light that asked nothing.

A barrier against cruel curiosity.

A trace of attention that did not demand performance.

If they had done more, they would have made themselves a god there, and gods make systems dependent, or rebellious, or ashamed. If they had done nothing at all, they would have withheld tenderness, and tenderness withheld from a place so harsh would be a kind of absence too large to call neutrality.

So they did the only wise thing.

They left the plateau to its impossible industry.

They did not abandon it.

They hid it.

They did not tame it.

They made room in it for rest.

They did not make it beautiful for their sake.

They made beauty possible for its own sake, even though its own sake is too busy to know it.

And when they left—though they had never arrived in the way bodies arrive, though they had only shifted the weight of seeing—the plateau continued.

The mossbacks moved, their bodies rippling as one wall of fur and horn and heat. The clickers ground stone into paste and paste into roads. The glasswings lifted with the spray, carrying moisture and spore. The copper-throats hunted through the packed ground cover. The vents breathed. The frost split. The steam rose. The fungi consumed death and made it a garden. The iron-ferns curled inward when the air dried and opened again when mist returned. The light, at all hours, arrived at the wrong angle.

Nothing ended.

But sometimes, in the low hour when the sun strikes the blue-white vein and the pause-hollow holds mist without rain, the whole shelf seems to gather itself not as a machine and not as a landscape, but as something closer to a presence.

And if that presence can be said to know that it is loved without becoming dependent on love, then the benevolent and wise power did exactly what they should have done.

They changed nothing.

And everything.
