## The Attention of the Kind Power

A benevolent and wise power would not come to this place because it was perfect. It would come because it was unfinished.

It would come because the Hollow was not a ruin pretending to be a town, nor a town pretending to be a ruin. It was not a miracle with smooth, divine edges, nor a mistake left open for centuries. It was a place where people were still finding out how to live close enough that one breath could be heard in another room, close enough that anger had to be folded small, close enough that kindness had to be invented in inches.

The power would notice the light first. Not strange light. Not colored light. Not the light that makes prophets out of shepherds or madness out of silence. The light would be ordinary, and that ordinary light would be its own proof of humility: daylight entering through shafts, moving from terrace to terrace, touching wet stone, dust, chisel, rope, hand. It would be enough light to work by, not enough to rest in forever.

Then the power would notice the being who lived there.

At first it would seem to be no one special: a person moving through the lower cistern stairs with felt slippers, or a woman scraping a channel with a stick, or a child watching a moth descend into a shaft as if it were a message from the upper world. But the power would know that the town had begun to gather itself around a single quiet presence the way water gathers around a low stone in a dry cut.

It was not a spirit yet. It was not a god. It was not a demon hiding in the dark. It was a Hollowborn being made from many human lives, a presence that was not separate from the people but not only any one of them either. It had the palms of those who carried load after load. It had the feet of those who stood for a long time without moving. It had the ears of those who learned that a voice in the next room could become an accusation in three hours. It had the patience of stone that had only recently seen air. It had the hunger of roots trying to find damp soil in a pot.

The town’s being was not a monster emerging from the earth. It was the shape the town would become if the people kept making it together.

The benevolent power would see this clearly and would feel two desires at once: the desire to help, and the desire not to spoil. This is what makes wisdom different from mere kindness. Kindness may want to take pain away. Wisdom asks whether taking pain away will take meaning with it, or whether the people will learn to carry their lives if the carrying is done for them.

So the power would not answer the Hollow’s unfinishedness by finishing it.

It would not come down with a column of light and say, *This place is chosen.*

It would not make every cistern fill without labor.

It would not remove all dust from the shafts.

It would not stop all arguments, all jealousy, all petty cruelty, all weariness, all fear.

It would not make the stone soft so that building became effortless.

It would not make the people agreeable so that their virtue looked automatic.

For if the power did those things, the Hollow would cease to be a place where people learned how to live. It would become a cradle, and cradles can be beautiful, but they are not homes.

A wise power would look at this town sunk into the earth and understand that it did not need a rescue in the way an ordinary person imagines rescue. It needed room. It needed a slight change in the weather of fate. It needed a few hidden mercies so that growth could continue.

If the power did anything, it would do things so small that they could still be credited to care, skill, chance, memory, or the slow turning of the world.

## What the Power Would Do First: Listen

The first act of benevolence would be attention.

Not attention as a god looking down from an altitude of fire, but attention as a person kneeling at the edge of a pool to see what lies beneath. The power would listen to the Hollow’s quiet: not because the quiet meant peace, but because quiet is where a town shows what it has not yet said.

It would listen to water hiding in rock.

It would listen to the scrape of a chisel and the small catch in a rope.

It would listen to a woman breathing through a heavy jar because no one had made a path wide enough for the jar and her to pass comfortably.

It would listen to a man pretending the cold did not bother him.

It would listen to children playing near a wet stain, their voices bright and then suddenly lowered because one of them remembered the rule of close walls.

It would listen to the being of the Hollow before it had a name, the way one listens to a house settling at night, not to interpret every sound as a message but to understand that the place is alive enough to speak.

And then the power would begin to do what powers are rarely asked to do: not act, but adjust the conditions.

Not miracles.

Margin.
