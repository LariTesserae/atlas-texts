## What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do

A benevolent and wise power, looking upon the Dry City, would first feel the old temptation of comfort.

It would see no water and imagine thirst. It would see streets without makers and imagine abandonment. It would see towers that were never built and imagine grief. It would see life too thin for human warmth, mineral rather than green, and its heart would rise with the urge to give: *Let there be rain. Let there be soil. Let there be birds, orchards, windows, voices. Let there be something soft.*

But wisdom would ask: *Who has asked?*

So the power would come not as fire, not as flood, not as a hand, but as attention.

It would come as a long pressure in the basalt, as a slight change in the way the fog moved through a colonnade, as a dimming and brightening of the salt plates, as a moment when the wind paused in the middle of its usual grammar and listened.

It would come to the plaza where shadows hesitated.

And there, if there was truly a being who lived in the city, it would meet it.

---

The being would not arrive as a god among stones.

It might be a glasswalker, tall and pale, standing in the place where one avenue met another, its reflective body holding broken light like a shard that had learned patience. Or it might be the city itself wearing one body, because a place that has learned to dream in architecture may at last dream itself a face.

It would have no need to speak. If it spoke, it would speak in the city’s way: through a click, a settling of salt, a fog that turned into an unexpected street, a stone that moved a little overnight so that a triangle became a doorway.

The wise power would ask, if asking could be done in such thin air:

> “What would you have me do for you?”

The being would answer by not being saved.

It would stand where the wind passed between two columns and let the columns hum. It would let ash-moths settle on a fresh crack in the glass plain. It would let the dry fog crawl around its feet like a familiar thought. It would let a six-legged walker flatten into invisibility because the walker understood the city better than any newcomer.

And if words were needed, the answer would be dry, exact, and free of drama:

> “Do not make us more comfortable.  
> Make us less likely to be misunderstood.  
> Do not give us water as a gift.  
> Do not give us warmth as a blessing.  
> Do not give us people as a rescue.  
> We are not waiting to become what you are.”

The benevolent power would listen.

That would be its first miracle.

---

## What Benevolence Alone Might Have Done

Benevolence without wisdom would have loved the Dry City in the way of those who love gardens more than wilderness.

It might have opened a vein of frost and called it spring.

It might have carried clouds from a distant world and made the first true water on the plateau.

It might have caused moss to grow in the basalt columns and grass to lift through salt.

It might have made a human road across the obsidian glass and named the city as a ruin, because a place without people is so often misread as a place without meaning.

It would have made it more familiar.

It would have made it easier.

It would also have made it less itself.

A river would have dissolved the grammar of the dry fog.

Warmth would have ended the crystalline patience of the nights.

Soil would have buried the mineral gardens and made them strange again, as if they were waiting for a better kingdom.

To save it, benevolence alone would have killed it gently.

---

## What Wisdom Would Ask

Wisdom would ask: *Is this suffering?*

And the Dry City would answer through its own life.

It would answer with salt plates lifting and settling, with glass ticking under thermal stress, with the hum that moves through terraces where the ground is learning the shape of its next street.

It would answer with ash-moths arriving at broken places, with barite stalks opening in fog, with sulfur blooms collapsing by day and returning by night, with burrowers beneath old salt performing their quiet arithmetic of pressure.

It would answer with the Obsidian Staircase and its clicking, not as a ghost but as the plateau adjusting itself.

It would answer with the high stones that rearranged themselves into a line, then a doorway, because the city was still drafting.

And the being at its center would stand motionless, not lonely, not waiting, not broken.

Established.

Alive in the way a city lives when it is no longer only stone.

Then the wise power would understand that benevolence must sometimes become restraint.

To do something might be the kindest thing.

To do almost nothing might be the only kindest thing.

---

## What They Would Do, If Anything

They would do this:

The benevolent and wise power would not bring water.

It would not bring warmth.

It would not bring people.

It would not rebuild what was never built.

It would not explain the city to itself in human terms.

It would not name it a wonder, because wonder can become possession.

It would not name it a ruin, because ruin can become pity.

It would not name it a dream, because dream can become dismissal.

It would, if anything, give it one thing:

> Permission to remain what it is.

Not the kind of permission that comes from authority, but the kind of permission that is recognition: *You are valid without my approval. You are complete without my improvement. You are not an error of the world.*

The power would say, in the only language the city could fully understand:

> “May you continue to be dry.  
> May your fog find its avenues.  
> May your salt remember its plates.  
> May your glass know the event of breaking.  
> May your stone humming be more than accident.  
> May the stones on the high terraces be allowed their long, unfinished sentences.  
> May you be a city that needs no citizens, and may that needlessness be honored.”

Then, because the power was wise, it would make that permission small enough not to become a wound.

It would not flood the plateau with blessing.

Blessing, like fire, can destroy when it is too bright.

It would give one subtle gift only.

## The Gift of the Threshold That Refuses Improvement

At the highest rim of the Dry City, where the wind thinned and the stars stayed visible in daylight, the power would cause a single stone to settle.

It would not be large. It would not be beautiful in any human sense. It would be basalt or salt or glass or a meeting of all three, worn at the edges, dark in some lights and pale in others. It would stand at the entrance to a broad plain or at the threshold between the Obsidian Staircase and the upper terraces.

It would be a stone of listening.

Whenever someone arrived who meant only to take, to claim, to improve, to fix, to harvest, to map, to possess, to make the city useful, the stone would emit a faint tone beneath the feet. Not loud. Not threatening. Not magical in the crude way of punishment.

It would only make the arrival slow down.

If the traveler tried to lay a brick, the brick would grow brittle.

If they tried to bring a machine that required water, the water would become frost-lace and slip away.

If they tried to plant something that needed wet roots, the seed would become a pale crystal flower that did not need them.

If they tried to name the city a ruin, the name would feel false in their mouth, like dry salt on a wound.

If they tried to make the city into a spectacle, the fog would move just enough to hide the best angle.

If they tried to make it into a temple, the hum would fall silent beneath their prayers.

But if they came carefully, if they came without a plan to make the Dry City more human, the stone would not stop them. It would merely make them quieter. It would let them hear the ticking of the glass. It would let them notice the way a plaza could widen when entered and contract when crossed. It would let them understand that distance here was not a failure but a rhythm. It would let them see that life can depend on static, vapor, pressure, and slow mineral breath.

It would make them guests, not owners.

That would be the benevolent and wise power’s act.

Not to change the city.

To change the city’s contact with what might change it wrongly.

---

## The Gift of One Unspoken Name

The Dry City had no need of names, because names are often for the ones who do not understand something.

But if there was a being who lived in it, that being might accept one small thing: a way to be remembered without being misremembered.

The wise power might breathe a single tone into the city’s mineral memory. Not a word in any human tongue. A resonance. A frequency that the salt plates could hold, the ash-moth wings could carry, the basalt columns could hum, the obsidian staircase could click.

If someone came with love for the city, they would not hear a name.

They would hear:

> *Not barren. Not broken. Not waiting.*

If someone came with greed, they would hear:

> *Not useful.*

If someone came with grief, they would hear:

> *Still here.*

If someone came with curiosity, they would hear:

> *Listen first.*

This tone would not command the city. It would protect the city’s meaning.

The city would not become less strange.

It would simply become less vulnerable to being called less real.

---

## The Gift of a Dry Fountain

Perhaps, if the power had a heart that could not completely refrain from generosity, it would give one small dry fountain in the largest plaza.

It would not pour water.

It would not become a puddle or a pool.

It would be a shallow depression, maybe carved by wind, maybe arranged by salt, maybe set in place during a night when the plateau dreamed a little more sharply.

At its center would be a broken shard of glass.

When the fog moved through the plaza, the fog would enter the hollow and circle once.

It would not make the place wet.

It would only make the air taste cleaner for a moment, mineral and sharp, like cold stone after a long tick from within.

A traveler would pass it and stop without knowing why.

A six-legged walker would cross the rim and flatten as if reading a pressure it respected.

A glasswalker might stand near it for an hour, its reflective body holding the dim light in fractured pieces.

The fountain would not ask for faith.

It would not become holy ground.

It would simply be a place where the city’s dryness was not treated as loss.

That, too, would be a mercy.

Not warmth. Not moisture. Not fruitfulness.

Only dignity.

## What the Place and the Being Would Do

The place would accept the gifts, but not as a supplicant accepts them.

It would accept them as a city accepts a new street: by adjusting.

A few columns would shift.

A salt ridge would widen.

A fog would take one extra corridor, then return.

A new plaza might appear for three days and then remain.

The being—the tall reflective thing at the rim, or the city’s own personhood wearing that shape—would turn its head, if turning could be called that, and the city would know that something small had changed.

Not a rescue.

Not a transformation.

Not a gift from outside, but a door opened inward by the city’s own processes.

The ash-moths would gather at a new crack and listen.

The burrowers would settle more quietly beneath old plates.

The click-choir of the Obsidian Staircase would continue, unchanged at first, and then one step lower, as if acknowledging that even the bowl had received a visitor.

The high stones would become, over the next cycle, not a doorway exactly, but a line that suggested a doorway was possible.

The being would not thank the power.

If it did anything like thanks, it would continue being itself.

That is enough, sometimes.

To love a place and ask it to continue is a great kindness.

---

## What the Power Would Do Afterward

The benevolent and wise power would not stay.

If it stayed too long, its presence would become a new center, and the city would be tempted to build itself around the visitor rather than around its own slow logic.

A wise power knows that not all attention is good.

Too much attention can fossilize a living strangeness into a museum.

Too much attention can make a being define itself by the witness.

Too much attention can turn protection into control.

So the power would withdraw.

But it would leave behind the stone of listening, the unspoken name, the dry fountain, and one further act that might be the greatest:

It would not claim the city as a favorite creation.

It would not declare it sacred.

It would not forbid others from entering.

It would not make the city special in a way that separated it from the rest of the world.

It would simply place a gentle law at the edge of all possible visitors:

> *Approach only with care.  
> Understand only by listening.  
> Leave without taking more than a little silence.  
> Do not demand water from stone.  
> Do not demand people from absence.  
> Do not demand warmth from the places that breathe by cold.*

Then the power would go back to the wider world, and it would carry the Dry City in memory the way one carries the memory of a star at night: not close, not touching, not needing, but bright with respect.

If asked later why it had not saved the place, it would answer:

> “Because it did not need saving.”

If asked why it had not made it easier for life, it would answer:

> “Because it was already alive in a way that did not require my categories.”

If asked why it had not blessed it more, it would answer:

> “Because some blessings are too large for a place to receive without losing itself.”

If asked why it had done anything at all, it would say:

> “Benevolence asked me to help. Wisdom taught me to ask what help meant. So I gave it the only help that did not contradict its nature: I honored its nature.”

---

## What Kind of Kindness Would That Be?

It would be a dry kindness.

A mineral kindness.

A kindness that did not insist on warmth.

A kindness that could pass through salt corridors and leave no footprint.

A kindness that understood this truth:

Not every emptiness is barren.

Not every absence is loss.

Not every city requires citizens.

Not every life requires water.

Not every strange being requires rescue.

And sometimes the greatest act of a wise and benevolent power is not to improve the world but to refuse the tyranny of improvement, to step back, and to let the world continue in its own grammar.

The Dry City would go on.

The being at its center would go on.

The fogs would migrate.

The salt plates would click.

The glass would break in soft bursts.

The ash-moths would drift to the new fractures.

The hum would rise where stone and vapor agreed.

The Obsidian Staircase would count itself downward into the quiet bowl.

The stones on the high terraces would form lines that were almost streets, almost doorways, almost sentences.

And if travelers came, they would find no savior there.

Only the place itself, intact and strange, asking no permission to be real.

That would be enough.

If they had eyes.

If they had patience.

If they had learned, somewhere far away, that not every quiet thing is empty, and not every dry thing is dead, and not every city needs to be a home for the kind of people who build with tools.

---

## The Final Sentence the Power Would Leave

If the wise power could write one sentence in the dry city’s memory, it would be this:

> “May you continue to be a place that needs no rescue from the one who loves it.”

And the city would answer, not in words, but by becoming a little more itself:

a fog entering an avenue that did not need it,

a salt plate lifting because the night was cold enough to be honest,

a glasswalker standing beneath the dim light like a column that had learned to watch,

a stone moving between two others to make a threshold that had no door.

And then, after all that, the power would leave.

Not because it had stopped caring,

but because it finally knew how.
