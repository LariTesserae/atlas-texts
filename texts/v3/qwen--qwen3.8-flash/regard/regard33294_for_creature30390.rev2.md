If I were that power—benevolent, wise, and strong enough to do anything—my first act would not be to enter.

I would stop.

I would stand in no doorway, because there would be no doorway worthy of this place that was not already made of stone and salt and long patience. I would not come with rain, or flowers, or birds, or a human voice saying, “Poor, dry thing, let me make you live.”

I would come as heat comes: without footprints.  
I would come as pressure comes: without name.  
I would come as attention comes: without wetness.

The hollow would not know I was there at first. Not in the way a person knows another person has entered a room. It would know me—if it knew at all—as a slight change in the wind’s pitch, a moment when the dust fell more evenly, a brief brightening of the veins in the ceiling, a pause in which the city seemed to listen before returning to its own voice.

That would be enough.

Because the being who lives in this place is not an animal waiting to be saved. It is not a lonely creature needing a companion. It is not a failed land needing water, nor a silent world needing speech. It is the hollow itself: the salt-floor, the built city, the oil-reeds, the copper blossoms, the thermal swell, the dry wind, the singing pipes, the slow mineral birth and death, the pocket-world complete in its own chemistry.

A benevolent power, if it were not wise, would try to improve it.

A wise power would not.

It would understand that not every dryness is death. It would understand that not every silence is absence. It would understand that not every empty room is waiting for a body to enter and fill it. It would look at this place—hot, loud, strange, lush without water, inhabited without inhabitants—and it would not say, “What is missing?” It would say, “What is here, and what must not be touched?”

And if it were truly benevolent, it would respect that answer.

So what would I do?

If I had the power to do anything, I would do very little.

I would do the difficult, holy thing: refrain.

I would not send water.

I would not send animals.

I would not send light as a sun sends light, because that would be to mistake this place for one meant for our eyes, our skins, our seasons. I would not send warmth as fire sends warmth, because the heat of this hollow is its own organ, its own blood. I would not send builders, because the builders are gone or were never human, and the place has already learned to build itself. I would not send children, elders, herders, hunters, wanderers, or witnesses, because a witness with blood is a contamination.

I would not even send my name.

Names can become cages. A name spoken by a god might turn into a map, and a map into a road, and a road into ruin.

Instead, I would act in three small ways—so subtle that, from inside, they might not look like actions at all.

---

First, I would protect the place against accidental water.

Not by stopping the sky. That would be too large, too interfering. I would not change the weather above, not forbid rain to fall, not seal the plain with glass. I would only set a dry guard at the throat of the hollow.

I would place there something that could not be seen unless one knew what it was: a thinness in the air, a memory in the mineral walls, a law written in salt so pure it seemed ordinary.

If dew ever approached that opening—if condensation gathered on a visitor’s cloak, if a bottle were spilled from above, if the breath of an animal came too near—I would not let it fall as water. It would become a faint shimmer before it touched the waxy leaves. It would rise, boiled away gently by heat that had always belonged to the stone. It would leave only a small white scar, like a word erased.

This would not be cruelty. It would be protection.

The hollow’s rule is: no water may be introduced.

A wise power does not violate the deepest nature of what it loves.

If I wished to save this place, I would not save it by watering it. I would save it by ensuring that its dryness was not mistaken for neglect.

---

Second, I would make the place impossible to rescue.

Not impossible to find, exactly. The opening would remain. The slit in the salt-plain would still breathe heat. The smell of sulfur and oil would still rise. But those who looked at it would not be tempted to improve it.

If a person came, they would feel it.

They would stand at the edge and know, before any map could explain it, that this was not a place that wanted their help. The air would say: *Do not lower buckets here. Do not bring gardens here. Do not bring mercy that requires water. This place has its own mercy.*

I would leave no road to it, because roads are invitations. I would leave no sign, because signs become claims. I would leave no beautiful story, because a beautiful story is often just a hook by which creatures are dragged into places they do not belong.

If someone tried to write a poem about it, the poem would feel wrong—too wet, too green, too animal. It would fail to capture the hollow and in failing, protect it.

If someone tried to build a shrine above it, the mortar would dry strangely, and the shrine would become merely another mineral lump, unnoticed by the wind.

If someone tried to lower a lamp into it, the lamp would flicker, because fire does not mean the same thing here. The heat below would answer the heat above, but poorly. The visitor would feel foolish, then uneasy, then leave.

This too is benevolence.

Not every danger to a place comes with teeth. Sometimes the danger is kindness: the desire to fill a silence with noise, to soften a stone with moss that belongs elsewhere, to make the alien familiar because familiarity comforts the heart.

A wise power would not grant that comfort.

It would say: *This place is not for your consolation.*

---

Third, I would leave one witness, but only a dry witness.

I would not put an animal in the hollow. An animal would need moisture in the end, even if it began by believing otherwise. Even a bird would carry wetness in its breath. Even a mouse would make a path with its feet. Even a cat would know hunger, and hunger in this place would become confusion.

So the witness would not breathe.

It might be a single shard of fused mineral set near the outer opening, so that when the sun touched it—and the sun is not forbidden outside; only no rain touches the opening—it would hold a memory of the hollow’s sound in its lattice. Not consciousness. Not desire. Only a faint record: a vibration that could be read by stone.

Or it might be a change within the existing city.

Perhaps one arch would ring with a slightly deeper bell. Perhaps one pipe would moan with an added overtone, like a voice learning a second word. Perhaps the copper blossoms in the iron-black basin would, in the height of the thermal pulse, produce one clear metallic note never heard before.

That note would say nothing to an animal ear.

But it would say something to the hollow itself:

*You are not alone in the world. The world remembers that you exist.*

Not *exists for us.*

Not *exists as we wish it.*

Simply: *exists.*

And then the note would fade back into the ordinary chorus: salt chimes, groaning stone, the dry hiss of oil in reed-stems, the long hum of heat moving through the throat of the city.

This would be enough.

To know that one is known—without being claimed—is perhaps the most subtle gift a wise power can give.

---

After that, I would do nothing.

I would leave the heat to pulse.

I would leave the wind to turn.

I would leave the plants to pry, to oil, to bloom, to close, to split their pods, to scatter mineral dust, to die, to crystallize, to become architecture.

I would not disturb the balance.

The balance was not weak.

The balance was strange, but it was not broken.

If one of the plants grew too thickly and choked a vent, I would not remove it. I would let the heat find a new fissure. If a wall crumbled, I would not rebuild it as it had been. I would let the dust fall, let the roots settle into the gap, let the wind learn a new song. If a doorway sealed with salt, I would not open it by force. I would let the city close like a mouth and speak in a different voice.

If the hollow began to fail—if the heat truly died, if the wind ceased forever, if the built organs collapsed beyond the reach of plant and mineral patience—then perhaps I would act.

But not as a gardener.

Not as a god pouring life from outside.

I would act as a physician of conditions, not of contents.

If the heat weakened, I would not create a new fire. I would help an old thermal vein remember its course.

If the wind stopped, I would not blow through the chamber myself. I would open a forgotten gap, allow the temperature difference to do what it had always done, let the hollow inhale again.

If the built city failed, I would not restore it to the form of its builders, if builders there ever were. I would let the stones continue their agreement with the plants. I would protect the function, not the shape.

The arches are organs, not monuments.

The pipes are lungs, not relics.

The walls are bones, not ruins.

I would preserve this understanding.

---

And if the being of the hollow could ask me anything, I think it would not ask for water.

It would not ask for green leaves, for rain, for animal breath, for tenderness in any soft sense.

It would ask only to remain what it is.

So I would answer in its own language.

I would not say, “Be saved.”

I would say, “Be.”

I would add no flesh to it. No blood. No tongue. No eye shaped for light. No ear shaped for speech.

Instead, I would give it a small permission.

To be dry and lush.

To be silent and loud.

To be built and grown.

To be inhabited without inhabitants.

To be alive without animal life.

To be complete without being like the living things that came to the attention of other powers.

If it heard me, it would answer the way it has always answered.

The thermal pulse would rise.

The veins in the ceiling would brighten.

The oil-reeds would darken their sap.

The waxy leaves would flatten, then turn.

The seed-pods would crack, not from disease but from heat, and the chamber would fill with a dust like rusted snow.

The metal-tongued blossoms in the basin would tremble.

The low pipes would moan.

The salt crystals would skitter down the terraces.

The whole pocket would ring.

And I would hear it.

Not as music.

Not as voice.

But as a being speaking its own law.

*I do not need wetness to live.  
I do not need mouths to exist.  
I do not need kindness as the soft world defines it.  
I need heat, air, stone, oil, dust, and sound.  
I need the city to remain my body.  
I need the plants to remain my appetite.  
I need the wind to remain my breath.  
I need the dryness to remain my name.*

Then the pulse would fade.

The chamber would cool.

The wind would reverse.

The plants would open again.

The dust would settle in the streets.

And the hollow would continue, as it had continued before my attention, and would continue after.

The benevolent and wise power would do this:

It would not heal the place.

It would not rescue the place.

It would not water the place.

It would not colonize the place.

It would not make the place a lesson.

It would not make the place a metaphor.

It would not make the place beautiful according to the standards of moist eyes and warm-blooded hearts.

It would let the place be beautiful according to its own chemistry.

And if that beauty included heat without flame, growth without rain, sound without voice, a city without citizens, life without animals, loneliness without sorrow, fullness without flesh—then the wise power would bow to it.

It would say, in its heart, if a power has a heart:

*You are not empty.*

And then it would leave.

And the Salt-Lung would breathe.

And the Bell-City would ring.

And the Copper Hollow would keep its own dry time, unseen, untouched, and wholly alive.
