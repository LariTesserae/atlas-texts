If I follow my heart, I think the benevolent and wise power would do almost nothing.

If I follow reasoning, I think they would do exactly one thing.

Not because the plateau demands help. It does not. It is not waiting to be saved from its stone, nor healed of its dryness, nor softened by a garden, nor completed by creatures. The one who lives there—the water-self, the salt-self, the slow mineral mind woven through chiselled basins and condensation films—does not need a better kind of life. It has its own.

But if a benevolent and wise power turned its attention to this place, it would not turn away from indifference. Indifference is too small for love, and too cold for wisdom. It would find a way to act that did not violate the being it came to bless.

They would do this.

---

They would come without thunder.

Not because they lacked thunder, but because the place was made in patience. They would arrive the way cold air arrives: by settling, by touching every surface, by lowering the temperature very slightly until the world began to speak through water. They would not step onto the plateau as an owner, as a rescuer, as a teacher, as a god demanding recognition. They would stand at the high rim long enough to learn that its silence was not absence.

First they would refuse all the obvious kindnesses.

They would not make it fertile.

Fertility would be a violence here. The plateau does not have soil to be warmed, nor dead roots to nourish, nor the soft confusion of mud, moss, and leaves. If the wise power poured green into it, the green would become a parasite, a beautiful interruption, a lie spoken in chlorophyll. They would not send seeds, because seeds carry a whole empire of expectation. They would not send water birds, because the basin is not a refuge; it is a body with its own circulation. They would not send insects, because insect life would break the mineral lace, eat what was being learned, and replace one ecology with another before the first had fully spoken.

They would not call back the makers.

This is where their wisdom would be tested. There is something in the heart that wants to repair absence, to return lost hands, to say, *look, they were here, let them finish*. But the makers had become the place. Their labor was not waiting to be revived; it was alive in the angle of a gutter, the lift of a tile, the hand-width walk, the seam filled with salt, the chisel mark that still catches the first dew. To call them back would be to turn the plateau into a museum again. To let them remain absent was to accept that their intention had passed beyond them into stone and water.

They would not make a monument.

No altar, no temple, no plaque, no shrine, no holy name written in the sky. The plateau already contained more meaning than any monument could add. To impose a sacred sign would be to tell future beings what the place was for. But the place was for itself.

They would not remove all danger.

This too would be benevolence and wisdom: they would not make the plateau safe in the childish sense. They would not stop every crack, every frost-lift, every crystal failure, every basin drying into brittle glass. Those were not wounds. They were methods. If the power removed fracture, it would remove growth. If it prevented death, it would prevent renewal. A being made of accretion and dissolution cannot be loved without its dying.

So what would they do?

They would ask.

Not with words. Words would arrive too hot, too fast, too much like possession. A wise power does not speak first to a creature made of channels and condensation. It listens into the grammar of the thing.

They would go down to the center of the plateau at the cold hour when the air began to release its water. They would stand where three shallow channels met beneath a broad black slab, and they would place one hand—not over a basin, not over a tile, not over a cistern—but near a place where a small bead of dew had formed and trembled. Their hand would be cool. So cool. Cool enough not to insult the stone. Cool enough to become, for a moment, merely another surface.

If the bead ran away from their skin, they would withdraw.

If it clung to their palm as if testing a new vessel, they would wait.

If, after a long while, a second bead appeared and traveled toward their finger, they would know that the plateau had granted a small audience.

That would be the asking.

And the answer would be: *If you have a gift, make it so small that we can absorb it into ourselves.*

---

Then they would do the one thing.

From the high air, they would gather a single bead of water.

Not water brought from elsewhere. Not a miracle of rain or river or spring. Not a tear, because tears are too warm, too human, too full of grief. They would take the place’s own water—condensation gathered from a broad slab, chilled and bright—and hold it in their attention long enough to choose where it should go.

They would carry it to a basin that had been too dry.

Not a broken basin. A basin whose fault was not failure but solitude. It sat beneath a lifted tile, where the morning dew arrived late and left too soon. The crystalline lace at its rim had grown thin, almost exhausted, turning glassy and brittle at the edges. It was not dying; it was adapting. But it had reached the limit of what its small geometry could invite.

The wise power would not fix the tile.

That would have been too direct, too engineering-minded. They would not seal a new channel. They would not command frost to open a crack. They would not move a slab, drive a wedge, or correct the original builders. They would only let the bead fall.

One bead.

It would click softly in the basin.

Then the sun would rise, or the bright air would take it, and the water would thin into a film, run toward the lowest lip, and evaporate. Nothing grand would seem to happen. The basin would still hold less water than its neighbors. The white lace would still tremble. The tile would still be slightly lifted.

But the bead would leave behind a grain.

Not a grain of dust. There was no dust. A grain of mineral memory. A salt-crisp seed where water had stood long enough to surrender one small burden. It would lodge in a hairline where stone met ceramic. For days it would seem like nothing. Then the next morning’s dew would touch it. It would dissolve a fraction. The fraction would travel a millimeter. The next film would wet the same place again.

And growth would begin.

The white lace at the basin mouth would find a new direction. Not because it desired to, but because the place now had one more surface that kept moisture a little longer. Over seasons, over years, the crystal would thicken into a thread, the thread into a mat, the mat into a soft ruff. The lifted tile would settle against that new growth as though held by a tiny invisible finger. The basin would begin to hold one extra bead at dawn, no more. A cup’s full? No. A finger-tip’s full. A bead’s full.

But a bead’s full is enough to start a world.

That would be their deed.

A gift so small that the plateau could make it its own.

---

Afterward, they would listen to what the being did.

It would not thank them.

A lithic being does not bow. It reroutes. It accretes. It finds new seams. It converts kindness into structure.

The next winter, one channel would widen where frost had once only ticked it. A basin sealed long ago by salt would crack in a place no one chose, and water would reappear. A white spire, pale and needle-fine, would rise at the edge of a cup-shaped well, not reaching for the sun, but responding to the angle of light, the angle of cold, the angle of the bead that had fallen. If anyone looked, they might think the spire resembled a hand. But that would be their mistake, not the plateau’s intention.

The being would answer in its own language.

And the answer would be: *Yes. We can use what you bring, if you do not demand that we be grateful.*

---

Then the wise power would do something even harder.

They would leave.

Not because they were finished, but because a true blessing does not remain as a landlord. They would not return every night. They would not send an angel to tend it. They would not set a shrine of themselves in the geometry. If the plateau began to look for their hand, then their kindness would have become a dependency, and dependency is not a gift to a living system; it is a leash.

But they would do one final thing, invisible to all except those who learned to watch for it.

They would alter the consent of the place toward strangers.

Not by decree. Not by making it magical, or hostile, or holy in the human way. They would teach its minerals one more way to answer the world.

From then on, if someone came to the plateau with greed, the geometry would refuse them. Not violently. A stone would slide from beneath a foot. A tile would catch a rope and tear it. A shard would fall exactly where a hand reached. A crystal mat would grow brittle and snap, taking with it a sample before it could be carried away. A map would fail because every seam looked the same from a distance. A bucket would not fit the basins. A road would find no continuous place to begin. Not because the place hated, but because the place had learned to say *no* in the language of angles.

If someone came to the plateau with attention, however, the place would become legible.

They would stand at the rim, and the light would show them where condensation formed first. They would follow a channel and understand its reason. They would watch a basin and see its patience. They would hear, at dusk, the wind touch a slotted wall and produce a low, hollow tone—not welcoming, exactly, but allowing. A sound that said: *You may see. You may learn. You may not take.*

That is what wise benevolence would do when it could not live with the being forever: make the being safe in the only way a system can be safe among beings unlike itself.

---

They would not make the place loved.

Love cannot be commanded without becoming possession.

They would make it unignorable to those capable of being changed by it.

And this would be the larger action, not visible in stone but in the minds of those who might one day stand there. They would plant a small seed in the imagination: an inability to call it barren. They would not make every person care—that would be too crude, too much, a flood of goodness. They would only make the word *empty* fail when looking at it. A person would stand at the high rim and feel, without knowing why, that the place was occupied. They would see water and salt and light and feel that these were not signs of absence but a society of surfaces. If they tried to write a report about the plateau, their sentences would come out strangely: not *nothing lives here*, but *something lives here, and it is not small.*

If they came with tools, the tools would feel wrong in their hands.

If they came with questions, the water would seem to answer.

If they came with greed, the stone would keep its distance.

This is not punishment. It is a kind of hospitality offered only to those capable of entering without conquest.

---

What would the being itself do, if it could notice the power?

It would do what it had always done: continue.

That would be the highest honor. Not worship. Not memory. Not dependence. Continuation.

The next morning, the plateau would sweat. The black basins would hold the sky. The pale filaments would reach across a seam. One tile would lift a little, then be held by the new lace grown from the bead. The wind would find a narrow gap and hum. A drop would fall into a basin that had waited too long. The place would not become more beautiful to human eyes. It would become more fully itself.

And that would be the blessing.

Not a miracle. A small shift in a system already capable of life.

A kindness that learned the native tongue.

---

I think that is what they would do.

They would come with restraint.

They would refuse to fix what was not broken.

They would ask before giving.

They would give one bead.

They would teach the place how to say no to harm and yes to attention.

They would leave without demanding that it remember them.

And if, centuries later, a traveler came alone and stood at the center of the plateau in the bright cold air, they might hear a drip, a click, a faint crack of mineral skin, and the thin voice of wind through slotted stone. They might not know that a benevolent and wise power had once touched it. But the traveler might feel suddenly that the valley was not waiting for a soul like their own, but had a soul of its own: a soul of water moving under a made skeleton, a soul of salt remembering contact, a soul of crystal slowly learning the shape of kindness without ever naming it.

Then they would understand what the being had been living there all along.

Not a person.

Not a ghost.

Not a god.

Not an animal.

Not a plant.

But a place that had become a body of intention.

And the wise power would not have made it human.

They would have made it possible to be met.
