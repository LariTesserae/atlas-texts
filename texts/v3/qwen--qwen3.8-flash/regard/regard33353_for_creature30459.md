When a benevolent and wise power looked upon that place, it did not at once come down. It knew that descending, in the ordinary sense, was already too violent. A footfall there would be a fact. A voice would become a new organ. A name would become a border. The plateau was not waiting to be rescued, nor was it lonely in the way a child is lonely, though a human heart might project its own hunger onto its silence and call that projection compassion.

If the power had been benevolent but not wise, it would have tried to make the Vessel more like a living thing than it already was. It would have brought birds to the black basins. It would have scattered seeds into the mineral lace, not because the Vessel needed green things, but because the power would have thought: a place without leaves is missing something. It might have made a spring that sang too loudly. It might have made a mouth in the stone so the Vessel could speak. It might have made eyes so it could be seen seeing. It might have made a child to play in the channels, because human kindness so often believes the best cure for emptiness is presence of the kind that can be hugged.

But the power was wise, and it understood the danger of those gifts.

To give the Vessel a plant would be to give it a new hunger. To give it an animal would be to give it predators, death, noise, waste, need. To give it speech would be to give it the capacity to call for someone who might not come. To give it a face would be to teach it what it lacks. And lack, once named, becomes a wound that did not exist before. The power did not mistake its own pity for the Vessel’s need.

It also understood the danger of doing nothing.

A merely wise power could have looked at the plateau and seen perfect adaptation, a coherent ecology of stone and water, a system maintaining itself without error. It could have reasoned that no intervention was needed because the Vessel had no suffering to relieve. It could have withdrawn, leaving the pattern to continue its slow mineral dreaming. That would have been correct, but perhaps not merciful.

For there was something almost like suffering in the Vessel, though not suffering in the ordinary way. It was the suffering of total conservation. It did not waste. It could not waste. Every disturbance became structure. Every intrusion became material. Every thing that fell into it, every boot that slipped, every drop of salt from a stranger’s skin, every bone of any animal that might somehow cross the air and land in the wrong basin, all were taken up into the pattern. It had no mercy because it had no forgetting. It was pure memory. It was pure incorporation. It was, in its own slow grammar, incapable of saying: this is not needed. Let it pass. Let it end.

The benevolent and wise power understood that such a being could be trapped not by walls but by its own completeness.

It would not rescue the Vessel. Rescue would imply failure. It would not animate the Vessel. Animation would imply absence. It would not praise the Vessel. Praise would imply an outside audience that the Vessel might begin to crave.

Instead, it did something so small that, from the high rim, it might not have seemed like an act at all.

It came at the hour when the plateau sweats.
