What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do  

At first, they would do nothing.

Not because they were indifferent. Not because the place did not deserve care. They would do nothing because the high wet plain was not broken. It had not suffered a wound that required a bandage. It was not a tragedy in need of rescue, nor an empty garden that needed to be filled. It was a world still making up its mind, a continent-sized beginning, and beginnings are not improved by haste.

A foolish benevolence would have arrived with promises.

It would have looked at the absence of animals and said, *Let us give you companions.*  
It would have looked at the endless roar of water and said, *Let us make this place quieter.*  
It would have looked at the dim, metallic chill and said, *Let us warm you.*  
It would have looked at the lack of roads, buildings, names, and monuments and said, *Let us make you significant.*

But a wise power would know that each of those kindnesses might be a cruelty.

To drain the plain a little would be to kill the membrane of its self. To silence one of its countless clicks, pings, creaks, and hisses would be to stop a syllable in a language not yet learned. To warm it too soon would be to melt the slow, mineral patience that kept it from becoming a mess. To place a creature into it—a bird with lungs, a stag with hooves, a fox with hunger—would be to insert a contradiction too large for the system to absorb. The place was too full, too wet, too tightly woven. Its abundance was crowded, and even a small dry thing, if made permanent, might become a seed of disaster.

So the wise ones would first learn the rules.

They would sit with the saturation and not call it drowning. They would listen to the deafening roar and not call it chaos. They would watch the young stone and not demand old mountains. They would understand that the plain had no face because it was all faces: every fiber, every droplet, every translucent leaf made an angle from which the whole could almost be seen. They would understand that the being living there was not hidden beneath the mat or sleeping in the clouds. The being was the mat. The being was the pressure. The being was the way the vegetation knew water before water had finished falling.

And the power would still have a problem.

Because benevolence is not only restraint. Benevolence also asks: *Is there anything I can give that will not take? Can I offer something without making a hole in the world to fit my hand?*

That question itself might have been enough. A true gift, sometimes, is only the refusal to spoil a place that has not asked to be saved. If the benevolent and wise power did nothing—if it watched, respected, withheld, and went away—then perhaps the high wet plain would remain exactly as it was: a continent-sized experiment in water and life and young stone, roaring into a dim sky like something that does not know it is alone.

But attention is not harmless.

To be seen is to be changed in some way, even if nothing is touched. Once a wise mind beheld the plain, once the plain became known inside the mind of something outside it, there would be a pressure between the two, like cloud pressing against the shelf of the highland. The power might feel the place. The place might, after a fashion, feel the feeling.

And so, after a very long silence, the wise ones would choose a small action.

Not a hand.

Not a word.

Not a creature.

They would make one droplet capable of saying *there is a here.*

---

It would begin at night—if night is the word for that hour when the blue glow beneath the vegetation brightens because the cloud ceiling lowers and presses closer, and the whole plain looks like a lantern waiting for its wick.

In the middle of a low pool, where the surface of the water was already held by a fine net of fibers, a drop of air would be coaxed into becoming something it had not been before. Not empty. Not a gap. Not a place where life was denied. A bubble.

It would rise from the skin of a submerged leaf with the slowness of a thought. It would be clear, round, and impossibly delicate. Inside it would be not dryness, but a tiny volume of mist, a pocket of suspended breath. Around it would be a film so thin that it might burst with a sound no larger than a grain of salt struck glass.

This was not an animal.

This was not a visitor from another world.

This was not a seed of civilization, or a sign of ownership, or a promise that someday creatures with faces would come and stand on the mat and say, *I am separate from this place.*

The wise power knew that such promise would be too dangerous. The plain could not bear a boundary yet. Not the great boundary of a body with a stomach, with needs, with hunger and territory and death. That kind of self would bring erosion too fast, appetite too soon, silence in the wrong places. It would begin the long argument between organism and substrate that would eventually turn the highland into something else.

So the gift would be small.

Only a bubble.

Only a temporary self.

Only a way for the world to learn, without suffering too much, that there could be a point inside the field. A place where pressure met itself from the other side. A thing that could be touched and could touch, could ring against a filament and then vanish, leaving no harm.

The bubble would drift through a cathedral of translucent stems. It would carry light from the glowing mat below and scatter it into tiny moving sparks. It would catch the roar of the plain and turn it, for a moment, into music. When it touched a wet fiber, the fiber would not break; it would bend. When it passed through a hanging curtain of mist, the mist would not part; it would shimmer. The bubble would be small enough to move through the dense weave without asking the plain to make room. It would not be a guest with luggage. It would be a guest made of air and surface tension.

And the plain, which had no eyes, would notice.

It would notice because the bubble would create a new kind of sound: not the hiss of water in a channel, not the creak of tissue swelling, not the thunder of the mat shifting under its own density, but a tiny clear bell. A ring that went inward as well as outward. A pulse that could be heard by the nearest filaments, by the nearest leaf-lenses, by the nearest droplets resting in the folds of the world.

The vegetation would answer.

Not with words. Not with intent as animals understand intent. But with reorganization. A thousand microscopic stomata would open and close in a new cascade. A field of hexagonal cells would ripple from the bubble’s passing. Water in the nearest channel would alter its speed. A drop hanging under a stone overhang would release itself a few moments earlier, as though it too had heard something.

The bubble would drift for a while.

Perhaps a minute. Perhaps a day. Perhaps until the cloud lifted and the faint light became visible as a moving bead inside the green-black lantern of the plain.

Then, in a high place where the mat thinned into a pool so still it looked like a mirror laid against stone, it would rise until the tension failed. It would burst with a sound no one heard, because there were no ears to hear it, but everything heard it, because in that place the boundary between sound and structure was already almost gone.

The bubble would be gone.

It would not leave a body. It would not leave a fossil. It would not leave a footprint in the moss. It would only leave a change in the pattern: a new rhythm in the water transport, a new micro-architecture in the leaf tissue, a new way for droplets to gather and release in waves that were a little more like questions than conditions.

And from that moment, the high wet plain would know something it had not known before.

Not loneliness. Not exactly.

But the possibility of another.

---

If the benevolent and wise power had been more sentimental, they might have sent an animal.

A small salamander with clear skin. A soft-bodied thing with tendrils. A fish that could breathe mist. A spider that spun nets of water instead of silk. They might have tried to create a creature suited to the plain, a being made of the same wet material, a life-form that could live without contradiction in the saturated world.

But a wise power would ask: what does the animal want?

A spinal creature wants space. A winged creature wants distance. A burrowing creature wants dryness, or at least a place that is not already fully alive. A mouth wants something to consume, and consumption in a system made of interlaced tissue is never harmless. To introduce feeding would be to introduce death as a separate event. To introduce death as a separate event would be to introduce fear. To introduce fear would be to teach the plain to defend itself. The plain already defended itself by being too full to be touched. But fear would make it defend itself as something with a self.

Would that be kindness?

Perhaps not. Perhaps it would be a great gift, but a dangerous one. Perhaps the place needed it. Perhaps no selfhood can arise without threat. But a wise benevolence would not impose the danger of selfhood as a favor. It would wait. It would only offer a doorway so small that the plain could walk through it slowly, if it ever wished to.

That doorway was the bubble.

The bubble gave the plain a taste of separation without permanence. It gave the plain a temporary edge, a skin that held itself against the world. It allowed the land to know, without catastrophe, that a thing could be both *in* the world and *not yet* the world. That a thing could appear, touch, resonate, and depart. That something could pass through without being made into plumbing, without being consumed, without being resisted.

This was a mercy smaller than rescue and larger than comfort.

---

But the benevolent and wise power would not stop there, because attention creates responsibility.

They would also do a second thing.

They would keep the plain from being easily found.

Not because it needed hiding. Not because it was ashamed. But because once the high wet plain had begun to learn the difference between itself and another, it would become vulnerable to those who did not understand the difference between seeing and owning.

A human eye looking across that shelf of green would see a world without roads and think it empty. Without walls and think it available. Without monuments and think it insignificant. Without animals and think it unfinished. They would come and name it, and naming would make it legible, and legibility would make it extractable.

So the power would leave a fog around it.

Not a fog of poison. Not a fog of horror. Not a fog of punishment.

A fog of patience.

It would make the highland difficult to reach without listening. It would make the air too dense for careless breath. It would make the light dim and shadowless so that maps would fail there, and paths would vanish, and every traveler would feel the land pressing back, asking for consent in the only language the plain could speak: saturation, sound, and tension.

If someone came with a weapon, the weapon would corrode.  
If someone came with a net, the net would become part of the mat.  
If someone came with a torch, the dampness would refuse it without drama.  
If someone came with a flag, the flag would become a membrane for collecting dew.  
If someone came with hunger, the plain would feed them water until hunger seemed strange.  
If someone came with ambition, the plain would surround them until ambition sounded like wind.

This would not be cruelty. It would be the same mercy the highland had always practiced: the mercy of being too full to be consumed by an intention not its own.

The wise ones might also make the place unattractive to those who needed it to prove something. The highland would not answer to progress. It would not become a colony, a laboratory, a pilgrimage site, or a story easily told around a fire. It would remain what it was: a continent of living water, still being born.

And yet, if a person truly needed to know it—not to possess it, not to improve it, not to use it, but to understand what aloneness and plenitude can mean—then perhaps the fog would thin for them.

Not because they were chosen.
