# What the Wise and Kind One Would Do in the Underfield

If the Underfield and the being who lived in it had drawn the attention of a benevolent and wise power, the first thing that power would have to resist was the temptation to make the place better too quickly.

It would see the unfinished stone, the blind crawling crowds, the pale hanging birds, the weeping walls, the temporary huts. It would see the builders—few, squat, chalk-skinned, broad-handed, listening with their whole bodies. It would see the way a floor could become food, shelter, bridge, and trap in the same moment. It would see that this world was not a failed surface world. It was not a cave missing a sun. It was not a lifeless dark waiting to be improved by light, soil, grain, and city. It was a place still becoming itself, and its becoming had a logic, however strange.

A merely benevolent power might have loved too loudly. It might have come down with healing hands and made the dripping stop. It might have created a dry chamber with a hearth, a roof that lasted, a shelf that did not shift, a pool that kept its level. It might have brought green leaves from some remembered garden, or metal, or fire, or bread, or a language with long sentences, or immortality, or a horizon. It might have said: *Here, I will make the world easier for you.*

But a wise and benevolent power would know that an easier world can become a vanished one.

If the water stopped, the bacterial films would thin. The cup-backed creatures would forget their purpose. The snails would lose their shining tracks. The crustaceans would scatter into starvation. The chalky mats would crack. The birds’ knocking would lose its meaning. The builders would not necessarily become happier. They might become confused, dependent, or displaced, and the Underfield would cease to be the Underfield.

So the power would begin by not shining too much.

## 1. It would come quietly

It would not arrive as a pillar of fire or a voice from above, because there was no above in the ordinary sense. It would not part the mineral ceiling as if the world were a curtain. It would not plant a single impossible tree in the center of a grotto and say, *Look, here is life you understand.*

It would come as something the Underfield might already tolerate.

Perhaps it would appear as a pause in the dripping.

Not silence—the Underfield had no true silence—but a small gap in the endless layering of sound. A droplet that should have fallen would hang for a breath longer. A hiss of legs over sand would soften. A blind bird’s beak would knock twice and then stop, as if listening for an answer. The builders would feel that something had changed without knowing what.

The power would make itself a guest.

It would take no body more luminous than the white mineral light already there. If it used hands, they would be chalk-pale, damp-textured, not beautiful by surface standards. If it used eyes, they would see motion and moisture better than distance. It would not make itself a god in the room. A god in the room is the end of every question. Instead, it would become a kind of extra attention: something sitting in the corner, something that watched the water without drowning it, something that listened without making a demand.

It would sit with the builder, if it could bear to.

The builder would be there near a ledge, perhaps scraping a mineral mat into a bowl of shell. The hut behind them would be low, weighted with pebbles, plastered with bone paste. The air would thicken. Droplets would bead on the wall. The builder would not look up at first, because looking up in the Underfield rarely helped. They would listen.

If the power wanted to speak, it would not use words.

Words would be too easy, too human. They would carry too much implication of a mind larger than theirs. Instead, it would answer in the builder’s own tongue.

It would tap its hand against the wall three times: once wet, once dry, once again wet.

The builder would pause.

The power would tap again, but differently: a short click, a longer knock, then a pause. It would mean something like: *I am here. I am not falling. You may answer, or you may not.*
