If the mat dies, the people will not know they are dying. They will know only that the ground has stopped answering. They will feel it first as a thinness underfoot: the cushion that once softened a fall now breaks like dried reed. Then as silence. The insects lose their rhythm because the moss has lost its water. The birds leave because there is no longer shade in which their eggs can hold a brief season of heat. The grazers stop returning to the basins, and the predators move elsewhere, because even hunger on the Anvil learns not to hunt a pattern that has broken. Then the wind takes the dust. Then the salt rings spread. Then the stone, no longer wearing skin, begins to wear itself away faster, as if the mountain had died twice: first when it finished being a mountain, and then when the living mat that had made it gentle withdrew.

A benevolent and wise power, drawn by the quiet enormity of the Anvil, would know this before arriving. It would know that the place was not asking for rescue in the simple way a drowning creature asks for air. It was asking to remain what it had become: a completed thing, a worn thing, a high and patient floor of life. To save it badly would be to unsave it. To give it walls, roads, permanence, easy water, or divine commandments would be to teach the people to stop listening. And on the Anvil, listening is not merely a skill. It is the form in which life exists.

So the power would first do the hardest thing a powerful being can do: it would not appear as power.

It would not rise in light over the plant-towers so that every band would know it had been visited. It would not split a rim with a voice. It would not set a fire that did not need feeding. It would not make any water-basin fill overnight, because that single mercy, repeated, would erase the memory of thirst and turn the elders’ stories into idle superstitions.
