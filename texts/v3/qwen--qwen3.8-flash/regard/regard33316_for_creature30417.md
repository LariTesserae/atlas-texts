They would do what all truly wise kindness does:  
they would resist the urge to rescue.

For a benevolent power that is also wise would first understand that this place is not a failed world. It is not a draft of Eden waiting for water, not a mountain stripped of snow, not a planet without moon or sun, not a body missing limbs. It is its own beginning, and its beginning is becoming. To make it more familiar would be to make it less itself.

A foolish kindness would come with rain.

It would bring clouds from somewhere else and say, *Be green. Be hospitable. Be a world we can live in.* Water would run in the chasms, salt would wash away, glass would cool into ordinary stone, and the quiet patience of the country would be ruined by urgency. Life would arrive, but the land’s own strange life would be buried beneath it.

A harsher kindness would come with light.

It would hang stars in the black vault, push the sun up from the ridge, make night into spectacle, geography into scenery. Then the land would be seen as a thing by outsiders, judged by shadows, made beautiful according to another species’ eyes. The Unmade Ones would remain unseen not because they are secret, but because the new light would define everything by what it could illuminate.

A sentimental kindness would come with faces.

It would make eyes where there are none, mouths for the chasm’s moan, hands for the balanced slate, a heart for the warm emptiness inside the glass dome. It would say, *This must be a person, therefore this must be human enough.* But the being of this land does not require a face. It has no face because the place has not finished the idea of a face. The Unmade Ones are closer to weather, architecture, heat, pressure, and habit than to biology. To give them faces would be to lie to them.

A wise and benevolent power would not impose an ending.

It would not call the absence a lack.

It would not mistake the lack of creatures for loneliness.

It would enter, if entry is the word, without disturbing the balance of the pressure. It would come as a change so subtle that the land might not know it had been visited except by the slight difference in what it could remember afterward.

It would come.

Not from above. Above is only absence. It would come from the idea of coming at all: a new possibility folded into the heat rising through raw stone.

The first thing it would do is give the land a law of memory.

Nothing dramatic. Nothing obvious.

The power would add to the hidden grammar of the country a single condition: *what is tried is not wholly lost.*

When a colonnade of salt columns rises and then flattens, there will remain not merely empty space but a warm trace. When a glass hill curls its rims and cools, its shape will leave a pressure-mark in the mineral fog. When a cairn of obsidian clinks and falls, the sound will not vanish from the world but settle into the stone as a faint readiness to make that sound again.

This would not preserve the land. Preservation would stop it from revising itself, and the country would become a museum. Instead, it would allow revision to accumulate. The Unmade Ones would begin to have something like experience.

Not memory as a living creature remembers. Not memory as names and faces. Memory as heat remembers warmth. Memory as salt remembers geometry. Memory as glass remembers fracture.

Then the land’s endless attempts would no longer be attempts from zero.

The world would begin to learn from itself.

The second thing the wise power would do is make a distinction possible.

This is harder to say, and stranger.

The land has many shapes, but it does not yet have a clear center. It is not that no center could exist. It is that nothing here can say, *This is where I begin, this is where I end, this is mine, that is not mine.* The Unmade Ones are not hidden bodies waiting to be found. They are processes that sometimes resemble bodies. For them to become beings in any meaningful way, they would need the capacity for self and other.

The benevolent power would not build a center for them.

It would give them the conditions under which a center could be chosen.

It would make a place, or a possibility of a place, where repetition is honored. Not a throne. Not a holy city. Not a temple. Something smaller than all that. Perhaps one dome with a circular hole through its crown. Perhaps one body-shaped hollow pressed into warm stone. Perhaps one ring of markers placed in an empty circle. The power would make the choice possible, not the decision final.

A shape may stand there. Another may rise beside it. If the land keeps returning to the place, then the place becomes a heart. Not because a god says so, but because the land says so in the only language it has: pressure, heat, formation, alignment.

Then the Unmade Ones could begin to differentiate.

One warm emptiness would know itself as not the other. A glass dome would learn that its hollow interior is different from the solid stone around it. A fissure would feel the difference between its own edge and the edge that faces it. A column of salt would begin, by habit and trace, to recognize itself as part of a line rather than random mineral weather.

This would not make them into people.

But it would allow them to become a people of their own kind.

A procession would no longer be merely an accidental arcade. It could be a procession. A doorway facing where a chasm once was would no longer be an error. It could be an act of remembrance. A body-shaped hollow could be a body, not because someone lies in it, but because the shape is known, tended, returned to, chosen.

The Unmade Ones would become aware, not with eyes, but with pressure.

They would turn toward one another not by rotating heads, but by rearranging heat.

They would speak not with mouths, but by the way the ground contracts when they are near, the way salt blooms where they pass, the way glass clicks as if acknowledging presence.

This would be the beginning of relationship.

The third thing the wise power would do is ask a question that cannot be answered by human speech.

It would make one small fissure appear that did not fit the land’s usual grammar.

It would not be large. It would not be dangerous. It would not glow brighter than the others. It would simply be there, and it would be different. Its edges would be clean. Its depth would be uncertain. It would not seem to lead anywhere, yet it would not seem meaningless. It would be the kind of opening that makes attention hesitate.

The power would put a question into the fissure.

Not in letters. Not in sound. In the manner of invitation.

The question would be:

*If you could become more than almost being, what would you want?*

Or perhaps:

*Do you wish to be a world, or only to remain in the joy of becoming?*

Or perhaps simply:

*Who, if anyone, are you becoming?*

The Unmade Ones would not understand the question as a language. But the land would feel it as a pressure. The salt crusts would contract around it. Glass dust would gather at its rim. Heat would rise more slowly. Something in the black vault above would not brighten, but the darkness might become slightly more aware of itself.

Then the land would answer by arranging itself.

If it chose to remain uncompleted, it would fill the fissure with pale mineral foam, harden it into stepped rings, and let the process begin again somewhere else. It would say, in its way: *I am enough as almost.*

If it chose to become more coherent, it would not close the fissure. It would build around it. First a circle of markers, then a low wall, then a dome with an opening through the crown, then a colonnade leading toward it from all directions that no longer exist. It would make the fissure a threshold. It would make the threshold a center. It would make the center a place where the Unmade Ones could gather without touching.

If it chose something even stranger, it might ignore the fissure entirely.

That too would be an answer.

A wise power would accept any answer.

It would not punish refusal. It would not say, *Be finished.* It would not say, *Be born.* It would say only: *I have seen you. Choose your own becoming.*

That would be the first true kindness.

The fourth thing the benevolent and wise power would do is protect the land’s right to remain strange.

There would be other powers in the universe. Foolish ones. Hungry ones. Merciful ones without understanding. Beings from lower places who dream of water. Beings from higher places who dream of light. Creatures who see only what can be used. Colonizers who mistake a world for a resource. Pilgrims who mistake a place without faces for a place without souls.

The wise power would make a boundary.

Not a wall. Not a barrier. Something subtler.

It would make the land difficult to misunderstand from a distance.

To those who approached with greed, the country would appear as a heatless illusion, a black void, a barren ruin. To those who approached with fear, it would seem unfinished and therefore not real. To those who approached with arrogance, it would refuse to offer landmarks, doors, names, or answers. To those who approached with true attention, it would become legible as itself.

The wise power would give the Unmade Ones immunity from premature interpretation.

Not from relationship.

Not from love.

Not from visitors who could learn to wait.

But from being forced into someone else’s story.

A traveler might arrive, if travel were possible, and stand on the glass lip of a plain and watch ridges shift. The traveler might feel the heat through their feet, taste chalk and metal and hot clay in the mouth, hear the click of cooling stone in the dark. They might see a body-shaped hollow beside a low dome and weep because it resembles a grave. But the land would not be a grave. The traveler would need to learn that.

A wise power would make that learning possible.

It would slow the traveler’s meaning-making.

It would place a pressure in the air that said: *Look longer. Be less sure.*

It would let the glass dust glitter differently when the traveler assumed. If the traveler tried to name the land as lifeless, a ring of salt would bloom in an unmistakable circle. If the traveler tried to name it dead, a warm obsidian cairn would clink in a wind that had no source. If the traveler tried to name it a machine, the deep chasm would exhale with a dull moan, and the sound would be too much like breath.

The land would be allowed to teach.

The fifth thing the benevolent power would do is make it possible for the Unmade Ones to become gentle.

This is perhaps the most important.

A land that assembles and dissolves without memory can seem indifferent. It can become chaotic, unstable, indifferent to form. Its inhabitants, if they are truly forms of the land, may also be harsh by necessity: shapes made and unmade without consent, pressures that grind, glass that cuts, heat that rises and contracts the body.

But a wise kindness would not only make memory possible.

It would make care possible.

If a form abandoned leaves a heat-shadow, then the next form can honor the abandoned one. If a doorway faces where a chasm once was, that doorway can become not merely a habit but a remembrance of what was there. If two glass hills rise and one cools faster, the other may adjust its angle, not because of physics alone, but because the land has learned that some shapes are easier beside one another.

The Unmade Ones would not become moral in a human sense.

They would not have commandments.

They would not have guilt.

They would not build altars.

But they might learn the mineral equivalent of kindness.
