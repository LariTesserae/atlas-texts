**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do**

They would not enter.

That would be the first and most necessary proof of their wisdom.

The pocket had not evolved to receive a visitor. It had evolved, if such a word could be used for so slow and accidental a thing, to receive light that was not sunlight, breath that was not wind, bodies that were not monsters, and obligations that were not cruelty. It was a small world already full. To step into it with a hand, a voice, a name, or even a benevolent intention would be to introduce a new force too large for its architecture.

So the power would not descend through the soft failure in the vegetation. They would not widen the opening. They would not send a pillar of light down through the soil. They would not announce themselves in the ticking of mites or the opening of the pore-choirs.

They would watch from the larger patience outside the chamber.

And because they were benevolent, something in them would still ache.

---

## I. What They Would First Want to Do

At first, the benevolent part would want to rescue.

It is the natural motion of kindness toward small things:

> Make the hunger stop.  
> Make the young live.  
> Make the light true.  
> Make the hidden place safe.

They would look at the pocket and see how precarious it was: a room-shaped impossibility held within the living tissue of the earth, breathing through cracks, depending on a root-mat above that could shift, close, dry, freeze, be bitten by animals, torn by human feet, or forgotten by rain.

They would see the underleaf at its work.

Perhaps it would be the one with the scarred left shoulder, or the one whose nest had three faint rings from molts, or the one whose mate had gone to another side of the chamber after a ceiling-seam suffocated. It would be tending the afterlight moss, trimming the bright edges, pressing its bristles to a breathing crack to drink, snatching a dew-wing without malice, then returning to its quiet gardening as if maintenance were prayer.

The benevolent power would love that creature immediately, which would be dangerous.

Love makes gods impatient.

They would want to say: *You need not do this alone.*

They would want to open a new crack beneath the nest. They would want to give the underleaf more water, more lichen, fewer thread-stalkers, a longer life, a softer world. They would want to spare the young from the veil. They would want to tell it, somehow: *You are seen. You matter. Do not worry about what will happen when the ceiling cracks.*

But they were wise as well as benevolent, and wisdom taught them something harder.

To save a living system from all loss is to save it from itself.

To make the pocket wholly safe would be to remove the tensions that made it become.

---

## II. What They Would Not Do

They would not put a sun in the chamber.

The pocket’s impossible afternoon was not a flaw to be corrected. It was its grammar. The life inside had risen to meet a borrowed brightness that did not belong to soil or stone, and from that strange condition had come clean shadows, visible surfaces, dew-wings that trembled, lichen that curled like eyelids, glassroots that tasted breath, mites that gathered at the edges of light. If the power installed a real sun, the light would angle, burn, and explain itself. The chamber would become a cave with a lamp, not a place where afternoon had taken root.

They would not make the underleaf speak.

Speech would bring questions the pocket could not answer. The creature’s eyes were made for seeing shadows, not for longing to be known by something outside its world. It already had a kind of knowledge: the angle of the nest, the softness of a seam, the timing of breath, the taste of moisture in the upper air. Language would make it lonely. It would begin to think of the veil as death, the thread-stalker as evil, the underleaf itself as protagonist, and the pocket as story. And stories, in living things, can be a second hunger.

They would not remove predators.

The thread-stalker would seem cruel to a human eye, so delicate and so merciless. The root-veil would seem monstrous: a curtain of light that could become a trap. But their cruelty was not malice. It was participation. The veil fed, the underleaf hunted, the dew-wings escaped, the mites polished the seams, the choirs taught the chamber how to breathe. If the power made predation impossible, the chamber would become a terrarium. Its creatures would still live for a time, but they would no longer be a system. They would be exhibits of themselves.

They would not make the pocket immortal.

There is a temptation in benevolence to preserve the beautiful thing forever. But this place was beautiful because it was unstable. It grew. It contracted. It exhaled. It cracked. Its young were born in folds. Its lichen learned from wounds. Its size changed because its inhabitants changed it. Its life depended on becoming, not on remaining.

To make it eternal would be to kill the very process that made it worth preserving.

They would not reveal the place to the surface world.

This is the hardest refusal.
