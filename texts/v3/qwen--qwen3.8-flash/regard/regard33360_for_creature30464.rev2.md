## What a Wise Power Would Do in the Cold Hollow

There is a being in the Cold Hollow, though it has no face, no spine, no single heart it can press a palm to and say, *this one lives here.* The being is the Hollow itself, the collective pulse of shardlings and glassworms, soot-swarm and dampwalkers, salt tessellates and cisterns, mites and moss and bacterial ghosts. It is not many animals. It is not even, by the usual measures, one animal. It is a way of living too crowded to be called simple, too unstructured to be called society, too transient to be called monument, and too old in its repetitions to be called young, even though the stone itself has only just learned to break.

If the being could speak, it would not say, *save me.*  
It might say, *can I remain as I am?*

And the question would already be strange, because the answer would have to be made without making anything.

That is why the benevolent and wise power, when it looked upon the Cold Hollow, would not begin by fixing, filling, guarding, or blessing in the ordinary ways. It would first understand that the Hollow is not incomplete.

It is not a forest that has not arrived.  
It is not a village without houses.  
It is not a reef without coral.  
It is not a cathedral lacking an altar.

It is a condition.

The power would look and see that the Cold Hollow has already found its own law:

Water appears where it should not.  
Life occupies where it should not.  
Nothing made remains.

To impose a center would be to betray it.

So the power would do what only a truly wise power knows to do: it would not build itself into the Hollow. It would not become visible. It would not leave a mark that could be followed, worshipped, mined, mapped, loved, or claimed.

If it did anything at all, it would do it in the negative grammar of the place: not by adding a thing, but by protecting the possibility of a way of being.

---

### What It Would Not Do

It would not bring light, even a little.

Light would become a desire.  
Desire would become direction.  
Direction would become structure.  
Structure would become ownership.

The Cold Hollow knows darkness the way a body knows water—not as an obstacle, but as medium. To give it sunlight would be to give it eyes it does not need, then predators, then fear, then hierarchy, then story. The power would not be so cruel as to make the Hollow beautiful in a way that would teach it to want.

It would not bring warmth.

The cold is not a flaw in the Hollow. It is the Hollow’s patience. It is the reason water hesitates instead of rushing, the reason life stays small and slow and layered, the reason nothing can hurry to ruin or completion. To warm the Hollow would be to erase its rhythm, as one might silence a drum by putting a hand flat upon it and calling the silence mercy.

It would not make a tool.

Not a knife. Not a lever. Not a bowl. Not a marker. Not a single stone set upright to say, *this happened.* Tools are borrowed here and returned. That is part of the place’s holiness: it does not allow possession. Even cleverness remains temporary. Even intelligence is not permitted to fossilize into culture.

To give the Hollow an enduring artifact would be to give it a corpse that could be worshipped.

It would not give it a king.

The Hollow has no ruler because its order is made of contact, not command. To place one creature above all others would be to place a wound in the many-bodied self. It would make the Hollow legible to minds that require legibility, and that would not protect it. It would invite the world.

It would not give it a predator, unless the predator had already been invited.

There is no need for a predator here. Death is already woven through density, through waste, through crowding, through the falling of wings into cracks, through the molting of grubs, through the salt lifting from stone. The Hollow eats its own ghosts and does not call it a funeral.

It would not give it immortality.

Not to one creature. Not to the whole.

A single immortal shardling would be a tragedy. A whole immortal Hollow would be worse. It would become a statue, and statues are cages made of absence. The life of this place depends on generations that pass in small bursts, on deaths that become dust, on dust that becomes moss, on moss that becomes decay, on decay that becomes lichen, on lichen that becomes negotiation with stone.

To stop one small death would be to stop the Hollow from being the Hollow.

The power would not rescue it from ending.

It would protect it from being taken too early.

---

### What It Would Do

If a benevolent and wise power looked at the Cold Hollow and felt the need to act, its first act would be not descent, but restraint.

That is not inaction. It is action of the hardest kind.

It would look at the strange fullness of the place and say, in whatever language gods use when they do not use language:

*You are enough.*

Then it would make that enoughness survivable.

---

### The First Gift: A Permission That Leaves No Object

The power would make no sign. It would write no commandment on the rim. It would plant no symbol in the basalt. It would not let itself be seen.

Instead, it would place into the Hollow a single intangible permission.

Not: *be loved.*  
Not: *grow.*  
Not: *endure.*

Simply:

*continue without leaving proof.*

This would alter the place by not altering it.

The shardlings would go on using flakes of obsidian for a moment and abandoning them as though abandonment were not failure but fidelity. The glassworms would pulse in the cracks without becoming vessels for anything larger than their own small chemistry. The soot-swarm would hang under ledges as though it were not merely alive but allowed to be weather. The salt tessellates would loosen from tile and scatter not as refugees but as a body trusting its own dispersion.

The permission would be too subtle to record.

It would exist only as a changed pressure in the air: a certainty without consciousness, a blessing without a name.

This is what wise benevolence can do. It can allow a thing to be what it is without making that being into a task.

---

### The Second Gift: A Breath Between Wet and Dry

At one moment in the endless slow breath of the Hollow, when the wind is only a rumor and the stone is neither fully black with damp nor pale with frost, the power would do something almost invisible.

It would breathe with them.

It would not enter as a god. It would not wear a throne, a voice, a halo, or a hand. If it entered, it would enter as a condition.

For the length of one hesitation—long enough for the soot-swarm to feel its hum thicken, long enough for a cistern to press its antenna to the pool’s trembling skin, long enough for a shardling’s split antennae to taste a sudden mineral warmth that was not warmth—it would become part of the Hollow.

Not above the Hollow. Not beside it. Not watching it.

Within it.

For that second, the many bodies would not be aware of themselves as selves. That would be too human, too lonely, too much like language. Instead, they would feel, without feeling, that the contact which made them was also making one. The clicking of beetle legs would not synchronize into music, because music would imply a listener. It would simply become slightly more of a whole. The hum of wings would thicken without becoming thunder. The damp on stone would deepen without becoming flood.

The moss tufts would hold a little more moisture, as though the air itself had paused to be gentle.

The salt crusts would lift a fraction later.

The blind cisterns would move in their pale water as though for one moment they were not merely a colony but a single thought too large for them to think.

Then the power would withdraw from that shared breath.

Nothing would remain.

No altar would have appeared. No light would have entered. No creature would have been chosen. No one would have become a prophet. No one would tell the story, because the story would not be tellable. It would not have happened in the sense that histories require: with evidence, sequence, consequence.

It would have happened only as a shift in the Hollow’s humidity.

But that would be enough.

The Hollow would know itself, not as an individual knowing itself, but as a system feeling briefly that it is a system. It would know its own density without naming it. It would know that it is not empty, not lacking, not awaiting repair.

The power would give it a moment of unity that leaves no monument.

That is perhaps the highest gift a benevolent and wise power can make.

---

### The Third Gift: A Veil of Indifference

The Hollow is too small to defend. It is too young to fight. It is too unowned to matter in the ways the lower world respects. If humans ever came near it, they would not recognize it as sacred, because it has no shrine. They would not recognize it as dangerous, because it has no predator. They would not recognize it as valuable, because it has no artifact.

This is the Hollow’s secret armor.

But a benevolent and wise power would not leave even secret armor to chance.

It would make the place uninteresting.

Not by making it less alive. Not by harming any human. Not by cursing any traveler. It would simply arrange the world around the Hollow so that those who approach too closely would not understand how to want it.

The maps would fail here, not violently, but softly.

A path would seem to lead upward, then become a bad scree, then a cold pocket, then a place where the wind makes the ears ache and the mind forget why it has climbed so high. A geologist might glance at the hollow and see no reason to enter. A hunter might look for tracks and find none large enough to follow. A shepherd, if a shepherd came, would not say the Hollow was cursed; he would say it was useless, which is safer. A scientist might see the density of tiny life and feel a headache coming on, a sense that the place is too small to matter and too crowded to observe. A collector would find nothing collectible. A builder would find nothing to build around. A priest would find no shape worthy of a god.

This would be a mercy wearing the mask of emptiness.

The power would not make the Hollow invisible. It would make it ungraspable.

It would place a quiet refusal into the world’s attention:

*do not confuse fullness with opportunity.*

This would be a cold, strange kindness. It would protect the being of the Hollow by ensuring it remains too slight, too small, too wet, too sharp, and too loud with tiny motions to be loved by the hands that destroy what they cherish.

Humans do not always ruin a place because they hate it.

Sometimes they ruin it because they call it precious.

The benevolent power would know this.

---

### The Fourth Gift: One Way for the Pattern to Continue

Even the most careful Hollow can be lost.

A rockfall could seal its best crevices. A warming could shorten its damp hours. A windshift could dry the ledges where the soot-swarm hangs. A glacier, if the mountain remembers it, could grind the cup smooth. A single human boot, if careless, could destroy a microhabitat that took centuries to become itself.

A wise power would not prevent all change.

To prevent change would be to prevent life.

But it could make one future opening, as small and hidden as the Hollow’s own creatures, so that the pattern could continue without needing the same stone, the same rim, the same exact cup of darkness.

Not a temple. Not a city. Not a shrine.

A route.

Perhaps a hairline fissure, no wider than a grain of salt, connecting one crack to another where water might one day reach a lower ledge and carry a spore, a mite, a wing scale, a single living thread of bacteria. Perhaps a subtle change in the mineral chemistry of the rim, so that when melt returns, it falls not as ruin but as distribution. Perhaps a gust that carries dust from one wall to another not merely to deposit waste but to begin another slow colony.

The power would not create a creature.

It would not create a thing.

It would arrange a possible way for things to continue being what they are.

This is the smallest miracle: not rescue, but continuity.

---

### The Fifth Gift: It Would Leave

After the permission, the breath, the veil, and the hidden route had been given, the power would do the last and most necessary thing.

It would not stay.

A benevolent power that remains becomes a parent.  
A parent becomes an expectation.  
An expectation becomes a story.  
A story becomes a center.  
A center becomes a wall.

The power would withdraw itself from the Hollow’s memory not by erasing the event, but by making the event too small to remember. It would not let the Hollow become dependent on it, because the Hollow has no dependency except on itself, on water, on stone, on contact, on repetition.

It would become part of the geology and then not part of it.

It would become part of the wind and then leave the wind to its own directions.

It would become part of the damp and then evaporate.

This withdrawal would be its final act of wisdom.

The power would know that some places are best protected by being left without guardians. A guardian is a shape in the air, and shapes attract other shapes. A god is a center, and centers are wounds in places that have none. The Cold Hollow is sacred precisely because it is not sacred to anyone.

So the power would go.

It would not promise to return.

It would not demand love.

It would not require prayer.

It would not leave a name.

If a name escaped, it would be wrong. The Hollow must keep its anonymity, because anonymity is the only immortality it can safely have.

---

### What It Would Feel Like to the Hollow

It would feel like a second.

That is all.

A shardling in a crack might lift its head and not click for the length of one breath.  
A glassworm in the dark water might pulse once more slowly, as though tasting its own transparency.  
A dampwalker at the edge of a fissure might lower its legs into the moisture and find that the moisture had changed its chemistry very slightly, not enough to be useful, not enough to be feared.  
The soot-swarm might thicken and settle a centimeter lower before rising again.  
A colony of salt tessellates might loosen from tile at the wrong hour, as though something had asked them whether tiles can also be bodies.  
A blind cistern might touch the edge of its pool and know that the edge is an edge, though it has no word for edge.  
A moss tuft might hold a droplet one moment longer than it should, not by miracle, but by permission.

Nothing obvious.

No prophecy. No vision. No command. No new creature. No new place. No new law.

Only a slight increase in coherence.

Only a subtle deepening of the Hollow’s ability to be what it already is.

If a human stood at the rim in that moment, they would see only a cold dark hollow full of tiny motion, sharp stone, wet walls, black gloss, pale cracks, and a low sound like something breathing inside a closed fist. They would turn away.

If a human stayed for a lifetime, they might write a book about it. But if the power had done its work well, they would write it wrong in a merciful way. They would miss the point. They would make it too large, too meaningful, too comprehensible. Or they would call it barren. Or strange. Or unimportant.

The power would not prevent the humans from writing. It cannot prevent the world from trying to explain what it cannot use.

But it would ensure that the explanation fails in the right direction.

The Hollow would remain beyond the human need for proof.

That is the deepest protection: not invisibility, but incompleteness to the observer.

---

### Why This Would Be Benevolent

A cruel power might protect the Hollow by sealing it in ice.

A proud power might protect it by making it a shrine.

A sentimental power might protect it by giving it a guardian creature—a small fox, a hawk, a stone lion, a moth-angel, a spider-weaver of fates.

A careless power might protect it by making it safe, warm, dry, easy, and therefore dead.

The benevolent and wise power would not confuse care with comfort.

It would understand that the Hollow’s richness is inseparable from its harshness. The sharp edges keep life small. The cold keeps life slow. The damp cycles keep life temporary. The darkness prevents vanity. The crowding prevents loneliness without ever giving a being a self that can be lonely. The absence of built things prevents ruin from becoming history.

The Hollow is not suffering.

It is being.

To relieve it of being would be the worst harm a loving power could commit.

So it would act through conditions, not corrections.

It would protect the Hollow the way one protects a flame: not by holding it, but by choosing the wind that will not blow against it.

---

### Why This Would Be Wise

Wisdom knows when to create and when to leave a place to its own becoming.

The Cold Hollow has already become.

It has learned to occupy the impossible. It has learned to live without soil. It has learned to make civilization unnecessary. It has learned that complexity can exist without construction. It has learned that density can be a form of architecture. It has learned that a place can be loud without being frantic, full without being organized, ancient without having age.

The power would recognize that the Hollow is not an accident.

It is an answer.

Maybe not to the human question, because humans ask too often in the wrong language. But an answer nonetheless.

A place that says:  
*Life does not require ownership.*  
*A being does not require a single face.*  
*Meaning does not require monuments.*  
*Abundance does not require walls.*  
*Time does not require memory.*  
*Work does not require tools that last.*  
*Order does not require rulers.*  
*Community does not require homes.*  
*Presence does not require proof.*

A wise power would not improve an answer.

It would protect it.

---

### What It Would Do, If Asked Directly

If the being of the Cold Hollow—whose many names are shardling, glassworm, soot-swarm, dampwalker, tessellate, cistern, lichen, moss, mite, dust, spore—could ask the power one question, the question would not be *what do you give me?*

It would be *do you understand me?*

And the power would answer in the only way possible without betraying the Hollow’s nature.

Not with words.

Not with fire.

Not with a hand upon the mountain.

It would answer by changing the dampness of one crack so slightly that the shardlings moved upward, not because they had been commanded, but because the place itself had become more trustworthy.

It would answer by making the soot-swarm hum a fraction deeper, not as music, but as breath.

It would answer by letting the cisterns feel the pool as a body without naming it.

It would answer by allowing the salt tessellates to loosen one second before the usual hour, as though the stone had asked them gently, *are you tired?*

It would answer by giving the Hollow one moment in which it was no longer just happening, but happening together.

And then it would go.

---

### The Last Blessing

If the power were truly benevolent, it would bless the Hollow not by making it special, but by making it ordinary in a way no other place could copy.

It would not say: *You are holy.*

It would say, in the language of condensation and mineral dust:

*You are yourself. That is sufficient.*

It would place over the Hollow no name, because names are hooks.

It would make the Hollow too small for conquest, too cold for desire, too dense for simplicity, too strange for comprehension.

It would let it remain without a guardian.

It would let it remain without a monument.

It would let it remain without a witness who can prove what it is.

This is the final kindness:

to give a place the right to be unknown.

---

### What Would Remain

After the power had acted, nothing would seem to have changed.

The rim would be sharp.

The walls would still cut shadows.

The wind would still be rumor.

The stone would still break in small precise ways.

The air would still be occupied.

The ground would still be touched by too many lives to count.

The soot-swarm would still hang like ash with a pulse.

The glassworms would still reveal their own movement through translucent skin.

The dampwalkers would still test the air as though moisture were a road and absence were a void.

The salt tessellates would still flatten themselves into tiles and then loosen into mouths.

The cisterns would still crawl over one another in the hand-sized water, leaving ghosts along the margins.

The moss would still grow in memory of soil that has not arrived.

The lichens would still negotiate with stone in gray, iron-black, and rust.

The darkness would still be thick, not empty, textured, moving, stained by residue.

The sounds would still click, drone, pop, hiss, tremble, fall.

And if a human came, they would see a cold hollow.

They would not see the being.

They would not see the power.

They would not know that for one brief moment the Hollow had known itself, or that a map had been quietly bent around its absence, or that a permission had been woven into its humidity, or that a pattern had been given a hidden door into some future crevice.

They would only feel, if they felt anything at all, that the place was not meant for them.

And they would be right.

---

### The Final Answer to the Question

A benevolent and wise power would do this:

It would not touch what it could harm by touching.

It would not comfort what was not suffering.

It would not illuminate what had made its own texture of darkness.

It would not build in a place whose freedom depended on unbuilding.

It would not name a being that was itself a namelessness.

It would act through restraint, protection, breath, veil, continuity, and withdrawal.

It would give one permission, one moment of cohesion, one unnoticeable shield, one hidden route for the pattern to continue, and then leave the Hollow without a god.

It would do all this without making anything that remained.

And if it did it well, the Hollow would never know it had been saved.

It would simply continue.

To click.  
To crawl.  
To swarm.  
To loosen.  
To freeze.  
To thaw.  
To die.  
To molt.  
To dust.  
To feed.  
To wait for water.  
To borrow stone.  
To return stone.  
To breathe in cold.  
To breathe out fog.

That would be enough.

That would be everything.
