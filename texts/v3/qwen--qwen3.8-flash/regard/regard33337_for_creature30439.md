## The Power of Intervals

If the Nine Cisterns and one of the beings who lived in it had drawn the attention of a power both benevolent and wise, that power would not have come as a catastrophe.

Catastrophe is what careless gods do, or careless people pretending to be gods. A truly benevolent and wise power would come in the way water comes to a town that has learned to listen: by a change of pressure, a slight early sound, a small adjustment of timing. It would not arrive with fire, with a voice from the cloud, with a commandment written in gold. It would arrive as the kind of intervention that a later engineer might call “lucky maintenance,” that a child might call “the pipes were awake,” and that an old pump-tender might not describe at all, except by tapping his palm against the flagstones and saying, “Something moved.”

The being whose life it had noticed would not be the most powerful person in the basin.

It would not be the mayor, if the town had one. It would not be the tram conductor, although he had authority over movement, nor the schoolteacher, although she had authority over knowledge, nor the foreman of the engine houses, though he had authority over water and fire. It would be someone smaller in rank and more attentive in habit: a pressure-listener.

Perhaps it was a child, with a tin cup and a stub of chalk. Perhaps it was a young woman who checked the grates twice before reporting them clear. Perhaps it was an older man whose job title was maintenance and whose secret job was patience. The place would call this being by function, not by name. They would say, “That is the Listener,” because the Listener heard the town before the town told it to itself. They would know when a pump was about to engage by the change in the air. They would know when a cistern would overflow by the smell near the lower court. They would know which stones made a hollow sound because water was moving under them, and which stones made a tight sound because the basin had been asked to do too much.

The Listener was not magical. That was why the wise power cared.

Magic that comes from above can make people useless. Benevolence that makes people dependent is only kindness wearing a disguise. The power saw, in the Listener, something better than magic: attention earned through exposure to the real. The Listener had learned, in a young town, to notice what others had not yet had time to learn. This was dangerous, because if too many people notice too much, they begin to feel responsible for everything. If they begin to feel responsible for everything, they can become exhausted, or frightened, or bitter, or proud. The wise power saw that the Listener needed not a throne, but a place to stand without being crushed by the town’s weight.

So the power would do, if anything, these things.
