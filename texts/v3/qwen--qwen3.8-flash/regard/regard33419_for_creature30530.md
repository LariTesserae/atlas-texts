A benevolent and wise power would do the first thing wisely: it would not come.

It would not stand in the crack with a lantern. It would not descend through the wet cleft with a body or a name. It would not make the fog thin so the moor could admire itself, nor would it widen the mouth of the chamber so that the hidden place could finally be seen. Some rhythms are possible only because they are not performed. Some beings live because they are not looked at directly. A power that is truly wise would know that the first harm often begins with a bright interest, a clean path, a hand reaching into darkness with the intention to be kind.

So the Power would not come.

But not to say that it would do nothing is not to say that it would not act. A benevolent and wise power would understand the chamber already well enough to leave its surface alone. It would not add a mouth where there is already a throat. It would not introduce a voice where there is already the long argument of water against stone. It would not improve the place into something human minds could possess.

If it had a heart, and if that heart were large enough to avoid the tyranny of small kindnesses, it would first keep the place unobvious.

It would send no angels to the headland, because angels could become proof, and proof could become pilgrimage, and pilgrimage could become road, road could become wall, and wall would be too much. Instead it would make the ordinary world do what the ordinary world does best: confuse attention. It would let the gorse grow in such a way that a cleared path would always seem like more effort than it was worth. It would keep the heather stubborn, the stones wet, the tide unpredictable, the air cold enough to make people think of going home. It would arrange the fog sideways, not because fog is dramatic, but because fog is a mercy to the unseen.

It would make the pale wound in the moor look like only a flaw in soil. It would make the shallow shiver in the air seem like heat rising, though heat is rare there, or the memory of heat, or nothing at all. It would give the crack just enough danger to discourage the curious, but never so much that a careful animal, a patient plant, or a frightened child might mistake it for a trap. It would keep the entrance narrow. Narrowness is a kind of protection. Too much opening makes a secret into an invitation, and an invitation is sometimes the beginning of ruin.

Then, after the concealment, it would tend the throat.

Not by stopping the sea. The sea must come and go, or the chamber would become something it cannot be. A benevolent power would not save the place by making it quiet. Quietness there is already too much violence, too much abandonment. It would not plug the submerged passage with concrete or stone or any modern answer to an ancient need. It would not make the breathing gentle so that people could sleep there. It would not make the roar tolerable so that someone could name it beautiful and take photographs.

Instead it would do what all careful hands do in places of constant strain: it would relieve the burden by inches.

It might move a sandbar far out under the water where no human eye would notice. It might shift a current by a small angle so that winter storms did not all arrive at the same weak point. It might let one old crack widen a little deeper than the first crack, so the chamber would have another shoulder to lean against. It might cause a thin mineral seam to carry a little more salt, or a little less, so the stone could breathe without tearing itself apart. It might allow a lower cavity to fill and drain in a way that lessens the shock when the tide lifts, not to silence the voice, but to keep the voice from becoming only an act of breaking.

It would not prevent collapse. It would prevent sudden shamelessness.

If the sea came too high, it would not build a wall around the chamber. It would make another way for the water to speak. It might lower a shelf in the submerged throat. It might let a hidden cleft become a second path for air. It would allow the chamber’s voice to change, because benevolence that cannot suffer change is only another kind of fear. If the climate shifted, if the salt crept farther inland, if the rain fell in new patterns and the frost found new weaknesses, the Power would help the place adapt without forcing it to become a museum of what it once was. It would let the moss migrate upward. It would let the ferns decide which faults still held enough moisture. It would let some pools become shallower, some deeper, some temporary. It would not insist that the chamber remain the same chamber forever. The chamber would know what the chamber could know, in the way stone knows: by the pressure of the water and the patience of the shape.

The fringe of life would matter, but not because the Power wanted beauty for its own sake.

It would care for the moss cushions, but it would not make them into a green carpet of comfort. It would send, perhaps, one small seep of freshwater along a particular fault line during a dry year, enough for a few leaves to remain curled rather than dead. It would let a fallen blade of grass rot in a crevice so that a few woodlice had shelter. It would permit a spider to build where the wind did not sweep too often, not because the wind was wrong, but because the web had a right to one quiet geometry. It would allow the blind things in the pools to remain blind, because blindness is not a punishment when there is nothing to see. To give them eyes would be to make them into a miracle, and miracles, when they are visible, attract the wrong kind of love.

It would tend the green at the edge of the mouth without making the edge into a garden.

A benevolent and wise power would know the difference between care and ornament. It would not plant an orchard in the crack, nor bring a stream, nor make the moss glow. It would not add birdsong to drown the roar. It would not soften the chamber into a sanctuary. It would keep the lushness punctual, isolated, hard-won, small. It would protect the ferns only as much as the place could protect ferns, which means it would let them struggle. But it would let them struggle without letting them end. Not every ending is kindness. Not every persistence is cruelty. Sometimes the mildest winter is the one that allows a single frond to open and close one more time.

Then it would meet human visitors, if they came.

It would not meet them like a guardian with a sword. It would meet them like a room meeting a body: by pressure, by temperature, by sound, by the sudden awareness that one is not alone in the way one expected to be alone. Some should come. Some should not. The wise power would know the difference only partially, because some mistakes are necessary for places to remain places and not become altars.

It would make the entrance hard. If someone came out of greed, out of curiosity that wanted to own, out of a need to prove that the world contained wonders, the chamber would not receive them. It might be the rain that washed a foothold clean and made them turn back. It might be the fog that swallowed the headland and gave them a strange, unreasoning certainty that they had wandered too far. It might be a gull calling so sharply that they thought they had heard their own name, then realized they had not, and felt embarrassed without knowing why.

If someone came with a measuring tape, or a rope, or a machine, the Power would not curse them. Curses make places into monsters, and this place is not a monster. Instead, the Power would let them hear too clearly. The roar, which is only the sea speaking through stone, would seem to press into their skulls until they could not distinguish it from their own thoughts. The damp would get into their papers, their cameras, their plans. The crack would feel too narrow. The tide would feel too near. They might look down at the spiral on the wall and mistake it for a warning, though it is not a warning. They might look at the black pools and see nothing, and the seeing of nothing would be too much. They would leave, and when they were far enough away they would not remember exactly why they had left. That is one way the world keeps its secrets.

If someone came in grief, the Power might allow them to be heard without being explained.

Not every grief needs comfort. Some grief needs only scale. The chamber would not promise that the person lost had gone somewhere else, because the chamber does not speak in that way. It would simply breathe. The person would feel the pressure in the chest, the cold on the skin, the strange slow movement of the air. They might sit near the mouth of the crack, too far down for the deepest dark, where the roar is not total but partial, where one can still hear the wind above and the sea below at the same time. If they cried, the chamber would not care. If they listened, the chamber would teach them. The lesson would not be words. The lesson would be that a thing can be full of roaring and still not be destroyed. That a mouth can open and close without wanting anything. That pressure is not always pain; sometimes it is the condition for air.

They might leave with a small dryness in the lungs and a strange steadiness in the hands. They might not understand what had happened, but something would have been taken from them. Not their sorrow. That would be theft. Something smaller: the certainty that the world owed them explanation.

If someone came in danger, the Power would not necessarily save them. A benevolent power is not the same thing as a rescuer of bodies. It would understand that some deaths are not failures of love, and that a hidden chamber cannot be made into a hospital without ceasing to be a hidden chamber. But if a walker fell, and if the darkness took them from their own direction and placed them near a shelf where a little rain could reach and a little air could move, the Power might allow it. If a child was frightened, the Power might let the roar change at just the hour when the tide was easing, so that it sounded less like tearing and more like something vast turning over in sleep. If a person stood at the threshold and felt that the chamber knew them, the Power might not correct them, because the correction would make the place smaller.

It would not punish the living.

It would punish nothing, because punishment is a very human way of making fear look meaningful. It would only let the place be fully itself. And itself is punishment enough for those who would force themselves upon it.

It would also tend the old human traces.

The flint blade in the pool would remain. The Power would not retrieve it for a museum, nor would it sharpen its edge. It would let the water continue the slow erasure that had already been chosen for it. The bone awl would stay in its crack, perhaps covered by sediment, perhaps found again in some later season, perhaps never found again. The fire-ring would not be made into a monument. The spiral would not become a symbol anyone could carry without understanding that symbols are only the shadows of what happened before symbols.

This is one of the hardest things a wise power knows: to leave remains in place, not as messages to be decoded, but as the chamber’s way of carrying the human mind without becoming human.

The Power would let the spiral fade. If one rain came too hard and took part of it away, it would not restore the line as it had been. If mineral staining tried to claim it, the Power would not make the stain disappear. It would allow the ambiguity to deepen. It would let future beings see the curve and not know whether it was made or not made, whether meaning was there or whether meaning was in their hunger for meaning. This would be a kindness to them. Not all mysteries should be solved. Some should only be survived.

If new marks were made, the Power would not encourage them. It might allow one cup mark to fill with rain for thirty seconds and catch the sky it could not see. It might let a pebble, moved by ice, strike a ledge and leave a shallow scar that looked as if it had been made by a hand. But it would not write a name. It would not leave a clue. It would not speak.

It would keep the human trace embedded, as moss is embedded in stone.

There may be a question: would it ever speak to the being that lives there?
