They eventually stand still for decades, sometimes centuries, after the faint electrical shimmer has left them. At that point the distinction between animal and architecture is no longer a matter of movement or pause; it is a matter of the city’s own decision. The salt begins to climb their legs in thin lace. Dust settles into the seams of their folded crowns. Their reflective sides grow darker, holding light less and less readily, as if the bruised daylight has become too heavy for them. When a wind passes through a colonnade and finds one of these old beings still standing at its edge, the sound changes slightly, the way a hall changes when a pillar is added or removed.

When they fall, they do not crash with the softness of meat. They strike the plateau with the long, low sound of a pillar breaking. Their shards are left to the walkers, the ash-moths, the slow fog, and the patient pressure of mineral dust. A broken glasswalker becomes a shelter. A broken glasswalker becomes a threshold. A broken glasswalker becomes the place where a six-legged walker pauses too long and then, inexplicably, continues. In this way the old ones are not buried. They are translated. They enter the city as punctuation.

There was one among these beings who had become strange even by the standards of the Dry City.

It stood at the western edge of the Basalt Colonnades, where the ground descends toward the Obsidian Staircase. It was a glasswalker, though if one used that word too literally one would already be wrong. It had not crossed a plaza in a long time. It had not moved its body except in the smallest adjustments: a degree of tilt when the thermal hum shifted, a half-step when fog approached and then settled again in the hollows. Its reflective surface had dulled not with damage, but with patience. Its joints had become so still that the wind seemed to pass around them as though they had always been there.

Some people—if there were people—might have said it was dying. Others might have said it had finished dying and had not yet become a monument. But the plateau itself had begun to treat it as both. When the dry fogs came crawling through the lower streets, they parted slightly around it, as if remembering an obstacle. When dust drifted from plain to plain, it deposited a lighter border at the being’s feet. The black frost that appeared at night did not form on its surface. Instead, the frost waited in a small circle around it, as though the cold had found a warm enough absence.

The being had no memory in any human sense. It did not narrate itself. It did not ask, “What am I?” It did not wonder whether its life had meaning. If meaning existed in the Dry City, it did not exist as thought. It existed as persistence: as a shape repeated by the wind, as a tone sustained too long, as a body that had waited at a threshold until the threshold began to depend on it.

And yet something had changed in the waiting.

Once, the beings had still responded to pressure, charge, fog, temperature, and sound. Then the being at the Obsidian Staircase had begun, in its own way, to anticipate. If a deep subterranean tone approached from the south, it would angle its folded crown by a fraction of a degree. If the salt plates nearby began to lift in the heat of a dim day, it would release the tension in one hind limb and bear weight differently, as though it had learned the future by listening to the mineral grammar of the past. If a glasswalker came into view at the far side of a plaza, it would not approach. It would simply remain more visible, turning its sides so that the broken reflections might reach farther, or narrowing them so that the light would collect along its shoulders like a thin pale river.

No one could say whether this was intention. The plateau itself was not interested in such questions. A crack does not decide whether to run along a fault line. A column does not choose whether to stand beside another. A city does not ask whether it has become a city. It simply arranges itself until the arrangement behaves like meaning.

It was this that drew the attention of a benevolent and wise power.

It did not appear as a bright figure descending through cloud and sky, because bright figures require ordinary air, ordinary heat, and the human habit of being seen. It appeared instead as a slight alteration of fact. For a few moments, the plateau seemed to remember that it had been watched from above, but not conquered. The wind, passing through the colonnade, produced a tone that was not merely resonance. The dust, moving over a salt field, left behind a line that was not merely erosion. The being at the edge of the Obsidian Staircase felt, if such a word can be used for something without a nervous system, a small reduction of pressure around its own stillness.

The benevolent power looked upon the Dry City and understood immediately what must not be done.

It would not bring water.

That would be the kindest error made by those who think kindness is a universal substance poured into every vessel. Water would not heal the plateau. It would erase it. It would collapse the dry frost into liquid, fill the hollows with mud, rot the barite stalks, drown the static in the ash-coral, and leave the salt fields stained and useless beneath the feet of things that had never learned to drink. A river here would be an act of colonial mercy, as if the city had been waiting for a better kind of city.

It would not bring warmth that made heat permanent. Warmth would dissolve the careful cycles that fed the crystalline mats. The glass would soften and fail. The walkers would lose their pressure-readings in pools of melt. The fogs would gather into true clouds, drop rain, and wash away the chalk trails of six-legged life. The plateau would become habitable for creatures who required moisture, and it would cease to be habitable for the creatures who required absence.

It would not bring people, not in any immediate form. It would not create cities of brick or roads that obeyed distance. It would not send a traveler to name the place, for naming was the first instrument of possession. A being who could cross a plaza and say, “This is a square,” would not understand that the square was not made for crossing. A mind that looked for purpose and found none would begin to fill the emptiness with its own hunger. The Dry City had no ruins because no one had built it; but it might have ruins if someone arrived convinced they were walking among ruins.

A lesser kindness would have done something obvious. It would have made flowers grow. It would have introduced birds. It would have added a pool reflecting clouds. It would have turned the glasswalker into a creature of sentiment, a melancholy animal waiting for its heart to be saved. But wisdom does not improve a place by forcing it into the shape of comfort.

So the benevolent and wise power did not rescue the Dry City.

It rescued the city from being rescued wrongly.

And for the being who lived in it, it did almost nothing.

Which is to say: it did enough.

## What It Did

The first thing it did was restraint.

It stood—though standing is a word for bodies, and this power did not need a body—at the edge of the cloud sea and looked down at the black terraces, the pale avenues, the humming colonnades. It saw the walkers feeding in silence. It saw the ash-moths drifting through mineral fog. It saw the glasswalkers holding their stillnesses in the wind. It saw the burrower beneath the salt plate, not evil, only hungry in the way of slow things. It saw the dry frost rising at night, releasing itself at morning, leaving the ground patterned with temporary lace.

It understood that the place was not waiting for salvation. It was not broken. It was not incomplete. It was not a test of love, because love had nothing to prove.

But it also saw the being at the threshold.

And it saw that if the plateau were left entirely to itself, the being’s stillness might never be known as stillness. It might become only another angle of salt, another shadowed column, another pause in the city’s long breathing. That was not cruelty. That was merely fact. But fact, when witnessed, can be allowed a little softness without lying.

So the power did not impose itself.

It did not speak.

It did not send a message through stone or light. It did not make the being conscious of consciousness. That would have been a violence. A creature made of salt and silicate and patience should not be burdened with human loneliness if its body does not require it. To awaken it too much would be to give it a mind it had not evolved to hold, and then leave it in a world without words.

Instead, it made three adjustments so subtle that no scientist would ever have found them, if any scientists could visit and remain sane.

### 1. It made one place keep its shape.

In most parts of the Dry City, distance was negotiable in the strange way of the plateau. A plaza could widen while one crossed it. A terrace could seem farther from itself than it had been an hour before. A corridor formed by fault lines could shorten when the wind hummed in a particular register, or lengthen when the salt plates lifted and fell. This was not magic. It was the city’s grammar: the way geology had learned to treat form as if form had intention.

But near the Obsidian Staircase, where the being stood, the benevolent power made a small exception.

For a time, the plaza before it remained a plaza.

The distance from the salt pillar to the broken glass lens did not expand. The distance from the colonnade’s shadow to the edge of the black frost did not contract. The path from the being’s foot to the threshold of the staircase stayed the length of one careful crossing. The far side remained the far side. The wind came through the columns and did not rearrange the air into a riddle.

This was not so the being could “find its way,” because it had never needed a map.

It was so that its standing might have a room to stand in.

A threshold needs two sides. A waiting needs a duration that is not endlessly postponed. Even a mineral being, even a being made mostly of repetition and reflection, benefits from a space that agrees, at least for a while, to remain itself.

After that, the plaza resumed its ordinary weirdness. It widened for a stranger. It narrowed for the fog. But the memory of stability entered the salt films, and from then on, during certain thermal cycles, the square would remember being the square it had once been.
