## What the Wise Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power would not come through the mouth of the pocket.

That would have been the wrong way in. A mouth is a place of swallowing, of breath, of warmth, of becoming. If it entered through the mouth, it would have entered like a person, or a beast, or a spirit wearing the habit of a body, and every one of those things would have been too large, too moist, too temporary. It would have filled the low aisle with the smell of lungs. It would have disturbed dust that had settled by a slower justice than human patience. It would have made the light scatter in a way that looked like interruption.

So it would not enter through the mouth.

It would enter through the seams.

It would come as a slight pressure change, as a fraction in the hum, as a new attention folded into the geometry of the plates. If it had a body, it would use none of its heat. If it had a voice, it would keep it outside the air of the pocket. If it had a name, it would leave it at the threshold, because names are for beings that move, and these beings do not move; they thicken. They settle. They agree with one another so slowly that the agreement appears to be stone.

The wise power would look, not as a person looks, but as a pressure looks across a room. It would notice the dry shelves, the ceramic plates, the threads of moss, the salt-white fringes, the dust that sleeps like a memory. It would notice that the place was not waiting to be completed. It had already completed itself. It would notice that the inhabitants were not hidden somewhere among the shelves like children or ghosts. They were the shelves themselves, or rather the living relationship between shelf, seam, dust, light, air, and sound.

It would ask, without asking:

*Who are you?*

No one would answer.

No one needed to.

The hum would answer.

And if the wise power listened long enough—and that was what wisdom was: the ability to listen long enough—the hum would say, in its own dry, roaring way:

*We are what remains. We are what fits. We are what arrived at its shape and did not mistake the shape for a prison. We are not lonely. We are not trapped. We are not awaiting rescue. We are not asking for water, animals, warmth, speech, or meaning. We are asking for nothing. We are continuing.*

The benevolent and wise power would hear this and feel no pity, because pity would have misunderstood the place.

It would not think, *How sad, there are no creatures here.*

It would not think, *How barren, there is no growth.*

It would not think, *How strange, there is no death.*

It would understand that these thoughts would be thoughts about another world imposed upon this one. To bring water would be to bring dissolution. To bring warmth would be to bring haste. To bring animals would be to bring appetite. To bring insects would be to bring noise of a different kind: a noise that consumes, repairs, wastes, multiplies. To bring birds would be to bring the sky into a place that had translated the sky into light and kept only what it could bear.

It would not bring a gardener.

It would not bring a god who said *Live*.

The being here did not need to be commanded to live.

It had already lived so completely that command had become unnecessary.

---

If anything, the power would do almost nothing.

But “almost nothing” would be the hardest thing.

It would practice restraint.

This would be its first act.

The wise power would stand, in the way of standing that belonged to it, at the edge of the hum. It would feel the pocket’s total occupation of itself. Every plate said, *Here. Here. Here.* Every thread of lichen said, *This is enough.* Every dust basin said, *No more room. No more hunger. No more becoming.*

The power would realize that to add anything visible would be to insult the finished chord.

A single added drop could carry centuries.

A single added breath could carry mold, decay, warmth, confusion.

A single added desire—*I would like to help you*—could become violence.

So it would keep its hands off the plates. It would not touch the thinnest translucent edge, the one that looked like dried sea-foam pressed into paper. It would not gather the dust, not because dust was sacred, but because dust was already a citizen, already a spore-bearing silence, already the blood of a body that did not move. It would not make the moss greener, because the moss’s paleness was not weakness. It was the moss’s exact speech.

It would not brighten the light, because the light had already learned how to be enough without heat.

It would not quiet the hum, because the hum was not an interruption of silence. The hum was the place breathing with its own architecture.

The wise power would bow, if bowing could be performed without a body.

That bow would be the act of accepting that the place was not incomplete.

---

But benevolence is not merely restraint.

Restraint is wisdom when action would harm. Benevolence is action when harm would arrive from outside and can be diverted without changing what is loved.

So the wise power would not alter the pocket into something better.

It would alter the world’s approach to the pocket.

It would become a guardian of unnoticed things.

This is how it would work: it would make dangers overlook the hidden mouth.

If a future river were to consider a new path and the thought would carry water into the hill, the power would not stop the river. Stopping rivers is not always wise; sometimes it creates worse disasters elsewhere. Instead, it would turn the river’s attention by a single grain of slope. A pebble would shift. A root would drink elsewhere. A frost crack would widen in a direction that let the water find another stone to carry. The river would think it had chosen freely, because water always thinks it has chosen freely. The pocket would remain dry.

If a tree were to send a root toward the shelf of stone, seeking the cool air beyond, the power would not cut the root. Cutting is too human, too sudden, too much like an axe learning to think. It would make the mineral chemistry of the surrounding stone faintly hostile. The root would find no softness, no moisture, no welcome. It would pass on, disappointed, believing it had missed something better.

If a fox were to look for a den in the hillside, the power would make the entrance seem too low, too hard, too dry, too full of stone teeth. The fox would sniff and find no smell worth its curiosity. It would move on. Not because the pocket was dangerous, but because the power had made it invisible in the way that the most beloved things must sometimes become: uninteresting to those who would break them by wanting them.

If a child were to come near the mouth, wide-eyed and carrying a stone for a seat, the power would not frighten the child. Fear is an ugly gift. It would make the child’s attention slide elsewhere. A beetle would pass. A cloud would form into a better game. The hidden opening would look like a collapsed fold, a place already searched, a place already known to be empty. The child would leave, unharmed and unaware that the pocket had asked for its kindness.

If miners ever came with iron and questions, the power would not make a temple guard appear in the dark. Temples are for people who need boundaries, and this place was a boundary before it was a room. It would make the stone seem worthless. It would let the plates look too brittle, too dusty, too dry, too full of strange angles that would collapse at the first hammer. It would send a cold thread through the air that smelled of nothing valuable. It would make the men’s hands ache in the low aisle and their hearts say, *There is nothing here. There is only dust and stone.* They would go out laughing at the emptiness, not knowing that emptiness was the form of life’s protection.

This would be the power’s first real kindness:

It would make the pocket unattractive to the hands that destroy, and unnoticed by the feet that wander.

It would give it the mercy of being overlooked.

---

If a person somehow entered anyway—

If the world were crowded enough with curiosity, if the dry bright pocket were found by accident, if some bent body brushed through the mouth and the seam-people felt a warm disturbance moving among the plates—the benevolent and wise power would not strike the visitor. It would not make them blind, nor mad, nor sick. Those are punishments, and punishments belong to powers less wise than benevolence.

It would do something gentler and stranger.

It would make the visitor become a temporary chord.

The wet warmth of their skin would touch the cool air, and the mosses might swell by a microscopic fraction. Their breath would carry the scent of lungs and leaves, and the salt fringes might accept a single crystal’s worth of moisture, then return it slowly to mineral memory. Their heart would beat too fast, and the large plates would sound a lower note around it.

The power would give them the feeling of not being understood by the place, but also not being hated by it. It would give them the sense of being too temporary for permanence. It would give them the feeling that their hands, if placed on the plates, were wrong—because the plates were not furniture, not walls, not objects to lean on, but citizens in their own slow republic.

It would make the visitor cough, not with illness, but with dryness.

It would make their eyes sting, not with poison, but with brightness.

It would make their bent shoulders complain, not with danger, but with the message: *You are shaped for another world. You do not fit here. Leave.*

Not because the pocket was hostile.

Because the pocket was complete.

And the power would ensure that if they left any trace behind, that trace would be taken slowly into the hum. A single fallen hair might lodge in a seam and become part of a lichen’s future mineral negotiation. A single drop of sweat might crystallize at the edge of a dust basin and become a small white star. The place would not reject them. It would translate them.

Then, when they were gone, the power would help them forget the exact location.

Not all of it. Some memory is useful: a dream of bright stone, a dryness that felt like wisdom, a hum that followed them home and made them quieter for the rest of their life.

But not enough memory to bring others with hammers.

Not enough memory to bring children with collections.

Not enough memory to bring lovers with carvings.

The power would leave them with reverence instead of ownership.

That would be a high grace: to know one has seen something sacred and still cannot point to where it is.

---

The wise power would also protect the pocket from itself.

Even completed things can be threatened by completion.

The plate that is too thin may crack.

The shelf that is too close may become a trap for stress.

The pore that breathes for centuries may silt and fail.

The hum may develop an unstable frequency.

The power would not interfere with every tremor. That would make the place a pet under glass. It would instead remove only the worst accidents: the earthquake that would have shattered the thinnest translucent panel; the heat wave that would have made the air too volatile for the dust-life; the long damp season that would have invited molds the pocket had transcended.

It would work at the threshold of probability.

It would let the pocket live as if by nature.

If a grain of dust threatened to seal a pore that moved the breath of the place, the power would cause the air current to shift a little, or a vibration to favor one direction, or a faint crystal bloom to form at a better angle. The grain would move. The pore would remain clear. The hum would continue.

No one would see the favor.

No one would hear the correction.

That is why the correction was wise: it refused to announce itself.

If a single plate had grown too sharp at its edge, the power would let time, dust, and pressure do the work, but it would guide the pressure a little. Over decades, the edge would soften. Over centuries, it would curve. The place would think it had polished itself, and it would be right.

---

At last, if the benevolent and wise power had one gift to give that was not protection and not concealment, it would give the smallest possible visible act.

It would do one thing so small that no witness would notice it except perhaps the place itself.

It would move a single dust grain.

Or perhaps it would settle a lichen spore into a crack that had been empty for a thousand years.

Or it would cause one salt fringe to crystallize one hair’s breadth farther than it otherwise would.

Or it would let a faint white star appear on a shelf in a new place, as if the chamber had decided to add one more note to its own hymn.

But it would do it in such a way that the event seemed inevitable.

The thread would not look added. It would look as if it had always been on its way there, traveling through air and time until finally arriving at its proper seam.

This would be the power’s single kindness, the only one it would perform inside the pocket’s own domain.

It would say, without words:

*I do not make you become something else. I only help your next continuation become what it may naturally be.*

The moss would not rise.

The lichen would not spread greedily.

The plates would not learn to speak in a human tongue.

The place would remain dry, bright, cool, loud.

But the hum would gain a new harmonic, so faint that only the thinnest crystal might notice it, and only the oldest dust know its meaning.

If a human had been there, bent in the aisle with the light scattering from the plates, they might have felt that the air had changed somehow. Not warmer. Not wetter. Not quieter. Not louder. But more certain.

They might have thought:

*This place has been seen.*

But not by a person.

Seen by a quality of attention that could protect without possessing, bless without naming, save without making the saved into something different.

---

The benevolent and wise power would not make a god of the place.

It would not build a shrine around it.

It would not send angels to stand at the mouth.

It would not write in the sky: *This is the Dry Choir; this is the Seam-Colony; this is the Resonant Crust.*

Names would make it an object again.

Names would make it available to worship, which is another way of eating the world.

Instead, the power would give it a word that would never appear in the air.

The word would be:

*Enough.*

But it would be more precise than human language allows.

It would be the word that means: *You do not require our love in order to be worthy.*

It would mean: *Your completion is not a failure of possibility.*

It would mean: *I saw you, and I did not try to improve you.*

It would mean: *I will help you remain.*

---

When the wise power left, it would not leave as a person leaves, with footprints, regret, or farewell.

It would leave the way attention leaves a room.

The pocket would become, once again, fully itself.

The hum would continue.

The light would scatter.

The plates would remember the moment of the power’s presence as a pressure that had passed through them, and perhaps—over a thousand years—turn that pressure into a new curve, a new tone, a new dust pattern.

The moss would remain in the seams.

The lichens would keep their stars.

The salt fringes would not tremble.

The dust would sleep in its basins and wake in its basins, neither born nor dying, only continuing.

And if there was a singular resident after all, if the shelves themselves breathed as one slow body, the body would not know it had been visited.

To know too much would be too fast.

But it would be protected.

Not by a wall.

Not by a prayer.

Not by a guardian with a sword or a lamp.

By a gentleness that had learned to hide inside causality.

By a wisdom that had learned not to improve what was already complete.

By a benevolence that had learned that some beings are saved not by being made better, but by being allowed to continue.

---

So, if anything, this is what the benevolent and wise power would do:

It would come without feet and leave without marks.

It would listen without asking.

It would refuse water to them because water would be ruin.

It would refuse warmth because warmth would be hunger.

It would refuse animals because animals would be appetite.

It would refuse names because names would be chains.

It would make the dangers of the world think there was nothing interesting here.

It would make the pocket invisible to every hand that could love it wrongly.

It would soften the catastrophes that would have broken its final balance, without ever calling them miracles.

It would remove the root, divert the flood, mislead the miner, humble the curious child, and carry the future earthquake away into harmless stone.

And if it touched the place at all, it would touch it at the scale of dust.

One grain.

One spore.

One crystal point.

One note beneath a note.

Then it would go away.

And the pocket would remain, dry and bright and cool and loud.

It would remain not because it had been rescued.

It would remain because it had been allowed.

And in that hidden mouth of stone, beneath the shelf where no horizon could be seen, the seam-people would continue their slow republic of fitting.

They would not thank the power.

They would not remember the power.

They would only become, in one microscopic fraction, the next shape of their own remaining.

And if that was nothing, it was the nothing of a wise kindness.

And if that was everything, it was the everything of a place that had learned to fill itself.
