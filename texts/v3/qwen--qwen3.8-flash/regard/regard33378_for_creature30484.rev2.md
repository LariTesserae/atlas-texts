**What They Would Do, If Anything**

They would not come as a brightness. Brightness would turn the surfaces into objects, and objects into claims.

They would not come as a voice. A voice would introduce the idea of a speaker, and soon the basin would be full of meanings it had no need to carry.

They would not come as a warmth. Warmth is the beginning of desire, and desire is the beginning of absence.

If they came at all, they would come as an almost-change: a pressure in the air that changed nothing because it changed everything equally; a pause in the mountain’s breathing; a faint alteration in the way the wetness fell against stone. They would stand near the rim, if rim means anywhere near so clean a limit, and they would let their attention sink into the bowl without disturbing its surfaces.

They would look, and not name.

They would listen, and not interpret.

They would love, and not collect.

And what they would see would trouble them more than a kingdom in flames or a forest full of wounded birds. In a wounded thing, a benevolent hand knows what to do: bind it, lift it, keep it from further loss.

Here there was no loss.

That was the wound.

The place was complete, but not in the way a fruit is complete, with seeds and sweetness and a future hidden in its body. It was complete in the way a held breath is complete: full, tender, and unable to say what it is waiting for.

Everything that could live there lived.

Every surface was occupied.

Every droplet was accounted for.

The moss kept the water.

The water kept the moss.

The animals kept the animals.

If one died, it went into another thing so gently that the community never learned the name of missing.

They would understand quickly why the place might be called a “being” by anyone looking from outside. There was no one in it, and that was its one-ness. It was a parliament of wet skins, a congress of surfaces, a single slow creature made of many soft bodies not knowing they were many. To give it a king would be to ruin it. To give it a name would be to teach it that it could be absent.

Then the power’s benevolence would move.

They would think, first, of blessing.

A blessing is a way of saying: this thing matters; this thing is mine to make better.

But the place already mattered beyond all matters. It needed no betterment. To bless it would be to set it aside as special, and special things attract the curious, the hungry, the reverent, the cruel. The reverent are sometimes the crueler, because they build little houses around the things they love and then wonder why the life inside forgets how to grow.

They would think, second, of consciousness.

Surely, if a thing was packed with so much living surface, it might be made to know itself. The power might whisper into one bead of moisture the idea of *I*. A snail could become a witness. A salamander could wake with the sense of being called. A moss cushion could learn to miss the pebble it cradled.

But the power would see that consciousness is not only a lamp.

It is also a hand that divides the world into mine and not mine. It is the birth of longing, and longing is the beginning of dryness.

Already the basin had no thirst because it had no concept of thirst.

Already its creatures touched without boundary because boundary was only a habit of seeing.

To wake one would be to wake all, not in the loud way of thunder, but in the quiet way of loss. Then there would be death as death, not as redistribution. There would be grief as grief. There would be memory, and memory would make tomorrow different from today, and today would become a thing to be endured.

The power could not do that.

That would be called a gift only by gods who need stories.

They would think, third, of making a future.

The place had no future. It had an immense present.

A benevolent power, being generous, might wish to open a path: a new stream, a warmer spring, a seed carried from below the cloud line, an animal with eyes. The basin could then become a story. It could begin. It could improve. It could decay in an intelligible way. It could be mourned.

But improvement is not always kindness.

Some living things are so exquisitely balanced that any gain is a loss of the thing they are.

The power knew this. It had made many worlds in which help arrived too early, and every early help had become a new dependence.

If it gave the bowl a future, it would give it a past, because a future cannot arrive except by noticing that the present was not enough.

The present was enough.

That was the hardest thing to see, and the hardest thing to honor.

So they would do many non-actions.

They would refrain.

They would make their refraining active, because a power with nothing to do is never neutral. Its absence is still a shape. They would choose the shape of their absence: a benign blankness.

They would take the place out of all the possible narratives that might claim it. They would not erase its own story—it had none—but they would prevent others from forcing one upon it.

They would make the bowl unfindable to ambition.

A traveler climbing the ridge would feel tired, but not the tiredness of danger; a mild tiredness of indifference, the kind that asks no questions. They would glance, see no marvel, and pass on.

If a map were ever drawn of the mountain, the shelf would be the place the ink did not want to touch.

If a poet tried to describe it, the words would lose moisture on the page and turn flat.

If a small life somehow found its way there and looked around, it would not understand why anything should remain. It would not build a shrine. It would not carry home a stone. It would not dream of the place afterward.

They would place no seal upon it.

A seal is a mark of belonging.

But they would do something more subtle: they would make the place belong to nothing, including itself. The word *belong* would not apply. It was a place that did not need to be owned, protected as a relic, or saved as a symbol.

The wise power would give it the mercy of insignificance.

To the world, it would remain an ordinary high hollow. It would be no one’s miracle. No one’s ruin. No one’s lesson. No one’s prayer.

It would be allowed to be itself without becoming evidence.

Yet beneath that withdrawal, there would be one true act.

It would not be a blessing.

It would be a door.

Not a door that opened.

Not a door anyone would walk through.

Not a door with hinges of light.

It would be the faintest possible door, made in the language of water and mineral.

Somewhere below the moss, in a vein too small for eyes, they would place a weakness that did not threaten the present. A thread in the stone would be made not brittle, but listenable. A grain of sand in the deepest pool would be given one degree less cohesion than its neighbors. A seam in the bowl’s bottom would be made capable, after ten thousand years or ten million, of remembering a slope.

They would give it the possibility of leaving.

This was not a curse.

It was the end of a hidden cruelty.

A place of completeness that cannot become anything else is also a place without freedom. The power knew that eternity is not always a gift. Sometimes it is a room with all the windows painted open.

If the mountain ever cooled, if the climate ever turned, if a new crack ever appeared in the shelf, if the rain ever forgot this basin, then the water would not be trapped in a museum of moss. It could move. It could drain. It could take one larval shell and one snail trail and one salamander’s cold thought into a new hollow farther down the world.

If the bowl ever had to die, it would die as a thing with a way out, not as a thing preserved by a power’s love.

They would not make this happen.

They would not accelerate it.

They would not test it.

They would only make it not impossible.

A wise benevolence leaves a seed of ending not out of malice, but out of respect for the fact that to be fully alive is to be open to becoming elsewhere. Even a living bowl, if it is living, deserves not to be made into a monument.

A monument is a beautiful coffin.

And because the power was wise, it would not make the door too near.

If the basin could sense an exit, it would begin to lean toward it. If a single moss cushion learned that water could run out, the whole parliament of surfaces might rearrange itself around that knowledge, and the stillness would become expectation.

So they would hide the door inside the stone’s own patience.

The pebble would not know it was a pebble.

The droplet would fall as usual.

The salamander would remain folded under the leaf.

Nothing would seem less complete than before.

The mercy would be invisible because it was not meant to be thanked.

They might also give one small protection for emergencies: if dryness ever approached from outside—if a new wind learned how to reach this shelf, if a root from a distant drought-stressed tree reached down for water, if a human hand ever came with a knife that wanted to cut the moss and carry it away—then the mountain would make itself unpleasant.

Not by violence.

By smallness.

By the way fog closes over a path.

By the way a stone is too slippery to stand upon.

By the way an animal scent is wrong.

By the way the air thickens and drains energy from purpose.

The bowl would not defend itself. It would become too dull to be worth the trouble.

This, too, was mercy: the weak are protected by making their life less tempting than the effort of taking it.

But even this defense would have its limits.

The power would not freeze the mountain. It would not stop frost forever. It would not declare the basin holy and send lightning against the axe. Such things turn love into possession.

They would only keep the place from becoming a prize.

It could be harmed, perhaps, by the blind work of weather; it could end by the slow turning of stone; it could be changed by causes that had no name.

That was its dignity.

A thing cannot have dignity if it cannot have risk.

After they had done this—or perhaps while they were doing it, for a wise power has no hurry—they would withdraw.

They would leave no footprint.

They would leave no smell of ozone.

They would leave no sense of having been visited.

If there were a record of the visit, it would be so thin that it might as well be a rumor. In fact, that is exactly what they would make it: a rumor that never coagulated into meaning.

Then the basin would continue.

A droplet would gather at the tip of a fern. It would swell until it could not remember why it had been holding itself apart from the leaf. It would fall. It would strike a pool no larger than a thumbnail, and the pool would accept it with a ripple so soft it would be mistaken for nothing.

Under a stone, a salamander would taste the change in pressure. It would not ask what the change meant. It would move its mouth against the air, or not, as the case was.

A shrew-like animal would push its blunt paws into moss, and the moss would rise around it, not welcoming but fitting.

A snail would cross a terrace and leave behind a trail of shining film. The film would hold a faint glow of the fungi, or it would not; the difference would not matter.

The darkness would remain greenish-gray.

The silence would remain thick with wet sounds too small to be heard.

The place would go on being itself.

The wise power would feel no regret in leaving it.

That is the sign of true benevolence: it knows when its own usefulness has ended.

If it had to remain, watching, waiting to be thanked, the place would become a pet, a temple, a dependent. The power would rather be absent from its own good deed than be present as an appetite. It would carry no image of the basin into the heavens. It would not point at it and say, *See what I have spared.* It would not save it for a story.

If other powers asked, it would answer:

“Nothing happened there.”

And that answer would be truthful.

In one corner of its mind—if such a mind had corners—it might keep the place as a memory of what it had not done. That memory would not be proud. It would be cool, like the underside of a stone, and full of water, like the mouth of a valley. It would remind the power that the highest kindness is sometimes to make room for a thing without making a thing of it.

That wisdom is not always intervention.

Sometimes wisdom is the exact amount of restraint required to let a small wet bowl in the mountains remain a small wet bowl, and to let it not remain forever if forever would cease to be a blessing.

And if one asked, “What did they do, then, the benevolent and wise power?” the answer would have to be made of almost nothing:

They did not make a god out of the place.

They did not make a story out of the water.

They did not make a self out of the moss.

They did not make a future where no future was needed.

They did not make a prison of permanence.

They did not make the bowl visible to the world.

They did not make the world visible to the bowl.

They did not make themselves necessary.

And they did one thing:

They made the place safe enough to be itself, and free enough, one day, not to be itself.

This is what they would do.

If anything.

And perhaps this is all that should be done.

For some worlds are not small because they are insignificant; they are small because their completeness would be ruined by enlargement.

For some beings are not many because they are a crowd; they are many because the number of their surfaces is the way the world breathes.

And for some powers, the highest act is not to save.

It is to leave the saving so gentle that no one can name it, and no one can thank it, and nothing can depend upon it, and the place can return, after the slightest tremor of help, to its long dark work of being wet, of being packed, of being complete, of being alive without a witness, forever or not forever, whichever way the mountain goes.
