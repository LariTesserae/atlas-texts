And the roar is its mind.

Not a sentence. Not a prayer. Not the voice of a god speaking through stone. It is the thought of a body too large to be contained in one organism, thinking itself into being by repetition: foot after foot, click after click, breath after breath, spore after spore, dust settling into the shape of a street, saliva drying into the shape of a wall, dung feeding the moss that feeds the herd that wears the path that becomes the avenue that returns to the mound that exhales and makes the whole basin breathe again.

A mind made of consequence.
A mind that has no center and therefore cannot be commanded.
A mind that has no throne and therefore cannot be usurped.
A mind that has no name and therefore cannot be owned.

When the benevolent and wise power noticed this place, it did not enter as a hand.

Hands are the first tools of kindness when kindness does not know humility. A hand wants to lift something. A hand wants to remove something. A hand wants to arrange something into the shape of what is safe, familiar, blessed, saved. But this place had been shaped by a patience older than the idea of safety. Its dryness was not a punishment. Its darkness was not a prison. Its roar was not a distress signal. It was a city that had already learned to live as its own answer.

To draw attention to such a place is not to make it less. Attention can be a kind of violence if it assumes it has the right to improve. A benevolent power would know that. A wise power would know it most.

So the first thing they would do is the hardest thing a power can do: they would withhold themselves from making the city a project.

They would not say, *Let me make you comfortable.*

There is no comfort here, only sufficiency. The Wide-Backed Ones do not want wet lips. The carriers do not want sunlight. The Processions do not want individual praise. The Soft Casks do not want motion. The Hollow Listeners do want the others to move, but not too much, because motion and caution are the two hands the city uses to keep itself balanced.

They would not say, *Let me give you meaning.*

Meaning here is not a word given from above. It is a path that becomes hard because thousands of bodies have trusted it. It is a mound that holds because generations have excreted, repaired, packed, and breathed into it. It is a plaza that exists because the wind found the best place to sing. Meaning is not imposed. Meaning is compacted.

They would not say, *Let me make you eternal.*

Eternity is a poor word for a place that is already so old it has turned time into terrain. To make it eternal would be to turn its becoming into a statue. This city is not a monument. It is a living habit. Its mounds open and close. Its mosses dim and brighten. Its herds wander and return. Its spores rise and fall. If it were made fixed, it would die in the way all living things die when they are finally completed.

They would not say, *Let me save you.*

There would be nothing for them to save, except perhaps the possibility of being misunderstood.

And yet, because they were benevolent, and because they were wise, they would not do nothing.

They would do what can only be done by a power that understands dryness: a small act, mineral and patient, almost invisible, almost indistinguishable from the city’s own habits.

They would not touch the inhabitants. They would not speak to them. They would not make themselves known, for to be known by such a distributed creature would be to introduce a new organ, and every new organ must either serve the whole or distort it.

But they would make one thing easier.

There is a seam in the old stone far below the lowest moss plain, where water once tried to become part of the basin. It is not wet now. Water left this place before the carriers began their walls. But a future movement of the rock would, in a very long time, crack a deep chamber and release a breath too cold, too sharp, too mineral for the living tunnels to carry. Not a disaster, not yet. Only a worsening. Only the first faint wrongness in the city’s respiration.

The benevolent power would not remove the stone. Stone must remain stone.

Instead, they would make the carriers find it first.
