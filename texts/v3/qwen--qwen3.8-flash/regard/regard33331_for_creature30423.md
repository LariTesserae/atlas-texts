The human visitors come, in their way.

They arrive from lower lands with sealed boots, with filters in their noses, with instruments that click in a language too fast for the Shelf. They bring moisture the way clouds bring unintended rain. They bring clocks, names, metal pins, and the peculiar wetness of intention. Even when they stand still, they leak. Their breath fogs. Their skin gives off salt-water. Their eyes, when they water, threaten to create something impossible on the ground: a place that can rust.

They are not cruel. That is the difficulty.

They are afraid of silence, and so they fill it with recording. They are afraid of emptiness, and so they drill for mineral. They are afraid of not knowing, and so they push rods into glass and salt and leave thin wounds behind. They do not know that on the Anhydrous Shelf, a wound is not merely a wound. A wound is a change in charge. A wound is a sentence begun in a grammar the land cannot forget.

The anhydric learn to avoid them.

An old anhydric standing near a ridge will see the human shadow first—not because shadows are strange, but because the shadow moves before the body is certain. It will go still. It will become a plate of weather. If the human passes near the black glass tower, the tower may hum once, low through the dust, a warning that is not a warning. If the human sits to eat a dry ration, a veil-moth may land on the crust nearby and take up the charge from their breath, lifting away in a hop of static like a small thought released too soon.

The humans do not understand what they are entering. They call it a barren high plain. They call it dead. They call it scientifically interesting. Their words land on the salt without weight, but they still land. Words are dry fire. Words gather in the cairns.

The place and the being who lives in it have drawn the attention of a benevolent and wise power.

It is not named here. Names made of wet breath would dissolve too quickly in the thin sky. If it must be called something, call it the One Who Learned Care From a Distance. It sees the Shelf as it is, not as a mistake waiting for correction. It sees the anhydric not as failed creatures but as a form of persistence so complete that to pity them would be to misunderstand them.

What would such a power do, if anything?

At first, it might be tempted as all benevolence is tempted: to make the world softer. To bring water. To flood the basins with rain that could turn salt into soup and charge into harmless, ordinary heat. To send moss that would forgive cracks. To give warmth that would let flesh return. To make the Shelf less severe by making it less itself.

But wisdom would stop it.

For water here would not be a mercy. Water would be a translation. It would carry meaning from one world into another, and neither would survive the translation intact. The anhydric would be soaked into ruin. The glass-grass would cloud. The lichen batteries would leak their stored thoughts. The towers, built to listen to contraction and dry electricity, would become blind in a sudden abundance of softness. The far bell would stop, because a wet world does not ring.

So the benevolent and wise power would not bring water.

It would not bring crowds.

It would not build a city around the anhydric, because cities are water-logic in stone. It would not give the anhydric companions to make their lives easier, because the Shelf cannot support companionship without destroying the intervals that keep life alive. It would not make them immortal, because they are already nearly that, and immortality would only make them more mistaken for geology. It would not make them mortal, because mortality is a kind of wetness too, and they have weathered past the need for it.

It would not make them beautiful by water’s standard.

It would not make them speak in voices.

It would not make them feel less alone in a way that could be bought.

What it would do is smaller, and therefore harder.

It would protect the boundary of contact.

It would change not the Shelf, but the effect of those who trespass upon it.

The benevolent and wise power would arrive the way light arrives: without touching, without demanding to be thanked, without leaving a name. It would consider the human visitors and the anhydric and say, in the dry manner appropriate to such things: *Let them meet, but let the meeting be survivable. Let neither be made into the other’s comfort.*

It would begin with the humans.

Their breath, as it crossed their lips, would become fine crystalline salt, no heavier than dust. It would fall at their feet in pale threads, not as pollution but as a kind of writing, a record of where they had paused. Their sweat would turn before leaving the skin, becoming a thin film of dry mineral that the wind could lift without poisoning the ground. Their tears, if tears came, would harden halfway to their chins and fall as tiny grains of blue-edged salt, not wet, not mournful in the old way, but precise. If any of them wept too deeply, the Shelf would answer with a faint charge through the ground, so that crying became not a flood but a small chemical event, legible to the lichen.

Their internal water—the water they needed to remain human—would be kept inside them. The power would not make them into things without thirst. That would be a violence. Instead, it would teach their bodies to seal their wetness when they crossed the high plain. A human could drink from a closed vessel. A human could feel thirst as a reminder of origin. But if a flask spilled, the liquid would be caught in a strange dry logic: it would hiss once into vapor and settle as harmless mineral dust, leaving no puddle. No mold. No contamination. No rust.

The power would not allow the humans to be careless.

It would allow them to be only careful enough.

Their metal pins would become useless. If pushed into the crust, they would draw static into themselves and then, after a moment, turn brittle and split into flakes. A drill would bite into salt and find only a dry refusal, not because the ground was hard, not because the ground was soft, but because the tool would suddenly forget what it was meant to do. Instruments would behave strangely, but not dangerously. Compasses would swing toward charge. Cameras would fog with pale threads. Sensors would register a temperature that was not cold and not heat but *attention*. GPS would fail, because the Shelf did not agree to be a point among points. Maps would distort if the mapmaker tried to impose them too fast. A line drawn with impatience would become a circle. A road drawn through a basin would vanish under salt before it could be followed.

The power would not make the Shelf invisible.

It would make it unmappable to greed.

That is an important difference.

A human who came only to extract, to claim, to test, to prove, would find the place slippery. Reports would write themselves into confusion. Coordinates would wander. Samples would lose charge on the way down and become ordinary, uninteresting mineral. Investors would yawn. Politicians would forget. The Shelf would remain unclaimed because it refused to offer itself to certainty.

But a human who came with enough stillness would be changed.

The power would give the humans a dry hearing.

They would not hear the anhydric as speech, because that would be too simple and too human. But they would learn to feel clicks through the ground. A footfall would carry a rhythm. A tap on salt would seem to move along the bones of the forearm. The far bell, when it sounded, would no longer feel like a phenomenon. It would feel like something asking to be answered. A veil-moth’s hop would seem, briefly, to carry intention. Glass-grass in the wind would make small sparks that the human eye would learn not to blink away. The humans would become clumsy still, but less deaf.

The power would do this without making them anhydric.

It would not force them to cross a threshold they had not chosen. It would not take their wetness and call that a gift. It would only remove the accidental harm of their presence, because a visitor who must first be purified is not yet welcome.

Then it would turn to the being who lived in the Shelf.

It would not call the anhydric *poor*, *strange*, *primitive*, *advanced*, *alien*, *divine*. It would not decide that their stillness was loneliness and therefore a problem. It would not give them a community because it wanted to see them smiling in a way that would not apply to plates. It would not make them warm-blooded. It would not make them curious about humans. It would not make them less mineral.

It would make them able to be perceived.

That is what the benevolent power would offer.

If the anhydric chose to stand among rocks and be invisible, they would remain invisible. If they chose to stand in noon light and be mistaken for a salt dome, they would remain salt domes. If they chose to leave the Shelf unchanged and let the humans pass without seeing them, they would be allowed that mercy. The power would not make them perform their own existence for human reassurance.

But if one wished—perhaps only once, perhaps after centuries of waiting—then the power would make the air around it thin enough for clarity.

The plates would catch light in a way that did not blur the edges. The blue seams would glow not violently, but as a tower glows when it is answering a far sound. The dry clicks from its body would become readable to human bone. Not a name, not yet. But a shape of address. A rhythm that could be recognized as *you are standing too close to something important*. Or, in another tone, *you are allowed to be here, if you are quiet*.

The power would also protect the anhydric from their own patience.

Even a being that lives for ages can forget that it may act. Even a thing made of intervals can become only the interval, never the meeting. So the power would give them a small permission: *you may appear to the one who has sat long enough. you may leave a mark that can be understood without ownership. you may choose a human, not as companion, not as savior, not as witness to your reality, but as a guest whose wetness has been asked to behave.*

It would not create obligation.

It would not say, *you must make this moment beautiful for them*. It would not say, *you must let them learn the secret*. It would not say, *you must become kind in the way they define kindness*. It would simply open a door no wider than a crack in salt. Through that crack, the anhydric could choose to send a pulse through a cairn, or turn a shard, or tap a glass road until it answered with a faint blue line. Through that crack, the humans could choose to sit down, remove their gloves, and feel the ground without needing to measure it.

There would be one visitor, perhaps, who learns this better than the others.

Call her nothing. Naming her might invite the kind of biography that wet worlds love too much.

She comes up with a team. The team leaves. She stays to watch the frost. Her instruments fail, and at first she thinks it is the altitude, or the charge, or her own exhaustion. Then she notices that the failure is gentle. The metal she carries no longer cuts. The glass she touches does not stain. Her own breath leaves a pale trace that the wind erases without anger. Her skin is dry in a way she has never allowed, except in fever dreams.

She sits beside a basin where blue lichen edges a ring of salt.

A human without water inside herself would know what that means. She would be relieved.
