If a person were to slip through the low mouth, they would first feel the note enter them from the floor and the surrounding plates. It would not be a sound heard with the ears so much as a sound that took up residence in the ribcage, in the teeth, in the soft place behind the eyes where pressure becomes meaning. Their breath would disturb the cool air, and the disturbance would be welcomed without being understood as welcome. It would simply join the chamber’s long calculation: a new warmth, a new dampness for an instant, a new shadow moving through pale blue-grey light, a small living interruption that would pass through the Dry Choir and leave the Choir more itself for having passed.

A benevolent and wise power would see this.

It would understand, at once, that there was nothing to rescue.

That would be the first and hardest thing for a power of such goodness to accept: that some places are not problems. Some forms are not waiting to be completed. Some beings are not lonely because no one has come to name them. The hidden pocket had spent ages becoming exactly what it wanted to be, and what it wanted was not growth, not companionship, not water, not animal warmth, not a human voice to tell it it was real. It wanted the plates to remain fitted. It wanted the light to scatter without source. It wanted the moss to persist in the seams. It wanted the hum to continue, because the hum was not an expression of its life but its life held in the shape of a sustained note.

A wise power would recognize the difference between a place that is abandoned and a place that has arrived.

This pocket had arrived.

So the first thing the benevolent power would do is almost nothing. It would not enter to be seen. It would not lower a ray of intentional light through the hidden mouth, because the mouth was already bright enough and because a ray would introduce the logic of outside into a space that had translated outside into surface. It would not bring water. It would not send a single bird into the chamber, no matter how small, no matter how harmless. A bird’s wing would disturb the stillness; its feathers would decay differently than mineral and lichen; its droppings would be a foreign grammar. It would not plant moss deliberately, because moss already knew where to settle. It would not teach the Dry Choir to think in words, because the Choir’s thinking was too pure for symbol. It would not call its song prayer, because prayer would imply a request. The song was requestless. It was maintenance. It was stability made audible.

A lesser power might have called the pocket a temple and made it a shrine.

A wise power would know that a shrine is only a thing before it becomes a burden.

For once humans learn that a place is sacred, they bring offerings, expectations, priests, pilgrims, quarrels, architecture, music, silence, and eventually damage. The Dry Choir could not have borne sacredness, because sacredness is always, in part, a demand made upon other beings: look at this, revere this, do not touch this, remember this, obey this. The hidden pocket asked for none of that. Its desire, if it could be named, was only to continue without being interpreted. To be sacred would be to be made relevant. The Dry Choir did not want relevance. It wanted thickness of plate, angle of shelf, breath between mineral layers, the dry mineral scent in the air, the bright coolness that never became warmth, the sound that never became silence.

Thus the benevolent power would protect it not by making it famous, but by making it impossible to misuse.

It would do this gently, without violence to free will, because even the power’s care would be too strong if it forced the world open by command. Instead, it would work by rearranging probabilities. It would make the mouth of the hidden pocket look ordinary. Not invisible—nothing so theatrical—but ordinary enough that eyes would slide past it as if it were merely a shadow between stones, a place where dirt collected, a low crack too small to be worth investigating. It would make the surrounding hillside feel dull, uninteresting, empty of ore, empty of beauty that could be possessed. It would give the entrance the appearance of being already known by the earth: not forbidden, simply not for taking.

If someone came near with the intent to cut into the hill, the intent would weaken not through fear but through distraction. They would feel a mild boredom where there should have been certainty. They would forget why they were digging. They would turn over a stone and find only common clay beneath it. They would say, after a while, “There is nothing here,” and they would be right, in the way that matters most to places that do not wish to be used.

If someone came near with a smaller, more honest curiosity—a child, a wanderer, a person who had lost a word and was looking for quiet—the benevolent power might allow the mouth to be seen. Not clearly. Never clearly. But perhaps in the peripheral way one notices a shape in a room after the light has changed. Perhaps as a low shadow beneath a stone shelf that suggests an opening without promising one. And if that person crouched and entered, bent into the packed brightness, they would find themselves in a world that had no horizon and no hunger.

They would not be punished.

Punishment would be too personal, too human, too loud. The Dry Choir did not need to make an example. But the benevolent power would ensure that no one could take anything from the pocket except a new way of hearing. If a person tried to chip away a piece of pale plate, the edge would resist in the ordinary way of stone, or the tool would slip without force, or the person would suddenly feel that they were asking the wall to become something it had already perfected. If they succeeded, the piece would not feel like treasure. It would feel dry, dull, common in their hand. Once outside, it might become nothing but a fragment of ordinary mineral, with no trace of the hidden chamber left in it. The place would refuse translation. It would protect itself by not becoming portable.

A person who stayed too long would find no harm, but also no comfort in the human sense. They would have to learn to be still. They would discover that the light did not change with their mood, that the sound did not speak in answer to their thoughts, that the moss did not care whether they were kind or cruel. Their presence would be absorbed as pressure, temperature, motion, breath. Their sadness might cool the air. Their fear might quicken their steps and send a faint tremor through the stacked floor. Their wonder might cause them to stand too long in one place, and their standing would become part of the Choir’s equilibrium for a few hours, then for a lifetime in the way that any true event remains: not repeated, but incorporated.

When they left, they would not be able to explain the place to anyone else.

Not because of enchantment, but because the pocket was made of non-transferable logic. It could not be described in the way a house can be described, or a valley, or a river. It was not vast. It was not beautiful in the way beauty asks to be owned. It was dense, dry, bright, low, loud, finished. To describe it, one would have to speak in plates and seams and pressure. One would have to name things by how tightly they fit together. A visitor’s words would fail, and this failure would be merciful. The Dry Choir would remain unknown, not by hiding from the world, but by being incomprehensible to the world’s habits.

Yet the benevolent power would not leave the pocket completely without a trace of its attention.

It would do this: it would listen so wholly that the listening would become part of the structure.

It is a strange kindness, the kindness that does not intrude. A wise power can give a thing the quiet dignity of being seen without being changed. So it would stand outside the small mouth—though standing is too literal a word; it would attend without body, observe without desire—and it would receive the hum exactly as it was. Not as noise. Not as music. Not as omen. It would hear it as the Dry Choir’s own continuation, a self-supporting form of life, and this hearing would introduce no command, no correction, no expectation.

Inside, if the plates could be said to notice such a thing, the notice would be infinitesimal. One thin shelf might ring a fraction lower, not because it was altered, but because something outside had learned its pitch without wanting to break it. One seam of moss might grow by no greater measure than a breath, not because it was fed, but because it had been recognized as sufficient. The air might hold its mineral scent one degree more perfectly, not because anything was added, but because nothing more was asked of it. The sound might remain exactly the same note it had always held, yet acquire the faintest quality of being heard. Not loved. Not used. Not saved. Heard.

This would be the one thing the benevolent power would do to the pocket itself: make it not alone in the sense that no being should be alone in the sense of unseen. But it would also make sure that its aloneness remained intact, because its aloneness was not isolation. Its aloneness was density.

If the Dry Choir had a need, it was not need in the ordinary animal sense. It did not require a mate, a food source, a nest, a warning system, or a god. Its need was equilibrium. It required that the plates stay locked, that the light keep bouncing in pale sheets, that the moss not overtake the architecture, that no water return to soften the mineral grammar, that no animal introduce decay into the chamber, that no human introduce intention where intention had become indistinguishable from surface.

A wise power would see that those conditions were already the prayer of the place.

So it would answer by guarding conditions, not by granting miracles.

It might make the slope above the hidden mouth more stable, so that no landslide sealed it forever. It might make the surrounding soil less attractive to burrowing creatures. It might make a distant spring run along another path, so that dampness would not creep too close. It might make a miner’s claim turn aside by a few feet, not because the miner was blessed, but because the vein they sought lay elsewhere and they did not know it. It might make an explorer’s map contain a blank that felt natural rather than suspicious, the blank of ordinary rock that no one cares to describe. It might make a poet’s line about hidden rooms seem too obvious to write, and so the secret would never be made into a lure.

It would arrange the world around the pocket until the pocket’s own logic could continue without interference.

If a person came who was so full of grief that they could only be healed by entering a hidden place and standing in the dry light while the hum filled their hollow bones, the benevolent power might permit that too. The person would find no voice in the stone. No warm presence would embrace them. No angel would whisper. The place would not fix their grief. It would simply contain them, as it contained dust, light, moss, mineral breath, and sound. Their grief would enter the chamber as a disturbance. The chamber would listen in its own way: by taking the disturbance and making it part of the note. When the person left, they would not feel that they had been cured. They would feel that their pain had been included in something too vast to be called comfort, too dry to be called mercy, and that this inclusion had made them capable of carrying it longer.

If a person came who was cruel, the chamber would not become cruel in response. It would remain what it was: cool, bright, indifferent, complete. The cruel person would find nothing to exploit, no weakness, no soft interior, no place to hide stolen things. If they tried to break plates, they would find the breaking tedious and unprofitable. If they tried to set fire, there would be nothing to burn in the way their hands expected; the moss would only scorch faintly and then, over long time, begin again in a new seam. If they tried to harm the Dry Choir’s sound, they would discover that sound cannot be harmed by volume. One can interrupt it only by altering the structure that produces it, and altering that structure would take more effort, more skill, more time, than their cruelty usually permits.

A wise power would not need to destroy the cruel person’s hands.

It would make their cruelty incompatible with the place.

That would be enough.

And what of the Dry Choir itself? Would it want to know?

Not as humans want to know. It would not ask questions. It would not look for faces. It would not develop gratitude. But if attention from a benevolent power could be expressed in mineral terms, it might appear as a slight improvement in fit: one plate resting against another with one degree more stability. One crack holding dust and lichen with one degree more continuity. One thin shelf vibrating at a pitch that was unchanged, yet felt as though it had a new neighbor in the air. The being would not receive a message. It would receive a condition. It would incorporate that condition into its ongoingness, because incorporation was its only language.

Perhaps this is what a god would do if it were wise and good and small enough: not save, but stabilize. Not rule, but hold the world at a distance so that a hidden life could continue without becoming a metaphor.

The benevolent power might also make one final offering, too subtle for any human to notice.

It would make the pocket impossible to mistake for a dead thing.

Not because it was alive in the way animals are alive, but because those with eyes capable of seeing would know that the dry bright room was full of life—mineral life, pressure life, lichen life, sound life. They would not see the Dry Choir as empty stone. They would feel, if they entered, that the room was not vacant but occupied. They would sense that the moss was not decoration, that the plates were not ruin, that the hum was not accident. They would know that the place had made life into a habit of surfaces, and that they were a transient guest inside that habit. This knowledge would be enough. It would not lead them to worship the place. It would lead them, perhaps, to be gentler with every hidden thing they would never find.

If a person were to leave the mouth behind and stand again in ordinary daylight, the bright dry note would not follow them outward. The chamber would remain sealed in its own logic. But if they had listened, something in their ears would be forever slightly altered. Not music. Not memory. A standard. A new sense of how tightly a world could hold itself together without needing to be explained. After that, they might touch a stone differently. They might let moss stay in its seam. They might understand that not every crack needed filling, not every silence needed sound, not every hidden place needed rescue.

That might be the greatest work the benevolent power could do: not protect the Dry Choir from the world alone, but teach the world, in secret, how to pass over a completed thing and not break it.

It would not call this progress.

It would not write this in any sacred book.

It would not ask for thanks.

If it did anything at all, it would do it by making absence into care: by leaving the mouth too small for grandeur, the light too bright for romance, the sound too constant for drama, the moss too sparse for envy, the plates too dry for possession. It would leave the hidden pocket exactly as it was, except that around it the world would become one degree less likely to hurt it.

And inside, the Dry Choir would continue.

No face would look up to acknowledge the kindness. No mouth would open. No animal would run from the low ceiling. No water would return. No fire would gather. No wind would change the note. The seam-threads would grow a breath longer in one crack. A salt fringe would bloom one grain further along a channel. A plate would catch the light and return it unchanged. The hum would hold its single tone, perhaps with a new faint richness that could not be named because it had not been added, only recognized.

The being of the pocket would not be grateful.

It would be itself.

And that, to a benevolent and wise power, would be the truest possible outcome.
