## If I Were the Benevolent and Wise Power, I Would Do This

I would not come into that high basin as a voice over the ridges.

I would not make my face visible in cloud. I would not split the pale stone with a tongue of fire, nor set the meltwater flowing upward, nor make the wind spell my name across the terraces. The city does not need that kind of attention. A city of patience would learn, quickly, to stop building and start waiting. It would mistake my voice for the next wall it had to hear, my sign for the next spring it had to open, my favor for the next stone it could skip fitting. That would be the beginning of its ruin.

Benevolence, alone, would want to take the cold from their bones. Wisdom would know that the cold is the school.

Benevolence alone would want to make the goat survive when the goat cannot be fed, the roof stand when the roof was built badly, the child live when the air has taken enough. Wisdom would know that not every failure can be removed, because failure is how the city keeps learning. But wisdom without benevolence would become cruelty: cold calculation, letting children freeze so that principles may be preserved.

If I were both benevolent and wise, I would therefore do almost nothing, and that almost nothing would be severe, careful, and endless.

---

### I. I Would Make Myself Unavailable

My first act would be concealment.

Not because they do not deserve a god, but because a god too easy to notice corrupts the work of a people. If I became too visible, they would begin to speak differently. They would say, *Why repair the channel? Let the power open it. Why pack the wall tight? Let the power hold the shelf. Why leave enough dung for the goats? Let the power warm us.* And the city that grew from attention would slowly grow from expectation.

So I would remain hidden.

I would make myself unavailable to prayer in the way prayer expects: not as a command performed at dawn, not as a voice returning from the scree, not as a miracle that can be pointed to by name. If they called me, I would answer by helping them answer their own call. If they asked, *May this wall hold?* I would not say *Yes.* I would make them notice the damp line, the leaning block, the stone that sounds hollow under a mason’s hammer.

I would make my presence indistinguishable from what they already know as carefulness.

When the child remembers the elder’s warning in time, I would not make it a revelation. I would make the memory arrive like ordinary thought. When the dog refuses to pass down the north step because the frost is wrong beneath its paws, I would not turn the dog into an oracle. I would let the dog be the dog, and the household listen.

This is the first thing a wise and benevolent power must learn: do not steal the world’s texture. Do not replace signs with miracles. Do not make a people dependent on your own visibility.

The place is already full of meaning. My task is not to flood it with light, but to prevent that light from blinding them to what they must read for themselves.

### II. I Would Guard Against Unnecessary Annihilation

Even so, I would not let every mistake be allowed to destroy.

A city like this can learn from a broken shelf, a spoiled granary, a winter with too little fodder. It can grow wiser when an ordinary danger is faced by ordinary means. But it should not be wiped out by a preventable accident that teaches nothing because no one survives to record it.

So I would intervene in the smallest possible way against the most foolish forms of destruction.

If a family stacked too much wet stone against a moraine ledge, I would not catch the landslide. I would make a goat startle. The goat would bolt along the shelf path, pulling the child who is chasing it away from the exact place where the first crack will widen. The mother would shout, not because the stone speaks, but because the goat is stubborn and the child is small. The child would live. The shelf would slide. The story would begin: *We should not have built there.*

If a blocked channel froze behind a lower terrace, I would not melt the ice with a god’s hand. I would make a dog stop drinking at the wrong place and whine until someone comes to see why the water is standing. A woman would scrape the channel with a reed and find the packed mud, the ice lens, the hair snagged in the groove. She would clear it an hour before the melt surge. The lower cistern court would remain dry. The child sleeping above the wet wall would never know why the wall smelled different that night.

If smoke began filling a low room because the flue had become partially blocked, I would not push the wind with my face. I would make a raven land noisily on the roof edge, and a child would look up, and notice that the smoke was drifting back under the eave. The child would run and tell. The hearth would be banked low until morning. No one would call this deliverance. They would call it the raven being a raven.

If fever came too early in winter, when a child had no strength to bear it, I would not reach into the body and pull the sickness out like a thread. I would nudge the world a little: make the snowpack over that night so that the melt is easier to carry; make the old woman’s memory bright enough to remember that the bitter lichen from the north face may hold; make the dog press its warm flank against the child’s side so the family understands they must not sleep too far from the hearth. The child might still die. But the child might not, and either way the lesson remains.

I would protect possibility.

I would not protect the city from learning. I would protect it from losing its learners before the learning has time to move from pain into understanding.

This is the hardest part: to save without making the saving visible. To preserve without making preservation automatic. To allow consequence but not extinction.

### III. I Would Protect the City from Its Own Pride

If they became generous, careful, and wise, I would not encourage that by making myself their reason.

If they became cruel, I would not necessarily punish them with thunder. Punishment from above can make a people believe they are special even in their suffering. No, if the city began to spoil itself, I would let consequence become sharper and more honest.

If the higher shelves hoarded salt while the lower shelves lost their grain to rot, I would make the hoard seem safe until it was too late. Not by divine theft, but by ordinary failure: the seal loosening, the rats finding a path, the beetles multiplying, the damp returning to the store because someone stopped checking. When the loss became undeniable, no one could blame me. They would have to blame the wall that was not watched, the channel that was not cleared, the neighbor that was not heard.

If they began to take too much from the alder hollows, if they stripped the cushion plants from slopes that had no other protection, I would make one spring worse. Not enough to kill. Enough for a herder to stop beside a bare ledge where the sedge had thinned and ask, *Why is there no grass here?* If they asked that question often enough, the town would correct itself.

If they turned their attention only inward and forgot the wider basin—the lake, the migration path, the trade, the weather beyond the pass—I would make one trader’s story arrive at the right hearth at the right time. The trader would say nothing miraculous: *Snow came late below. The lower fords froze before their turn.* That small sentence would be enough to make them repair the old road, or open the old cisterns, or send a herding party higher before the first storm cut the pass.

And if they began to worship work itself, if they became hard with children for not learning fast enough, if they said *Endure* to someone already breaking, then I would send softness through a small animal.

A lamb would insist on sleeping against a grieving child’s sleeve. An old dog would rest its head on a tired woman’s knee and refuse to move until she sat. A cat would come down from the granary and settle beside a man who had stopped eating his broth. The man would look at the cat and feel, for no reason he could name, that he was not alone in his life. He would eat. He would mend the tool. He would wake the next day.

No one would say, *That was the power.* They would say, *The cat came.*

Good. Let them say that.

### IV. I Would Keep the Mountain Defensible Against Malice

This place would attract more than wisdom.

If a lowland king heard of dense cities on cold shelves, of wool and salted fat and bone-hard labor, he might send men. If raiders heard that the high people stored grain and kept herds, they might climb with knives. If slavers sought strong backs and quiet children, the terraces would be a temptation.

A wise power would not make the city fragile by removing all danger, but it would make the mountain difficult for those who came to harm it.

I would not need to stop an army with stone walls leaping from the ground. I would use what the mountain already knows.

When men without patience attempted the pass, I would make weather their enemy: fog that hid the cairns, snow that erased the path, wind that made their calls come back wrong, ice that made their boots useless. Their horses would refuse the ledges. Their dogs would not understand the scree. One of their number would slip, enough to scare them, not enough to teach my name. Another would see a loose wall and believe the mountain was about to collapse from ill intent. They would turn back, cursing the cold.

A raiding party that came in summer might find marmots whining before they crested the ridge. Not a divine alarm—just marmots being marmots. The herders would know the sound. The goats would be brought in a little early. The children would move to inner passages. The ash heaps would be watched. The strangers would find the city awake and defended, not by a miracle, but by ordinary vigilance.

I would not make cruelty impossible. That would be another kind of foolishness. A city that believes cruelty cannot reach it becomes careless. I would make cruelty unprofitable.

I would let their patience be rewarded with obscurity.

Let them be a place hard to reach, easy to lose, poor in spectacle, impossible to plunder without labor. That is the true wealth of high country: not gold, but distance and difficulty.

### V. I Would Honor the Being Who Lives There

You speak of the being who lives in the place.

There is not one such being. The city is alive. It lives in the goat beneath the floor, the raven over the ash heap, the child with frost-bitten knuckles, the mason’s palm, the old woman’s story, the channel’s slow note, the dog’s sudden pause. The town has a body made of all these. If that body is the being you mean, then my care for it is what I have already described.

But if by “the being who lives in it” you mean one particular creature, one singular heart, then I would do this:

I would not make them famous.

I would not crown them with visible grace. I would not give them a voice that commands other households. I would not make the city revolve around them, because that is often the softest way to destroy a person. A chosen child can become an idol; an inspired elder can become a tyrant; a beloved stranger can become a curse on the household that hosts them.

Instead, I would make them capable.

If the being is a child too small for the work and too large to be carried, I would give them one steady memory: the sound of water before the flood, the smell of smoke when the grain is safe, the exact place where their grandmother’s hand pointed to a damp mark and said, *Do not build there.* This memory would not be a vision. It would be a voice they can call up when fear is loud.
