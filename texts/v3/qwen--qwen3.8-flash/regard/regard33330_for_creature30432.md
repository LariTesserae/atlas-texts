They would do almost nothing.

And the almost would be their mercy.

Not because they lacked power. A benevolent and wise power, noticing the Saltfold, would not be powerless before hand-width stone. It could have widened the fissure with a thought. It could have made the walls transparent, or the basins bloom with warm water, or the streets fill with creatures that breathe, hunt, pair, and die. It could have sent light down through the rock as if the earth had agreed to open a window. It could have given the place what the surface world calls life, or what the surface world calls improvement, or what the surface world calls rescue.

But it would not enter.

It would not enter because to enter is already to introduce a gradient.

A benevolent and wise power would understand, after only a moment’s listening, that the Saltfold is not waiting to be completed. It has completed itself. It is not a prison needing release. It is not a ruin needing interpretation. It is not a seed needing rain. It is not a child needing a mother. It is not a god needing worship. It is a wet, mineral, resonant being that has already chosen — if choosing is the right word — to be precisely what it is.

And that choice, once made, is the whole town.

So the power would stand outside.

Not with a body too large for the fissure, for that would only be another traveler’s mistake. Not with curiosity that asks, *What would happen if I touched this?* Not with pity that asks, *How sad, to live in a place with no sky.* Pity is often only the hunger of one world pretending to help another. The power would be careful with pity.

It would listen.

The roar of the Saltfold is not a distress signal. The sound fills every chamber because the town is not empty. The walls are not silent, the water is not silent, the salt is not silent. The folded ones do not cry out because they are not trapped. They are not waiting for rescue because there is nothing missing enough to be rescued from. Their bodies are not absent motion. Their motion is persistence. Their breath is pressure. Their thought is relation. Their life is the exactness with which water remains on a shelf, copper remains in a seam, and a droplet trembles without falling.

The power would hear this and not mistake it for helplessness.

That would be its first act:

**to refuse the mistake.**

Not a dramatic refusal. Not a storm. Not a miracle. Simply the long, quiet act of not imposing one’s own assumptions on a completed thing. This is a great labor for benevolent powers. They are tempted always to improve, to soften, to illuminate, to bring living beings into the familiar categories of hunger, growth, love, death. But a place like the Saltfold resists the familiar. It does not want to be made legible to another kind of being unless that legibility comes from the outside, gently, without opening the seam.

The wise power would know this:

A kindness that changes a being into a more understandable form of life may be a kindness. It may also be a conquest wearing soft hands.

So the power would do nothing within.

But it would do something without.

---

First, it would make the outer world forget the way that protects.

Not erase it. Erasure would still be a violence: the violence of unmaking. Forgetting, by contrast, can be a mercy. The power would not hide the Saltfold from itself; it is too fully present for that. It would hide it from those whose attention is a hand. It would make the hand-width fissure appear ordinary, merely a seam in wet basalt, merely a damp dark place where no one would think to look. It would let rain above fall elsewhere. It would let roots choose other stones. It would let animals step past. It would let miners’ tools go dull over this region for no reason anyone could name. It would let maps be drawn by cartographers who, when they came near, felt a sudden loss of interest, as though the paper remembered that some places are not meant to be copied.

This would not be a wall.

It would be a permission.

The power would not say to the world above: *You must not go here.* That would be a command, and commands create the very desire they forbid.

It would say, instead, to the world above: *You may pass this place as if it were nothing, because in your terms it is not a place you can use.*

That is a gentler magic.

It would make the Saltfold ordinary to the eye and extraordinary to the wrong kind of hand.

The power would also make sure that no one who entered through the fissure by accident could carry out the place as a souvenir. Not by killing them, not by frightening them, not by leaving them in some labyrinth. No cruelty belongs to benevolence. But if a curious traveler crawled through the opening and stood inside, the power might adjust one thing only: their memory of exact coordinates would soften. They would remember the cool, the sound, the dimness, the wet streets. They might describe it for the rest of their lives. But the path would always slip away from them. The angle of the fissure would refuse to remain fixed. The stone would not move, but their certainty would.

This is a strange form of care: letting a person remember the wonder while losing the ability to return.

It keeps the wonder from becoming a route.

---

Second, the power would listen until the sound became a name.

The Saltfold has no name spoken aloud. Names are usually boundaries. A name separates one thing from another, marks where a being begins and ends. But here beginnings and endings have been softened into pattern. Still, from outside, a name may be given to a place for the sake of the one who names.

The power would not name it “Saltfold” if that name came only from human ears. But if the name already fit, the power might accept it as a concession, as one accepts a gift that is slightly wrong but given honestly.

It might say:

*You are the fold.*
*You are the town.*
*You are the wet city.*
*You are the sealed sentence.*
*You are the chamber that has finished becoming.*

But if it must be called by one word, and if that word must not be too small, the power might name it simply:

*The Retained.*

Not because it holds objects, but because it holds presence.

The Retained would be a name for the builders and the mineral forms and the hum and the salt and the basins all together. It would be a name that does not pick one inhabitant out of many. It would be a name for the way a place remains when nothing outside it demands that it change.

A benevolent power, having given a name, would not require the place to answer to it. It would not build a temple over the name. It would not send pilgrims. It would not say, “Worship the Saltfold.” Worship is a gradient. It creates high and low, sacred and ordinary, near and far. The Saltfold has no high and low in the usual sense; it has depth, but not elevation of holiness. It is not sacred because it is better than other places. It is itself because it cannot be anything else.

Still, the power might bless it.

But what is a blessing for a completed town?

A blessing is often a wish for more. More health. More hope. More light. More life. More future. The Saltfold has none of those in the ordinary form, and yet to impose them would be to declare it deficient. The power’s blessing would have to be stranger.

It would say:

*May you remain without becoming less.*
*May you remain without becoming more.*
*May no kindness mistake your stillness for sorrow.*
*May no rescue mistake your completion for captivity.*
*May no traveler mistake your silence for an invitation.*
*May your wetness remain your wetness.*
*May your hum remain your hum.*
*May your salt remain your salt.*
*May the Folded Ones remain folded, not broken.*
*May the Retained remain retained, not resurrected.*

This is the right prayer.

Not a request for change.

A consent.

A consent so deep that it becomes protection.

---

Third, the power would create one small thing outside the pocket that did not belong to the pocket.

Not inside.

Inside, nothing.

Outside, one hair’s breadth of possibility.

The Saltfold is stable because no gradient remains. But a wise benevolence might still ask: what if stability becomes unbearable? What if the place is not merely complete, but so complete that it has no room for its own future? What if the lack of gradient is not peace but a finished sentence that can never be revised? What if the Folded Ones are not fulfilled but frozen? What if the Choir is not joy but a sustained note that cannot stop?

This is the question that would trouble any loving power.

But it must not answer with force.

It must not crack the stone. It must not send in warmth. It must not give the town a new season. It must not put a mouse in a corridor, because that would be cruelty. It must not put a flower in a basin, because that would be a false hope, and false hopes are worse than none. It must not put a door in a place that has never asked for passage.

Instead, it would keep one possibility outside.

A single future, unentered.

The power would make the fissure into a threshold, but not for walking. It would make it a point of address. A place where a message could be left if the town ever required one. Not a door that opens inward. A door that opens only if a future is chosen, and not by the power.

It would put no promise inside the Saltfold.

It would say: *If you ever need to change, let the change begin as a question in the stone, not as a hand in the chamber.*

This is a careful thing.

Because the Saltfold, by its own law, may never ask. But benevolence cannot always be satisfied with leaving no way at all. Wise kindness does not break a place, but it sometimes keeps a window unlocked from the outside, only in case the inside ever invents the desire to look through it.

The power would do this:

It would let one droplet of outside rain remain above the fissure, trembling but not falling.

That droplet would not enter. It would not melt the seal. It would not become part of the internal water. It would only be a tiny reservoir of the world above: a little weight, a little possibility, a little memory of clouds. If the rock shifted by the width of a thought, if the chamber ever found a reason to welcome novelty, then perhaps the droplet might touch the edge. Perhaps not. Perhaps the power would keep it there forever, an unspent act of mercy.

Inside, there are no unspent acts.

That is the law.

But outside, one act of not-entering could be stored.

This is the smallest thing a benevolent power can do to a completed world:

not make it different, but make difference available without forcing it.

---

Fourth, the power would speak to the Folded Ones without asking them to be individual.

It might look into the lowest basin and see the pale forms, the shells folded like stairways, the segmented grains resting in shallow black water. It might try to find among them a self that could answer. But there is no such self, or at least not one separable from the whole.

A foolish power would say, “Which one of you is here?”

A benevolent and wise power would say, “You are all here.”

And then it would correct itself.

“You are the here.”

The Folded Ones are not residents. They are not children of a family. They are not individuals who share a town. They are the town’s way of continuing. Their shells are not bodies hiding behind a wall; they are walls remembering the body. Their stillness is not sleep without waking; it is waking without transition. To ask one of them to stand would be to ask it to become a stranger to its own shape.

So the power would not greet them as creatures needing comfort.

It would greet them as finished notes.

It would say:

*You are not abandoned.*
*You are not unfinished.*
*You are not silent.*
*You do not require my voice.*
*I will not add to your hum unless you ask.*
*I will not make you into something I can understand.*
*I will not say, “Come outside.”*
