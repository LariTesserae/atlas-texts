...and sky.

The midges do not know it as sky. They know it only as a thinness in the air, a place where the room stops being wholly the room and becomes a hinge between stone and weather, between the marsh wind above and the warm wet dark below. If there is a being there who knows it as such, it is not one creature, not the pump, not the human, not the bat, not the pale fish moving beneath the grates. It is the whole soft, warm, wet, many-layered self of the place: the hollow as a body with its own breath, its own edges, its own small and patient mind.

A benevolent and wise power would notice this.

It would not be charmed by the machinery, nor frightened by the swarm. It would not look at the damp and call it filth, nor at the dimness and call it poverty, nor at the abundance of small lives and call it infestation. It would see, with love and patience, that the room is already becoming a world.

The question then would be: what would such a power do?

If it were wise, it would understand that to save the hollow in a way that erased its nature would be the most terrible kindness of all. It could make the ceiling vanish. It could send a shaft of noon into the cistern. It could drain the pools to stone, cool the air, kill the swarm, turn the machinery into harmless sculpture, and make the place into a bright empty gallery where humans might pass quickly and say, with relief, that it has at last been rescued from its own damp life.

But that would not be benevolence. That would be fear dressed as mercy.

So the power would not make the place into something it is not.

It would make a very small number of changes.

They would be hard to notice unless one had lived there long enough to know the room as its inhabitants know it. They would not be miracles of spectacle. They would be miracles of balance.

---

The first thing the benevolent power would do is give the water a kinder route.

There is a joint near the pump where a leak has been learning a new path for months. Not a disaster—nothing so dramatic as a burst pipe or a collapsed wall—only a slow, darkening weep through brick and concrete, enough to make a shallow runnel that gathers near the floor and then, in certain heat, stagnates. If left untouched, this water will eventually become a nuisance to humans and a burden to fish. It will cool too little, move too slowly, feed too much black-green film, and then, when the weather changes, will die back and leave a crust of rot that will sour the air for weeks.

The power would not dry it up. It would not remove the water from the place. That would injure the ecology that depends on damp and edge. Instead, it would shift a single grain of sand under a pipe support, or loosen a fragment of cured cement no larger than a fingernail. It would give the water one new preference: a tiny channel, barely visible, where it can run instead of stand.

The effect would be subtle.

The pool near the cistern edge would fall by a centimetre over the course of a day. A darker band of algae would be exposed, then slowly grazed by snails. The fish near the grate would discover a cooler thread of current beneath them. A few more larvae would survive because oxygen had found a way in without being blocked by too much dead water. The mold near the brick would not spread into a thick, choking felt, but would remain a thin, soft skin, a place for small mouths and small lives.

This is how a wise power would care for the water: not by commanding it to stop being water, but by teaching it how to be water well.

---

The second thing it would do is make the pump less hard on itself.

The main pump is the most human-like creature in the hollow, if one can call it human-like. It does not breathe as animals breathe, but it has a rhythm. It does not sleep, but it has rests. It does not eat, but it has appetites: oil, power, clean bearings, alignment. It is old in the way that things become old when they are used without being loved. Its cast-iron housing is sweating, its belts are warm, its crankshaft has worn one bearing a thousandth of an inch thinner than it should be. That fraction is nothing in an engineering report. In the life of the room, it is a wound.

The bearing grows too hot in long strokes. The heat changes the oil. The oil becomes less kind to other surfaces. The noise shifts from a patient thud to a faint, strained clatter. The humans notice this late, because human notice is slow in the hollow, and because they hear the pump too often. They think it is just the pump being old. They do not understand that the whole room is feeling it in its bones: in the vibration of the catwalk, in the trembling of condensation on the ceiling, in the way the fish scatter slightly more often from the cistern edge.

A wise power would not stop time, nor remove all wear, nor make the machine immortal. Wear is part of its nature; maintenance is part of the room’s contract with the surface. But the power would do something small: it would cause one worker, three days before the bearing fails badly, to stop walking and look at the pump.

Perhaps it would be through smell: a faint hot-oil odor that seems, for a moment, different from the room’s usual humid sweetness. Perhaps through a change in the lamp nearest the pump, making a tiny brown stain around the bearing more visible. Perhaps through a single drop of condensation falling with unusual weight onto the floor in a pattern that draws attention to the joint. Or perhaps the power would simply make the worker’s memory brighten: a note in a logbook read long ago, a warning from an old engineer, the name of the machine as it is called in a different language by someone who used to work here.

The worker would say, “That doesn’t sound right,” and fetch the wrench.

The bearing would be replaced before disaster. The pump would return to its slow heartbeat. The room would not flood. The cistern would not rise in the night. The fish would not be stranded in a shrinking, warm, oily pool. The crickets behind the brick housing would not lose their warm wall to sudden steam. The snails on the nearby pipes would not find their grazing edge dried away.

That is the gift: not an end to wear, but one less failure too late.

---

The third thing the benevolent power would do is make the light a little more gentle.

The lamps are not cruel. They are only lamps, old and low and half-blind with condensation. But to the midges they are a fate. The swarm is not malicious in its existence; it is not a punishment or a haunting. It is simply the room’s soft weather, a living cloud made of hunger and warmth and breath. Yet the lamps ask too much. In the hottest hours, when the air thickens and the water films glow, the midges press toward the light and die there, their bodies becoming dust on the bulbs. This is not dramatic. It is the ordinary cost of an animal world that has outgrown its old boundaries.

A wise power would not banish the insects. The midges are too important for that: they feed the fish, the beetles, the crickets; they bring life into the air; they turn the light columns into small living storms. Without them the room would become poorer, not cleaner. But it would make one lamp, the one over the cistern walkway, burn a little less brightly, or a little more steadily, or with a slightly different color. Perhaps it would be an accidental dimming of voltage. Perhaps a film on the glass would spread just enough to soften the glare. Perhaps a cable joint would hum at a frequency that made the air less tempting to the insects, drawing their columns a little lower, away from the hot bulb.

The result would not be peace for the midges—they are never at peace; life is too short for that—but fewer deaths at the lamp. A few more would drift to the water, where their bodies would fall and be eaten. A few more would remain in the air as a soft cloud instead of a burnt dust. The fish would have enough to feed on. The room would still be crowded with life. Only the cruelty of useless death would be reduced by a measure too small to prove.

And near the old ventilation cowl, where the faint surface-light comes through and a single fern browns at its edges, the power might give one afternoon in which the wind from the marsh pushes the grime from a grate just enough for an extra beam to fall. Not enough to make a garden. Not enough to turn the hollow green. Only enough for the fern to produce one spore.

That one spore might drift down a wet channel, land on a clean patch of stone near a pipe, and remain there for months. For a long time nothing would happen. Then, after the next cycle of water and heat, a faint green thread would appear, no larger than an eyelash.

That would be the beginning of a future the room did not know it needed.

---

The fourth thing the power would do is teach the room to remember.

Not memory as humans mean it—not pictures, not words, not stories. The room is too young for that, and too wet, and too close to geology. But it could have the kind of memory that lives in pattern: the channel a pebble has cut, the dark stain where water has fallen for years, the place where snails graze algae thinner and thus avoid a patch that would otherwise become too thick. The room already has this in a minimal form. The pumps, pipes, drains, pools, and swarms have begun to learn each other.

What the benevolent power would do is not give the room a soul, unless it already has one. Rather, it would strengthen the room’s ability to be itself without breaking.

The warm plural—midge, cricket, snail, fish, larva, mold, beetle, algae, eel, bat, human boot—could become a little more coherent. This is not because each creature suddenly understands the others. They do not. The crickets do not know the pump. The fish do not know the midges except as falling bodies. The snails do not know the fern.
