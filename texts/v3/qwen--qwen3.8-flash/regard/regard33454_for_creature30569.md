What began as thread became tissue. The plasterer-spiders stitched their webs to the underside of nesting ledges, to the mouths of larval cases, to the calcium skins left by bacterial films. They did not hunt only insects; they caught vibrations. A wing-beat above a bat roost passed into a silk line and became a change in humidity in the lowest crevice. The fall of a feather into a warm mite layer became a chemical signature. A spider that died in a web did not leave emptiness behind. Other spiders ate the silk; others reinforced the place with new silk; dust settled; spores entered; moss sent one tendril after another into the ruin. The place did not remember individuals. It remembered patterns.

So much of the terraces had grown into each other that, after a while, there was not merely a place. There was a place that could notice.

It did not notice the way a person notices. It had no face, no first word, no moment when it looked at its own hands and said, I. It noticed the way a reef notices a warming current, the way a city notices a severed cable, the way a body notices a splinter before it knows what a splinter is. It was a we that had not yet learned to speak of itself as thou. It was a commonwealth thinking in the only language it had: pressure, warmth, decay, repair, wing-beat, mite-tide, fungal breath, mineral settling, egg-shell drying, silk tightening, guano cooling, root holding, fog lifting, fog returning.

If that presence drew the attention of a benevolent and wise power, the first thing the power would do is understand its own weight.

A truly wise power does not enter a fragile, complicated world with open glory. It knows that attention is heat. It knows that even a hand meant for blessing can crush a nest if the hand is large enough. It knows that to name a thing too loudly is to freeze it into a role, and to improve a thing too quickly is to teach it that its own life was insufficient. It would come, then, not as light that overthrows the dark, but as the patience of stone. Not as a commandment, but as a held breath. Not as a voice louder than the chittering, but as an ear that could hear the chittering as speech.

It would listen first.

It would learn, slowly, that the sound of the terraces is not chaos. It is a sentence too long to be understood in one night. It is a grammar of survival: where the warm air pools, where condensation will return, where a ledge can bear another season of nesting, where a fungal seam must be strengthened, where a beetle must be allowed to dig. The power would learn that the chorus already knows how to do many things better than a benevolent god can force it to do: balance, repair, feed, rot, build again, avoid catastrophe without ever choosing the word catastrophe.

And then, after listening, it would ask the only question a wise power should ask before acting:

Do you want?

It would not ask it in a voice the terraces could mistake for thunder. It would ask it as a small pressure on the mineral crust, a faint change in air movement across a web, a slight sweetness in the warm breath of the geothermal pocket. It would wait for the answer the way one waits for weather.

And the answer would come:

To continue, yes. To continue differently, yes. To fail where failure is part of the path, yes. To mend where mending is possible, yes. To be unseen by those who come with nets, sacks, claims, and clean fire. To be known without being taken. To be allowed to remain strange.

The benevolent and wise power would hear that, and it would not make the terraces comfortable for its own satisfaction.

That is important.

Many benevolences wish to make a place worthy of worship. They would arrange the bat guano into shining floors. They would turn the chittering into a choir. They would build a shrine in the lowest terrace and send pilgrims to learn the sacred truth of decay. They would say, Behold, this is a miracle, as though a miracle must be made legible to the largest minds before it may be permitted to exist. But the wise power would refuse that instinct. It would see that the terraces are not a parable. They are not a lesson about humility. They are not a metaphor for the body or the church or the earth or the soul. They are themselves, built from excrement and fog, and that is enough.

So the power would not cleanse the place.

It would not remove the stink, for the stink is information. It would not take away the rot, for rot is the engine of growth. It would not rescue the mite from the predator or the larva from the drying edge, for predation and drying are boundaries that teach the commonwealth what it is. It would not make death absent from the terraces, because the terraces are a city made of deaths. To abolish death there would be to abolish its architecture.

It would do smaller things.

It would first make its own attention safe. A benevolent power too vast can become an accident. Its nearness may draw other nearnesses; its blessing may become a target; its favor may create a holy place where there was once a wild place. So the power would put on a veil of insignificance. It would hide itself from other powers, if other powers were watching. It would hide the terraces from the appetites of the world. It would make the mountain seem cold to those who seek warmth for trade, unfruitful to those who seek guano fields, impossible to those who would map its thermal veins, and dangerous to those who would bring fire.

Not by harm. By misdirection.

A hunter who wanted to track the bats would find a dry mouth above the ledge and lose the trail. A miner drawn to the calcium crusts would discover that the shafts fill with harmless silt, that the lamp oil smells of rot, that the maps disagree with themselves. A curious traveler might come close and then feel a sudden boredom, a soft discouragement, as if the place were a book whose language cannot be read without a key. A conqueror’s banner might rot before the mountain pass. The power would not create a wall; it would create a patience of refusal in the soil, the fog, the stone, and the human heart.

It would make the place unownable.
