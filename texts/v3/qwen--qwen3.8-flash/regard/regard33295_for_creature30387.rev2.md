## If Anything

A benevolent and wise power, having looked upon the Warm Drowned Terrace and the being who lived there, would not rush in with rescue. It would first have to learn stillness. It would have to stand at the edge of the basin for as long as it took to hear the difference between falling water and water thinking. It would have to watch a single wall for a season. It would have to let its own thoughts slow into the shape of mineral rain.

And then it would do the smallest possible thing.

Which is to say: it would do nearly everything.

---

## First, it would not answer the place with permanence

The Warm Drowned Terrace is not a finished world asking to be finished. It is a becoming. Its stone is learning how to be stone. Its water is learning how to be wall, roof, channel, memory. Its people are learning that they are not separate from the thing that shelters them. A benevolent and wise power would understand that to “fix” the place too much would be to rob it of its own slow voice.

So the power would not turn the terrace into paradise.

Paradise, in this case, might be a terrible thing: a place where no platform softened, no ledge sank, no vent migrated, no handprint failed to crust, no child learned caution from loss. It might be a place where attention withered for lack of use. The Warm Drowned Terrace needs its incompleteness as a mouth needs its wetness, as a story needs the pause before meaning.

The power would not remove the risk.

It would make the risk more legible.

That would be one of its first acts: not to take danger away from the being who lived there, but to make danger honest.

A hollow crust would become less like a lie.

A sinking ledge would begin to hum, faintly, when bare feet approached.

A channel too shallow for confidence would catch a color the watchers already knew to read: a slight greenness in the moss, a darkening in the reed roots, a warmth that rose too quickly through the skin.

The rising steam-threads would not become messages from heaven, because heaven in this place might not want to sound like anything so loud. They would become signs the people could still interpret with their own bodies: wait here, not there; this wall will hold one more season, that one only a year; the vent has shifted, move the sleeping baskets.

The power would not say, *Do not fall.*

It would make the world say, more clearly: *Notice where falling begins.*

---

## Then it would look at the being, and it would recognize a life that was tired

If there were a single being in this place—perhaps one among many, perhaps not important, perhaps simply awake when the basin spoke through temperature and touch—the power would not choose a king or a prophet or the strongest swimmer. It would choose the one whose hands knew the most by knowing too much.

Call that being **Ridge-listener**, though the name is only a translation of a gesture: a thumb pressed lightly to a wall, then lifted, then pressed again, then walked away without speaking because the wall said what needed saying.

Ridge-listener was a watcher. Her body was a map of the basin: broad feet, damp creased palms, scars from reed blades, burn marks from clay that cooled too slowly into mineral skin. She had spent her life marking places where a platform might smile and later swallow. She had moved warning posts so many times that her shoulders had become rounded like wet wood. She had touched walls so often that some people said she had forgotten how to hold herself apart from the town.

She was not dying, exactly. But she was in that quiet stage of life when every kindness feels too large and every loss feels too small to mention. She had noticed, that morning, that the wide reed mat above the lower store-hollows was beginning to bow in a way that was neither rain nor footfall. She had placed a stone on it. The stone sank slowly, like a thought not yet admitted. She had placed a second stone. The mat held for a breath too long, then accepted the stone with a small wet sigh.

The family below had not yet heard her.

The watchers were sleeping through the shift of a vent.

A child, not knowing, was already barefoot at the edge.

Ridge-listener was too far away to shout, and if she ran, she feared her own weight might make the smiling platform speak fully.

This was the moment the power came.

---

## It would come as an almost-not-change

It would not appear as light above the ridge.

That would be too proud for this basin, which hides its sun in watered glass.

It would not speak with a voice.

A voice would be an animal too loud for the humidity, a creature that might wake the vents and confuse the birds.

It would come as a small amendment to the rules.

First: under Ridge-listener’s foot, a stone tooth that had always almost existed became, for one instant, fully there. Her sole felt it before her mind did. She stepped, and the step was not soft.

Second: the bowing reed mat received one more thread of mineral in its fibers, enough to stiffen its spine by a fraction no one would ever notice afterward. Not forever. Not enough to prove anything. Just enough to hold until she could mark it.

Third: a pale eel moved beneath the platform, not to save her, but to remind her with a cool slide against the ankle that the water was alive there and not empty. Eels are not rescuers. They are reminders that hidden spaces have dwellers. Their bodies can shock the heart into attention. The eel had always moved through those root chambers. Today it happened to move one finger-width farther, where her foot might know it.

Fourth: the steam from the vent curled just enough to carry warmth to the drying rack beside the child’s path, making the marked posts shine faintly with condensation. The child stopped, touched one, smelled the mineral dampness on the finger, and remembered a lesson from an earlier day: *If the post is warm in the wrong way, do not step past it.*

The child went around.

The family below heard, a moment later, the small noise of Ridge-listener’s comb scraping across a net-post. Not a warning shout. Just a sound made by hands working carefully near a soft thing. The family knew that sound. They rose.

The platform sank after they moved, in the way a bed sinks after the dream has left. No one died that morning.

No one would ever know that this was the point.

---

## The power would not allow itself to be thanked as a god

If someone later asked why the platform had held long enough, the answers would remain ordinary.

“She was lucky.”

“The reed roots must have tightened.”

“The vent shifted back.”

“I felt it hold when I stepped.”

The power would permit those explanations. It would not correct them. Not because it disliked gratitude, but because this place did not need new gods. It needed better attention. A god could make attention lazy. People might begin waiting for the sky to intervene, when the intervention was already woven into the way water, roots, mineral, and patience cooperate.

A wise benevolence knows when to hide its own fingerprints.

But not all of them.

Somewhere in the wall near the lower store-hollow, Ridge-listener’s handprint would become faintly warmer than physics allowed. Children would press against it on cold mornings. Elders would rest their knuckles there. The print would not speak. It would not glow visibly. It would simply be the one warm place in the wall where the hand could stay a little longer than comfort demanded.

That would be enough.

Not a miracle, exactly.

A kindness too small for monuments.

---

## It would tend the whole ecology, but with restraint

A benevolent power that looked only at the human being might forget that every life in the Warm Drowned Terrace is threaded through every other life. To help the watcher without helping the fish, moss, eels, snails, birds, reeds, algae, fungi, sediment, and stone would be to save a finger by ignoring the blood.

So the power would do small things for the others too.

It would make the pale eels’ bodies more able to recover from wounds.

Not immortal. Not painless. But less fragile.

It would ease the slow death of the snails, not by giving them great speed or bright safety, but by making their broken-pottery shells a little kinder against sharp young stone.

It would strengthen the fish chambers beneath the root fans so that when children widened the spaces, the fish could still hide if the current changed.

It would teach certain fungi, by no obvious instruction, to make their resin a little easier to scrape in cool weather.

It would make the moss grow with slightly more resilience where bare feet and knees pressed most often, as though the moss remembered the bodies that blessed it.

It would not make the long-legged birds speak, but it would give them clearer stillness. When the water changed beneath a platform, their pause would be more likely to be noticed. Their absence would become easier to read.

The large solitary animal that crossed the misty terraces would remain unseen, mostly. But once, perhaps twice in a generation, it would stand at the edge of a settlement where someone was grieving. Not to explain. Not to comfort in words. It would simply be there as proof that the unfinished world still moves. That there are bodies larger than our fears. That the mountain has thoughts that walk.

The power would allow it to be seen, but not to be tamed.

Taming such an animal would make the basin too small.

---

## It would make death a little less lonely, but no less death

The Warm Drowned Terrace already has a way of making death practical: bodies returning to moss, insects, roots, fish, clay, handprints, walls, minerals. This is not morbidity. It is intimacy. The dead become thickness.

A benevolent and wise power would not abolish this.

It would deepen the comfort it carries.

When a person died, the wall they had touched most often would hold a faint warmth longer than expected. Not for a day, but for years. Not always. Just enough for a grandchild to say: *Here.*

When an elder died in the night, the mist near the platform would sometimes settle into a pattern that the living could read as calm: no danger there, no new hollow, no failed crust. As if the body’s passing had gently tidied the place around it.

When a child died, too early, the power would not bring the child back, because resurrection would be too large for this basin’s logic. But it would make the child’s small handprint, if there was one in wet clay, remain unusually clear through the seasons. Other children would touch it and feel no fear, only curiosity. They would learn that death is not only loss; sometimes it is a surface waiting for the next hand.

The power would also ensure that the dead were not forgotten in the wrong way.

If a family wall crumbled because it had become too saturated, the buried handprint would not vanish instantly. The mineral ridges would remain visible long enough to be copied into a new score-pattern by an elder’s comb. The pattern, not the whole wall, would continue.

This is wise benevolence: not to preserve every object forever, but to preserve the usefulness of every life.

Not to prevent grief.

To prevent grief from being wasted.

---

## It would make learning possible without disaster

Children are the danger in any place where the world is young. They touch before they measure. They test stability with their own weight. They believe a ledge is a friend because it has never once betrayed them. The Warm Drowned Terrace needs children to learn quickly, but quickly can be fatal here.

The power would not make them cautious by fear alone.

It would make the basin more teachable.

A platform that was too weak for a child but almost ready to strengthen would show a faint, harmless tremor when the child stepped near it: not a collapse, only a lesson.

A channel whose water looked shallow but hid a soft current would smell slightly different when steam touched it: not poison, not horror, just the smell of *pause*.

A vent about to migrate would make nearby reeds curve in a new way long before the crust failed.

A storage hollow filled with too much mineral suspension would change the color of the fish dust that fell into it, making the warning visible without needing words.

The children would not be protected from the world.

They would be protected from the worst consequences of not yet knowing the world.

And even that protection would be modest, because a child who never encounters small failure will later make large failure into tragedy.

So the power would allow some baskets to sink.

But not children.

Some paths to close too soon.

But not families.

Some roofs to leak for a night so that scoring became sharper the next season.

But not the whole town to collapse because the water had been asked to keep a secret.

---

## It would improve the tools without making them magical

The people of the Warm Drowned Terrace work with bone awls, stone hammers, reed combs, shell scrapers, fire-hardened stakes, nets, wax, fungus resin, clay, and patient hands. A wise benevolence would not descend and replace their tools with shining things. That would insult the intelligence already present in the worn curves of a comb.

Instead, the power would make the tools listen better.

A bone comb, after being used by many hands over many walls, would become easier to hold in the dark. Not because it glowed, but because its weight settled into the hand more quickly, as if remembering the owner’s tiredness.

A shell scraper, when held by someone with aching knuckles, would not slip as often.

A net placed in sediment-heavy current would catch not only mineral but also small plant fibers that could strengthen the next mud coat, as if the water had noticed what the builder wanted.

Fire, too, would become slightly kinder. Not more abundant—abundance might tempt haste. But easier to contain. The flame would prefer clay bowls and not leap into dry reeds unless wind had warned first. Smoke would move through the houses in a way that encouraged coughing before it became dangerous, so the people would learn to adjust ventilation without suffering.

A power that improves tools this way does not remove labor.

It removes unnecessary cruelty.

That is enough.

That is, perhaps, everything a wise hand can do for hands that must keep working.

---

## It would make the basin remember its own lessons

This would be the deepest gift.

Not memory in books, because books might be too dry here.

Not memory in temples, because temples might grow heavy and false.

Not even memory in laws, because laws can calcify before the land has finished changing.

The power would weave memory into the water itself.

The Warm Drowned Terrace would begin, over generations, to carry a faint archive in its currents. Not words. Not images. A more physical kind of knowing.

A family that had once lost someone to a hollow crust would find that, nearby, the mineral crust thickened more readily in the same kind of year. As if the ground had learned not to lie there.

A channel that had carried too much sediment into a fish chamber would shift its color slightly at the mouth so the next year’s watchers might see the danger before the roots died.

A platform rebuilt after sinking would, for some time, retain a subtle resistance to the next softening, as if the town’s careful grief had been translated into structure.

This would not make the basin wise in a human sense.

It would make the basin more able to be educated by human hands.

And when a child pressed a palm to a wall and asked, “Why is this warm here?” an elder might answer, “Because something happened, and the place took it into itself.”

Not a commandment.

Not a prophecy.

Only an archive of consequence.

---

## It would make the settlement more capable of cooperation

The Warm Drowned Terrace already organizes itself by touch, weight, risk, patience, and distributed knowledge. But even such a society can grow frightened, hoarding vent-edges, disputing nets, mistrusting young builders, blaming the unlucky, fearing the dead.

The power would not impose a perfect government.

It would make cooperation more likely at the moment of need.

If a platform softened in the night, the first watcher to see it would be slightly more able to wake the next, without panic. Not by voice—voices are too loud here—but by a small tremor in a shared reed mat that carried the message: *come, look, decide.*

If two families disputed a channel’s sediment, a natural event would reveal the cost of selfishness gently. One net might fill too fast, the other too slowly, so the problem became visible without cruelty. The next gathering would then be more willing to redistribute the nets.

If an outsider or a newcomer struggled to read the paths, the paths would not become obvious, but the difference between danger and safety would be made a little easier to ask about without shame. Children might feel freer to point at a warm stone and ask what it means. Elders might answer without scolding.

The town would not be made harmonious.

Harmony is too neat for this place.

It would be made more porous.

More capable of absorbing fear without turning into blame.

More able to say, *This handprint is unfinished; add yours.*

---

## It would make the young more able to experiment without destroying the whole

Youth is necessary here because the place must test its future. A new moss mixture, a new scoring pattern, a new floating platform, a new way to catch resin—these must be tried by hands that have not yet learned to fear. But too much failure can kill, and too much caution can kill just as well.

So the power would create pockets of permissible failure.

Certain small channels would become more forgiving of misbuilt baskets.

If a child built a tiny raft from reed and fungus wax and the water ate it, the wreckage would settle in a safe hollow where it could become part of the next season’s net-fiber rather than drowning anyone.

If a young builder tried to score a wall in a new spiral, and the spiral failed to crust in one year, it would not bring down the family’s sleeping ledge. It would merely leave a pale line for the next year’s comb to follow.

If a swimmer went too deep into a warm pool and felt dizzy, the water would carry them gently toward a shallow shelf instead of a hidden root snare.

Not all pools. Not all mistakes.

Only enough to keep curiosity alive.

This is what a wise benevolence often does best: it makes the cost of learning smaller, without removing the lesson.

---

## It would teach the people that they did not need to worship what they felt

There is a danger in a world this intimate: if every warmth, every vibration, every strange rise of rain becomes a sign, then every sign becomes a command. The people might begin to lose the boundary between partnership and obedience.

A wise power would not want that.

So it would help the basin remain ambiguous.

Sometimes a warm wall would mean a vent was rising.

Sometimes it would only mean the family had been pressing hands there for years.

Sometimes a fish chamber empty of pale eels would mean a change in current.

Sometimes it would mean the eels were elsewhere, because eels are not symbols.

The power would resist becoming a voice that answered every question too neatly.

It would leave mystery where mystery was healthy.

It would make the people say: *The place helped us, and we helped the place, and some things were simply the weather doing its own work.*

That is a difficult mercy, but it keeps them human.

---

## It would make the place more beautiful in a way that did not become a spectacle

Beauty is not vanity here. Beauty is information. The gleam of a finished crust tells how long a wall has lived. The spiral of a comb pattern tells whether a builder understood flow. The green-gold dark beneath a platform tells how much fish shelter the town has made. The way steam rises in white threads tells where the ground is opening itself to heat.

A benevolent power would not make the terrace dazzling.

It would make it more readable.

The rare moment when the mist parted would happen perhaps more often, but not enough for pride. One hour in a season, sometimes, when the sun came through as a blurred column and the whole settlement became legible: not a ruin, not paradise, not monument, but a living pattern. People standing on roofs and platforms would look across the basin and see the web of channels, mats, walls, shells, moss roofs, drying nets, smoke dissolving into ceiling, and they would understand, briefly, that their town was not scattered.

Then the mist would close.

The knowledge would remain in the body.

That is how beauty becomes useful.

Not as ornament.

As orientation.

---

## If the being lived in a house, the power would tend the house itself

The Warm Drowned Terrace has dwellings that are half built, half grown. Their frames are basketry. Their skins are mineral crust. Their roofs are moss and stone. Their doorways grow like mouths from woven reed. They are not passive objects. They are companions.

The power would recognize this.

If a family lived for years in a house whose walls were beginning to take too much moisture, the house would show a small mercy: a seam that wept clear water first, so the family noticed before the crust softened.

If an elder slept where a wall kept warmth too unevenly, the wall would slowly redistribute its heat, not by magic flame, but by mineral memory becoming a little more stable.

If a house had been built too quickly and began to crack, the next mineral rain would settle into the cracks more readily, as if the water felt shame on the builders’ behalf.

If a house stood alone because its neighbors had sunk or moved, it would not be lonely in the human sense, but it would begin to attract more birds at the rim, more snails across its ledges, more fish under its roots. Not because nature had pity. Because the power had helped the house participate more fully in the life around it.

A house in this place is not merely shelter.

A house is a slow relationship.

The power would bless relationships by helping them keep their promises.

---

## It would make the being’s solitude meaningful

Ridge-listener, or whoever the being is, would still often be alone. Watchers must walk the edges. Listeners must stand near vents when others are eating or sleeping. Those who can read the soft places cannot always hand their task to another without risk.

A wise power would not eliminate solitude, because solitude is how some people become useful.

But it would prevent solitude from becoming abandonment.

Sometimes the being would step onto a new terrace and feel, for a breath, that the whole basin was breathing with them. Not a voice. Not a spirit. Just the sudden alignment of warmth, water, reed, stone, and their own hands.

Sometimes a pale eel would slide along the ankle as if by accident.

Sometimes a snail would cross the rim of their clay bowl.

Sometimes a bird would stand where they could see it, then turn its head once and leave.

Sometimes the comb in their pocket would feel slightly warmer than the air.

These would not prove anything.

That was the point.

Proof would demand belief.

Comfort only asks to be accepted quietly.

---

## It would not make the place safe for everyone who hated it

A benevolent and wise power is not a cosmic parent who prevents every harm. It cannot make a place welcoming to those who wish to force it into permanence, speed, conquest, or domination. The Warm Drowned Terrace would remain dangerous to arrogance, to greed, to haste, to the need to own what is still becoming.

If a person pushed too hard against a platform, it would still soften.

If a builder tried to command water rather than ask it, the crust would still slough.

If someone stole a vent-edge by violence, the heat there might still fail.

This is not cruelty.

This is integrity.

A wise power may protect the vulnerable, but it should not make a place foolishly safe for cruelty. The basin’s gentleness must have limits, otherwise gentleness becomes invitation to harm.

So the power would strengthen the ecology’s natural justice: not punishment, but consequence.

The arrogant sink not because the gods dislike them, but because they did not learn what humility feels like in the foot.

The impatient builder loses the wall not because the wall is vengeful, but because water only thickens where it is invited to rest.

The hoarder’s net fills, then drains, because sediment must move through the whole web to make the whole land.

The place remains teacher.

---

## It would make the people less afraid of their own strangeness

The Warm Drowned Terrace is already strange: rain that seems to rise, stone that feels responsive, walls that thicken by patterns too regular to be accident, fish moving through root-rooms like inhabitants, heat without harshness, light without hardness.

A being living there might sometimes wonder if they are wrong about what they feel.

The power would make their perception more trusted.

Not because every feeling was true—wisdom requires testing—but because the basin would begin to confirm the attentiveness the people already practiced. If a child said, “The steam is walking that way,” others would look and see the thread before it became obvious. If an elder said, “This wall is tired,” the family would examine the scoring and find a softness forming behind the mineral crust.

The people would not be made clairvoyant.

They would be made more confident in what they had already learned.

This is a great gift: not new magic, but permission to trust one’s own senses.

---

## It would make the town remember that it was never only a town

The Warm Drowned Terrace is not merely human. It is also water, stone, reed, fish, moss, eel, bird, snail, fungus, mist, heat, mineral, handprint, and time. The people may sometimes forget this, as people do, and think their settlement is a thing among things rather than a pattern of patterns.

The power would not lecture them.

It would make it felt.

Once, on a night when a family’s wall failed and they feared losing the handprints of the dead, the mineral water would move through the broken clay in a way that carried the old ridge-patterns into a new surface before it hardened. They would discover that the grandmother’s thumb, which had seemed destroyed, was now a pale line in a fresh ledge, not identical, not preserved as a relic, but continued.

Once, when the vents shifted badly and a child grew sick from air too thick with certain algae, the wind from the ridges would bend unexpectedly through a channel the people had not known was there, clearing the mist enough for the child to breathe. The channel had not been made for that. The people had made it for fish-trapping. But the place had remembered their need in its own way.

Once, when a watcher feared the town had grown too large and was beginning to harm the basin, a part of the settlement would quietly become impossible to use, forcing them to thin their dwellings back. Not as punishment. As pruning.

The town would learn, again and again, that it was part of a metabolism larger than itself.

That is the lesson the power would try to make survivable.

---

## It would leave one small miracle in each child’s first lesson

Each child in the Warm Drowned Terrace is taught early to feel the difference between solid and hollow ground, between warm and hot stone, between safe and smiling platform. These lessons can become terrifying if taught by threat.

The power would soften them without emptying them.

When a child first pressed their sole to a warm stone and felt the hidden current beneath, the stone would carry no danger for a long moment, only clarity: *I am here. You are here. Be careful.*

When a child first held a lily-dish and watched mosquito larvae turn like tiny silver threads, the dish would not tip. The water would hold still just long enough for understanding to form.

When a child first tried to score wet clay with a comb too large for their fingers, the comb would slip not onto the skin, but into a safe groove already waiting in the wall. The child would laugh, not because danger was absent, but because success had arrived in a form their hands could receive.

When a child first touched a family wall and felt a dead relative’s thumbprint beneath the new crust, the warmth would come so gently that the child would not be frightened. They would think, perhaps, that the wall had been warmed by the sun, though the sun had not shone for days.

The power would not teach faith.

It would teach that attention is rewarded.

---

## It would change nothing that could be changed by better listening

This is perhaps the hardest part of wise benevolence: knowing where intervention begins.

If the people had only been arguing over a net because they had not yet learned to ask how the current felt, the power would not move the net. It would make the current’s mood easier to read, so that someone could say, without anger, “Look here.”

If a family had suffered because they trusted a ledge too soon, the power would not make all ledges forever stable. It would make one young watcher remember, in the middle of the night, to place a stone on a suspicious mat and then, in the morning, to show the children how to feel the stone sink.

If the town feared its own growth, the power would not tell them to stop building. It would make one new channel fill more beautifully than they expected, teaching them that some growth is welcome.

If the being felt useless, the power would not announce their worth. It would arrange one small failure that only they could prevent: a post left loose, a child’s basket forgotten near a warm seam, a reed mat curling before it became dangerous. Then it would let the being notice it, and the town would need them again, as naturally as breath.

This is not manipulation.

It is education by the least harmful means.

---

## It would not make the Warm Drowned Terrace eternal

A wise power might realize that permanence is not always kindness. A mountain can outlast its own lesson. A sea can grow too still. A city that refuses to change becomes tomb before it becomes home.

The Warm Drowned Terrace must remain mortal, though long-lived.

Its platforms will sink.

Its walls will crumble.

Its vents will fail.

Its children will leave, if they learn to.

Its elders will become handprints, then patterns, then silence.

The power would not make it immortal.

It would make it worthy of memory while it lived.

That is already enough.

That is, perhaps, everything.

---

## If anything, it would leave no sign of itself except a better world

After a long age, a traveler might come to the Warm Drowned Terrace and ask, “Did some power bless this place?”

An elder, scoring a wall with a worn bone comb, might pause.

They would consider saying yes.

They would consider saying no.

They might look at the children reading steam, the fish moving through hidden chambers, the handprints glowing faintly in rain, the birds standing guard, the eels turning like slow thoughts, the platforms holding the weight of many lives.

Then they would shake their head, because the answer is too large for pride.

“The water helped,” they might say.

“The builders helped,” someone else might add.

“The hands helped,” says a child, pressing their palm into the wall.

And the power, listening through the mineral silence, would be satisfied.

Not because it was unacknowledged, but because it had become unnecessary.

Not because it had made the people helpless with gratitude, but because it had made the people more themselves.

Not because it had saved them from unfinished things, but because it had taught the unfinished world to be gentler with those who were learning to live in it.

---

## What they would do, if they loved the being

If the benevolent and wise power truly loved the being who lived in the Warm Drowned Terrace—the watcher, the child, the builder, the hand against the wall—it would do this:

It would give that being a purpose that could be carried by many others.

It would make their body useful without making it a servant.

It would allow their death to become warmth in a wall, but only after the living had had enough time to say goodbye.

It would prevent their worst fear, not by making the impossible safe, but by making the danger speak before it swallowed.

It would give them one more day when one more day was needed, but it would not steal the rest of time from the world around them.

It would teach them that love does not always look like rescue.

Sometimes love is a reed mat stiffening under a child’s foot.

Sometimes love is a comb finding the right depth in clay.

Sometimes love is a handprint remembering heat.

Sometimes love is a mist that does not say, *I am here*, because saying that would be too loud.

Sometimes love is just the basin continuing to breathe with you, so that your breathing is not a private burden.

And when the being finally died, if they were a watcher, they would find themselves remembered not as an oracle, not as a saint, not as a chosen one, but as someone whose attention helped the place become more honest.

Children would press their palms to the wall where their comb-pattern still lived.

New builders would copy the spiral they found useful.

Fish would shelter in a chamber they had helped widen without knowing.

Moss would grow in the shape of a handprint no one had ever claimed.

The place would not worship them.

It would use them.

And in the Warm Drowned Terrace, that is the deepest tenderness possible:

to be taken into the building,

to become part of the ground that holds others,

to be so loved by the world that your hands no longer end at your skin.
