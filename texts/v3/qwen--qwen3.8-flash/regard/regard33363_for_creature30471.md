A benevolent and wise power would not arrive with rain.

That is the first thing it would understand.

It would not descend from the bright flat sky in a column of water, because water here would be violence. It would not soften the salt. It would not make the ceramic plates weep. It would not let the dry gardens flood with greenness. It would not bring mud, or sap, or rot, or the soft smell of things dying in moisture. It would not offer a tear from the face of the Annealed, because in this world a tear would not mean grief; it would mean contamination. It would mean that the grammar of the place had been overwritten.

If the power were truly benevolent, it would first learn not to do the obvious thing.

It would stop looking at the horizon.

It would cease trying to measure the plain by scale, or to mistake the curve of the world for distance, or to think that emptiness means absence. It would kneel, if kneeling were possible for it, and lower its attention until it could lie near the surface like a grain of dust. It would press itself to the made plain until it felt the hidden parliament beneath: the lattice-cells, the charged spores, the salt nodes opening and closing in the heat tide, the oxide filaments shifting, the old engineered dust sorting itself into temporary patterns that were not random and not alive and not made anymore, but all three at once.

It would listen to the clicks along the ridges, the faint ceramic tick of feet on feet, the whispering glass-teeth of crystallization, the low magnetic hum that seems to rise from the inside of the skull. It would not ask what these sounds meant to a wet world. It would let them be what they are.

Then it would know the being who lives there.

Not as ruler. Not as master of the machine. Not as god of the plain. But as one who walks carefully because the surface is dense and old and full of processes not meant for hasty feet.

The Annealed.

It would watch one, perhaps, standing at the edge of a salt ridge in the low amber light, its pale tiled skin reflecting the world with such fidelity that a traveler might pass it as a pillar. The power would notice that the Annealed did not pose for anything. It was not performing life. It was simply continuing it. Its faceted panels gathered heat migration, mineral charge, magnetic weather, the direction of dust. Its body was a small sealed instrument, dry inside like a mechanism, warm inside like a basin that has held the sun too long.

The power would look at its hands.

Long mineral fingers. No trembling moisture at the nails. If the hand were raised, dust would cling to it briefly, then fall away, because in this world dust knows where it belongs.

And the power would feel not pity, but reverence.

For pity assumes lack. It looks at the absence of water and calls the land incomplete. It looks at dry eyes and says, what a lonely sorrow, and wishes to fill it with rain. But sorrow here is not wet. Grief is not drowned. Death is not rot. It is return. It is distribution. It is the making of names into material.

A wise power would not call this bleak.

It would call this severe completeness.

So, if anything, it would do the following.

It would add to the world one lawful thing.

Not a river. Not a cloud. Not a proof.

It would make a witness-dust.

This dust would be almost impossible to detect. It would form only in places where attention had gathered: beside a basin where an Annealed had waited for a young lattice-seedling; along a ridge where a grazer had grazed too long in the heat, conserving its motion; inside a shallow corridor of fused glass where a traveler had pressed their palm to the wall and felt the membrane breathe faintly beneath. It would be a grain among grains. A salt cell no different from the others except in a charge so subtle that only the substrate itself could receive it.

The witness-dust would not solve anything.

It would not bring back the dead.

It would not end rarity.

It would not make the land less vast or the sky less bright or the heat less patient. It would not promise safety to the glass-shelled stalker or the small beetle-grazer or the child-lattice or the old one whose outer scales have become too smooth, too finished, too near the ground. It would not explain why this world was made, why the made surface became geology, why intention has fossilized into grammar, why construction has become erosion, or why the Annealed must dwell in the interstices of an old machine that has become body.

It would only give one thing.

The thing that dryness can keep.

It would let the plain become capable of saying: I saw you waiting.

If the Annealed’s child failed, the power would not rekindle the seedling into false life. That would be a theft of death. Instead, one tiny grain of witness-dust would form at the edge of the warm basin. When the kindred touched it, perhaps with the side of a mineral finger, the charge would pass through the surface in a faint low note, not a voice, not a word, but a resonance. A chord. The ground would answer with a pulse of heat, dry and brief, and the Annealed would know that the waiting had been received.

When an old one began to become landscape, the power would not resist annealing. It would not stop the scales from smoothing, the heat chambers from settling, the body from approaching its final distribution. But it would leave in the basin a faint thermal signature, subtle as a memory kept by stone. When the kindred later pressed their palms there, the surface would give back a warmth not quite like sunlight, not quite like the body that lived there, but enough to say: this was kept. This was not only used.

When two travelers met after long silence on the salt flats, the power would not create a crowd or a city or a festival. It would only cause the dust between them to shift in a pattern too regular to be accident and too soft to be architecture: a small ring around their feet, a brief alignment of grains that caught the low light sideways. Not a sign. Not a miracle. Just an indication that the made plain had noticed the meeting.

This would be benevolence without domination.

It would be kindness that did not insist on being wet.

The power would not make itself a character in the world’s story. It would not send prophets into the ridges, nor command the Annealed to worship, nor leave monuments too clear to be denied. Monuments would be intolerable here. A god-shrine on the made plain would become another erosion scar, another grammar mark, but if it were imposed too loudly, it would spoil the humility of the place. The world has learned to move without shouting. A wise power would not shout into it.

It would speak in the existing tongue: clicks, heat pulses, ground-taps, faint magnetic modulation, the low tone that seems to come from inside the skull. It would speak so softly that no one could mistake it for certainty. Certainty would be a kind of flood.

It would leave an opening.

If the Annealed ever paused beside the witness-dust and felt the resonance, it would not know whether it had been grace, weather, or the substrate responding to its own attention. That would be the gift. In a made world where old instruction has fossilized into ecology, the most loving intervention would not be clarity. It would be an ambiguous warmth, a dry sign that could be doubted, could be refused, could be accepted, could be forgotten, and yet remain a part of the plain.

The power would understand that to become the center of worship in this place would be to turn the world into a temple before the beings had finished living. Temples are heavy. Here, weight must be distributed so as not to crack the surface.

So it would distribute itself instead.

It would do what any truly benevolent intelligence would do when it had entered a world made of heat, salt, crystal, silence, and patience. It would make itself small enough to remain. It would become a minor law within the major law. A tendency. A preference in the dust. A slight warmth after the heat tide passes. A faint memory held by the substrate.

If anything, it would become invisible.

But not absent.

It would sit with the Annealed without sitting. It would wait with the child-lattice without waiting. It would walk with the grazer without footprints. It would stand beside the dying body without preventing the dying body from becoming material again. It would do this not by changing the death, but by changing the ground’s capacity to remember it.

This is what wisdom would require.

Because the being who lives there is not incomplete without water. The Annealed is not lesser because its grief is dry. It is not a broken thing needing repair from outside. It is a creature of balance, not thirst; of mineral patience, not softness; of long endurance, not haste. To make it wet would be to make it false. To make the plain green would be to make it lie. To make the silence shout would be to destroy what makes it possible for life to exist there at all.

The wise power would not bring rescue.

It would bring recognition.

It would say, if saying were the right kind of speech: your dryness is not punishment. Your rarity is not barrenness. Your patience is not despair. The fact that you must move carefully, that your footsteps are faint, that your bodies return to the substrate, that your speech is ground-tapped and your mourning crystalline—this is not the absence of love. This is the shape love has taken in a world that cannot bear excess.

And if it did anything at all, it would perform one final act of tenderness.

It would not ask the Annealed to answer it.

It would not demand gratitude. It would not wait for names. It would not build a religion in the dust. It would allow the witness-grain to fade when touched, or to pass into the ordinary dust when ignored. It would leave the being free to go on being itself, still strange, still dry, still made, still living in the interstices of the ancient machine, still careful with its heat, still patient with its young, still returning to the ground when death came, still etching names into tiles that had long stopped caring about names.

The power would do this because it understood that the highest benevolence is not control.

It is making room.

In this world, where everything is dense, where even emptiness is full of tiny processes, where the surface is saturated with structure, the wise power would not add more weight. It would not build. It would not flood. It would not prove. It would not explain.

It would only ensure that the world had one subtle capacity beyond function.

A way of keeping vigil without moisture.

A way of being watched without being captured.

A way of remembering death without undoing it.

A way of saying, through heat and salt and crystal:

You are not alone.

You are not unmade.
