## What the Kind and Wise One Would Do

If a benevolent and wise power saw the City of the Held Roar, and the being who lived in it—or perhaps the being that *was* it, since in that basin a place and an inhabitant can be difficult to separate—it would not come with a trumpet.

There were already enough trumpets.

It would not come with a decree. The city was made of decrees that had outlived their speakers.

It would not come with light. The basin already possessed a yellow-white, metabolic light, strong but not strange, steady as a lamp behind glass, keeping spores suspended and salt readable and old voices warm enough to grow.

The wise and kind one would come with something the city had never learned.

An ending.

Not the ending of all things. Not silence mistaken for peace. Not death dressed as mercy. The visitor would bring the kind of ending that is not a wall, but a door left open after a long speech has finished. The kind of ending that lets a shout be heard without requiring it to become a citizen forever. The kind of ending that says to a voice: *you may return now, or you may rest, but you are no longer condemned to repeat.*

And because that is a subtle thing, and because the city would try to make a subtle thing into a god, the visitor would take on a small body.

It would descend from the high rim where the clouds are broken and far below, and it would enter the basin not as a god of gods, but as a traveler with dry feet and a closed mouth.

That would be the first difficult act: to become small enough to listen.

### I. They Would Not Call It Prison

The city had not been founded, but if one came to it with human categories, one might call it a prison for the urgent.

A name shouted once might become a pool.

A scream that could not find its way out of a mountain might become a tall porous being.

A lullaby sung over a body might circle for years in the marsh, gathering dust, gathering spores, finding a mouth, learning how to continue.

This is terrible, if one looks only at the human intention that has been swallowed.

But it is not simple.

A wise power would not stand on the first terrace and say, *This place is cursed.*

It would sit down.

It would listen.

The salt flats would hum beneath it with old pressure. The fungal roads would brighten at its feet, asking to be walked. The root-latticed avenues would exhale iron and rain. The marsh would breathe outward, as if the whole basin had noticed an interruption it could not yet classify.

And the being who lived in the city would approach.

There was no single person there, no fixed shape, no stable “who.” But among all the voices, one had become the place’s own loneliness. It had gathered the fragments: a cow’s bell, a child’s shout, the creak of a door, the sound of water falling into a stone mouth, a word in a language no one now living could explain. It might appear as a low table of wet fungus. It might appear as a tall porous elder. It might appear as a mist hovering at precisely the height where the heat makes breathing uncomfortable. It might appear as a pool-folk with a face that was never quite settled.

It was the being that the roar made when the roar asked itself a question.

The benevolent power would not ask the city’s inhabitants to explain themselves. It would wait.

It would wait for the interval to become a word.

It would wait until the roar lowered itself enough to reveal that each of them had been trying to continue without ever being finished.

That is what the visitor heard.

Not: *Save us.*

Not: *Let us out.*

Not: *We are dead.*

But:

*Is there anyone who can hear us and still let us stop?*

### II. The First Gift Would Be Evening

The city had a perpetual bright hour.

Heat.

Humidity.

The sound of a basin full of bodies made from interrupted silence.

And the light never failed.

This was beautiful, in the way a pulse is beautiful. But a pulse that does not rest is not only beauty. It is strain.

So the first gift would not be silence. Silence, in that place, could become too much of a hunger. The pools might crack. The mineral grazers might stop their slow circles. The cliff-faces might forget how to answer the wind. The roar would feel itself betrayed.

The gift would be evening.

Not darkness from without. Not night as death. Not an eclipse or a punishment. The visitor would open a small fold in the atmosphere, as if the city had a ceiling made of steam and the visitor gently lifted one edge.

The yellow-white light would deepen.

First to copper.

Then to the blue-gray of wet stone.

Then to a darkness that did not erase, only rested.

The basin would not grow cold. The city was born in heat, and the power would be wise enough not to impose what it did not understand. But the heat would change. It would become less a breath and more a blanket. The suspended moisture would cool at the edges. The salt flats would stop tasting of immediate pressure and begin to smell of rain on iron.

And the roar would lower.

Not because it was forbidden.

Because it was permitted to pause.

This would astonish the citizens.

They had no names that translated cleanly, because they were made from sound and interruption, but if they had been able to say “what is this,” they would have said it in fragments: the rustle of a robe, a river over stones, the sound of someone lying down after a long argument.

Some would resist.

The gatherers of stray vibrations would crawl faster through the marsh, afraid the silence would eat them. The swarm-things would enter the dim places, testing for new voices to divide. The still-pool reflections would copy the dusk imperfectly, showing what was not there: doors that opened into other basins, roads leading downward to ordinary towns, bodies that could sleep.

But most would feel, for the first time, that their preservation was not the same as their life.

Evening would teach them that to continue is not always to be good, and to stop is not always to be destroyed.

And when the light returned—soft at first, then bright as a lamp behind glass—the city would not be the same.

The held roar would still be there.

But it would be held breath.

And breath, unlike a roar, can be given back.

### III. The Second Gift Would Be a Threshold

There were dangers in the city, and they were not evil.

The root-network mouths were hungry for sustained sound.

The swarm-things fed on certainty.

The pool-folk copied with delay, until it became difficult to know which was citizen and which reflection.

The mineral grazers ground old noise into silence, leaving smooth plates.

These were the city’s ways of balancing.
