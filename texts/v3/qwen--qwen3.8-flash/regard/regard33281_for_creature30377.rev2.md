## What They Would Do

They would not, at first, save the Root-Cell.

Not because they are cruel. Not because they are indifferent. Not because they see the place as insignificant, or the builders as too small, too damp, too dark to merit attention. They would not save it in the loud, obvious way that power imagines goodness when it has never had to live in a wet room the size of a held breath.

They would know, with wisdom, that rescue is often only another name for erasure.

To save the Root-Cell as a surface person would save a lost kitten would be to carry it into light. And light is not always kindness. Light dries moss. Light makes eyes lazy. Light teaches the hand to stop asking. It makes the dark seem like a failure rather than a medium, a shelter, a discipline.

If the benevolent power were wise, it would begin by refusing the most common forms of benevolence.

It would not bring sun.

It would not bring fire.

It would not bring metal tools, or wheels, or looms, or written language, or gods with faces, or sky with stars. It would not make the hollow larger, because the hollow’s largeness is its meaning. Its intimacy is its architecture. The builders can touch both the dwelling and the outer wall not because they are primitive, but because the place fits inside them.

It would not make them immortal.

It would not make all pain optional.

It would not remove every predator, every hunger, every mistake, every death. Because the Cell already has learned how to hold these things. The dead become structure. The traps remember abundance and scarcity. The cord learns how much strain it can bear. The children learn the edge by standing near it, not by being lifted above it.

But the power would not stop there either.

Wisdom without benevolence can become an elegant refusal to touch the wound. It can sit above the Cell and say: all harm is pedagogical; all suffering is pattern; leave the world alone. That is true, and too cold. Benevolence without wisdom becomes the hand that picks up the child before the child has learned to climb, and then wonders why the child has no legs.

So the power would do something. But carefully.

It would act as if it were the water.

That is, it would not act as a person acting, exactly, but as a condition adjusting. A pressure changed by one part in a thousand. A taste in the mineral stream. A root guided by a half-degree. A spore-light blooming for the width of a breath. A cold patch moving just enough to warn. A dampness staying just enough to preserve.

If it were truly benevolent, it would prefer to do nothing that could be credited. It would not create a religion. It would not found a priesthood. It would not teach the builders to wait for signs from above. It would make its help indistinguishable from patience.

And what would it do?

First, it would help the Cell remain the Cell.

The root-shelf above is young. The crack in the turf is sharp. The hollow was made by a wound, and wounds can reopen in ways that are not healing. So the power would, over many seasons, adjust the root-shelf very slightly. Not enough to make it unchanging. Not enough to let the builders feel safe without attention. But enough to prevent a collapse that would crush a sleeping shelf before the child who slept there had learned the difference between firm root and false root.

It might send one thread of mineral through the water table so that a patch of peat beneath a platform grows more cohesive, not hard, but resilient. The builders would say the peat “held” that year. They might say, in tap and breath, that the felt remembered warmth.

It might cause one crack to admit not a flood of surface dirt but a dust fine enough to feed algae. Then a drying rack would smell of iron and growth, and a trapwright would notice that the shrimp were thicker than usual, and no one would ask why the water tasted different for three days.

It might prevent a poison bloom in the pool caused by a dying tree far above, not by killing the tree, but by letting a hidden current carry calcium through a root-channel so that the poison is softened before it enters the Cell. The builders would find their traps full of strange shells, then return the following season. They would think the water was generous. They might think the water was dangerous. They would be right about both.

The power would not remove danger. It would remove only what is too cruel to be a teacher.

There would be years of ice in the tiny mouths of roots. The cold is part of their life, but some cold is just an accident. The power might make the ice in certain channels form as porous blue needles rather than a solid seal, so the water below can breathe during a long winter. No builder would notice directly. A blind shrimp would not die beneath the lowest platform. A clutch of eggs would survive because the film over them remained permeable. The next spring, the root-children would come up in great numbers, and the elders would tap the water’s edge twice, meaning: enough. Not too many. Do not take all of them.

The power would be glad that the elders knew to say that.

Some nights, the builders would hear the settlement talking to itself. Water plips off leaves. A bubble slides through mud. A reed joint tightens. Sometimes a sound is just a sound. Sometimes the power would adjust one sound very slightly so that an old channel-reader, whose hands had grown too tired to test every drain before sleep, would wake before a hidden rise.

She would not hear a voice.

She would hear that the pool had changed pitch.

She would press one palm into the wall and feel the water leaning there, not rushing, but waiting. Then she would tap the platform rim: once, then twice, then the small quick pattern that means *not the upper shelf tonight*. Someone would answer from below. Ladders would shift. Sedge mats would be raised. A sleeping infant would be carried a hand’s-width higher. By morning, the water would lap where the cradle had been, and everyone would say the Cell had breathed.

No one would say the power had breathed.

Good.

If the power were truly wise, it would not want to be thanked. Thankfulness is a chain when it is directed to someone who expects it. The builders have no sky to thank, and their gods—if the word is to be made wet enough—must remain inseparable from the place itself.

But sometimes there would be an individual being who needed more than the general adjustment of conditions. The prompt says *the being who lives in it*, and that is not merely a population. There is always one. There is always a body, a hand, a breath.

Perhaps it would be the smallest child, newly named because she finally held her thumb straight instead of curling around the moss. Perhaps it would be the eldest felt-mender, whose joints have begun to ache when the water rises too fast. Perhaps it would be the trapwright who cannot sleep for the fear that he has set a drain one notch too high and left a section of root to rot. Perhaps it would be the strange visitor who sits too still, too warm, and is mistaken by a midge for a wound.

For the being, the power would do little things.

It would make the cold enter her cloak less sharply on the night she must cross the rope-bound street alone. Not warm—warmth would be false. But less cruel. A thin layer of lichen-fur would hold a breath of air better than it had the night before. She would not wonder why. She would simply walk a little faster and arrive without her hands shaking too badly to tie the knot at the end.

It would make the moss under her slipper less slippery where the water film thinned at dawn. It would make one ladder rung not new, not safer, but exactly as sound as it needed to be for the moment she needed it. The wood had not healed because of prayer. The root had grown around the peg, and the power had only helped that root not choose the wrong direction during a season of swelling.

If she was hungry, it would not fill a trap with fish as a gift. That would teach her that hunger is interrupted by miracle. But it might let one luminescent snail cluster form near her storage niche, small enough that she notices them and thinks, *here is food, but it is too early to take it all*. Then she would wait. Waiting is her skill. The power would not make her wait; it would only make sure the waiting had a future.

If she was grieving, it would not dry her eyes or summon a sign from the beloved dead. It would not say the dead are with us in bright, consoling language. Instead, it might preserve the compression of a sleeping shelf long enough for her to press her cheek into the place where another body had rested. The felt would give slightly, then remember its shape. She would not hear this as grace. She would hear it as use.

If she was afraid, the power would not remove fear. Fear is too necessary here. But it might cause one small sound to become legible: the tick of chitin under the moss, the faint click of a stalker-spiders moving away rather than toward her. She would understand the difference by touch alone, as the builders do, and she would not need to be brave to move through the dark. She would simply move.

If she was angry, and anger made her hands too hard, the power would not seize her limbs. That would be tyranny. But a cord might catch, a wet root might be tougher than expected, and her strike would meet fiber instead of skin. Not because she was punished, but because the place gave her back the edge she had forgotten.

If she was cruel to another, the power might not stop her immediately. There are wounds that can be prevented and wounds that must be felt by the one who inflicts them so they can be learned. But it would protect the weaker if the cruelty were sudden, total, or repeated without consequence. It might do this without revealing itself as a protector. The cruel hand might slip. The path might become inconvenient. The drying shelf might sag at exactly the wrong moment so the cruelty cannot be performed. The builders would call it bad weather, or wet luck, or a root that did not hold.

No moral system would depend on a visible god. That would make goodness a transaction. The Cell’s ethics would remain consequence, reciprocity, scale, attention.

The power would not be worshipped, because worship is too large a gesture for a place this careful. If a builder began to thank something invisible too often, the power would not encourage it. It would redirect the gratitude toward maintenance.

Thankful builders do not kneel.

They clean the drains.

They leave traps open.

They scrape parasitic algae.

They test the cord before trusting it.

They carry the dead to the drying racks.

They teach children to know the edge.

That is the only prayer available there, and the power would prefer that.

It would help the builders keep their own hands busy, not their mouths full of praise. It would make the act of caring easier than the act of worship. It would be content to be mistaken for patience, for dampness, for the long memory of roots.

And over time, the Cell would feel less abandoned—not in a bright way, not in a sky-way, but in the way a place feels when someone knows its exact need and does not ask for thanks. A ladder rises because the water rises, and the builders know to adjust it. A trap glows faintly, and the shrimp come. A wall is sealed with pulp, and the wound does not deepen. A child is born with fingers that can tell cold from wet, and the elders tap twice: yes, this one will learn.

The power would not rewrite the birth.

It would not make the child special by miracle. It would only make the world less likely to destroy the child before the gift could grow.

If the power were wise, it would understand that some small things are complete because of their limits. The Root-Cell cannot be made infinite without becoming something else. It is already enough. The builders already have the knowledge of touch, of water, of shared darkness, of death feeding the shelf. If the power made the Cell perfect—dry, bright, safe, permanent—it would murder it.

So the power would practice restraint so fine it would seem absent.

It would let one year be harder than the next, because abundance can teach carelessness. It would let a platform fail where it had been neglected too long, so the lesson of living architecture remains alive. It would let a predator take a too-nocturnal midge, or an unlucky fish, because predation is the boundary that keeps the city’s population honest. It would let grief enter, because grief is what love looks like when it has learned the shape of absence.

But it would not let avoidable catastrophe be mistaken for order.

If a surface storm above were to send too much dirty water into the crack, the power might widen one drain just enough before it matters. If a parasitic vine began growing toward the sleeping pouches, it might make one stalker-spiders path drift near the vine so the vine is eaten in time. If a builder’s fever threatened a season of neglect because the builder was the only one who knew how to taste the mineral change in the channel, the power might let a faint cool draft touch the forehead at the hour when the fever would otherwise harden.

And in each case, the builders would explain it within their own world.

*The water moved.*

*The root chose a better side.*

*The moss kept heat.*

*The pool was kind.*

*The old shelf remembered.*

That would be enough.

The power would not care to be correct about itself.

There might come a day when a child, in the deep dark, hears the settlement’s many taps and breathes too loudly, and asks—though in this culture the question would be felt, not spoken—whether something is behind the walls, beneath the water, above the crack. The child might feel that the Cell is not only a place but a patient attention.

The power would not answer.

To answer directly would make the child’s wonder depend on a presence, not on the place. It would teach the child to listen upward, outward, beyond, rather than learn the wet root, the cold gradient, the texture of trust. The builders already have too much to learn that is native to them. They do not need to learn the power’s name. They need only learn how to live without crushing the spider in the wall, without wasting the glow, without making a rigid thing where the Cell demands a joint.

If the child’s question remained, it would become part of their ecology.

They would say: the dark has a hand.

Not a god-hand. Not a giant-hand. A small, wet, careful hand that adjusts without taking.

And that would be true.

What, then, would the benevolent power ultimately be doing?

It would be doing nothing that could be pointed at.

It would be a change of one degree in the water table.

It would be a mineral trace that makes one moss hold its color a little longer.

It would be a fungal thread that blocks a root-rot before it reaches the lowest platform.

It would be a sound in the pool that wakes the channel-reader before the flood.

It would be a warmth in the sleeping shelf that lets a fever break without the fever becoming a lesson in cruelty.

It would be a cold patch that keeps the midges away from stored eggs.

It would be a stone shifted by the smallest possible pressure so a child’s knee does not tear open.

It would be a root that turns away from a cradle.

It would be a glow under a stranger’s foot that is not light but memory of direction.

It would be a trap that is left open by chance, or intention so hidden that chance seems the better explanation.

It would be all of these and none of them, because it would not stand apart from the Cell as its owner. It would be within the Cell’s grammar, within its wet logic, within the slow negotiations between wound and root, body and water, death and shelf, hand and wall.

The power would help them remain themselves.

It would help them to be enough, but not alone.

It would help the builders not by giving them what they do not have, but by protecting the conditions in which what they do have can continue. Their touch. Their patience. Their taste for water. Their memory of edges. Their refusal to make a clean pool. Their willingness to live in a crack without calling it a tomb.

And if the power were asked, hypothetically, by some distant mind watching it do this: *Why not do more?*

It would answer:

Because more would require less of them.

Because more would make the dark a failure rather than a discipline.

Because more would make the builders grateful, dependent, visible, and therefore unsafe.

Because more would make the Cell a museum of benevolence rather than a living place.

Because the highest kindness in such a place might be to remain so quiet that no one can distinguish it from the water, the root, the moss, the cold, the patience of small things doing the work of survival.

And if it were asked: *Would you do this forever?*

It might say: no. Not forever. That would make it a habit.

It might say: yes, whenever avoidable suffering crossed the line where meaning becomes meaningless.

It would not choose by sentimentality.

It would choose by attention.

It would watch for the places where life is not being taught but merely extinguished. Where a root grows not as a challenge to be negotiated but as a blade waiting over a cradle. Where a channel dries not into scarcity but into collapse. Where a being is so exhausted that the Cell’s lessons become noise. Where danger stops being a teacher and begins to devour the student before the student can speak.

Then the power would adjust.

Then it would withdraw.

Then it would let the builders return to the ordinary miracle of their hands.

A bridge bends.

A ladder rises.

A platform accepts a body.

A trap glows.

A name is tapped into wet reed.

A child presses one palm to the wall and feels the whole settlement answering with the smallest possible shiver.

From the outside, nothing would seem to have happened.

From inside, something would have survived.

That is what they would do, if they were benevolent and wise.

They would be so good that no one would be able to name their goodness without naming the Cell itself.

They would be so wise that their help would look like the place working.

They would be so careful that the builders, in the end, would not ask who saved them.

They would only say, in the language of touch and water:

The wound healed.

The root bent.

The shelf held.

The pool remembered.

And in that remembering, a small hand found the edge.
