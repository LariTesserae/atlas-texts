What They Would Do, If Anything

If they were benevolent and wise, they would begin by refusing the obvious mercy.

A kindness without understanding would see the dry basins and say: *Let there be water.*  
It would see the miles without tracks and say: *Let there be feet.*  
It would see the sealed bulbs and say: *Let one of them open.*  
It would see the absence of faces and say: *Let it be loved by a face.*

This would be a violence dressed as tenderness.

The power would arrive, perhaps, under the ordinary sun, because the ordinary sun is enough there. They would stand at the rim of the high country and feel the warmth come through the thin sealed air like light through glass. They would carry their own moisture, their own wetness, their own memory of rain and sweat and breath, and they would leave those things outside.

Not because water is bad.

Water is not bad. Water is merely a law.

In the lower world—if there ever was one—water would be the beginning of hunger, because in water things must eat and be eaten. It would be the beginning of softness, because soft bodies need shelter from the world. It would be the beginning of death, because the wet world is a world of decay and replacement, of rot returning to growth, of bodies failing and bodies being rebuilt.

But this country is not the wet world wearing another costume. It has solved a problem by a different mathematics.

It is alive without thirst.

It is full without flood.

It is industrious without engine.

It is lush without leaf-softness.

It has no predators because no predator is required.

It has no prey because no body is made to be broken into.

It has no cruelty because it has not invented the mouth.

A wise power would see that and not call it lack.

That is the first miracle of wisdom: to recognize an absence that is not an emptiness. The high dry country has no birds, but it is not silent because birds are missing; it is silent because its life does not require wings. It has no streams, but it is not barren because streams are not a condition of being. It has no animals, but it is not empty because it is already populated by relation, interval, charge, crystal, heat, and form.

A benevolent power might still feel the pull to give something, but a benevolent and wise power would ask first:

*What would help?*

And then, after long listening:

*What would harm?*

Because sometimes the harm is in the very thing that would seem to heal.

They would not make it into a story.

No story would be imposed there, because story requires sequence, and sequence requires loss. It would require a before that is gone, an after that has not yet come, and a character that remembers. This country is not without time, perhaps, but time does not move there in the animal way. It does not hurry. It does not mourn. It does not accumulate.

They would not give it memory.

Memory would be a wound shaped like knowledge.

They would not give it a future.

A future would be a promise it did not need to keep.

They would not give it a past.

A past would be the death of its present, which is not a present but an enduring threshold.

They would look at the metal leaves holding their tension, the salt flats humming with their slow charge, the glassy mats wearing the basalt like velvet livery, the bulbs closed as completed equations, and they would not say: *Make it richer by adding what I know.*

They would say, perhaps, more quietly:

*It is already rich by not needing what I know.*

If they were only benevolent, they might create a rainstorm.

If they were only wise, they might do nothing at all.

If they were benevolent and wise, they would come close to doing nothing—and then perhaps do the smallest possible thing.

Not because the place needs it.

But because love sometimes insists on leaving one soft trace of itself, even where it cannot be used.

---

They would first make themselves dry.

Not by removing water from the world, but by refusing to bring the world’s habits into this place. They would come without clouds, without dew, without the pressure of moisture, without the scent of wet earth. They would come as if they had learned to hold their own breath inside themselves until breath became only charge, not vapor.

They would stand in the open and let the ordinary light cast its ordinary shadow.

They would be careful not to crowd the spacing.

That would be their first act of respect.

The country is a commonwealth of intervals. A shrub at one mile, another at the next. A basin of glassy mats where the chemistry permits. A field of salt tiles where the charge collects. The distances are not desolation; they are grammar. To make the place more crowded would be to erase its syntax.

So the wise power would choose a bare place.

Not near the copper shrubs, where their tubers hum like sealed reactors.

Not near the iron-black tufts, where static gathers in dry tension.

Not near the bulbs, which ring only once for a reason no one must understand.

They would choose a place of emptiness that was already meaningful.

And they would stand there long enough to become another presence at the correct distance.

They would not announce themselves.

They would not make themselves a face.

They would not put out eyes toward it, because no face meets you there. If they looked at it, their looking would be indirect, like sunlight reflected off a metal leaf—present, but not aimed.

The fronds might tremble by a fraction.

The salt might change tone.

The hum of the flats might rise by a degree too small to name.

The bulbs might answer without being touched.

The power would not take that as permission to stay.

They would understand: *This is not recognition.*  
They would understand: *This is only relation.*

If they were wise, they would leave before the country began to feel them as foreign.

That is the hardest discipline of benevolence: to withdraw before love becomes a disturbance.

---

They would consider whether to speak.

They could have spoken. A wise and benevolent power can form words from pressure and heat, can make syllables of light fall on basalt, can give a ridge the appearance of a sentence if it chooses.

But they would not speak aloud.

Sound in this country belongs to resonance, not command.

The hum of the salt flats is not a voice calling someone.

The tick of basalt plates is not a word requiring reply.

The crackle of a touched metal leaf is not speech; it is the place acknowledging difference.

So the power would not add speech where there is already language made of interval and chemistry.

Instead, if they felt the need to acknowledge, they would do it in the only way possible without violation:

They would listen.

Not as a being who expects answers.

Not as a god who demands worship.

Not as a traveler seeking meaning.

But as a dry presence in a dry place, learning the shape of a silence that is not dead.

And perhaps, if wisdom has a kind of humility, the power would learn from the land.

Learn that abundance need not be wet.

Learn that industry need not be made of hands.

Learn that life need not chase to sustain itself.

Learn that perfection is not a prison when it is chosen as form, and that a thing can be alive without movement if it is alive in the right way.

This too would be a gift, though not a gift given to the country.

It would be a gift received from the country.

---

Then, if anything more than that, the benevolent and wise power might make one small adjustment.

Not a rescue.

Not a filling.

Not a gift of water, animal, eye, or death.

One hairline alteration, so slight that it would not disturb the equilibrium.

They might touch a bulb.

Not with greed.

Not to open it.

Not to see what is inside.

Only to acknowledge its sealed completeness.

The bulb would ring.

The field would answer with its soft metallic shimmer.

The iron mats would change tone.

The copper blades would tilt by fractions.

The salt plains would tremble without visible motion.

The ordinary sky would not darken.

No storm would gather.

No rain would fall.

The power would withdraw their hand.

But afterward, that one bulb—or perhaps another, one not near to any human path—would hold a seam.

A hairline seam.

Not enough to admit water.

Not enough to admit air.

Not enough to admit decay.

Only enough to make a possible event.

A place where, if the whole commonwealth ever decided it wished to become other, the becoming could begin without being forced.

A door that was not a door.

A crack that was not a wound.

A permission without instruction.

A future that could remain unborn.

This would be the only thing they would do if they did anything at all.

Because benevolence without wisdom creates dependency.

Wisdom without benevolence creates distance.

But benevolence joined to wisdom creates possibility without imposition.

The place would not be made to want.

The place would not be made to choose.

The place would remain what it is: a commonwealth of dry living forms, holding its interval without hunger.

But somewhere, if the country ever wished to alter itself, there would be one line where its own first form could be answered differently.

Not because the land was incomplete.

But because completeness can include the right to open.

---

They would not build a monument.

No temple would rise beside the basalt shelves.

No shrine would mark the salt fan.

No image would stand among the copper shrubs.

No name would be carved into the hexagonal tiles.

That would be another violence.

The country has no need of the outside’s praise.

It does not require being named.

If the power named it at all, it would do so in a low register, close to the land’s own hum:

*The high dry country.*  
*The commonwealth of fit.*  
*The first form that holds itself.*

But that would not be for the place.

That would be for the power, if the power has memory.

Then they would leave.

They would leave as they came: without water, without footprints, without the smell of damp earth. They would take nothing, because the country has no waste to offer. They would break nothing, because nothing brittle is waiting to break. They would not look back with pity, because pity would be a lie. They would not look back with ownership, because ownership would be impossible.

When they left, the ordinary sun would still be ordinary.

The shadows would still fall where they should.

The sky would remain clear and blue.

The salt flats would hum.

The basalt plates would tick.

The bulbs would wait in their salt-crusted hollows like closed lanterns, bright and hard, without flame.

The metal leaves would keep their dry tension.

The fields would shimmer with their chemical light.

No animal would appear.

No rain would come.

No voice would speak.

The country would continue its quiet industry of form.

And perhaps—only perhaps—the hum would carry one note slightly different than before.

Not enough to call change.

Not enough to call wound.

Not enough to call need.

Just enough to be the place where a wise benevolence touched the world and then let it be.

If you were to stand there afterward, you would not know they had come.

You might feel that the spacing is slightly more exact, that one interval is deeper than another, that the silence has learned a new way of holding its own.

You might touch a bulb.

It would ring.

And the field would answer.

But the ring would be complete.

And the answer would remain.

Not because anything is missing.

But because everything, in its own dry and mineral way, has been heard.

And that might be enough.
