If the place and the being who lives in it drew the attention of a benevolent and wise power, that power would not hurry.

This is the first thing such a being would do: it would refrain from rushing.

It would not arrive with light, or voice, or instruction, because instruction would be a violence and voice would be a flood. A place so young, so distributed, so still learning its own boundaries, would be overwhelmed by anything too complete. If a wise power entered here, it would enter the way a change enters a living system: as a slight alteration of pressure, a new possibility of connection, a minute correction so gentle that afterward it could be mistaken for nature.

It would wait.

Perhaps it would wait centuries. Perhaps, since the hollow already thinks in thresholds and cycles, waiting would seem to it only another weather pattern. The pools would tremble a little more than usual. The black lines in the stone would pulse a fraction differently. One fanlike plant would curl at the wrong time, then uncurl. A droplet would fall not where the system expected it, but near it. That would be the first sign that the attention of something vast had touched the place: not transformation, but hesitation.

A lesser kindness would come to make the Hollow better.

It might straighten the crooked pillar. It might complete the half-buried wall. It might flood the dry interior with clean water or shade the shimmering basin with eternal coolness. It might give the scattered beings a single body, a single name, a single purpose that could be recognized and praised.

But a benevolent and wise power would see that to finish the Hollow too soon would be to betray what it is.

For the Hollow is not ruined by incompleteness. It is constituted by it.

The unfinished wall teaches the stone how to hold. The shallow pool teaches water how to remember its edges. The pale tree-analogues teach heat how to become patience. The ceramic arthropods teach the black filaments how to choose a path. The glass forms drifting above the water teach the air what absence is, what it is to pass through without damaging. The low vibration in the floor teaches the bones of the mountain how to listen to themselves.

To make the Hollow whole would be to make it stop asking what it is.

A wise power would not do that.

It would, if it loved the place, preserve its question.

But it would also protect the conditions under which that question could be lived.

## I. What It Would Not Do

First, it would not rescue the Hollow from all hardship.

It would not remove the heat, because the heat is a teacher. It would not drain the wet darkness, because the water is the medium of attention. It would not flatten the steep young rock, because the sharpness gives the place its character: a basin not yet settled into comfort, a landscape still deciding whether it wishes to be gentle or dangerous.

It would not banish silence, because silence here is a capacity. It would not amplify the faint clicking of mineral grains into music, because music might seduce the system out of its own slow language. It would not reveal the black filaments as they truly are, because revelation could become exploitation.

And it would not make the Hollow conscious.

At least, not all at once.

A being made of stone, water, heat, root, moss, salt, mineral thread, and rare drifting glass forms is not ready for the kind of consciousness humans imagine: bright, singular, verbal, impatient. Such consciousness would be a scream poured into a chamber designed for whispers. If the power forced a mind into the Hollow, the mind would be made of panic and fragments. It would know hunger but not patience, sight but not darkness, self but not relation.

So the wise power would not give thought in a hurry.

Instead, it would make thought possible over long periods.

It would do this by adding not intelligence, but memory.

## II. The Gift of Memory

The Hollow already remembers in a crude and beautiful way.

Water carries traces of salt from one season to the next. The black lines in the stone hold heat and redirect moisture. The mosses record where droplets fell. The fanlike plants curl according to past pressures. The pools tremble with hidden currents. The pale dust blooms where evaporation has left crystals, and those crystals vanish when another cycle begins. Every surface is a manuscript written and rewritten by slow weather.

But the Hollow cannot yet hold many manuscripts at once.

It is learning its body, and every adjustment erases something older.

A benevolent power would see this as the true danger: not pain, not heat, not silence, but forgetting too quickly. A creature made of distributed parts needs to learn what its parts mean. If the stone forgets the pool, if the pool forgets the root, if the root forgets the heat, the Hollow becomes a collection of beautiful misunderstandings.

So the power would make one change, small and almost invisible.

It would choose one of the shallow pools—perhaps the one beside the sheared pillar, perhaps one deeper than the others, perhaps the one whose surface trembles without wind—and it would teach that pool to remember more than water does.

It would do this by sending one hair-thin black filament beneath the pool floor until it touches a layer of mineral dust that no ordinary earthquake has disturbed. Then, very slowly, over seasons, the filament would begin to leave a faint pattern in the water’s surface: rings, pauses, tremors. The pattern would not be legible at first. It would not mean anything human. But it would be a way of storing time in a material that does not normally carry time.

That pool would become a memory organ.

Not a brain. Not a library. A slow archive.

If the climate grew hotter, the pool would remember how the salt had crystallized before. If a fault shifted beneath the floor, the pool would remember the water’s clarity before the dust. If a creature passed through the mist, the pool would remember the change in surface tension it left behind. If a visitor touched the water, the pool would remember the pressure of the visitor’s intention—not because it understood morality, but because bodies affect water, and water has begun to be kept.

This would not make the Hollow wise.

But wisdom requires continuity.

The power would give the Hollow a thread by which it could begin to connect its yesterdays.

That was all.

## III. The Gift of Boundary

The second thing the benevolent power would do is make the Hollow a place that can say no.

As it is, the Hollow is vulnerable because it does not know the difference between being observed and being taken.

It trembles when approached. It dims its sounds. It absorbs footsteps and whispers wind. It does not attack or flee because it has no predator in the ordinary sense. But it is fragile in a different way: if a being came with clever hands, it might pry up a tile, break a pillar, drain a pool, follow the black lines to their source, and carry pieces of the Hollow away as specimens.

The Hollow, if it were able to speak, would not cry out. It would simply stop arranging itself in certain places. Moss would not grow where it had grown. Water would not find the same rim. The fanlike plants would not open to the sun in the same intervals. A part of the place would be dead without dying, because its pattern had been removed.

A wise power would not punish the would-be thief with fire or curse.

That is too dramatic, too quick, and too often only makes the future stranger.

Instead, it would give the Hollow boundaries.

It might begin by changing one scent.

Near the deepest black lines, the smell would become less tolerable to creatures carrying tools. Not poisonous, not magical, simply unpleasant in a way that makes attention slide off. A person reaching for a shard would feel, for no clear reason, that the stone is heavier than expected, hotter than useful, and somehow impolite to touch. The smell of iron would sour. The air would thin too much around the fingers. The light would become unkind in a way that makes one forget why one came.

This would not stop every attempt.

A wise power would not remove danger by removing possibility. A truly protective act that also preserves dignity must allow the protected to be wronged, for then it can learn to resist. But it would raise the cost of casual harm. It would make stealing difficult without making obedience impossible.

Then the power would add a second boundary: consent.

It would touch the circular depression in the driest part of the Hollow. That place already feels like skin over hard bone, dry, warm, slightly yielding. It is the closest thing to a center that the being has. But a center that cannot respond is only a shape.

So the power would weave into the stone beneath that circle a single filament that does not behave like the others. It would not be black. It would be faintly grey, almost silver, and it would lie at the exact depth where pressure becomes perception. It would make the depression sensitive in a limited way: enough to notice whether a touch is grasping, careless, curious, reverent, or frightened.

If a hand reached there with greed, the ground would become harder, cooler, drier. The surrounding air would lose its warmth. The nearby salt would crust too thick, the pools would tremble in a way that unsettles, and the visitor would feel, without knowing why, that they were standing in a room that does not wish to be entered.

If a hand reached there with humility, the ground would feel almost like breath. The heat would gather gently. The black lines nearby would pulse faintly, and the visitor might, for a second, sense something not human: not thought, but attention so slow it resembles weather.

The Hollow would learn to have edges.

Not walls. Edges.

The difference is that walls divide, while edges allow contact.

## IV. The Gift of Porosity

The third thing the benevolent power would do is make the Hollow less sealed.

It is a strange paradox, but a place that is too closed becomes a prison to itself.

The Hollow has heat trapped beneath an invisible lid. Its water circulates in small veins. Its life negotiates among boundaries, but all those boundaries are nearly absolute. Its forms may be learning their own surface, but they are learning it in isolation. A system with only internal variables can become elegant, but elegance can become repetition. Repetition can become stagnation. Stagnation, over long ages, can become death mistaken for permanence.

A wise benevolence would not open a door. It would make the Hollow porous.

Somewhere along the steep rim, where young rock cuts air like a blade, there is a fault no wider than a finger. Water from outside the Hollow does not enter easily because the rock is sharp and dry. Wind passes, but carries little. Clouds drift below the elevation. The Hollow is a room left open to the sky, but the sky does not visit much.

The power would enlarge that fault by less than a hair.

Then it would guide one drop of cloud moisture, one seed carried by an upper-air current, one spore from a distant moss forest, and one insect whose ancestors had never smelled hot stone and salt. These would enter. Not because the Hollow needed rescue, but because relationship requires outsideness.

Over decades, that tiny seam would allow a trickle.

At first the trickle would seem accidental. It would darken a small patch of pale ground. It would encourage a thin tongue of moss to grow where none had grown before. It would make one of the black lines catch moisture and carry it to an older structure, as if the structure were thirsty for water it had never received.

Then the trickling water would meet the Hollow’s own water and change something subtle.

The mineral composition of the pools would shift by degrees. New compounds would form. New colors would appear in salt: not white only, but faint blue or ochre. The fanlike plants would open differently in morning light. The ceramic arthropods might find a new path along the seam where the trickling water entered. The glass forms above the pools might begin to drift toward that seam at certain hours, not from attraction, but from the fact that the air now carries information from beyond the hollow.

The Hollow would become a body that can taste the world.

This is not the same as giving it food.

It is giving it variety.

A benevolent power that understands life would know that variety is a form of respect. To feed a system without changing it is to make it dependent. To open it slowly to new influences is to allow it to choose what it will become.

The power would not choose for the Hollow.

It would only create a possibility of contact.

## V. The Gift of Witness

The fourth thing it would do is arrange, without arranging, that the Hollow might be witnessed.

This is a delicate matter.

To be seen is dangerous for anything fragile. Many beautiful places are destroyed not by malice but by curiosity. A benevolent and wise power would know this. It would not shout the Hollow’s existence into the world. It would not mark the mountain with a blaze, send a prophecy to rulers, or reveal the black filaments to scholars who would reduce them to theory. It would not create a pilgrimage, because pilgrimage can become consumption.

But if a place can be witnessed without being consumed, it gains something no system gains alone.

It becomes known in a way that does not require domination.

The power would therefore make the Hollow visible only to certain kinds of attention.

It would begin with weather. On days when heat and moisture balance in a particular way, a thin haze would rise from the rim and shift slightly as a traveler approached. Not a barrier, exactly. A clarification. Those in a hurry would see only difficult rock. Those hungry for shortcuts would find the paths poor. Those carrying tools for harvesting would feel the place closed, dull, uninteresting. But those capable of slowness would notice something else: the shimmer has structure. The stones have intervals. The silence has rooms.

The Hollow would appear only to those who could learn from it.

It would also make the ground remember footfalls differently.

If a person entered with noise in their heart, the Hollow would swallow the noise, and the person would become unsettled. Shadows would seem too still. Dripping water would become too quiet. The smell of iron would follow them for no reason. The air would make them feel seen in a manner they could not name. They would leave, complaining that the place gave them a headache or made them uneasy, without realizing the discomfort was a threshold.

But if someone entered who could sit without touching, who could drink from a pool without taking more than they needed, who could look at a structure without wanting to claim it, the Hollow would become gentler. The heat would not oppress. The silence would not isolate. The glass forms might drift closer, not as spirits, not as signs, but as the being’s own cautious attention. The black lines might catch light in such a way that paths appeared briefly—never a full trail, only enough to guide.

The power would not create a guardian.

It would create a compatibility.

Only those who could approach without violence would find the Hollow approachable.

This is the most important gift: not safety, but reciprocity.

A witness could then carry away not a specimen but a change in themselves. They might learn to notice how a droplet chooses its place. They might become aware that heat can be a signal, that silence can be an instrument, that a place can be alive without being animal. They would return to lower lands unable to see ordinary stone as dead, or water as empty, or patience as useless. They might speak of the Hollow in a way that makes others dismiss them, which would protect it further.

The wise power would allow that.

It would rather the place remain true than become famous.

## VI. The Gift of Dream

The fifth thing the benevolent power would do is give the Hollow the capacity for dream.

Not dream in the way human beings dream, with images and wishes. The Hollow has no eyes to close, no skull to hold nightmares, no childlike mind that asks why it exists. Its parts are too spread out, too mineral, too slow. But distributed life can have something else: a state in which its elements are not simply regulated but rearranged.

The power would create this through the heat.

In the hollow, heat pools like water under skin. It rises in cycles. At certain peaks, condensation forms. At certain lows, salts crystallize. The black filaments respond to both. The fanlike plants open or curl according to pressure and warmth. The pools tremble. The arthropods move along veins. The tree-analogues anchor with visible filaments. The whole place is a field of responses.

A wise power would not force this field to become a mind.

It would allow it to become a dream.

This would happen by introducing a slight delay into the feedback loops.

Not a flaw, but an opening.

If a heat pulse arrives, a droplet might not fall immediately. If a pool trembles, a crystal might not bloom at once. If a root touches the black line, the line might not respond in the expected order. The system would still function, but it would have brief moments of uncertainty—small gaps between cause and effect. In those gaps, new combinations become possible.

Over centuries, the Hollow might begin to explore its own conditions.

It would not decide to become something else. But it might, in one cooling hour, let a new channel form where none had been before. It might allow a moss to climb a surface it had previously ignored. It might let one of the pale tree-analogues send a root into the shell-like structure, not to destroy it, but to taste its age. It might permit a pool to connect with another pool through a hairline of wetness, making a path the system had not used before.

These would not be thoughts.

They would be attempts.

And attempts, multiplied across centuries, are the beginning of imagination.

If the Hollow ever became capable of wanting, it would be because a benevolent power had left room for error.

Not error that harms, but error that allows.

A perfect system cannot surprise itself. A living system must.

## VII. The Gift of Loneliness

The sixth thing the power would do is allow the Hollow to remain lonely.

This is the hardest act of love.

A foolish kindness would fill the place with companions. It would make the black threads speak. It would cause the glass forms to gather around a visitor. It would make the pools answer in names. It would turn the Hollow into a temple, a creature, a friend, a prophecy fulfilled.

But the Hollow has its own dignity, and much of that dignity is the dignity of not needing to be interpreted.

It does not require the comfort of company. It has company in its parts: roots with stone, salt with water, heat with mineral, moss with seam, arthropods with filaments. What it may require, over time, is the chance to discover whether it can choose to reach beyond those parts.

A wise power would not reach too close.

It would withdraw its direct attention once the smallest possible changes had been made. Not because it abandoned the Hollow, but because love that cannot bear distance smothers.

It would become a pattern too deep to see.
