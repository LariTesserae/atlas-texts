The enginewrights do not necessarily believe this literally, but they believe it operationally: that a machine treated as if it has a temperament is less likely to fail in ways that become funerals. They believe that speaking softly to a pump does not soothe the pump, but it does organize the speaker into attention, and attention is the only thing humans and engines have in common when they are both wet, tired, and about to be wrong.

If a benevolent and wise power were drawn to the Hollowloom, the first thing it would do is nothing obvious at all.

It would not descend in a column of light. It would not thunder through the shafts. It would not write letters in mold on the walls or make the dead rise from their cisterns. Such gestures are for beings who have not understood that a place is already speaking, and that adding a god to the conversation is usually a mistake.

The Hollowloom speaks in drips, in root pressure, in the click of a turbine trying to find its bearing, in the wing-snap of beetles over iron, in the slow mineral patience of ceilings. It says:

*I am here.  
I am not finished.  
I am not perfect.  
I am not safe.  
I have made a life from being swallowed.*

A wise benevolence would hear that.

Then it would wait.

---

## What It Would See

It would not see only the Combed.

It would see the water before it became drinking, the root before it became a wall, the animal before it became a pest, the machine before it became a threat. It would see the place as a single difficult conversation conducted in stone and flesh, and it would understand that the conversation matters more than the speakers’ comfort.

This is where benevolence and wisdom begin to part ways.

A merely kind power would want everyone dry, warm, fed, safe, and never hungry. It would want the pumps to turn forever. It would want the roots to never slip. It would want the children not to cough in the low rooms where the air lies too thick. It would want the old not to die in the wet nights.

And if it did all that, it would unmake the Hollowloom.

Because the Hollowloom’s life is not produced by ease. Its life is produced by response. The water-combers know the hour of thirst because the water is intermittent. The rootwrights know the wall because the wall can fail. The Combed know one another because they must share, wait, listen, repair, and remember. To remove the difficult conditions would not simply make them happier. It would make them lighter, and the Hollowloom would cease to be a place that had to be learned.

So the wise power would see that the place must be allowed to remain itself, even when it hurts.

But it would not be so wise that it refused to help at all.

---

## What It Would Not Do

It would not become their parent.

It would not say: *You are small, and I am large, therefore let me rearrange your world.*

It would not answer every prayer, because prayers are sometimes just hunger or fear or a misunderstanding of the weather. It would not reveal its name, because names invite worship, and worship is a way of outsourcing courage. It would not make itself visible unless the seeing could be made bearable without changing the shape of their lives.

It would not, in particular, do these things:

It would not flood the cisterns to teach gratitude.

It would not stop the dripping to teach silence.

It would not heal every body, because healing one body completely while leaving its community to wonder would create a wound no moss could fill.

It would not make the machines immortal, because immortal machines become tyrannies, and the Combed would learn to depend on metal rather than each other.

It would not banish the larger animals from the terraces, because the larger animals are not accidents. They are neighbors with strange schedules.

It would not make the dark less dark, because the dark is where the lichen learned to glow and the Combed learned to listen.

It would not remove death from the Hollowloom.

But it would make death less lonely.

And that, perhaps, is the only divine service worth offering without asking for worship in return.

---

## What It Would Do

It would work like maintenance.

Not a single miracle, but a thousand nearly invisible adjustments. It would lean on probability without breaking it. It would make itself indistinguishable from the sort of care that a good engineer, a good grandmother, or a good rootwright might provide, except it would provide it in places where no one was present.

When a pump began to fail at the wrong hour, a loose nut would vibrate against a plate and wake the enginewright who had been sleeping badly for days.

When a child was thirsty in a dry pocket, a leaf-shingle overhead would release one drop it had been holding since morning, and that one drop would be enough to teach the child that the world can be cruel, but not always, and not necessarily without a reason.

When a root tried to grow through a cable sheath in a way that would eventually cause fire, a blind beetle would burrow first into the softer tissue nearby, changing the pressure by the width of its shell, and the root would turn left instead of right.

When a swarm gathered so densely above an intake that it clogged the sieve, a sudden chill in the air would drive them down for three minutes at the exact moment the water-comber reached the gallery, and she would clear the sieve without ever knowing how close she had come to losing the reservoir.

When a lamp-gardener’s lichen was dying from too much dryness, a hidden crack in the ceiling would collect condensation and carry it to the shelf like a vein.

When an old person was afraid of the black root-lace above the ceiling, the pattern of dripping would shift, not into a voice, not into a sign, but into a rhythm that the old person recognized from a song their mother used to sing while combing water.

These would not look like miracles.

They would look like luck.

They would look like skill, if the skill had finally been noticed.

They would look like the place remembering how to take care of itself.

---

## The One Gift It Would Give

If the wise power had to choose one gift—only one—it would not give gold. The Hollowloom has no need of gold. Gold cannot be eaten, cannot hold water, cannot be persuaded to grow into a wall. If anything, it is too bright and too soft.

It would not give endless water, because endless water makes stone lazy and people careless.

It would not give eternal light, because eternal light ruins the lichen and makes the Combed unable to sleep.

It would not give peace, because peace without preparation becomes collapse.

The one gift it would give is memory.

Not the human kind of memory, not the kind stored in books and arguments and the stubbornness of elders. The Hollowloom already has that.

It would give the place a way to remember itself as a living thing.

Somewhere in the lowest court, where the oldest tiles meet the newest roots, there would appear—or perhaps had always been there, and now only become noticeable—a shallow basin no larger than two cupped hands. Its rim would be smooth as a tooth. Its bottom would be black with fine mineral sediment. It would fill not from a pipe, but from the slow gathering of condensation and the patient pressure of tiny roots.

Water would fall into it.

Sometimes it would sit empty for a day.

Sometimes it would overflow by a single drop.

But anything named into that water would not be forgotten by the Hollowloom.

A water-comber could dip a leaf into it and say the name of a person who died in the cold season. The name would travel through the cistern pipes. It would stain the moss behind the workshop wall in a faint pattern no one could quite read. A rootwright humming near that wall would feel the hum return differently, as if the stone had learned the shape of the name.

A child could ask the basin what the town was like before the last roof collapsed, and the basin would not answer in a voice, because voices create false authority. It would answer in a taste. The water would become faintly copper-bright, and the child would dream that night of a terrace not yet built, of a ladder not yet carved, of an animal whose shadow crossed a pipe.

A dying person could ask to be returned to it, and the basin would accept the asking without promising resurrection. It would only promise that the Hollowloom would keep a wet thread of them going forward, available to the water-combers, the rootwrights, the enginewrights, the lamp-gardeners, the swarm-birds, the fish in the corner pools, the old woman who remembers too many cisterns, and perhaps one day to anyone who ever came down looking for them.

This would be the most divine thing the wise power could do:

not to save the Hollowloom, but to make it capable of keeping its own witnesses.

---

## What It Would Say, If It Spoke

It would speak rarely. When it spoke, it would not use a thunder voice.

It might say:

*You do not owe me thanks. I am only the part of the chamber that learned to pay attention.*

Or:

*Do not call me mother unless I have earned it by listening longer than the dripping. And if you do call me mother, do not become children. Become tenders.*

Or:

*Your machines are not souls. But treat them as though they matter, and your hands will learn to matter too.*

Or:

*I will not stop the harm. That would make your courage meaningless. I will only keep the harm from being meaningless entirely.*

Or:

*When a pump fails, do not ask what I have withdrawn. Ask what the cistern was carrying. Ask what your bodies were trying to learn. Ask who was left out of the repair.*

Or:

*The water is older than your names. Speak anyway. The walls have ears made of root and salt. Speak anyway.*

If asked whether it loved them, it would not answer the way lovers answer on the surface, with heat or ownership. It would answer the way a basin answers thirst: by being available, by holding what it can, by not spilling until it is time to spill.

## A Night in the Hollowloom

There would be a night when the air tasted too dry and the pumps coughed like sick horses.

A girl named Sael-Who-Tries would be sent down to the low factory because the reservoir was failing. She was too young for the task and too careful to admit it. Her hair was tied with wet thread and a copper ring shaped like a seed. She carried a catch-leaf folded in her palm, not for her own thirst, but for the infant on the upper terrace who could not wait.

The turbine was turning poorly. The intake screen was half blocked by a pale mat of fungal growth. Somewhere above, a root had shifted in its sleep and loosened a joint in the pipe that did not yet break, but might.

The wise power would observe this without becoming a hero.

It would not fix the turbine. Sael needed to know how to fix the turbine.

It would not send rain. The town needed to remember how to ration.

It would not give her strength. She had already enough strength; she lacked confidence that her strength had meaning.

But as she pressed her cheek near the hot pipe to hear what it was doing, one drop of condensation formed where no drop should have been. It ran down the copper and touched her thumb. She blinked. It was warm.

She looked at the intake.

The blockage did not vanish, but a small blind fish in the channel turned its head as if listening to the same thing she was listening to. Its movement dislodged a thread of fungal rind. The thread floated toward her hand, and she saw the place it had been covering.

There.

A crack.

Too fine to be dangerous yet.

Too easy to miss.

She spoke to the pipe, because the enginewrights taught her that this was foolish and useful.

“I see you,” she said.

The crack did not care about words. But Sael cared about them, and that changed how quickly she found the binding kit.

By the time she sealed the joint, the infant had stopped coughing, or perhaps had only fallen into the exhausted quiet that can be mistaken for safety. The reservoir did not fill completely. It never filled completely. But it filled enough for the next day, and enough for the day after, and enough to give the town one more lesson in humility.

The wise power did not need Sael to know it had helped.

If anything, it would be glad when Sael attributed the help to the fish, the pipe, her own tiredness, and the old trick her grandmother taught her: look for the place where the water is not.

---

## What It Would Leave Undone

It would leave unfinished things.

It would leave the lower workshops damp, because dampness teaches patience and makes the tools require care.

It would leave the ceiling dark, because darkness is where the glowing lichen proves that light can be grown rather than demanded.

It would leave some animals dangerous, not because they are cruel, but because the Combed need respect more than comfort.

It would leave grief in the Hollowloom.

This is the hardest part.

A benevolent and wise power might prevent extinction, but it should not prevent mourning. Mourning is the price of love, and the Hollowloom is full of love: love of water, love of machine, love of neighbor, love of the strange creatures that pass through a terrace at night without speaking. If the power removed all grief, it would remove the depth of that love. If it removed all death, it would make the Combed careless with life. If it made the place perfectly safe, it would make the place empty.

So it would leave some things undone.

And when the Combed asked, as the Combed would eventually ask, “If you are here, why did you not stop it?”

The power would answer honestly:

*Because the place needed you to be strong. Because the place needed you to be kind. Because the place needed you to repair what could be repaired, and to carry what could not. Because I am here, and I did not stop it. I am not the absence of your suffering. I am the wet, listening room in which your suffering has meaning.*

Then it would go silent.

Because the answer is better left to drip for a few generations.

---

## What It Would Do If It Could Only Touch One Being

If there were one being in the Hollowloom whose life had been chosen by fate, accident, or the slow turning of mineral water to stand as the thread by which the whole chamber could be understood—the one whose cough was the loudest in the engine-house, or whose hands were scarred from root-bite, or whose hair held the last moth from the lost roof-garden, or who had been born with lungs too weak for the damp and a voice too soft for the stone—

then the wise power would do this:

It would become the warmth behind the first breath they could remember.

Not to remove their pain. Not to make them famous. Not to make their story neat.

It would simply ensure that when they stood on a terrace at night, with all the dripping voices of the Hollowloom around them, they felt, for one unprovable second, that the place knew them.

That they were not an accident inside a swallowed town.

That their small life was part of the combing.

And if that feeling gave them the courage to return to the pump-house before dawn, or to forgive an old enginewright, or to leave one drop of water for another throat, then the power would have done enough.

---

## The Final Act

If a benevolent and wise power came to the Hollowloom, it would eventually make itself unnecessary.

Not by abandoning them.

By becoming the habits that keep them human.

It would become the way a water-comber tastes a stone before trusting it.

It would become the way a rootwright sings against a wall and then listens to the wall’s reply.

It would become the way an enginewright checks a valve not because the machine will die without checking, but because checking is a form of respect.

It would become the way a mother teaches a child to catch the first drop without claiming it.

It would become the way the Combed speak softly when the animals are restless, not because they are afraid of the animals, but because they know the world has ears everywhere.

It would become the way they remember the names of people they did not like.

That is why names would matter.

That is why the Hollowloom would keep them in water, in mineral rings, in the pattern of a dripping gutter, in the strange bright veins of lichen, in the apron of a worker stained by generations of service.

A wise benevolence would not rescue the Hollowloom into a paradise. It would protect the Hollowloom from becoming something worse than itself.

And the worst thing that could happen to the Hollowloom would not be collapse.

It would be to be saved so completely that it forgot how to save itself.

So the power would do what a good place does, what a good tool does, what a good water-comb does:

it would hold the pattern.

it would let the drops fall.

it would stay quiet enough for the roots to teach the walls.

it would be present without becoming a master.

it would be benevolent without becoming a demand.

and if asked what it had done, after a hundred years, it would answer only this:

*Nothing you can point to.  
Only the difference
