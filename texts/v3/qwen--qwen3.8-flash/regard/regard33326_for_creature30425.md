It is supported by the Dripfold’s wet tension. It steps across droplets, moss tips, and thin water films as if the surface were made of many small drums. The silt-strider leaves no track because it does not commit enough mass to any single point. Its weight is distributed across a network of legs, droplets, and reflected surfaces, and because of this it is difficult to say where the silt-strider ends. One leg may rest in a pool. Another may rest in the pool’s delayed echo. A third may stand in a reflection cast by a stone that is not actually beneath it.

If there is a being who lives in the Dripfold as a person lives in a house, then perhaps this is it. Not large. Not central. Not a creature that claims territory. But something that knows the land too well to be separated from it.

And when the benevolent and wise power noticed, it did not descend with fire, nor with commandments, nor with the soft terrible hand that saves one thing by breaking another.

It came as a change in the way echoes answered.

A drop fell from a high reed, struck a black pool, and the echo did not come from the water but from the moss behind the observer’s shoulder, a half-second before the observer turned. Or a silt-strider crossed a sheet of shallow water, and the water remembered the crossing too long afterward, showing not the animal’s feet but the places its feet had considered. Or one hanging droplet, which should have fallen straight, fell with a tiny sideways hesitation, as though listening to something outside the world.

That was all. A misgiving in the physics of attention.

A lesser power would have built a shrine at the edge of the Dripfold. It would have placed a stone with a carved name, as if naming were the same thing as understanding. It would have tried to make the basin safe by making it legible. It might even have given the silt-strider a home—a dry platform, a roof, a fixed boundary—because small minds think comfort is always the same thing as shelter.

But the wise power listened first.

It listened long enough to understand that the Dripfold was not a mistake.

This land had not been forgotten by creation. It was creation in a mode too delicate for ordinary hands. Its lack of roads, walls, ruins, and tools was not emptiness; it was a form of completeness that did not require human categories. The absence of artifacts meant that no authority had ever pressed its palm flat onto the stone and said, “Be here.” The basin was not primitive. It was uncolonized by mind.

The silt-strider’s difficulty was real, but it was not the kind of suffering that could be cured by separation.
