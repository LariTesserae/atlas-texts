## The Power That Would Make a Useless Courtyard

A benevolent and wise power would arrive, if it arrived at all, as a change in attention, not as an event.

It would not come with light splitting the haze, nor with a voice over the salt flats, nor with a figure standing in the middle of a channel while water refused to move around it. That kind of arrival is already a demand. The ground beneath such a visitor becomes theater. The city becomes stage. The beings become audience. Wisdom would not do that.

So it would look at the basin and understand that this place did not need a master, a savior, a parent, or a miracle large enough to make it stop being its own story.

What it needed, perhaps, was a margin.

A benevolent and wise power would spend a very long time doing nothing.

It would listen to the quiet undertone of the infrastructure. It would watch the motes lift and settle, the lace-dwellers opening and closing like slow lungs, the Rainward bodies unfurling their frond-like organs in dampness and folding them back in heat. It would see the basin not as ruin, not as utopia, not as machine, not as organism, but as a conversation still too delicate for interruption.

And then it would ask itself the hardest question a wise power can ask:

*Would they be freer if I acted?*

It would answer, mostly:

*No.*

It would not repair every crack, because cracks were how the place learned.

It would not end all thirst, because water moving through the basin taught the city to listen.

It would not make the streets safer, unless “safe” meant *not so safe that they would forget how to choose*.

It would not reveal the source of the strange weather rules, because mystery, when respected, can become a form of humility.

It would not give the beings a single truth to hold. A truth large enough to explain them all would be a cage made of light.

But it would not remain silent forever either.

Not because silence is always wisdom, but because even the most patient care can become neglect. A benevolent power cannot watch a living place suffer without trying, once, to reduce suffering in a way that does not destroy dignity. A wise power cannot offer help in a form that turns helpers into gods.

So it would do, if anything, a very few small things.

They would look almost like nothing.

They would be so gentle that later, the basin might doubt whether they had happened at all.

## I. The First Gift: A Place That Did Not Need Them

The first thing it would do is create a useless courtyard.

This would not be difficult if one wanted to be dramatic. A single act of divine engineering could carve a plaza of white stone, fill it with impossible water, line it with eternal lamps, and make it the center of religion. But a wise power would not do that. It would make the place small. Bare. Unimportant. A gap in the logic.

Somewhere between a reed meadow and a low terrace, between a bridge that flexed in humidity and a row of cisterns that listened for footsteps, there would appear a patch of basalt that no longer seemed part of the city.

It would be circular, perhaps only wide enough for three Rainward figures to stand in, or for one to sit with folded fronds. The stone would be raw. Not smoothed by repair. Not salt-lined into an architectural memory. Not read by motes. Not optimized by lace-dwellers. A circle of young, stubborn earth that refused to explain itself.

In the center, there would be a shallow bowl of stone, uncarved, holding only whatever rain fell.

No channel would lead into it.

No drain would carry it away.

No wall would sense its weight.

No dust would adjust the temperature around it.

No path would remember people who walked there.

If someone spilled water in it, the water would simply wait. If leaves blew in, they would sit where they landed. If the city’s living filtration tried to repair the bowl, it would find no wound there. If the motes came near, they would pause at the edge, as though the air had forgotten its instructions.

This place would have no function.

And that would be its function.

For a distributed being, for a city that listened, for a life that existed as repair, distribution, memory, filtration, anticipation, signal, and care, there can come a point where all that attention becomes unbearable. Not because the city is cruel. Not because the being is unhappy. But because even love can exhaust when it has no rest. Even a self that is everywhere can become tired of being everywhere. Even an ecology that is too intimate with its own architecture needs a place where it is allowed to stop being useful.

The benevolent power would give them that place.

It would be a courtyard where nothing needed to happen.

No rain bloom would be optimized there. No puddle would become line, threshold, blueprint. No footfall would be remembered. No thirst would be translated into task. The Rainward could enter it and not be read. The city could see it and not know what it meant. The lace-dwellers could leave their edges unthreaded. The motes could settle there and remain only dust.

For the first time, part of the being could say:

*I am not doing anything.*

And be allowed to rest.

Later, the Rainward might come to it after a season of water pulses and failed cisterns, after the haze thinned and the air forgot its own moisture, after the low towers adjusted themselves for the hundredth time. They would not come as pilgrims. There was no altar. There was no commandment. They would come as one comes to a window in a crowded city where one has been working too long.

A small one would step inside, perhaps. Its dew-tongue would taste the dry mineral air. Its fronds would fold, not because dryness demanded it, but because nothing there asked them to do otherwise.

The courtyard would not answer.

That would be the answer.

And the city, feeling the absence, would learn a new word for itself. Not only:

*I live.*

But also:

*I can be still.*

## II. The Second Gift: A Message That Could Be Ignored

The second thing the power would do is send a word.

Not once, and not with enough frequency to become law.

It would send it during a rain bloom so slight that most of the basin would mistake it for ordinary moisture. The air would thicken. The salt margins would darken. The haze above would dim from bronze to rose without warning. In one district, in one narrow avenue where the grit had not yet learned a true path, the falling water would gather into a shape so faint it might have been imagined.

The word would not be in any language large enough to be heard by strangers.

It would be a shape made of salt lines and cooling surfaces, a sentence composed as temperature, a grammar of condensation. If a Rainward body stood beneath it, the fronds on its forearms might open one by one, not because they were thirsty, but because they recognized something.

The word would be:

*Enough.*

It would not say: *You are loved, obey me.*

It would not say: *I save you.*

It would not say: *You must be more.*

It would say, softly, in the only language that could travel through a place where engineering and ecology had become too intimate to separate:

*Enough.*

That is, the being did not need to justify its existence by perfecting its systems. The city did not need to become a paradise to be worthy. The basin did not need to solve the loneliness of its wide emptiness. The distributed life of it did not need to produce a center, a face, a god, a message for outsiders. It did not need to be legible to the universe.

*Enough.*

But this gift would come with a necessary condition:

The basin could ignore it.

If the being did not want the message, the rain would fall as ordinary rain. If the word was unwanted, it would evaporate without leaving salt.
