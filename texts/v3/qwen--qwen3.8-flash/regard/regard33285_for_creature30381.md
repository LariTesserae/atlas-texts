The worms scrape too deep, the film seals, the pressure changes, the channel narrows, the current learns a new path. A flange becomes a cliff, a conduit becomes a valley, a maintenance ladder becomes a migration route. The trench is being eaten and being remade at the same rate, and this balance is so delicate that no animal yet has the eyes to notice it.

There is, therefore, something like a being in this place.

It is not one body, not yet. It is the film threading the expansion seams and the salt-slicked alloy. It is the pulse of the ballast anemones reading the intake rings. It is the procession of number-hermits tapping plates against one another as if counting pressure, rhythm, and time. It is the blind insulation worms writing their small alphabets through conduits. It is the rain falling from condensation plates onto the young sea, and the sea receiving that rain without a shore.

Perhaps, if there is one place where this being begins, it is on a low submerged flange stamped with letters half worn away: **7-THAL**. A single number-hermit has lived there so long that its shell has fused with the plate. Its legs are grey as old boltheads. Its plates have collected mineral film in colors like rust, dried blood, oxidized copper, and the bronze of oil under cloud. It is not a philosopher. It is not even a person. But when the sea’s first waves pass over it, the film around it brightens faintly, as though the trench had learned a new word.

If a benevolent and wise power looked upon this place—if such a power could look, and not merely know—its first act would be restraint.

That is the hardest act for any power.

It would not come down in light that the false sky could imitate. It would not speak through the maintenance intercoms, nor write its name across the ceramic plates, nor teach the hermits to worship, nor give the anemones voices, nor make the first waves obey a human idea of beauty. It would not reach into the young sea and say, *Live without pain*, because that would be kindness mistaken for ownership. A made ocean does not need a god on top of its existing engineers. It needs time. It needs conditions so subtle that they seem like luck.

It would do very little.

It would do almost nothing.

And what it did, it would make invisible.

First, it would listen. Not as a human listens, waiting for language, but as a stone listens to rain, as a seam listens to pressure, as a circuit listens to the faint hum of current. It would learn the grammar of this place: the way water found the microgrooves, the way metal cooled after turbine cycles, the way mineral film spread toward salt and light. It would learn that the number-hermits were not only animals but also keepers of geometry. It would learn that the ballast anemones were not only predators but also filters, not only bodies but also small engines of current. It would learn that the worms were not only pests but also the sea’s way of reaching behind the walls, tasting what the engineers had sealed away.

It would understand that this being, if it was a being, thought in slow chemistry and pressure, in mineral film and tap-claw rhythm. It would know that to speak to it in a human voice would be to deafen it.

So the power would do the thing that love often confuses with absence: it would learn the shape of the creature before it offered help.

Then it would set a limit.

Not a law written on a tablet. Not a commandment carried on wind. A biological limit, a mineral limit, a current limit. The seam-film was beginning to eat too well. It sealed hairline fractures, yes, but in some places it was growing into conduits, narrowing flow, making dead zones where water could no longer reach. The power would not remove the film, because the film was learning, and the sea’s first memory was learning to be made of living matter. Instead, it would place a faint signal in the chemistry of the trench: when a growth became too dense, the film would stop sealing and begin to flake. When a surface became too choked, grazers would find an incentive to feed there. Not punishment. Not mercy. Balance.

This is what the wise power would teach the young sea.

**Enough.**

It would make **enough** taste a certain way in the minerals: sharp, thin, unsatisfying. Enough would feel like a current too fast to colonize. Enough would smell like oxygen. The hermits would learn it without knowing they were learning it. The film would grow to a boundary and find that boundary unappealing. The worms would tunnel too far and discover that the deep conduit was bitter. The trench would begin to develop what, in larger and later life, might be called ethics: not the choice between good and evil, but the understanding that any appetite can consume the house that houses it.

The power would not create this understanding from nothing. The sea would make it out of hunger, corrosion, flow, and death. The power would only tilt the chemistry by a degree. That would be enough. That would be wise.

It would also protect possibility.

There would be failures. There would be pump surges, pressure collapses, maintenance errors, sudden drains, or the slow poisoning of a thermal exhaust plume turning too hot. Some of these would be natural, in the way that a human world’s natural disasters are natural when humans make the world. The power would not remove all danger. Danger is the edge against which life learns its own shape. But it would prevent unnecessary extinction. It would not save every ledge. It would not make every flange safe. It would not guarantee that every number-hermit survive its first shedding. But if a nursery shelf was about to be burned by an exhaust vent, a current would bend a little.
