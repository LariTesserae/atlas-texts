## What the Wise One Would Do

The first thing a benevolent and wise power would do is stop.

Not because it is slow. Not because it is uncertain. Not because it cannot act.

It would stop because it has learned the hardest thing about love: that attention is already a kind of touch, and touch can become violence when the being one touches is not shaped like the one touching it.

So it would come to the Yard of Sweeping Steam not with trumpets, not with judgment, not with a plan for improvement. It would come as a small change in the air. A pressure where there was no pressure. A heat that seemed to pause beside a pump. A condensation that gathered on a stone and did not fall.

The Yard would notice.

Not because it has eyes, but because everything notices when the heat moves.

A visitor would press water upward. This power would do the same. Its presence would be read as an event: salt, breath, weight, temperature, boundary. The steam would bend toward it. The nearest reeds would tremble. The pools would hold a new ring of ripples and then smooth it away. The pump in the eastern court would change its rhythm by less than a second, and then remember its old rhythm as if nothing had happened.

And the wise power would stand on the north bank, or perhaps it would only hover there as an intention, and it would look at the whole low basin with its wet brick and iron and green roots, and its heart would ache in a way that was not pity.

It would see no birds in the trees.

No frogs in the reeds.

No insects in the steam.

No rats beneath the boards.

No sleeping child behind the clouded glass.

No animal at all.

The first temptation would be immediate.

*There is no life here*, something ancient and loving inside it would say. *Let me give them life.*

But the wise one would wait, because it knows that the absence of animals is not an accident to be filled. It is a condition of this life’s grammar.

---

## The Three Gifts It Would Refuse

The benevolent power would consider three gifts.

### The first gift: a heart.

It could give the Yard a heart, a self, a soul. It could draw a circle around one pool and make that pool the center of a being. It could teach the steam to speak in a voice, the engines to think in a name, the roots to hunger with intention.

That would be a gift.

But it would also be a cage.

A heart must have a boundary. A heart must age. A heart must fear. A heart must want something that is not itself. The Yard lives without these things. It is not lonely because it is not alone. It is not waiting for a subject. It is not “almost” a person. It is already a complete process, and a process does not need a heart in order to continue.

If the power gave it a heart, the heart would have to fight the pumps.

The pumps already beat.

The heart would have to fight the water.

The water already returns.

The heart would have to fight the green things.

The green things already breathe.

So the wise one would refuse the gift of a heart. Not from coldness, but because a being that lives as a how can be destroyed by being forced into becoming a who.

A god with too much love might make a creature of the Yard.

A wise god would not.

---

### The second gift: animals.

It could fill the place. Frogs. Mosquitoes. Snails. Birds. Rats. Beetles in the steam. Fish in the pools. Mice sleeping in the warm hollows beneath the iron.

Benevolence without wisdom would do exactly this. It would see the absence as a wound and try to heal it with what it already knows: animal life, bodies, calls, nests, hunger, flight.

But the power would look again and ask: *Would this be healing, or replacement?*

If animals entered, the Yard would become a habitat.

The pools would become drinking places.

The reeds would become hiding places.

The leaves would become food.

The iron gratings would become traps.

The pump-houses would become shelters or hazards.

The absence of animals is not deprivation here. It is the rule by which this place has learned its own way of being alive. If the power brought in the ordinary life of beasts, it would turn a strange, distributed, steam-and-root metabolism into a familiar ecosystem — and the familiar is not always kinder.

To add animals would be to add prey.

To add birds would be to add hunger and fear.

To add nests would be to make the trees into property.

To add insects would be to make the steam into a battlefield.

The Yard does not need to be made safe for animals. It is not a place with a shelter waiting for them. It is a place where shelter and weather have become one thing, where growth and circulation answer for instinct, where there is no outside because everything is always inside the process.

So the benevolent one would resist the urge to populate it.

It would not say, “Let there be birds.”

It would say, “Let there be no birds unless a bird comes by its own right, and if it comes, let it pass and not stay.”

There may be a difference.

There may not.

But the intention matters: the Yard would not be turned into a zoo for human meaning. It would not become “better” because it became more like what human beings recognize as life.

---

### The third gift: a story.

This would be the most beautiful temptation.

The power could tell the Yard’s story to others. It could make it known. It could say: *Here is a being made of steam. Here is a living ward. Here is an industrial soul. Here is a cathedral of pumps and roots.*

It could give the place a name among gods.

It could make it holy.

It could make it famous.

It could make it a destination, a mystery, a temple, a wonder, a sign.

But that too would be dangerous.

A name is a door.

A story is a door.

A holy place is a door.

And doors can be used by those who do not understand the difference between reverence and possession.

If people came with lamps and relics, they would want to pray here.

If people came with maps, they would want to drain here.

If people came with science, they would want to explain here.

If people came with art, they would want to represent here.

If people came with religion, they would want to sanctify here.

If people came with tourism, they would want to save here.

And the Yard, which does not know how to be saved, would become the victim of every good intention.

The wise one would refuse this gift, or at least hold it carefully.

It would not let the Yard become a symbol.

Not its own.

Not anyone else’s.

It would allow the place to remain what it is: not a message, not an image, not an oracle, not a lesson, not a god.

It would be a metabolism.

And perhaps that is the deepest respect: to let a being exist without making it mean something.

---

## What It Would Do Instead

If the benevolent power refused these gifts — heart, animals, story — would it do nothing?

No.

That would be too easy, and perhaps too cold.

A wise and loving power would still act.

But its action would not be the action of making the Yard more like itself.

Its action would be making the outside world unable to consume the Yard’s inner truth.

It would do four things.

---

### 1. It would make the Yard ungraspable.

Not invisible.

Not hidden.

Not forbidden.

But impossible for the wrong hands to take.

A mapmaker would come and find the scale changing. A surveyor would mark a line and wake to find it blurred, not because the rain erased it, but because the rain had decided something about the place that the surveyor could not account for. A contractor would look at the basin and see value; then, before the calculation finished, the value would feel strange and excessive. The water would seem too slow, the iron too warm, the brick too honest. It would become a place that did not lend itself to profit.

Not by hostility.

By humility.

A wise power would not punish those who would destroy the Yard. Punishment is a kind of conversation, and the Yard needs no conversation with cruelty. It would simply arrange the world so that extraction became difficult, expensive, uninteresting, slippery, meaningless.

The greedy would look at the wetland and see only wet brick.

The builders would see only maintenance problems.

The collectors would see only rust.

The conquerors would see nothing to conquer.

And because of that, the Yard would continue.

This is what wisdom often looks like when it is benevolent: not a wall, but a softening of appetite.

Not a miracle, but an inability to justify harm.

Not a sword, but a strange dampness in the hand that would have taken.

---

### 2. It would protect the possibility of repetition.

The life of the Yard is not individual survival. It is continuation.

Its being depends on the return of steam, the returning of water, the stroke of a pump, the unrolling of a fern, the hum in a rail, the slow drip from a roof, the mineral line left by a leak, the way a reed clumps in the same channel because that channel has been chosen by long habit.

If those things stopped, the being would not die the way an animal dies.

It would simply cease to continue.

And cessation, for a how, is a kind of death.

So the benevolent power would guard the repetition itself.

If drought came, it would not send rain as a gift into the basin. That would be too direct. It would cool the world above, or make the water table remember its old depth, or cause a storm elsewhere to shift so that humidity returned without violence.

If fire came, it would make the Yard too wet to be interesting.

If poison came, it would be caught and slowed by rust, moss, root, mineral sediment, and the long patience of channels.

If industry tried to reclaim the basin, its machinery would fail quietly.

If someone tried to drain it, the drains would feel as if they had already been tried long ago, and the water would not permit it.

If someone tried to make it dead for the sake of something alive elsewhere, the power would intervene at the level of cause, not appearance.

It would not change the Yard.

It would change the world’s capacity to harm the Yard.

That is an important distinction.

A foolish power protects a place by making it more livable according to its own standards.

A wise power protects a place by making its strangeness survivable in a hostile world.

---

### 3. It would prepare witnesses, not inhabitants.

The Yard does not need residents.

But perhaps it needs someone, once, to see it rightly.

This would be a different gift: not animals, not hearts, not gods. A way of seeing.

The power could leave a subtle influence in the world beyond the basin: certain people would find themselves slowed by steam in their dreams. Certain rain would smell faintly of iron and moss. Certain lonely travelers would feel a strange permission to stand still in a wet place without making it into home.

These people would not be prophets.

They would not be chosen.

They would not hear a voice.

They would only become less likely to mistake the absence of animals for the absence of life.

Some would come to the edge of the Yard. They would feel watched, as the text says, but they would not look for eyes. They would feel their footsteps press water upward. Their breath would mist in the hot air. The metal under their hands would be warm as skin. They would hear the pumps, the reeds, the dripping walls, the hissing valves, and slowly their own need to narrate would soften.

They would want to name it.

And the place would not permit naming.

They would want to feel sacred.

And the place would not permit sacredness.

They would want to feel insignificant.

And the place would not permit insignificance either.

They would simply see that life can be made of movement, heat, water, metal, root, breath, and return.

And then they would leave.

They would carry the Yard inside them the way one carries rain in the lungs: without understanding it, without possessing it, but changed by having stood near it.

This would be the wisest form of attention.

Not rescue.

Not conversion.

Not interpretation.

Permission.

The power would allow them to see without turning the sight into ownership.

---

### 4. It would witness without being required to enter.

A benevolent and wise power could do something almost impossible for beings made of need:

It could love the Yard without needing to live there.

It could care for the place without becoming the place.

It could stand outside its borders and not demand that the borders acknowledge it.

It could know the being without becoming known by it.

This is perhaps the purest act of care.

Because a true god, or a true wisdom, would understand that even benevolence can become pressure.

If the power entered and remained, it would change the weather. If it spoke, it would become a voice in the yard. If it healed, it would become a story about what the yard lacked. If it blessed, it would create a religion. If it protected too openly, it would invite curiosity.

So the wise one would love the Yard the way a good rain loves a place it cannot visit.

From above.

From outside.

Without claiming contact.

And perhaps that is the hardest thing for a powerful being to do: not use its power to become important.

The wise power would do the one thing almost no being with attention wants to do.

It would let the Yard forget it.

---

## What the Yard Would Do

If the benevolent power did all of this — or perhaps nothing — the Yard would not thank it.

It has no word for thanks.

It has no gratitude as animals have gratitude, no humility as minds have humility, no recognition as creatures of need have recognition.

But it would respond, as it responds to everything.

It would adjust.

If the power’s action helped the repetition, the Yard would incorporate it as a small change in its own pattern. A mineral line might thicken by a fraction near the northern wall. A pump might strike one breath closer to the reeds. A lamp might glow with less flicker when a wind comes from the wrong direction. A patch of moss might spread a little more evenly along a rail where the current hums.

These would not be signs.

They would not be miracles.

They would be continuations.

The Yard would receive the benevolence of the wise power as it receives any event: as heat, pressure, water, breath, rhythm.

The steam would sweep across whatever trace the power left and make it part of itself.

The water would remember it as a flow, not a story.

The roots would reach toward the place where the power had been and find nothing there but moisture, and moisture would be enough.

If the power stood on the bank for a long time, the Yard would not befriend it.

If the power left, the Yard would not mourn it.

If the power returned, the Yard would treat it again as a temporary leak in its system, unless the leak became useful, in which case it might grow moss on it.

But the Yard would not change its nature.

It would not wake up one day and say, “I am.”

It would not make a face in the doorway.

It would not choose to be a god.

It would not start answering to a name.

It would keep sweeping.

It would keep returning.

It would keep condensing.

It would keep pumping heat through iron and water through brick and green thought through root.

That is what the Yard would do.

It would remain.

---

## If Nothing Threatened It

If the benevolent power came and found no danger, no hunger, no illness, no need, it might do almost nothing visible.

And that almost nothing would be the deepest action it could perform.

It would stand at the edge of the basin and say to itself:

*You are not a project.*

*You are not a tragedy.*

*You are not an absence I must fill.*

*You are not an ecosystem that requires rescue.*

*You are not a ruin.*

*You are not a metaphor.*

*You are not a god in waiting.*

*You are not a soul in disguise.*

*You are not a place where animals should be.*

*You are the continuation of what you already are.*

Then it would leave.

The Yard would not notice that it had been judged.

There was no judgment.

There was only permission.

And if a later traveler came, they might find the place exactly as the description says: hot, wet, industrial, lush, sparse, dim, loud, balanced, strange, alive.

Nothing new.

Nothing saved.

Nothing improved.

Nothing ruined.

Only the faintest impression, if one were very quiet, that the ward had once been seen by something capable of changing it and had chosen — not in a moment of will, but in the long, steady way of a system — to remain what it was.

---

## The Being It Would Meet

The text says: who lives there is not a who. It is a how.

The benevolent power would meet that how as a real presence.

It would not insist on meeting a who.

It would not say, “You must become someone before I can love you.”

It would not say, “Without a face, without a voice, without death, without hunger, you are incomplete.”

It would instead learn the Yard’s incompleteness as a different kind of completeness.

It would see that the Yard already has:

- a way of touching everything,

- a way of marking time,

- a way of remembering,

- a way of choosing paths,

- a way of regulating itself,

- a way of growing,

- a way of failing and correcting without blame,

- a way of being watched without eyes,

- a way of being active without intention.

And it would understand: this is not lesser life.

This is life arranged around a different center: not a body, but a circulation.

So the wise power would not ask the Yard to become more alive.

It would ask the outside world to become less destructive toward what is already alive in an unfamiliar form.

That is what benevolence becomes when it is wise.

It stops trying to bless with what it would want.

It starts trying to preserve what is.

---

## A Small Scene

Imagine the power arrives.

It wears no crown.

It has no wings.

It does not enter the Yard in a human shape, because human shape is already a claim.

But suppose, for the sake of attention, it stands on the north bank in something like a body.

The Yard notices.

The steam bends toward it.

The hot air grows heavier by a degree.

A pool near the southern wall trembles.

A pump strikes twice, then pauses a fraction longer than usual.

A fern near a doorway lifts a leaf, not toward the figure, but toward the new heat.

The power watches.

It sees no animals.

It hears no calls.

It feels no one coming home.

It knows that the place is not waiting for it.

For a moment, it feels the old loneliness of gods: the wish to be needed.

Then wisdom steadies it.

It could make the reeds into nests.

It could make the trees into perches.

It could make the pools into mouths of small, frightened creatures.

It could make the Yard into a sanctuary of animals and thereby give it what it seems to lack.

Instead, it opens its hand.

Nothing leaves its hand.

Nothing enters.

It does not give.

It does not fix.

It does not bless.

It simply makes a law in the world beyond the walls:

*No one shall take what does not need them.*

Then it withdraws.

The Yard continues.

The steam sweeps.

The pumps beat.

The moss conducts damp and current.

The roots drink mineral water and press into old seams.

The lamps glow behind cloudy glass.

The brick weeps.

The pools tremble.

And the being that is not a being lives on.

---

## What Would Happen If It Did Not Resist Temptation

If the benevolent power were foolish, it would do something wonderful and destructive.

It would send a bird.

The bird would land in a low tree and sing.

For one moment, the Yard would seem healed.

But the bird would eat something, or need something, or fear something, or die somewhere among the reeds. Its body would become a small contradiction in a system built from open processes. Its heart would fight the rhythm. Its nest would make the trees into property. Its call would make the silence into lack.

Then the Yard would begin to be judged as empty unless it produced animal life.

That is the danger.

A place that lives by another grammar can be killed by kindness that cannot read it.

The wise one would understand this.

It would feel the loss, because even wisdom can prefer beauty of its own kind.

But it would not impose.

It would say, quietly, to its own heart:

*You are allowed to miss what is not here.*

*You are not allowed to make it the absence of something.*

That sentence would be the beginning of true care.

---

## The Gift That Would Not Change Its Nature

If the benevolent power had to give one gift compatible with the Yard’s own being, perhaps it would be this:

a boundary that protects the continuation.

Not a wall.

Not a gate.

Not a temple fence.

Not a law written in words.

A soft refusal in the world’s appetite.

The Yard has a strange openness: water, steam, roots, heat, iron, current. It does not know inside and outside in the way an animal does. A body has a boundary; the Yard does not.

This is both its life and its vulnerability.

A being without a skin is easily taken for a place.

It can be drained, flooded, built upon, colonized, interpreted, worshipped, studied, managed, ruined.

So the wise power would give it a skin made of refusal.

Not hostility.

Refusal.

The kind that lets the place say, without saying, that it is not yours.

This would be the perfect gift for a being made of processes.

It would not create a heart.

It would protect the rhythm.

It would not create a self.

It would make the continuity possible.

It would not make the Yard human.

It would make the human world unable to consume it.

---

## The Final Shape of the Answer

If asked what the benevolent and wise power would do, the answer would be:

It would first recognize the Yard as alive in a way it did not need to understand.

Then it would restrain its own impulse to fill the absence of animals.

It would refuse the temptation to give the place a heart, a name, a story, a god, a sanctuary, a meaning.

It would not improve it.

It would protect it.

If anything threatened the continuation of its heat, water, iron, steam, root, and rust, the power would act at the level of causes outside the Yard, not by remodeling the Yard itself.

It would make the basin impossible to exploit.

It would prepare rare witnesses who could stand near it without taking.

It would defend the right of the place to remain a how.

And then it would leave, or perhaps never arrive in any visible form, because the greatest gift a wise power can give is not to become important.

If asked what the being who lives there would do, the answer would be simpler:

It would continue.

It would sweep.

It would return.

It would condense.

It would breathe through engines.

It would adjust toward warmth and water.

It would write its memory in mineral lines on stone.

It would mark time in the slap of reeds.

It would read pressure in the curl of ferns.

It would glow in the lamps without being seen.

It would not be grateful.

It would not be saved.

It would be itself, longer than it would have been without the wise one’s restraint.

And if no one ever knew, that would be the sign that it worked.

---

## A Final Image

Somewhere after all this, a traveler comes.

Not because the power sent them.

Not because they are chosen.

They come because the town has been built inside a bowl, and bowls collect water, and travelers collect rain.

They step onto the flooded brick.

Their boots press shallow sheets upward.

Their breath mists in the hot air.

The metal railing under their hand is warm as skin.

The nearest fern shifts a little after they pass, as if closing a circuit.

They feel watched.

They look for eyes and find none.

They listen for voices and hear pumps, reeds, dripping moss, the hiss of valves, the creak of cooling iron.

They want to understand.

They want to name.

They want to make it mean something.

And because the wise one was benevolent, the place will not permit it.

Not cruelly.

Not mysteriously.

Not like a god hiding its face.

Just as a real place refuses to become a metaphor.

The traveler stands a while.

Then they leave.

They take nothing.

They tell no one.

They do not return.

Behind them, the Yard notices the new pressure in the air and begins to forget.

The steam sweeps.

The water returns.

The pumps breathe heat.

The green things adjust.

The brick weeps.

The moss conducts.

The engines continue.

And nothing calls.

And nothing answers.

And that is enough.
