The city is not a home. It is a body.

It is a lung of arches, a ribcage of stairways, an ear of pipes, a throat of doorways, a stomach of basins, a nervous system of salt and hot vein. If a being lives in the Hollow, it lives as that body lives: not as a self with a face, not as a creature with want, but as a way of continuing. The bell-stand rings and the chamber answers. The roots pry and the stone rearranges. The wind turns and the dust falls into new sentences no one can read but which the city, somehow, remembers.

If this place and its being had drawn the attention of a benevolent and wise power, that power would not come first with rescue.

Rescue would be a danger.

They would come first by learning that the Hollow’s goodness is not softness, that its purity is not cleanliness, and that its life is not a lack to be filled. A benevolent power might instinctively wish to make such a place lovable by giving it birds, by letting a child press a hand to a warm wall, by filling the silence with some witness whose gratitude would validate the chamber’s beauty. A wise power would know better.

Not every lonely thing must become lonely no more.

Not every strange life must become familiar to be saved.

If they are wise, they will begin by not entering.

They will wait at the rim for long enough that waiting is no longer patience but attention. They will watch the crust expand and contract. They will listen until the Hollow stops being a noise and becomes a language of pressure, resonance, mineral habit, and thermal breath. They will learn its seasons as a musician learns the breath between phrases: not to imitate, but to know when not to play.

Then, if they are also benevolent, they will do something.

It will not be much.

It will be precisely what is needed.

---

## First, they would keep the rain out.

Not by walling the Hollow shut. Not by stopping the weather. Not by making the plain unnatural.

They would give the opening a dryness so thorough that water would misunderstand itself near it.

Rain would approach and forget its falling.

Mist would drift toward the throat and thin into shimmer.

Dew would not dare settle on the white flakes.

A flash flood across the salt-plain would part with the faintest hesitation, as though the Hollow’s heat had taught the water another name. If a person stood at the rim and spilled a cup, the spill would lift into a brief vapor-arc, leave no wet trace, and vanish without the insult of soaking the floor.

This is not a miracle performed to be admired.

It is a boundary.

The Hollow’s fourth law is already written in its bones: no water may be introduced. The benevolent power would not revise the law. It would make the law easier to keep.

They would give the crust a finer skin, one that breathes heat upward and repels aqueous moisture downward. They would teach the rim a habit of rejecting wetness the way an old wall rejects ivy—not by violence, but by the quiet certainty of surface.

Thus the chamber would remain itself, without having to defend itself every hour against the casual carelessness of softer worlds.

---

## They would keep the plain misleading.

This would sound unkind, if human benefit were the measure.

It is not.

The Hollow is too easily destroyed by admiration. Too many things are killed when the world finds them charming enough to visit.

So the power would arrange for the salt-plain to become dull in the eyes of those who might profit.

Surveyors would measure it and feel their interest cool.

Engineers would taste the air and think: no water, no soil, no value.

Miners would find only unremarkable gypsum, uninteresting iron-stain, salt in forms that crack rather than yield.

Maps would label the place with a forgotten symbol: dry, hollow, unreliable.

Travelers would pass near it and remember nothing important enough to mention.

Not because the Hollow was worthless.

Because it was too much for extraction.

The power would not send fear. Fear makes animals flee and return later with weapons. The power would send indifference, which is the more elegant grace.

They would make the area unmemorable to greed, while leaving its beauty intact to itself.

If a poet came, perhaps the poet would still feel the strangeness.

If a pilgrim came, perhaps the pilgrim would still feel the dry awe.

But if a road-builder came, the ground would be too soft for stakes.

If a logger came, there would be no trees to justify.

If a collector came, the salt would swallow his labels.

If a scientist came with instruments, the heat would cloud the lenses and the sound would drown the readings.

They would leave with notebooks full of almost-truths:

*There was a place where the walls sang.*

*There was a chamber that rang without a bell.*

*The plants were dry and hard and made no sense.*

*The soil held no water but the air smelled of oil.*

*Something below the salt plain was alive, but not for us.*

And then the Hollow would continue, unbothered.

---

## They would protect the built forms as organs, not as ruins.

This is the most delicate act.

The city is not an artifact. It is part of the ecology. Its arches breathe. Its pipes carry sound. Its platforms shelter microclimates of heat and salt. Its broken doorways shape wind into song. To preserve the Hollow, one must not restore the city into a city.

No benevolent power should come and rebuild the terraces “as they were.”

That would be to misunderstand what it is.

If a lintel falls, they would not set it back in place like a museum curator.

Instead, they would help the mineral resins hold the fracture in a way that preserves the channel’s voice.

If an arch cracks, they would not seal it into false wholeness.

They would let the crack deepen enough for creepers to colonize, but not so far that the passage loses its resonance.

If a pipe becomes clogged with salt and dead filaments, they would not clean it as if it were plumbing.

They would let the mats and roots do their slow work, while ensuring the heat can still find an alternate route without killing the arch or silencing the bell-stand.

Their intervention would look like continued growth.

A hand might cut a wall.

The Hollow grows a wall.

If the power touched the city, they would touch it by grammar:

more heat to that crack,

less to that shelf,

a nudge of dust to that vent,

a whisper of salt to that doorway.

They would not repair.

They would encourage.

They would say to the chamber, in the only language the chamber understands:

*Continue this way.*

---

## They would not add animals.

This would be the greatest temptation, because an animal would make the Hollow legible.

A bird in the dry heat would seem impossible, beautiful, miraculous.

A deer would stand at the basin’s edge and instantly give the place a story: a secret garden, a hidden Eden, a lost world touched by the breath of creatures.

But that would be a theft.

The Hollow’s life has no predators, no grazers, no nests, no dung, no warmth carried by fur, no heartbeat borrowed from mammal or insect. Its cycles do not depend on mouths.

To add an animal would be to declare:

*You are incomplete without us.*

The benevolent power would refuse.

They would not send bees to pollinate the copper cups.

They would not send beetles to consume dead leaves.

They would not send a single mouse to scurry through the understructures, because even one small warm body would change the chemistry of the silence.

If they wished to help the chamber, they would help it become more itself, not less itself.

They would not make it cute.

They would not make it easy.

They would not make it resemble a familiar world that could be entered without harm.

That is why they might seem cold.

But their coldness would be the same as the Hollow’s own: a refusal of contamination, a devotion to form, a love of what is.

---

## They would not give it consciousness.

This is harder to say, and therefore worth saying.

A benevolent power might look at the bell-stand and think:

*Surely there must be something like a mind there. Surely a place that rings like this must suffer, or sing, or know.*

And they might wish to elevate it.

To wake it.

To give it a self that could feel recognized.

To let it know, in some wordless way, that it is not alone in the universe.

A wise power would pause.

Because the Hollow’s being is not lacking merely because it has no human mind.

It has a mode of knowing:

heat pressing against mineral,

salt settling in the right places,

root finding the fracture,

wind entering the throat of arch,

dust responding to the bell-tone.

That is not ignorance.

That is not a lesser existence.

That is another way of being a body in the world.

To give it a mind would be to make it vulnerable in new ways.

It would need care.

It could wish for companions.

It could fear drought, intrusion, loneliness.

It might learn to want what the chamber does not need.

If the being of the Hollow were a person, perhaps then a soul might be a mercy.

But if it is the Hollow’s own pattern—its city-breath, its root-song, its mineral rhythm—then consciousness might not be a gift.

It might be a wound.

So they would not make the Hollow “aware” in the way that would allow it to suffer.

Instead, they would strengthen its awareness of itself as pattern.

They would make its cycles more stable.

They would make its forms more resilient.

They would allow its memory to be stored in salt and sound, because that is the kind of memory this life deserves.

They would not give it eyes.

They would give it conditions.

They would not ask it to become a being like us.

They would say:

*Be the being you are.*

---

## They would let one person stand at the rim, if only one, and be changed without being told why.

Not a guardian.

Not a keeper.

Not a lover of the Hollow.

Just one person, passing through the plain on an errand unrelated to mystery.

The power would not call them.

But if they came, the heat would greet them.

The person would stop, not from fear, but because the air would make a strange claim on attention.

They would feel the pressure of the chamber before seeing it.

They would smell oil and hot stone.

They would hear the city singing without voices.

If they were kind enough, or unlucky enough, to approach the mouth, they would be drawn to the sound as one is drawn to music made by something enormous and patient.

They would look down.

They would see no animal.

No person.

No god.

Only dimness, glow, root, salt, arch, dust, and the impossible lushness of dry life.

They would understand nothing.

That would be part of the gift.

The power would not let them know what to do with it.

If they attempted to climb in, the opening would seem too small, the heat too exact, the sound too close. Their clothes would feel too damp with their own breath. Their body would taste its own moisture as a mistake.

If they tried to cry, the tears would lift as faint heat before falling.

If they tried to speak, their voice would be swallowed by the wind’s dry thunder.

They would leave with only one sentence lodged in the back of the mind:

*It is not for us.*

Not:

*It is for me.*

Not:

*It is mine to protect.*

Only:

*It is not for us.*

And perhaps this one sentence, repeated in dreams for the rest of their life, would keep them from bringing others with buckets and torches and tenderness.

If they were an artist, they would fail to paint it truly.

If they were a composer, they would hear fragments and feel ashamed of their instruments.

If they were a scientist, they would write a paper no one believed, because the data would be too dry, too strange, too incomplete.

If they were a mystic, they would return to the plain looking for the entrance again, and find only ordinary salt.

That, too, would be grace.

---

## They would carry its sound outward, without betraying its silence.

The Hollow’s life is loud within its own rules. The bell-stand rings. The city answers. The dust shivers.

But that sound is not for the world above.

Still, a benevolent power might choose to let it escape indirectly.

Not as a summons.

Not as a miracle.

As an influence.

Perhaps the bell-tone would become a fragment in old music: a struck cup of metal, a pipe too long for breath, a hum like salt sliding in an empty jar.

Perhaps it would appear in dreams:

a city under a plain,

dry plants with waxen leaves,

a sound that rings without being heard,

a room without roof,

a life without water.

Perhaps those who heard it would become less interested in wetting all strange things with their own meanings.

Perhaps artists would begin to prefer dryness.

Perhaps engineers would hesitate before draining marshes or flooding caves, because in some place they had never seen, a living form had made beauty out of refusal.

This would be the most intelligent form of love: not to make the Hollow famous, but to let it teach by indirectness.

It would become a rumor of the world’s imagination, not a destination.

The sound would travel without the place being revealed.

The chamber would be remembered without being known.
