A benevolent and wise power would not come to the Anvil of Umm the way a maker comes to unfinished stone.

It would not bring tools. It would not bring law. It would not bring light that demanded worship, nor a hand from the sky, nor a voice that shook the basalt and forced the world to answer.

If it came at all, it would come as a listener.

And because it was wise, the first thing it would do is the hardest thing for a power: it would consider whether its intervention would help, or whether it would only make the Anvil smaller.

Because the Anvil had already answered a great many questions with its own body.

It had answered height with patience.  
It had answered drama with flatness.  
It had answered emptiness with chorus.  
It had answered permanence by refusing to be permanent.  
It had answered death by becoming ground.

A foolish kindness would arrive and say: I will make the water certain.  
A wise kindness would look at the water-memory of the plateau and understand that certainty would kill its rhythm.

A foolish kindness would say: I will give you names carved in stone.  
A wise kindness would hear the human names as breath, clicks, whistles, and pauses, and realize that stone names would teach them to mourn themselves as if they were not already living.

A foolish kindness would say: I will remove the predators.  
A wise kindness would know that the predators were not the absence of mercy, but one of the ways the Anvil kept its attention awake.

A foolish kindness would say: I will make the dead shine in memory.  
A wise kindness would watch the moss close over the grave and see that forgetting in that place was not cruelty, but belonging.

So what would a benevolent and wise power do, if anything?

It would do almost nothing.

And that almost-nothing would be the truest thing it could do.

---

There was, among the scattered people of the Anvil, one whose name was a short rhythm: two soft clicks, a held breath, and the faint rasp of a finger drawn across dry reed. The power did not translate this name into letters, because the name was not a thing standing still. It was a way the group’s voices had learned to find the person in the open, the way a bird-call finds the bird before the eye can.

The being moved carefully, as all people there moved carefully, testing the living ground with their feet before putting weight upon it. They were old by human measure, though not old beyond the Anvil’s way of keeping age. Their feet had become instruments. Their hands could tell the difference between moss that held water and moss that merely pretended. Their ears could hear the thin ones before seeing them, and the birds’ ordinary songs before the first sharp note that meant the ordinary had changed.

They carried no treasure but a small bundle wrapped in root-felt: stone flakes, a bone awl, a length of sinew cord, a polished shell from a water that had never been seen at this altitude. The shell was not for prayer. It was a reminder that the world was wider than the Anvil, but not wiser because it was wide. Sometimes, in the dry hours, they turned the shell in their hand and listened to the faint whistle of wind through its chamber, because it was not their own breath and therefore easier to breathe with.

That season, the water-hollows had failed.

The mineral rings were broad and white. The fog came and went without giving itself. The plant-towers stood like tired columns in a green sea, and the living mat had begun to contract in places, growing thin and brittle where it had once been springy and soft. Herds moved farther, their hooves crackling less often. The dusk clouds of winged insects were smaller than they should have been. The predators moved through the edges with more patience than appetite. The people moved from basin to basin, and each time they left a place more exhausted than before.

The being sat at dusk near a split stone, back against a half-fallen plant-tower, and listened.

They were tired in the way that is not only tiredness of body, but tiredness of remembering. They knew too many routes. They knew which hollows had lied last year, which stones held warmth without offering it, which wind directions were not wind directions but promises. They knew that memory was a burden on a plateau that asked its inhabitants to leave everything lightly.

A child beside them—smaller, not small enough to be carried—was trying to make the insects repeat a rhythm by tapping a dry stalk. The child was learning, but that evening the lesson was difficult. The ground did not answer.

The being did not speak of gods. The Anvil did not permit that kind of speech. Even reverence there was shaped like attention, not like pleading.

But they felt, in the place where their name was made, a sound like a question:

*If I forget, will the ground remember what I taught it?*

The power heard.

And it wanted to answer.

If it had been only benevolent, it would have answered too quickly. It would have sent rain. It would have opened a spring. It would have made the split stone speak, or sent a column of light so gentle and terrible that the child would never again be able to hear ordinary wind without trembling. It would have saved them, and in saving them, it would have ended the life they had learned to live.

But because it was wise, it waited.

Because it was benevolent, it did not merely withdraw.

It found the smallest possible way to help that was still honest, still local, still kind.

---

The power entered the Anvil not as a visitor, but as a slight change in the grammar of the place.

It did not alter the rules.

It used them.

At night, when the living skin of the plateau gave off its faint wet popping as the air cooled, the power made one geothermal seam beneath the split stone a little warmer than it would otherwise have been. Not hot. Not miraculous. Not enough to change the shape of the season or to make the people depend upon a gift they could not return. Only enough for a human back pressed against the ground to feel, without knowing why, that the earth had not withdrawn from them.

The being slept there.

In their sleep, they heard a sound that was not a sound.

It was the rhythm of their own name, but older.

It was in the dripping of dew from a leaf-strut.

It was in the small crackling of the child’s fingers against the dry stalk.

It was in the distant movement of a flock shifting above the fog.

It was not a voice commanding. It was a voice reminding.

*You are not separate from what you carry. You are the way the place carries what it can.*

The child woke first, because children on the Anvil wake before they are called, as though they are born listening.

“Something is moving,” the child said.

The being opened their eyes.

The dawn fog was climbing from below. It slid across the rounded ridges in pale sheets. On the tips of the leaf-struts, dew formed and fell, striking moss with a soft, slow, patient note. The living ground exhaled its warmth. The wind was dry, but not empty.

And the insects were different.

Not louder. Not louder.

But there was a pause in their chorus, a small opening that happened when the fog thinned over a shallow basin, as though the plateau were breathing through a new mouth.

The being listened.

They had listened all their life.

Now they heard that the pause had direction.

The child followed their eyes.

“There,” the child said. “The moss there is darker.”

It was.

In the early light, a long shadow of green stretched across the dust-moss, faint as a remembered waterline. It was not enough to see from any other angle. It was not enough to call a miracle. It was only a place where a little condensation had stayed, where the mat had thickened, where something—perhaps an old burrower’s tunnel, perhaps a crack of basalt warmed and cooled—had carried water downward and then let it rise again in memory.

The being stood slowly. Their body felt the warmth still held in the stone, not as a gift from a god, but as the kindness of an old body agreeing to carry another body for a night.

The child waited, because waiting was a skill.

They walked toward the dark green.

The living ground yielded under their feet, then closed again, as if it had never been broken. They passed beneath a plant-tower, and the shade there was damper, the air tasting of green stone and mineral. They came to a low mound of cushion-plants. Around it, the surface had begun to heal from the season’s hunger: tiny tendrils knitting the pale dust, algal films drinking the fog, lichens holding the night’s damp in their thin bodies.

The being knelt.

They pressed their palm flat against the mat.

Beneath the skin, the ground was cool.

Then, as their fingers spread and found the old seam, cool became wet.

Not a pool. Not a spring.

A hollow, no larger than both hands together, full of clear water.

The child made a soft sound.

The being did not say, *I have saved us.* They did not say, *The ground has heard me.* That was not their way of speech. They cupped water, drank, then filled the small shell.

“Tell the others,” they said to the child.

It was enough.

---

Later, when the group came, they did not build a shrine.

They built a temporary windbreak, low and woven, anchored with stones that did not cut deeply into the mat. They dried what they could. They took only what the place could spare. They left within the season.

The being did not tell the others about the warmth in the split stone, except to say that the place had been good to them that night. They did not tell the child about the rhythm in the fog, except to make the child repeat the insect-pause until it became part of their memory.

And if someone much later asked whether they had been visited by something great, the being would only tilt their head and say, in the way such people have, “The ground was listening differently.”

That was, perhaps, the truth.

Because the benevolent and wise power had not done anything dramatic enough to be worshipped.

It had done the only thing a wise power should do:

It had helped a place continue to be itself.

---

If a benevolent and wise power looked upon such a place and the being who lived there, it might ask itself:

*Am I needed?*

And if it found the answer was *no*, it would still be benevolent enough to ask:

*Can I give without injuring?*

If the answer was *yes*, then it would give a small thing.

Not a kingdom. Not certainty. Not permanence.

It would give:

— one night of warmth in the right stone, so the body could rest;  
— one pause in the chorus of insects, so the listener could follow it;  
— one darker green in the morning light, so hope would remain humble;  
— one water-hollow where memory had not yet failed;  
— one child who could learn the difference between a warning and a welcome;  
— one old person who would die without needing to be made immortal to have been loved;  
— one name that would pass through the air and return to the place that made it, without being cut into the indifferent face of the world.

And then the power would withdraw.

Not because it did not care, but because care had become complete.

If it ever returned, it would return as something smaller still.

As a warm patch of basalt at dusk.

As a bird-call that meant fog was rising.

As a beetle’s back carrying one drop of water through a dry morning.

As a footprint that filled with moss.

As a body that became richer ground.

As a sound remembered so well that it seemed the place itself had spoken it.

A foolish power would say: I will make them remember me.

A wise and benevolent power would say: I will make the place remember itself.

And if that was all it did, it would be enough.
