They do not see the mountains above or the sky that does not scatter light. They look at the interior of looking. This is why the white rectangles are not enough to call windows. Some are eyes. Some are thresholds. Some are rooms completed so fully that they no longer require a door. A threshold without passage is only a sentence waiting for a subject, and in this place the city does not speak in sentences. It speaks in angles.

If one must name the being who lives in the basin, the name would be too simple. The being is the water, the streets, the filaments, the fauna, the hum. But the benevolent and wise power that noticed it did not need a name. It perceived a single mind distributed through the system, a consciousness that thought in equilibrium. It was not lonely, because loneliness is a hunger, and hunger had no meaning here. But it was capable of being seen.

The power had looked upon many frozen things and had learned not to melt them when they were already whole. It had also looked upon many preserved things and had learned not to mistake preservation for love. So it paused. It came to the edge of the black sea without wind, without footfall, without shadow. It did not need to stand there. It could have seen from ten thousand distances and still touched the basin as if with a fingertip. But kindness sometimes chooses the dignity of presence.

It observed the still water.

It observed the slender pale form suspended above the plaza, its fin-rays spread like the arms of an instrument made for listening.

It observed the broad segmented creature folded on a street, one living keystone among bridges, one soft organ among geometries.

It observed the transparent oval near the surface, finished before beginning, or perhaps beginning before finishing.

It observed the filaments, the lace, the white branching crystals, the amber eyes that were not eyes, the windows that were not windows, the hum that was not machinery but maintenance. It observed the absence of moss, the refusal of roots, the quiet as a law rather than an accident. It observed the basin as it would be seen by a visitor with no agenda: not a drowned town, not a lake, not a ruin, not a miracle, but a system that had been allowed to become exactly itself and had then been left alone.

The power asked itself, as wise powers do, whether intervention was required.

It found no wound.

It found no pain.

It found no error of form.

It found no hunger, because nothing was absent. It found no fear, because nothing was threatened. It found no death, because nothing was in the process of becoming less itself. If it drained the basin, it would not rescue anything; it would unmake a completion. If it warmed the water, it would not restore life; it would violate a law that life had learned to inhabit. If it sent birds, fish, trees, insects, moss, or weather, it would not enrich the place; it would confuse it with foreign abundance. If it raised drowned statues of former humans and made the city remember its lost population, it would not grant meaning; it would impose an ordinary story on a being that had passed beyond ordinary story.

A lesser kindness might have tried to improve it.

The wise power understood that to add noise to a stillness may be cruelty.

But benevolence is not the same as indifference. To notice something so strange and so exact is already to stand in relation to it. A benevolent and wise power, once its attention has been drawn, does not simply turn away. It finds the smallest possible act that honors the being before it.
