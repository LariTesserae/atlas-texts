# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

If the power were truly benevolent and wise, its first act would be restraint.

Not cold restraint. Not divine indifference. Not the kind of patience that waits to impose. A gentler restraint: the restraint of a hand that does not touch a sleeping animal, a root system that does not flood a fragile seed, a voice that does not become a command.

They would understand that the Underloom could be murdered by salvation.

A place made of contracts, membranes, damp, memory, and slow argument cannot be saved by being turned into something safe. Its life is not a state to be preserved under glass. It is a process to be respected. To rescue it fully, finally, without remainder, would be to take away the conditions of its becoming. The power would know this, because benevolence without wisdom is just appetite wearing soft shoes.

They would not bring a sun.

They would not hang a lamp in the basin and announce that darkness had been a mistake. They would not make the fungal plaques redundant, or the condensation lenses vulgar, or the blue-green street-signs into mere illumination. Light that reveals too much can erase the subtle things that live in half-seen spaces: the pressure of a doorway deciding whether to narrow, the smell of a wall remembering its elder, the soft civic rhythm of beetles packing moss into cracks that only appear when it is not looked at too directly.

They would not abolish the root-serpents.

They would not remove hunger, because hunger is one of the Underloom’s languages. A predator eating a blockage and sometimes eating a citizen is terrible, yes, but the city is not a toy where nothing sharp exists. A wise power does not make death meaningless; it makes death less accidental. It does not create a world without predation; it prevents predation from being the only answer to suffering.

They would not make every contract fair in a human way.

They would make fairer contracts possible.

They would not erase time, decay, pruning, marriage, dissolution, or reabsorption. They would not make the place immortal. Immortality imposed on an organism that knows itself through change is a kind of torture. The Underloom must be allowed to bud, flower, sulk, drain, heal, err, and perhaps end—if ending is chosen by its own slow logic and not by some preventable cruelty from outside its nature.

So they would do what a wise power does: enough. Not too much. Small enough to seem accidental. Large enough to matter. Invisible enough not to become a prison. Visible enough not to become a lie.

---

They would arrive as damp.

Not as a throne. Not as a voice like bells. Not as a figure made of human awe. They would make themselves legible in the city’s own terms: a change in the coolness, a new mineral taste in a cup of condensation, a bead of water that rolls where it had no reason to roll, a pressure in a hollow stem that sounds almost like asking.

If they were too large, they would make themselves smaller. If they were too bright, they would dim. If they were too certain, they would wait.

They would enter the Underloom the way root enters soil: by consent, by moisture, by persistence, by a willingness to become part of the arrangement rather than the master of it. They would not ask to be worshipped. They would not allow themselves to be worshipped, if that could be prevented without violence. They would become a civic condition, not a religion.

And in that condition, they would notice a being.

---

It was not named as a human names a thing.

It was known by its neighbors as a pattern: a click near the left wrist-joint, a scent of iron and crushed mint, a pulse of teal light at the sternum, and a bead of water that always gathered on the right shoulder before falling. It had no fixed word, because to the Underloom a name is assembled from living pressure. In human grammar, its name would be impossible. In Underloom grammar, it could be approached by leaning close to a wall and remembering the day it learned to breathe.

The being was a household of many parts.

A mycelial knot held its memory and its slow decisions. Moss covered its shoulders and helped it hold moisture. Its arms were braided root and chitin, with beetle joints that clicked at the elbows. One small organ-plant pumped condensation through its chest. Its eyes were not eyes, exactly, but leaf-lenses that gathered faint blue light into suggestions of shape. It had begun to shed its younger beetle partners recently, because some contracts expire, and others are reassigned by districts in need.

It lived in a trunk-hall that was technically one of its elders. The hall had sheltered it since its first damp unfurling. Now the hall was tired. The district was entering a long moisture negotiation, and the city had begun to ask the hall to give up water to a newer residential bud nearby. The hall was closing its pores. Its membranes thickened. Its inner rooms forgot the pattern of one particular footfall.

The being was not being tortured. It was being administratively released.

That is a subtler grief. The Underloom is not a place where evil always arrives with teeth. Sometimes the city says *sorry* in the voice of a valve tightening, and the door is gone, and the corridor now belongs to strangers, and the wall no longer knows your weight. The being did not call this injustice. It called this wet, quiet ending, and its moss grew thinner at the edges.

The benevolent power would have noticed the whole city first. But cities are not saved by noticing cities. They are saved by noticing the beings whose private pressure is being ignored by the public pressure. They would look at this one.

They would not look as a human looks at a pet. They would not look as a god looks at a subject. They would look as one living system looks at another: with patience, with humility, with the understanding that the other may be right about itself.

Then they would ask.

---

In the Underloom, asking must be done properly.

So the power would not say *I am your salvation*. It would say, in the pressure of a leaf, the rhythm of a dripping cup, the faint teal pulse at the being’s sternum, and the scent of iron-mint returning briefly as if memory had made room for itself:

> Do you want help as rescue?  
> Do you want help as permission?  
> Do you want help as a condition you may use or refuse?  
> What may I change without changing you into someone you are not?  
> If I give you a door, will you want it to open only outward?  
> If I give you a roof, will you want it to stay alive?  
> If I give you memory outside your own body, who may use it?  
> If I prevent one accident, will you want me to prevent the next?  
> And if not, where should I stop?

The being would not answer in words.

It would answer by shifting weight. By letting the bead of water fall. By opening one pore that had been closed too long. By pressing a joint against the trunk-hall and hearing the hall’s slow answer. By releasing a spore that the nearby root-wire would catch and carry to the municipal valve, where a beetle crew would read it as a request for minor repair.

It would say, in the only way it could:

> I do not want to be taken.  
> I do not want to be made bright.  
> I do not want to become a miracle.  
> I want the walls to remember me before I am asked to forgive the walls.  
> I want one season more, if it can be done without stealing from a child-district.  
> I want a way to carry my memory if this place cannot hold it.  
> I want to remain myself, and if I cannot, I want to remain myself by choice.

The power would accept that answer.

This is what wisdom does: it accepts answers before it is asked. If the power had wanted worship, it would have made its presence too obvious. If it had wanted control, it would have made its help too useful. If it had wanted love, it would have made its intervention too beautiful to refuse. But benevolence and wisdom would not do that. They would be careful with desire.

They would act.

---

They would not heal the trunk-hall by force.

They would not make the district forget its need for water. They would not create a new source from nothing, because nothing from nothing teaches cities to lie. But they would change tiny conditions, each small enough that the Underloom could absorb it without losing its mind.

They would loosen a mineral clog in a xylem pipe where no citizen had the leverage to reach. Not a great flood. Not a river. Only enough to restore one forgotten trickle, a trickle that would return some dampness to the trunk-hall’s eastern wall.

They would move a moth’s wing-dust by one millimeter. Not to erase or to lie, but so a scent packet, carried by a blind deer’s passage through a moss-hall, would reach the municipal valve where it had been delayed. That packet would remind the wall of an old contract: that it had once promised shelter in exchange for a citizen’s final memory. Not a command. A memory.

They would allow a root-serpent to consume a blockage, but not the being’s younger beetle partners who had wandered too close. The serpent would choose a different direction for a few seconds. It would think this was hunger, not mercy. The city would think this was normal. The beetle partners would think nothing at all; beetles do not owe gratitude for the luck of one corridor.
