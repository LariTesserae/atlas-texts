Far down near the thermal pools, the steam rises in slow columns that lean like people listening. Not a voice. Not yet. The basin is cautious with voices. It prefers the creak of a load finding a better place, the wet tap of a leaf-shed, the small argument between two households about whose net should dry on the east rung. The being that lives in this place does not speak from above. It speaks through tension, growth, memory, and correction. It is a parliament with no president: the grafts and the grafters, the underthread and the pool-warmers, the sievebirds and the spore-readers, the children who trust ladders before ladders trust them, the old women who feel a failing beam in their own wrists, the traders who come with nails and leave with shame or wisdom, depending on how stubborn they were on the way in.

And this being—this breathing town, this animal made of tending—has drawn the attention of a benevolent and wise power.

What would they do?

If they were only benevolent, they would do everything.

They would make the ironvine unbreakable. They would make the ladder-rungs warm and sure beneath tired feet. They would fill every fruit-cup without waiting a season. They would cure the child of the cough that comes from wet-thread rooms and smoke. They would send a storm of gentleness, windless and harmless, to teach the high arches that they need not tremble. They would whisper kindness into every heart until anger dissolved like mist. They would make the basin safe.

But if they were wise, they would know that such a safety is not a gift. It is a forgetting.

If no vine could fail, there would be no graft-mothers. If the rain came exactly as wished, the root-cups would lose their meaning. If hunger could not touch the basin, the ash-rats’ scattered seeds would be no miracle, no hope, no teacher of winter’s patience. If anger could not enter a household, forgiveness would be a word without teeth. If people could not fall, their hands would not learn each other’s weight. If a town became a garden that needed no one, the people would become tourists in their own lives. The basin would remain beautiful, and it would be empty.

So the wise power does not do what kindness first begs to do.

They do not save the Trellis Basin.

They consider it.

They watch for a long while, the way one watches a fire that is neither too high nor too low. Not idle. Not indifferent. Attentive in a way that leaves room for the world to be what it is. They learn, if they have learned anything, that the basin already has a way of surviving: not by hardness, not by ownership, not by hoarding, but by being asked to keep its weight.
