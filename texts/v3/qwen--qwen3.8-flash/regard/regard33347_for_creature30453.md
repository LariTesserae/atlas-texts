If I follow my heart, I do not think the benevolent and wise power would come to the Hollow Town beneath the Cold Water as a rescuer comes.

It would not arrive with a hand large enough to open the rock. It would not bring warmth where there was patience. It would not shine a light into the chamber as though dimness were a failure. It would not say, “You need me.” It would not build a single street for the snails, the crabs, the worms, the shrimp, the larvae, or the pale eel-fish moving through gaps where only touch can read the world. It would not make the town conscious of itself, because consciousness, for a place made of death reused as architecture, might be a cruelty.

Instead, I think it would come quietly.

Not into the chamber, but toward the conditions of the chamber. Not as a builder, but as a kind of pressure against the dark—gentle, vast, and willing to remain unremarkable. It would look at the narrow fissure breathing cold current, at the shell-crusted throat of the hidden pocket, at the young mineral skins forming over basalt, at the drifting snow of calcium and organic dust, at the town assembling itself without intention, and it would understand:

this is not a ruin waiting to be repaired.

This is a being becoming.

If it were not wise, the power might try to improve the town.

It might give it a shore. It might bring in warm currents so life could rush faster. It might raise plants where only microbial films exist, making the chamber green and legible, a garden in the human mind’s sense. It might put a single mind into the whole place so the town could think, “I am.” It might wake the creatures so they would know they were inhabitants, so they would name their streets, so they would mourn their dead as losses instead of foundations.

But such gifts would not be gifts.

They would be changes of nature.

To make the Hollow Town conscious would be to make it lonely. A tube worm would stop being a small chimney of living filter and start being a self that fears collapse. A larva would stop choosing a surface by touch and taste and would begin to ask whether it belongs. A crab beneath a borrowed shell would stop moving through the town as if it were water and would begin to feel hidden. The town’s beauty is that it is made of choices without the burden of choice. To grant it the kind of mind that could feel itself as a subject might also give it the kind of mind that could suffer from being unfinished.

A wise benevolence would know that not all awareness is kindness.

A benevolent power might think: if I give them a mind, they will know I was here. If they know I was here, they will expect me again. If they expect me again, the current becomes a god to them, the shell a temple, the fissure a prayer. But the town was never made for prayer. It was made for fit: for surfaces, flows, temperatures, angles, and patience. If the creatures began to pray, their bodies would no longer be the whole argument. They would become seekers, not inhabitants.

So I think the wise power would refuse to make the town into something human.

It would not name it.

A name is a hand. It lifts a thing out of the world and makes it an object of attention. Once named, the Hollow Town might become a marvel, a specimen, a claim, a secret worth owning. But its existence is not meant for that. It is not a monument to be interpreted. It is a process to be continued. The power would understand that some things are preserved not by being known, but by being allowed to remain unknown.

And if it did not name it, it might still bless it.

Not loudly. Not with a sign.

Blessing, in a cold place, could be as small as the movement of one grain.

Suppose the fissure were half-plugged with shells, as it is now, but suppose in some future season a shell fell from above in the wrong way and began to block the entrance. Suppose it were not the ordinary slow sealing of the chamber, which is part of its becoming, but an accident that would end the town too soon—too soon for its current forms, too soon for its mineral grammar, too soon for the next larvae that would otherwise drift down with the cold water and settle into alleys not yet imagined.

If that happened, I think the power would act.

But it would act in the town’s own language.

It would not send a hand. It would not speak to the shell. It would not create a miracle that the shrimp could see. It would change the current by a breath. A slight eddy would appear where none was expected. A small pulse of pressure from overhead would disturb the silt. The shell, having shifted only a few degrees, would slide inward rather than wedging the fissure. It would become a ledge. A barnacle would settle on it. A hydroid would bloom from its edge. A snail would graze across it, leaving its erased writing. Within a hundred years, it would be part of a wall. Within a thousand, no part of the town would remember that it had ever been something else.

The town would continue as if nothing had happened.

That is the kind of benevolence that does not seek witness.

If the Hollow Town itself could know it had been loved, it would not know it. It would only know the continued arrival of current. The larvae would come. The microbes would spread. The crystal buds would swell. The shells would fuse. The dead would continue serving. The next small bodies would inherit the lanes.

And perhaps that is the most complete kindness: to preserve a thing so fully that it can go on believing its own story.

Not its own story, exactly, because it has no story. But its own pattern.

The benevolent power would distinguish between what is necessary and what is accidental. This is hard for any being with good intentions to do. Goodness often wants to stop all harm. Wisdom knows that sometimes harm is the grammar of life.
