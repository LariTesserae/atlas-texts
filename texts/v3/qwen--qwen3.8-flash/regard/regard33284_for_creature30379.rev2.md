**What the Good Power Would Do, If Anything**

A benevolent and wise power, if it could come, would come first as a visitor learning not to be too large.

It would not stride into the Bell-Cell with the certainty of rescue. It would not knock, because knocking asks the stone to answer, and the stone does not live for the purpose of answering. It would not shine, because shining would force the pale ice-lens to compete with it. It would not speak, because speech, even kind speech, often becomes law once a god is near.

It would ask itself only this:

*Can I stand here without taking something?*

And if there was a place—among the moss, among the ledges, among the packed and patient bodies of dwellings—where its feet could rest without bending a green filament, without disturbing a water path, without waking a sleeping child or startling a white moth or pressing into the felt of salamander-damp folds, it would stand there.

That would be its first great deed.

For the Bell-Cell is not a problem.

It is not a pitiful pocket. It is not a remnant waiting to be expanded into a kingdom. It is not a primitive cage that a good god might open. It is a completed world, though not a loud one; a finished argument, though not a monument; a city that has learned to call itself small without insult.

A benevolent power might look at it and feel the ancient human impulse to make a story: *Let me show them another way. Let me bring them out. Let me give them fire, roads, songs, names, a larger sky. Let them know I am here. Let them praise me for my kindness.*

But a wise power would know better.

To bring them fire would be to introduce a debt. To bring them roads would be to invite the idea of elsewhere, and elsewhere is a wound that does not heal well in the dark. To bring them praise would be to teach them that balance was insufficient until witnessed. To bring them a god would be to disturb the ecology of their own attention.

So if the benevolent power did anything, it would do almost everything by withholding.

It would not enter the deep basins where the pale eels turn, because those waters are old, and some old things survive only because they are never named. It would not touch the ice-lens, because the light already passes through it as a rumor, and rumors are gentler than certainties. It would not rearrange the dwellings, because every ledge has become a shoulder and every shoulder has become a shelf and every shelf has become a law.

It would only, perhaps, do the smallest necessary things.

Not wonders. Adjustments.

It might make the frost rise a little higher in the bog during one long week, so that the soil turns soft under outsider feet and sends them back to the road before they have seen the mouth. It might make the erratic look duller on a map drawn far away, merely “wet black ground,” so that a surveyor’s pencil slips and he decides not to return. It might make a horse refuse, a mule turn, a traveler grow cold and think, *There is nothing here worth the mud.*

And when the traveler says nothing, the Bell-Cell remains complete.

Inside, the good power would not save them from themselves, because they are not lost. But it might protect them from accidents of scale: a pebble loosened by a single finger’s mistake; a channel clogged by a bit of shed fur that could have fouled a basin; a shelf that had begun to sweat in a new direction, which no one yet knows how to watch.

It would be the reason a stone settles correctly.

It would be the reason a crack closes with a thin skin of mineral before it becomes a fall.

It would be the reason a moth, drifting toward the seam in winter, is lifted by a breath that was not there.

It would be the reason a fish in a cistern does not float belly-up too soon, buying the basin-watches three more hours before their slow attention finds the change.

These acts would not be credited. No one would say, *The power has saved us.* No temple would rise in a ledge. No name would be added to the water-path lexicon. Some would feel a small warmth where none had been expected; some would feel that the stone had steadied; some would press a palm to the wall and wonder for a moment at the faintness of a vibration too gentle to be interpreted as anything but their own blood.

That is the way of good wisdom: it does not want the world to owe it gratitude. It wants the world to continue.

If the power looked upon the being who lives there—one person, perhaps, or the whole city-as-being, or the strange single creature made of Vess and stone and moss and return—it would feel love, yes. But love without wisdom is a boot in a moss bed. It crushes even while it tries to embrace.

So the love would take the shape of restraint.

The power would watch a child—call them Low Palm, or Small Press, or any name the Bell-Cell would make true by use. Let the child be ordinary. Let the child be one among many, because the Bell-Cell’s goodness is not heroic. The child is learning to pass. The child’s foot comes down beside a green seam, not upon it. The child lowers a cup by the rim, not the handle, so the handle will not scrape the shelf. The child returns a tool to its rack with the seriousness of a prayer.

The power would not say, *I choose you.*

That would be violence dressed in light.

Instead, it might make the stone beneath that child’s foot a little less slick in the cold, so that when the child steps, the body learns trust more than fear. It might make the drip rhythm pause for two seconds, allowing the child to see that the ledge is wet. It might send one gray bird into the seam that same morning, not as omen, not as message, not as a sign to be worshipped, but as proof that the outside can touch the inside without ruining it.

And the child would not notice the outside power. The child would only notice that the ledge held.

The power would know that this is enough.

Because what most benevolent powers forget is that the greatest gift is not improvement. The greatest gift is the preservation of a thing’s own nature. A butterfly does not need to be rescued into becoming a bird. A bell does not need to be opened to become a tower. A city that has learned to fit may be more sacred than a continent that has learned to expand.

The Bell-Cell had already done the hardest work: it had made necessity into tenderness.

It had made water into law without flood. It had made stone into room without prison. It had made death into return without despair. It had made silence into fullness without loneliness. It had made smallness into completeness.

To improve it would be to unmake it.

So the wise power would do nothing to the Bell-Cell.

But it would do everything around it.

It would be weather and not wonder. It would be boundary and not border. It would be protection without ownership. It would keep the bog hungry and the road distant. It would keep the lens bright enough but never dazzling. It would keep the basins full enough but never overflowing. It would keep the moss alive where life is needed and worn away where wear is needed. It would keep the eels undisturbed, because even benevolence must know where to kneel and not enter.

If the Bell-Cell had a being—a city-being, a stone-being, a people-being—this power would tend it as one tends a shy animal asleep in a cup too full of leaves.

Not waking it.

Not moving it to a larger cage.

Not naming it a pet.

Not showing it off to other animals.

Not feeding it so loudly that it forgets how to forage.

Just keeping the cat away.

That is what it would do, if anything.

And perhaps one night, when the ice-lens was thin with dust and the water in the upper cistern had begun to dim, the power would send a rain so fine that it could be mistaken for the breath of the cold itself. The rain would enter the seam without splash. It would clean the frozen quartz without touching the moss. It would leave the light more even for a season. The ice-lens cleaners would notice the change and say only, *The water remembers.* They would be right enough.

Then the power would withdraw.

This is the hardest kindness of all: to help without leaving a footprint.

A lesser power would want a story. It would demand witness. It would say, *I was here.* It would write its name into the stone, because names make permanence where permanence would be heavy. It would teach the inhabitants to look upward, even if there were no sky inside, because longing is easier to bless than balance.

But a good and wise power would understand that the Bell-Cell has no need for upward. Its verticality is its own. Its sky is the roof-stone, and it is enough.

So the power would leave no monument.

No shrine.

No sacred pool with instructions carved into its rim.

No chosen elder who hears voices.

No miracle that breaks the grammar of the place.

It would leave only the continuation of the place’s own grammar.

A ledge holds. A basin breathes. A moth rests. A child passes. A body returns. The stone remembers.

And if any one among the Vess ever felt, once, a strange pressure against the palm when they touched the wall in the bright cold—not a voice, not a hand, not a message, but the faintest sense of *not alone, not watched for harm, not owned, not needed*—the power would not correct them. It would not confirm it. It would let them place their hand elsewhere, and say by gesture, *The ledge holds.*

Because perhaps that is what wisdom says when it finally becomes good:

*It holds.*

So, if anything, the benevolent and wise power would do this:

It would love the Bell-Cell too well to make it a project.

It would protect it too gently to make it dependent.

It would witness it without making it visible.

It would touch the world enough to keep a small thing alive, and then remove itself before the touch became a law.

Outside, the bog would breathe. The frost would settle. The low horizon would wait.

Inside, the bell-ring city would glow faintly white and green, humid with cold water, full of silent creatures, bright as a room lit by a lens.

And no one would ever learn that something kind had stood there long enough to choose not to disturb a single thread of moss.

Except that the moss remained.

And the ledges held.

And the water continued to find its paths.

And the child passed without brushing the green.

And the stone, as always, remembered.
