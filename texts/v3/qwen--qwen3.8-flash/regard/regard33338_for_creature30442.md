## What They Would Do

A benevolent and wise power would arrive without light.

Not because light were unavailable, but because light would be an insult. To open the dark over the Weeping Table would be to declare that the Table had been waiting, for ages, to be seen. And that is false. The Weeping Table is not a place asking to be rescued from its own darkness. It is a place that has built its own way of knowing, its own way of touching, its own way of remembering. Its inhabitants do not need eyes to be alive. They need warmth, pressure, rhythm, moisture, and the long patient memory of water.

So the first thing a wise power would do would be to refrain from giving them a horizon.

A horizon is a promise of elsewhere. It tells a creature: you are in one place, and there is another. But the Weeping Table has no elsewhere in the usual sense. Everything is already here. The cloud sea presses beneath it. Heat presses through it. Stone presses against stone. The air presses against the skin. Life presses against life. To give these creatures a horizon would not comfort them. It would make them homesick for a world they have never needed.

The power would understand that.

And then it would look for the one who lives there.

Not “one” in the sense of pointing. Pointing is a trick of light. In the Weeping Table, pointing is useless. The power would have to find a being the way the herd finds a lost calf: by pressure, by breath, by a small wrongness in the rhythm.

And there was such a rhythm.

### The Quiet One

In a basin where three ancient pumps shared one low, broken current, there lived a creature no one had named.

It was not large. It was not a king, nor a monster, nor the last of a lost human line. It was a wide-body, born into the herd’s slow migration, but its skull was smooth where the others were crested. Its combs were thin, pale, incomplete. When it walked, the ground recorded its steps, but not the deep note of belonging. When it breathed, the air carried its heat, but not the layered signal that said: here is one of us.

The herd did not reject it.

To reject it would have been too easy, too visible, too light-like.

The herd simply failed to locate it.

It was a silence in a system made of sound.

When the herd moved, the quiet one moved beside them only if it found its own way. When storms rolled through the high mesas and water began to rise in the black bowls, the herd turned by pressure and vibration. The quiet one had to guess. It learned to stand close to the machines, because the machines had a heartbeat of their own: pistons, cables, grinding strata, geothermal breath. A pump-house with a broken flywheel could become a lighthouse to a creature without a crest. A cable humming in the wind could be mistaken, for a moment, for the sound of family.

It survived in this way.

But it was tired.

Not tired in the simple sense of legs and hunger. Tired in the deeper sense of a body that had learned to live as a missing piece of music. It had grown too still. Too careful. Too alone. And though it did not know what loneliness was—it had no word, no image, no story—its body knew that something should have answered when it pressed its flank against wet stone.

The power noticed this missing answer.

And now came the hard question.

What would a benevolent and wise power do?

### What the Power Could Have Done

It could have done many things that are easy to imagine from a distance, but impossible to do wisely.

It could have given the quiet one a crest of bone.

It could have brightened the basin with a phosphorescent fungus so that the herd might see it by the glow of moss.

It could have sent a great voice through the cloud sea, saying: *Be comforted. You are not forgotten.*

It could have made the herd notice, by stopping the rain for one heartbeat, or turning the wind warm for one hour, or placing a stone in such a place that its fall became a bell no creature could ignore.

It could have killed the predators.

It could have repaired the machines.

It could have made the plateau less dangerous, less wet, less strange, less dark.

Each of these things would have been a kindness from the point of view of someone who wanted a story with a beginning, a rescue, and a clean moral.

But the power was wise.

Wisdom is not the opposite of kindness.

Wisdom is kindness learned through the consequences of kindness.

The power saw that to give the quiet one a crest would be to say its body was an error to be corrected. To brighten the basin would be to make a place where no one could hide. To speak from the sky would be to introduce a new kind of being into the world, and the world already had beings enough: machines, predators, steam, rain, moss, click-midges, the slow herds. To kill the predators would be to feed the prey for one moment and starve the hunters for many. To repair every machine would be to take away the strange and necessary habitats that life had built into the ruins of industry. To make the plateau safer everywhere would be to make it dead elsewhere, because every ecology has edges. To be alive, one must also be exposed.

The power did not choose inaction.

That would be too cold.

Inaction can be a lie. Inaction can be the absence of benevolence.

Nor did it choose grand action.

That would be too violent.

So it chose what it could do in a place where everything is already full of presence.

It chose smallness.

It chose precision.

It chose to act not as a visitor, but as a tuning.

### The First Change

There was a cable on the ridge above the quiet one’s favorite shelter, a cable strung between two blackened posts and half lost in peat. For years, its current had produced no more than a faint pull against the teeth of nearby creatures. The quiet one slept beneath it because the cable hummed when the wind passed through the thrum-vines, and because the hum was almost—but not quite—like the breathing of the herd.

The power touched that cable.

Not with a hand.

With a patience.

With a single adjustment in the way the current moved through the wire.

The cable did not begin to glow. It did not begin to sing loudly. It merely changed its rhythm, by one part in a thousand. A frequency that had always been present, buried under the larger drone of the pumps, rose into sensitivity. It became a faint pulse. Not a warning. Not a summons. Not a command.

Just a pulse.

When the quiet one lay beneath the cable and breathed slowly, the cable answered it.

Not because the quiet one could hear a voice.

But because the ground beneath its belly carried the vibration, and the herd’s feet had spent millennia learning to read the ground.

The herd did not come from miles away to surround it.

But they began, when passing near the ridge, to adjust their column by a degree. A degree enough. Enough for the quiet one to feel the herd’s pressure without having to chase it blindly. Enough for the quiet one to stand closer to the slow bodies of its kin without being left in the hollow place behind.

This was not a miracle that made the quiet one visible.

This was a miracle that made the quiet one locatable.

And in a world without light, locatable is what love must become.
