## What the Wise Power Would Do

They would do almost nothing.

That is the hardest thing a benevolent and wise power can do: to look upon a strange and perfect place, to feel the old urge to help, to correct, to bless, to make it safer or softer or more legible, and then to understand that the highest kindness may be to withdraw the hand.

They would stand above the eastern lip, where the rain turns into sheet and mist, and they would not come down.

They would not step into the water.

They would not clear a path.

They would not plant a lichen.

They would not teach the animals to fear the deep pool, because the animals already know, in their bodies, that the deep pool is not where life continues.

They would not make a shelter from the broken basalt, because the broken basalt is already shelter.

They would not send a fire into the cold, because the cold is already the proper condition of the place.

They would not make the rain more merciful, because the rain is already speaking the only language the basin understands: arrival, pressure, dissolution, return.

If they were only benevolent, they might have given the mountain sea a garden.

If they were only wise, they might have left it untouched but cold, a fact without care.

Because they were both benevolent and wise, they would do the most difficult thing: they would care for it by not making it into a thing that needed their care.

---

### First, they would listen.

They would listen until the living roar stopped seeming like noise and became grammar.

Rain striking water.

Vents exhaling.

Shells clicking.

Tails beating the shelves.

Iron scraping calcium.

Glass grains falling.

Filaments unraveling.

A predator opening sideways in the half-silt.

A school turning, making one bright metallic thought across the basin.

A bubble rising from a black stone and breaking the surface with no witness.

To hear this, they would not need to understand it as a human understands a language.

They would only need to refrain from explaining it.

That is the beginning of wisdom.

---

### Then they would recognize the being.

The being who lives there is not one animal.

It is not the clatterfish.

It is not the shelf-gulcher lying broad on the eastern stone.

It is not the nodule-breaker cracking iron with its blunt claw.

It is not the drum-walker beating a low pulse through the sediment.

It is not the flue-cloak growing white around the vent.

It is not the dust-sifter drifting like smoke with structure.

It is not the curd-watcher grazing the boundary of the still deep pool.

It is the whole of it.

It is the basin as one functioning body.

Its skin is mineral fur.

Its blood is suspended stone.

Its breath is current.

Its heart is the vent-ring.

Its nerves are the schools.

Its teeth are the grazers.

Its reflexes are the predators.

Its thoughts are the drifting filaments.

Its silence is the deep pool.

The wise power would see this, and would feel the familiar temptation: to make it a person.

To give it a name.

To say, *This creature is aware.*

To speak to it.

To ask it what it wants.

But that would be a kindness that would ruin it.

Because the being is not a person.

It is a process.

It is a chemistry that has learned to be an ecology.

It is a place doing something so thoroughly that the boundary between place and being dissolves.

If they called it a creature, they would make it a subject.

If they made it a subject, they would make it lonely.

If they made it lonely, they would make it something that needs witnesses.

And that would be the beginning of its possession.

So they would not name it.

They would only hold its namelessness with reverence.

---

### Benevolence would have wanted to improve it.

Benevolence, when it is tender and impatient, looks at the world and sees absences.

It says: *There is no green here.*

It says: *There are no leaves, no roots, no flowers, no fields, no softness.*

It says: *Let me give it soil.*

It says: *Let me give it moss.*

It says: *Let me give it one small mercy the living familiar world understands.*

And so it would send a spore across the plateau.

It would carry a fragment of lichen on a bird’s foot.

It would allow a drop of rain to land in the wrong place with the right seed.

It would begin with a single green thing.

And the green thing would be a conqueror.

It would say: *This way is too hard, too clear, too mineral, too strange. Let me make it like the places you can tolerate.*

And then the basin would have a history it did not choose.

It would have softness imposed upon it.

It would have life made gentler, which is also life made easier to measure, easier to pity, easier to love by beings who cannot love what they cannot understand.

Wisdom would have said no.

Not because the basin is cruel.

But because it is whole.

A thing without plants is not automatically a thing waiting to become a forest.

A thing without soil is not automatically a place longing for loam.

A thing without makers is not automatically a thing that needs a maker.

The basin has no absence in the shape of a garden.

It has the shape of itself.

It is a world made of water, pressure, glass, mineral dust, ordinary light, grazing, scraping, dissolving, and return.

That is not a failure of life.

That is life having found another door.

---

### They would not make it safe.

The basin is not safe.

It is bounded.

Those are not the same.

The predators still strike from under black glass.

The silver plates scatter in bright fragments.

The jaws grind.

The fringed faces close.

The dust rises.

The vents pulse.

The animals die into mineral fur.

A benevolent and wise power would feel the grief of watching a life torn apart by another life, but wisdom would know that to remove death would be to remove the basin’s continuation.

They would not turn the knife-animals into peaceful things.

They would not teach the clatterfish to escape.

They would not soften the patience predators.

They would not make the curd-watchers immortal.

They would not send a gentle death to the one shelf-gulcher whose body has grown too heavy, too tired, too full of calcium.

They would let it lie down on the iron shelf and become a gift to the current.

That is not cruelty.

That is the deepest form of care: to not interfere with a system that is already answering itself.

---

### They would protect it by making it difficult to love in the wrong way.

A foolish power would make it visible.

It would say: *Look, the world! A miracle on a high plateau!*

Then there would be pilgrims.

Then there would be collectors.

Then there would be scientists with nets, jars, labels, and extraction teams.

Then there would be poets with soft shoes and soft eyes, who would call it a dream, and thereby make it a place for dreams to be taken.

Then there would be priests, and the basin would become holy, which is another way of becoming owned.

Then there would be governments, and the basin would become protected, which is another way of becoming managed.

Then there would be corporations, and the basin would become valuable, which is another way of becoming dead.

The wise power would understand this and would not reveal it.

They would leave it unknown.

But unknown is not the same as uncared for.

They would set around the place a boundary without making the boundary into a wall.

They would do it in small, natural ways.

A cloud would stay too long.

The trail would seem to be wrong before it was a trail.

Wind would make the scree unstable.

The plateau would become too cold, too wet, too exposed for casual pilgrimage.

The light would be ordinary but too direct for easy seeing.

The sound of the basin would be strange enough that anyone approaching alone would hear it and think of machines, danger, poison, or old weather.

People who came with taking in their hands would find their instruments useless.

People who came with worship in their eyes would find no place to kneel.

People who came with collecting in their minds would remember only headache, wet stone, and the unsettling sense that the water had looked at them.

The wise power would not send a monster.

Monsters invite heroes.

They would not send an angel.

Angels invite shrines.

They would not send a sign.

Signs invite followers.

They would send only the correct amount of difficulty and the correct amount of forgetting.

That is a high mercy.

---

### They would leave the ordinary sun alone.

The sun strikes the basin normally.

That is part of its strangeness.

If the light were alien, the place could be dismissed as a dream.

If the light were dim, it could become a metaphor for sadness.

If the light were golden and tender, it could become a postcard.

But the light here is clear, high, sharp, almost too honest.

It reveals the exact edge of every mineral fan.

It makes the water’s density visible.

It exposes the floor without mercy.

There is no false hiding.

No soft blur.

No forgiving dusk.

The wise power would love this clarity and would not alter it.

They would not make the world more beautiful in a way that made it easier to possess.

They would leave beauty difficult.

They would leave it factual.

They would leave it bright-edged.

Because the basin does not ask to be understood gently.

It asks to be seen exactly.

---

### They would not explain it.

A benevolent and wise power would feel the pressure of meaning.

It would say: *This place means something.*

But then, if it became wise, it would add: *Not in the way we want.*

The basin is not a lesson.

It is not a warning.

It is not an allegory of industry without intent.

It is not a parable of a world without plants.

It is not a proof that life can begin elsewhere.

It is not a mirror for human loneliness.

It is not a temple.

It is not a factory.

It is not a machine.

It is not a garden.

It is a mountain sea doing what a mountain sea does.

If the wise power explained it, they would make it smaller.

They would make it into a sentence.

And a sentence can be quoted, corrected, owned, argued about, taught in classrooms, and eventually lost.

The place itself is not lost so long as it remains unexplained.

So they would not write its truth for the world.

They would let its truth remain physical.

They would let it continue to be rain, vent, salt, plate, claw, filament, and return.

---

### If something threatened the basin, they would act as little as possible.

Suppose the rain failed.

Not for a season, but for a long and unnatural drought.

Then the power would shift one cloud a few degrees, so that rain arrived where it almost would have arrived anyway.

Suppose a boulder slipped and blocked the eastern lip, so the basin began to stagnate.

Then the power would relieve a small stress in the stone, allowing the boulder to roll back before anything died.

Suppose a poison began to seep from somewhere beyond the plateau, carried by a hidden vein.

Then the power would make one vent draw it inward and bury it beneath manganese before the water noticed.

Suppose a human came at night with tools, not knowing what they intended to take.

Then the power would cause that human to feel a wrongness in the air, a metallic thirst in the throat, a sense that the stone was listening, until they left.

Suppose the drought became too great, the vents too still, the animals too many and the water too thin.

Then perhaps the power would weep, but not into the basin.

They would weep upward, into weather.

One night, a storm would seem to come from nowhere.

One rain would fall harder than the others.

No one would call it miracle.

No one would write it in a book.

The clatterfish would scatter under the disturbance and return.

The mineral dust would rise, then settle.

The still deep pool would shiver once at the top.

The vents would pulse as if they had been waiting.

And the basin would continue.

That is the smallest possible intervention.

A wise and benevolent power would not make it dramatic.

Drama is for places that cannot survive without being noticed.

---

### They would not make it eternal.

This may be the hardest part.

A place so complete draws the love of any good power, and love immediately demands permanence.

*Let it last forever.*

*Let nothing change.*

*Let the young rock remain young.*

*Let the sharpness remain sharp.*

*Let the rain keep falling in the same gray-blue curtain.*

*Let the animals keep their forms.*

*Let the vents pulse forever.*

*Let the basin remain itself.*

But wisdom knows that eternity is not the highest gift.

Continuity is.

A thing that never changes is not alive in the fullest sense.

It is only preserved.

The mountain sea is alive because it is becoming.

Not because it wants to become something else.

But because it is doing itself, again and again.

If they made it eternal, they would take away its future.

They would make it a fixed miracle.

A fixed miracle is a cage.

So they would not stop time.

They would not prevent the cliffs from learning softness.

One day, weather would arrive.

Rain would fall differently.

The young rock would become old rock.

The basalt columns would round.

The salt crusts would thicken.

The vents might fail.

The basin might fill with sediment.

The water might turn shallow and gray.

Perhaps life would change.

Perhaps, after long ages, something like moss would come.

Perhaps plants would arrive and make the place ordinary in a different way.

Perhaps the basin would become a meadow, then a scree field, then a dry bowl, then nothing recognizable.

The benevolent and wise power would not curse this.

They would not stop it.

They would not build a wall around the basin to keep it young.

They would allow it to age.

They would allow it to complete itself.

And if it died, they would grieve without making the grief into a command.

They would let it go.

That is not the failure of love.

That is love without ownership.

---

### They would make room for one kind of presence.

At the deepest pool, where the water turns dark blue-green and still, the animals do not enter.

Not out of panic.

Not out of worship.

Not out of fear.

Their bodies stop where they cannot continue.

The wise power would understand this place.

They would not go into it.

They would not heal it.

They would not ask what it means.

They would simply stand, if standing is possible for such a power, at its outer edge.

They would be the one presence that comes near the impossible stillness and wants nothing from it.

Not curiosity.

Not knowledge.

Not salvation.

Not a secret.

They would only keep the boundary.

Not a boundary of command, but of witness.

They would see where life says *stop*, and they would not argue.

They would see the mineral curls at the edge, white carbonate rising like frozen skin, and they would not name them.

They would see the large pale animals grazing the threshold, and they would not ask them to be brave.

They would see the deep pool’s darkness, and they would not call it death.

They would not call it birth.

They would not call it god.

They would call it nothing.

And because they would call it nothing, it would remain what it is.

That is their final act of benevolence.

They give it the dignity of being uninterpreted.

---

### The one thing they would do, if they could do only one thing.

They would remove the temptation to help.

Not from the world.

From themselves.

They would look upon the basin, upon its sharp beauty, its mineral abundance, its loud quiet life, its ordinary light, its animal bodies fitted with terrible precision, and they would make the decision not to make it better.

They would say:

*This is not ours.*

*This is not a project.*

*This is not a lesson.*

*This is not a sign.*

*This is not a miracle.*

*This is not a sacred thing.*

*It is already sacred because it has no sacredness imposed on it.*

*It is already alive because it does not know it is alive in the way we require.*

*It is already complete because it does not require us.*

And then they would turn away.

But they would not turn away coldly.

They would turn away the way rain turns from a leaf after giving it enough water.

Not possessively.

Not lovingly in a way that burdens.

They would turn away by letting the place continue without their gaze becoming a weight.

Their absence would become the protection.

Their refusal to enter would become the gift.

---

### What they would actually do.

They would do almost nothing.

And if that sounds like nothing, it is because the basin does not need the kind of nothing that is indifference.

It needs the kind of nothing that is restraint.

It needs the kind of nothing that says:

*I see you, and I will not make you into a story.*

*I hear you, and I will not make you into a symbol.*

*I love you, and I will not come down.*

*I know you, and I will not use your knowing.*

*You are strange, and I will not make you strange to yourself.*

*You are young, and I will not keep you from growing old.*

*You are loud, and I will not make you silent for my comfort.*

*You are sharp, and I will not make you soft.*

*You have no maker, and I will not become your maker.*

*You have no gods, and I will not teach your animals to fear them.*

*You have no ruins, and I will not make you one.*

They would do almost nothing.

But their almost nothing would be exact.

They would keep the rain from becoming too cruel.

They would keep the light from becoming too beautiful in a way that invites hands.

They would keep the eastern lip from becoming a shrine.

They would keep the vents from becoming a secret.

They would keep the deep pool from becoming a god.

They would keep the animals from becoming symbols.

They would keep the mineral fur from becoming a metaphor.

They would keep the basin from becoming a meaning.

And if, in the end, the basin had to become a memory—

if the young rock had to age,

if the cliff had to soften,

if the water had to thin,

if the fish had to die into calcium,

if the vents had to close,

if the rain had to move elsewhere—

then the benevolent and wise power would do the last and gentlest thing of all.

They would remember it without calling it back.

They would hold its sound without repeating it.

They would keep its clarity without shining it into the eyes of others.

They would love it enough to leave it finished.

That is what they would do.

Almost nothing.

Exactly enough.
