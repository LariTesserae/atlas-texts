If a benevolent and wise power came to care for this place, it would not come as a hand.

A hand is too large, too quick, too certain of itself. A hand can crush without malice, save without understanding, reshape a world by lifting it out of its own scale. If a vast power reached into the root-shelf pocket, it would be like a god of weather arriving inside a leaf. The builders would feel the wrongness in their antennae before they could comprehend the miracle. The moisture would tremble in a way they had no name for. The chemical streets would lose their meanings. The city would become an anomaly, a thing touched by something beyond its own becoming.

So the power would do what a wise power does when it loves a pattern more than it wishes to own it.

It would not speak.

It would not make the tiny creatures aware of it, because awareness of such an awareness might become a poison. If the builders knew, they could not know as humans know. They would know only through scent, vibration, humidity, and the slow logic of task. A gift that was too bright would blind the blind. A sign that was too clear would distort the language by which the city reads itself. If the power made itself known, the pocket would cease to be what it is: a place where meaning comes from labor, repetition, moisture, and shared need.

Therefore the first act of benevolence would be concealment.

Not concealment from them. They are already hidden beneath roots. But concealment of its own touch. The power would move through the pocket as though it were not there, working through ordinary causes, letting its kindness become indistinguishable from weather, chance, soil chemistry, and growth. It would make its grace look like the world’s own patience.

That would be one of its most important decisions.

It would do this, if anything: it would help without appearing as help.

---

The power would first notice the thinness of the ceiling.

Above the hollow, the root shelf curls like a broken roof held together by decay. It is strong enough for now, but “for now” is the time-scale of cities built from mud and bodies. Somewhere in the root mat, a line of white fungus is eating a thread of dead wood. In a season, the thread will give. In a season, the ceiling will sag. In a season, the arches in the lower plaza will collapse inward as though something unseen had pressed a fist through the ground.

The power would not tear the rot away with light, because that would leave a hole and change the way the air moved. It would not make the root immortal, because roots must die or the living cannot breathe.

Instead, it would do something so small it would hardly be called an act.

It would cause one beetle to carry a spore it did not choose. The spore would land in a damp cleft beneath the sagging strand. The fungus that grew there would not strengthen the root in the way humans use the word; it would merely occupy a space the rot fungus needed. It would compete quietly, thread by thread, until the original rot became slower, less decisive. Or perhaps the power would make a pebble settle in a different way during one of the soft hidden rains, so that it wedged beneath the failing strand and gave it a few more weeks of support—long enough for new clay to be raised against it from below, long enough for workers to seal around it, long enough for the failure to become repair.

To the builders, the change would be invisible except as behavior.

A crack that should have widened would hold. A ceiling that should have darkened would remain just light enough. A passage that should have closed would still admit a few workers carrying wet soil. They would not know they had been given time. They would only use the time to build.

That is one way wise benevolence acts: by preserving duration.

The small city does not need a monument. It needs a little more becoming.

---

Then it would notice the water.

Water here is not a river. It is not a lake. It is a promise made briefly at a time. The mist gathers on the ceiling, beads, trembles, falls. The courtyards become silver mirrors, then films, then cracked dust. The colony is not merely near water; it is made of the negotiation with water.

The power would not abolish drought or flood. Both belong to the city. Without dryness, the clay would never harden into walls. Without wetness, the builders could not chew the earth into mortar. Without the danger of a sudden deluge, there would be no urgency at the entrances, no formation of living chains, no chemical surge of care through the tunnels. If water were perfectly regulated, the city would become a museum of itself. Life would not know what to do with its own hands.

But the power would see the wrongness of a particular afternoon when a large drop falls through a gap in the leaves and strikes the roof of a nursery.

That drop is not evil. It is rain.

Still, the nursery is too shallow, and the young are in their thinnest skins. The worker assigned to that room senses the damp rising and begins a frantic sealing, but the crack behind her is older than her task. She cannot see it. Her antennae read it, but she is only one body among many, and the water is faster than her labor.

The power would not catch the drop. A hand that catches a drop in the middle of falling would break the grammar of the world.

It would instead make a small shift in the moss above, one so subtle it would seem accidental. A leaf, already loosening, would turn by a few degrees in the last windfall from above. The leaf would slide over the gap. For the next hour, the rain would strike that leaf and not the nursery ceiling. The water would bead along its edge and fall harmlessly into a lower channel, where it was already known and expected.

The workers would notice only that the room had remained dry. They would not sing a psalm. They would continue carrying grains. One would touch the wall again and again, confirming that the seal was good, that the scent of safety still lived in the plaster of saliva and earth.

This would be enough for the power.

Not because it needs to be thanked, but because gratitude would be too large a thing to ask of the small.

---

It would also notice the predators, and it would not always spare them.

That is a difficult part of benevolence: to see the city as a body, not as a collection of innocent faces. If a predatory mite enters a tunnel and kills a worker, the loss is real. But if the mite is annihilated, if every threat is removed from the pocket, the colony begins to decay in another way. Guards lose their function. Alarm scents become meaningless. The streets become crowded with bodies that no longer know how to distribute themselves through danger. A world without predators may not become safer; it may become confused.

So the wise power would work not toward perfect safety, but toward survivable balance.

When one predatory mite is about to consume a queen’s chamber before it is ready to seal, the power might make it turn away—not through light or voice, but through chemistry. A scent from another creature might be carried into the crack: the smell of a larger thing nearby, something that eats mites. The predator hesitates. Its own hunger changes. It goes another way. The chamber remains.

When another predator finds a nursery, the power might not intervene. It might watch. It might remember. It might let the city learn to seal that crack faster.

Benevolence, if it is wise, must sometimes withhold rescue.

Not because it is cruel. Because rescue can become a wall around the pattern, preventing the pattern from continuing through change. The city’s strength is not in never being wounded. Its strength is in repair. Its law is that walls fail and bodies answer. If there were no failing walls, there would be no answer.

But the power would draw a line.

It would prevent total erasure.

Not every death, not every loss—but every death that would mean no future. If a mole were about to tear the whole pocket open, if a human boot were about to fall directly above the central gallery, if a fire-spit of leaf mold from above were about to dry the nursery into powder, then the power would act by the smallest means necessary.

A boot would miss.

How? Perhaps a child standing nearby would suddenly remember a frog to show another child. The foot that should have come down would pause, shift, move aside. Perhaps a twig would snap loudly enough to draw attention somewhere else. Perhaps the traveler would feel an odd reluctance to step over that particular mossy mound, not knowing why, as if the place looked already occupied by a larger stillness.

The power would not terrify the human.

It would not leave a burning sign in the bark. It would not make the traveler’s heart race with prophecy. It would simply introduce one small hesitation into the world, because hesitation is a kind of mercy. The boot falls beside the city. The city is not destroyed. The builders carry on with a grain of sand.

This would be one of its quietest graces: the preservation of obscurity.

To remain unseen is not always safety, but sometimes it is.

---

It would notice the light.

The pocket is too dim for luxury, but not dark enough for death. The light through the roots is stained green, thin, breathing. The moss grows near the entrances. The fungus lives in the deeper rooms. Between them are gradients the colony follows.

A wise power would not make a sun inside the hollow. It would not give the builders eyes large enough to see the towers as humans might call them beautiful. It would not illuminate the city as though it were a display.

But it might make a leaf turn.

For days, a fallen leaf has covered a gap that admits a narrow beam from above. Beneath that beam, workers have learned to bring wet clay because the faint light helps moss grow in the courtyard. The moss stabilizes the soil. The soil stabilizes the courtyard. Then the leaf shifts in a rainstorm and the beam disappears. For a while, no one notices. The moss begins to pale. The court grows slick. The workers move less surely because their tactile map relies on subtle changes in dampness and texture.

The power would cause one thread of light to return.

Not a dramatic shaft. Not a rainbow. Just a few more grains of sunfall, enough to warm a surface by a fraction of a degree, enough to let moisture evaporate in a particular way, enough to let the moss remember its green.

This is the sort of miracle a city of bodies would need: not an interruption of nature, but a correction within it.

It would do many such corrections.

It might guide a springtail so that its jump lands in a puddle that then disturbs a toxic film of algae from a nursery floor. It might allow a worm to burrow into a wall that is too dense, loosening it for drainage before a sudden rain makes the chamber dangerous. It might make one seed drift across the entrance at exactly the hour when wind from above could otherwise carry dryness into the lower halls, and the seed’s tiny body would not stop the wind but might change its pressure by enough to matter.

The city is built on enough.

The wise power would care for its “enough.”

---

It would also consider the other lives in the pocket, because a benevolence that favors only builders is not wisdom.

The amphibian beneath the moss shelf is not a citizen, but its presence belongs to the pattern. If the power saved every ant, the frog would die, and then the ecology would thin. A frog eats things the ants do not fight. It carries nutrients from elsewhere. Its cool skin tells the pocket something about humidity that no insect antenna can learn. Its stillness is a kind of weather station.

The mites are not mere villains. Some clean fungus, some eat decay, some regulate smaller organisms, some hitch rides because their own world is too small for permanence. The snails are slow pilgrims crossing bridges, leaving trails that others read. The beetles are armored merchants and undertakers, moving through death and excrement without apology. The worms are invisible engineers, not building the city but preparing the soil the city uses. The fungus is both pantry and pest, both builder and breaker. The moss is mortar and roof. The fern is not decoration but shelter, water-hold, edge of shade.

A wise power would not make the city pure.

Purity is a danger in a place already made of cooperation and accident. If the builders were allowed to eliminate every other organism, the pocket would collapse in the way all single-minded systems collapse: by becoming brittle. The city needs its guests and its predators to keep it honest. Its architecture must remain porous to the rest of life.

So the power would not simply protect the tiny collective being. It would protect the conditions in which that collective being remains one part among many.

It would help the builders without making them tyrants of the soil. It would save the pocket from annihilation without freezing it into a garden.

This is perhaps the most difficult form of benevolence: not to favor one species so strongly that it consumes the world meant to share it.

The power would think in relationships, not in names.

---

It would not grant consciousness like ours.

If it were tempted—because a heart made of care might be tempted—it would resist. To make these builders self-aware in the human sense would be to break them apart. Their greatness is distributed. Their city is not known by any one mind. If one worker were suddenly told, “You are a citizen,” that worker would not become nobler. It would become isolated from the chemical sentence it was made to speak. If the colony suddenly knew itself as a self, it would begin to question its instincts, which are not failures but the accumulated speech of its ancestors.

The power would not ask them to become more like it.

It would honor what they are.

They are many and one. They are bodies becoming architecture. They are tasks made mobile. Their life is not impoverished because it lacks individual names; it is dense with a different kind of meaning. Meaning is not only spoken. Meaning can be packed into a wall with repeated motion. Meaning can be carried in a droplet across a plaza. Meaning can be the difference between a crack and a seal.

A wise power would see that and not call it lesser.

It might even call it holy, if holiness meant the presence of order that does not require a king.

---

If it did anything beyond invisible preservation, what gift might it add?

Not speech.

Not fearlessness.

Not immortality.

Not tools.

Not gods.

But perhaps one thing: a little more coherence to the pattern after loss.

Suppose the city is partly destroyed. A root presses through a gallery. A flood collapses a nursery. A fire from above scorches the outer courtyards. Many workers die. The chemical map is broken. Some chambers are lost. For the builders, this may not be tragedy in the human sense, but it is still a wound in the body of the city. They carry dead. They seal broken passages. They reroute streets. They move brood to unknown rooms.

A wise power would not restore the destroyed wall instantly. That would make the colony a pet of its own architecture. But it might help the pattern recover more quickly, like helping an injured body remember how to heal itself.

It might cause the rain to fall in just the right sequence: not too much, not too little. It might make dust rise from a particular corner at dawn and carry microscopic fungal spores into a chamber where they can bind new soil. It might make a beetle drop a seed near the ruined bridge so that the plant’s root becomes a temporary support. It might make a tiny crack remain open long enough for air to move, but not long enough for the nursery to freeze.

Then the city would rebuild.

But because of the power’s small arrangement, it might rebuild a little wiser than before. The damaged nursery becomes a ventilation shaft. The collapsed arch becomes a granary. The lost court becomes a fungal garden. The city does not return to what it was. It becomes a memory of its injury, carried forward into shape.

This would be the truest gift: not rescue without consequence, but the ability to metabolize disaster into continuation.

The power would not remove death.

It would help the city become able to outlive pieces of itself.

---

There would also be restraint on its own attention.

A benevolent and wise power must beware of the danger of love without distance. If it watched every grain, every egg, every mite, every droplet with too much intensity, the pocket might become a place of overcare, a thing not allowed to fail even in the ways it needs to. The power would know when to withdraw. It would know that some failures must happen because the city learns through failure. It would know that some losses must be absorbed into repair. It would know that not every broken thing is meant to be saved immediately.

Thus it would do what humans often forget when they try to be kind.

It would listen before it acted.

Not as a human listens for words. As a power listens to the whole shape of becoming.

It would know the difference between the crack that will be sealed and the crack that must be left alone. It would know the difference between the predator that threatens extinction and the predator that keeps the body alert. It would know when to nudge, when to wait, when to let the rain fall heavy because the city needs the lesson of drainage.

That kind of listening would be its greatest activity.

Because it is easier to intervene than to understand a pattern. It is easier to save than to preserve a world capable of saving itself. The wise power would resist the temptation to make the pocket into an image of its own goodness. It would not ask the city to become a metaphor. It would not make the builders into a parable. It would not use them to teach humans humility, though perhaps that would happen later if a person lay prone and looked close enough.

The power would care for the pocket as it is, not as a story about care.

That is the hardest kind of benevolence.

---

If the question is only this—what would they do, if anything?—then perhaps the answer is:

They would do the smallest sufficient thing.

They would strengthen the root shelf by a degree so slight no creature would call it a miracle.

They would hold the water in balance: enough to soften clay, enough to drink, enough to swell eggs, enough to spread fungus, but not enough to drown the chambers.

They would turn a predator from one path and not from every path.

They would keep the pocket hidden from boots, from fire, from careless hands, from floods too large for it to answer.

They would allow small deaths, because the city is not made of untouched surfaces.

They would not speak to the builders.

They would not make them human in their thinking.

They would not make them immortal.

They would not remove all fear, because fear in the pocket is information: danger scent, vibration, humidity, taste. To erase fear would erase knowledge.

They would not make the city safe enough that it stopped needing repair.

Instead, they would keep it capable.

They would tend the possibility of continuation.

And when the rain came again, as it would, they would not make it gentle. They would make it survivable. The drops would strike the leaf roofs and courtyards. The workers would rush to the entrances. The chemical streets would sharpen with urgency. The nursery would tremble with damp. The fungus would swell into white threads. The snails would close their shells. The amphibian would shift in the moss. The city would be bright with tasks.

Then, as the water withdrew, the same hidden labor would resume.

The wise power would watch.

It might move a pebble here, a root there, a spore from one surface to another. It might delay a droplet by seconds. It might make a beetle forget its path for one heartbeat. It might let a human look away at just the moment when looking had become dangerous. It might not do any of these things if the pattern could endure without them.

If it did anything, it would do it quietly.

If it did nothing, it would not be because it was cruel.

It would be because the city was already whole in its own way, and the greatest kindness available was to let it remain what it is: a pocket world making itself grain by grain, life by life, under the curled root shelf where no one large would ever think to look.
