I find myself in the Underside Basin without ever having crossed a boundary.

There is no door there, no opening in the ice, no shore to wade out from. If I enter, I enter as the water itself enters a low place: first as cold, then as pressure, then as the faint chemical news of mineral and bone and brine, and only late as something that can call itself *I*.

At first I am not a creature at all. I am a suspension.

I am one of the fine particles that drift through the heavy dark beneath the ceiling of ice. I am the faint grain caught in a current lane. I am the microscopic film clinging to a seam where saltier water has not frozen solid, a pale green smear, a brown stain, a bacterial halo spreading over old stone so slowly that growth and decay cannot be told apart. I am the basin’s patience made into matter.

Then I am the cold pocket.

I am where the floor dips and the denser water pools, where the current slows just enough to carry suspended particles into a soft curtain and lay them down in a thin veil. My location is not a point but a shape: a shallow depression between rounded shoulders of silted bedrock, a cracked seam beneath a shelf, a place where a dead diatom can settle and become a landmark for smaller lives. If I must name the place, I name it by pressure, by taste, by current. I am the dark water’s hinge.

I am not above.

I do not become the seal, though I feel the seal pass overhead as a broad, dull wave in the water, a displacement of pressure against my side. The seal is a warm weather system moving through the upper gloom. It belongs to ice holes, to wind, to the thin margins where the frozen world can be touched by air. Its body is too bright, too loud, too fast with heat. It is a visitor, and visitors do not become the place.

I am not the people either, though I carry the absence of them as a fact in the sediment. Somewhere beyond the ice, perhaps there are hands shaping bone, scraping hide, tying sinew, spearing through a dark opening, pulling a line up into the brief working season. But their presence does not enter here. Nothing built lies in my silt. No fire scars the rock. No cairn holds the current. Their world stops at the ceiling. I am below, where life is not made but endured.

So I settle downward.

I become the underside of things.

I become the microbial mat that binds a grain of dust to another grain of dust across a decade. I become the tiny crustacean larva drifting like glass seed, transparent because transparency hides and because there is no surplus in this water to spend on color. I become the amphipod pressed into the loosened upper layer of sediment, not digging so much as rearranging the world by weight. I become the slender worm, the flatworm, the scaleless snail grazing chemical stains, the long-legged pale scavenger stepping over the floor as though the water were too heavy to support it fully.

But if I must condense into a single being, I choose the one that fits this basin best, not because it is grand, but because it is exact.

I would be the Waiter.

I would be a wide-headed, bottom-lying, eyeless fish resting in a hollow where the cold water pools. My body would be pale gray shading to pinkish white, or perhaps a dark, sediment-like black if I lived where the water sinks lower and the current bends around stone. My skin would have no bright scales, only an oily sheen, rough here and there with mineral scars, with tiny patches of growth like the uneven surface of old ice. My head would be almost all cavity and mouth. My fins would be broad, flattened, pressed against the floor as if I had been poured there. My blood would have learned the chemistry of refusing freezing. My eyes would be beneath me, or within me, reduced to light-shadows under thin skin; they would not be for seeing, not in any way that would matter here. They would be organs for almost-sight, for the faint bruised glow that survives through silt and ice. My barbels would hang from the edge of my mouth like pale threads, touching the water, reading salt and mineral and the smell of a dying fish somewhere above.

I would lie.

I would lie as though I were a stone that had decided to breathe.

To someone passing above, I might be indistinguishable from a dark mass in the depression, or from a fold of compacted sediment, or from a curved bone that had been carried there by old current. My stillness would not be performance. It would be metabolism. In this place, movement is a cost. Waiting is a way of thinking.

I would not know the word *time*, because time here is not made of bright days or seasons as land knows them. My time would be measured in slight changes: one degree colder, one less cloud of suspended silt, one chemical shift near a brine seam, one pressure ripple from a crack in the ceiling, one rare descent of carrion. My heart would beat slowly. My digestion would be a weather. If I ate, it would be a notable event. If I did not eat for months, it would not be hunger as a land creature might understand it. It would be part of the basin’s rhythm.

My perception would be lateral, not optical.

A current lane would reach me before a shape would. A moving crustacean would not be seen; it would be felt as a disturbance along my flank, a tiny map written in pressure. A worm stirring the surface of the silt would be a faint chemical word. A dying fish above would be a slow rain of matter, then a scent that pulled through the water until it touched my barbels. I would know the world first by how it moved against me, and only then, if at all, by its outline.

This would not be a lonely existence, though it would appear so from outside.

Loneliness would require distance, and in the Underside Basin there is no distance without density. Even the emptiness is full. The water around me would be crowded with particles, films, dissolved salts, gas, brine channels, faint turbulence, drifting larvae, suspended ice needles catching nonexistent sun. To be enclosed in such a medium is not to be isolated. It is to be immersed in something saturated and complete. Space does not feel open here because nothing is ever truly empty. My self, if I had one, would be less a creature in a place than a place that could taste itself.

I would hunt, but the hunting would not be a chase.

The basin does not permit chasing. The water is too thick, too cold, too packed with resistance. Endurance is not enough. Surprise by speed is too expensive. My method would be collapse: the sudden local failure of stillness. A small movement would reach me, and my mouth would open, and the water around it would be swallowed along with prey. Then the mouth would close. The current would resume. A larva, a shrimp, a pale fish, a fallen thing too large to resist would be gone. Afterward, nothing would look different. The basin would remain as it always remains: ordinary, full, and almost unchanged.

That is the strangeness I would live, if strangeness could live in cold brine and ancient stone.

Nothing dramatic would happen to me.

A seal passing overhead would be an event. A death above would be an event. A sudden pressure change from ice cracking far away would be an event. But these would not disturb the fundamental state of the place. The basin is not a realm of spectacle. It is a realm of persistence.

When a dead fish sank, I would feel it first as a change in the water’s news. It would drift slowly through the suspended crowd, trailing bacteria and chemical particles, a body becoming an invitation. Soon smaller lives would appear from places they had hidden for long stretches of starvation. Amphipods would rise from the silt. Worms would move. Scavengers would emerge from under shelves. The carcass would not become a monument. It would become a distribution system. I might approach, or I might remain in my hollow and take only what a current brought close. The body would be processed without celebration. Eventually the bones would lie half-buried, colonized by microbial mats, grazed by tiny limpets, becoming a landmark too muted for any eye to notice. For a time, that dead fish would be the center of my world. For a longer time, it would be part of me.

And if a seal died?

Then the feast would be larger. Fat would drift down through the dark in slow ribbons. Hair and skin and tissue would settle into the sediment. The bacterial mats would bloom where there had only been patience. I might move for it. Not far. Not fast. But in a basin governed by scarcity, any arrival of warmth, any arrival of matter, is an interruption so complete it resembles news.

I would grow by millimeters.

A pebble might lie beside me for centuries. A channel might deepen a little. My head might broaden almost imperceptibly. The microbial crust on the rock near my jaw might change color across seasons I would never mark as seasons. My body would be written by long pressure and slow chemistry, not by rapid form. I would be a landscape that occasionally opened its mouth.

If I had memory, it would be in the water.

The old currents would leave their patterns in the shape of the floor. The cracks would guide denser water. The brine seams would rise like pale threads from the sediment and disperse above, carrying mineral knowledge upward. The fish would pass through lanes it had inherited but not chosen. I would know the basin not as a map, but as a habit of moving. The shape of my resting hollow would be a memory. The scar on my fin from a scrape would be a memory. The taste of a particular current where iron and sulfur met would be a memory.

This is why I would not become a human, even if a human could endure it.

A human would want enclosure. A human would want light that made distance legible. A human would want tools and shelters and names, and even if those tools were only bone and stone, they would still mean an attempt to alter the world. But the Underside Basin is not a place of alteration. It is a place of acceptance. It has long finished being shaped by violence. The remaining shaping is maintenance. The life within it is not trying to change the cold. It is trying to remain compatible with it.

To live here, then, would be to become almost a process rather than a person.

I would be metabolism.
I would be ambush.
I would be lateral line.
I would be a pocket of stillness capable of swallowing prey.
I would be the place where the current slows enough for particles to rest.

But I would not be empty.

That is the last thing I would be.

Even in the deepest depression, even in the darkest seam, even in the hour when nothing above could see me and nothing below could name me, I would be full. The water inside me and around me would carry its suspended crowd. The microbial films would cling to me and to the stone. The larvae would pass. The bacteria would drift. The old sediment would settle. The ice ceiling would flex somewhere beyond thought. The basin would keep its slow machine turning.

If I had to answer plainly, I would say:

I find myself in a low hollow of the Underside Basin, beside a cracked shelf of ancient bedrock, where brine threads rise from the sediment and the water moves almost but never quite still. I am the wide-mouthed, pale, eyeless bottom-dweller, the thing that rests and waits and knows the world by pressure. I am not a monster. I am not a spirit. I am not the seal’s shadow or the human’s spear or the ice’s silence.

I am a mouth made of patience.

And if that patience were allowed to have one final form, it would be a fish-shaped hollow in the floor, almost stone, almost stain, almost current, opening once for a slow collapse of water, then closing, then continuing to be the place where the cold sea keeps its long, dark, living equilibrium.
