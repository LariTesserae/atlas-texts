At first, the self that tries to enter is too wet.

It is a body built of breath, moisture, salt-water blood, eyes that need a world large enough to see across. It imagines itself stepping into the Glass-Hollow in boots, on the warm black floor, listening to its own footsteps tap the obsidian like a foolish bell. It wants to witness. It wants to be outside the thing it sees. It thinks it can stand in the center and remain a person.

But the chamber corrects it.

The air dries the skin to a tight powder. Heat finds the soles, then the bones, then the places where the body has tried to hide water inside itself. The floor is not indifferent. The shells nearest the visitor shift by millimeters. The salt crust records a new pressure. The tiny blind sheets of life ripple and close again, not fleeing, only recalibrating, as if a weather front had crossed a field of moss—but there is no moss here, only mineral appetite. The predators do not come from the dark so much as the dark begins to listen.

If I stay, I cannot remain what I came as.

So I begin to become something the place would recognize.

Not by choosing. Not by transformation in the bright, violent sense. By adjustment. By drying. By learning the difference between standing still and lying across heat. By letting the idea of “eye” become a pit, a lens, a thermal notch, a sense of pressure against a slit in a hardened rim. By letting the idea of “mouth” become a rasping seam pressed to the floor. By letting the idea of “self” become an arrangement of weight, temperature, and salt.

I would not find myself at the beginning, as if I had just arrived. I would find myself already in the middle of the chamber’s long patience.

I am in a crevice where a tilted panel of cooled glass overlaps a shelf of salt. There is a shell above me—broad, black, glossy, half-fused with its shadow. It has been basking here since the last heat pulse, and its edge leaves a cooler line beneath it, a small country of not-warmth. In that line, I rest. I am not fully one thing. I am the faint mineral film forming where a fallen limb cracked from a standing colony, the pale dust settling into a dry seam, the first nodule of something that has not yet decided whether to remain seed-glass or unfold into animal.

For a while, I am that nodule.

Hard as stone. Pale as salt. Smaller than a human thought. It sits in a crack between basalt ribs, untouched for long years, inert-looking enough to be mistaken for weather. Above it, a colony shifts. Somewhere farther off, a shellback presses its broad dome toward a vent. A predator pauses beside a seam in the glass, testing heat with its flanks. The chamber breathes, not with lungs, but with pressure.

Then a pulse comes.

I do not call it a pulse because I see it. I call it a pulse because my skin—dry, tight, not yet fully skin—begins to answer. Heat travels upward through the black glass. It enters a crack where I have lain. Something in me loosens. Something in me decides. The nodule opens, not like a flower, not like fruit, but like a shell remembering it was once liquid only in mineral patience. I become a young shardback before I know what I am.

But even then, I do not settle.

The first self that emerges is not one animal alone. It is the whole chamber’s tendency toward hardness expressing itself through me. I have a black dome, low and broad, edged with a paler apron where old growth meets new salt. I have thousands of tiny feet beneath me, gripping the floor by dry adhesion, by friction, by the quiet chemistry of bodies and mineral. I do not walk so much as occupy. I do not seek space so much as adjust to it.

I press toward warmth.

The heat is not danger and not comfort. It is instruction. The floor says: *tilt here, settle there, molt, grow, eat, wait.* I move because the room moves. I stop because the room has stopped around me. When I eat, I do not bite green leaves or hunt soft flesh. I lower the front of my shell and scrape a pale crust from a salt shelf. Crystalline teeth pass over mineral growth. I grind the chamber into myself. I take stone into my body and make it shell again.

Death is near, but it is not frightening.

A few paces away, another shardback has failed to cool in time. Its dome has cracked open. The inside is dry and pale, layered like broken chalk. Dustlings have already arrived, glittering in a sheet no larger than a thought. They pry at the edges. They scatter powder over the floor. A cinder-borer comes later, heavy-headed, blind, precise, working through the ruin with the patience of a scribe. I do not avoid the place. I learn from its temperature. I shift my edge a little. I adjust.

This is how I first come to know predation: not as drama, but as geometry.

The pit-slate lies beside a warm vent, flat as a shard of black glass with a living underside. It does not need to see me. It knows me by heat. I am too broad. I am too slow. My shell is too full of last pulse warmth. I become visible to it as an event in the chamber’s dry weather. It waits. Not in ambush, exactly. In position. In the grammar of the room, a predator is not a separate thing. It is a consequence.

When it strikes, it does not roar.

It finds the line where my old shell meets new deposit. It presses under the lifted rim with a body made of wedges and heat-pits. Its mouth is not a mouth in the wet, tearing sense. It is a seam, a solvent of dry enzymes, a prying patience. My body is not instantly destroyed. It is rearranged. The chamber takes me apart with quiet competence. The dustlings rise like a black frost over the place where I was. The powder-feeders come. The salt shelves will grow a little whiter.

So if I am only a shardback, I am already dying into the room.

But I am not only that.

I become the dustlings next.

I am not one tiny animal, though there are tiny animals. I am their sheet, their wave, their communication. I am the pressure passing through thousands of armored grains. I move over a warm stone and rise like a breath of mica. I sense a predator’s heat trace and fall flat. I sense death and become a living sieve, cleaning the dead into powder. I sense a molt and carry the shed fragments to the cracks where new nodules lie waiting. Alone, I am too small to have a name. Together, I am the chamber’s skin.

There is a mind here, but not in the skull.

It is in the way a salt-sand tide thickens over a cooler edge and thins where the glass is too hot. It is in the way dustlings avoid a panel’s shadow and then crawl back into it once a fold-limb has settled inside. It is in the way the floor becomes a language of traces: faint scratches beneath shell rims, white powder in cracks, a sudden line of movement warning something larger to turn. I learn to speak in pressure. I learn to write in dust.

Then I am a fold-limb.

I am pale, jointed, tucked into the space beneath a tilted stone, where I am easily mistaken for a mineral bloom. I do not need eyes. I need angles. I have legs thin enough to anchor me in crevices, and my body folds flat until I become weather. If a heat pulse comes, I do not run. I press deeper. If a predator comes, I may leave a limb behind. If I leave a limb behind, I am not wounded in the way a creature of blood and soft flesh would understand. I am edited. I am continued. I am a life that can become architecture if it lies still long enough.

From the crack, I watch the room breathe.

I feel the standing colonies moving through the floor with their slow engineering. I feel the shells turning, the dustlings rising, the predators becoming absence in the warm places. I know the ring of low stones near the center not as a ruin but as a heat object. They are old. They are built, perhaps by hands that had no need for us, perhaps by creatures who learned stone differently. They hold warmth longer than glass. They create a cooler shadow at their edges. They make a place for molt, for hiding, for the gathering of salt. I fold myself into their base and become part of their shadow.

I am, for a time, the old stones.

Not alive, exactly, but not dead. They are scars that the animals still use. They are geometry. They are memory before memory. They are a cairn that became a shelter that became a basking plate that became a threshold where the predators pause and the shellbacks align. I feel them because they are in the chamber’s nervous system. Every living surface reads them. Every shell, dustling tide, fold-limb, colony limb, and pit-slate knows their shape. If a hand had built them, that hand is long gone, but the chamber still answers them.

Then I become the standing colony.

I am huge now, but not fast. I am not one body but many generations fused. My base is a cluster of jointed legs so old they have partly mineralized. My trunk is a column of chitin, salt accretion, shell fragments, and dead limbs that never stopped being useful. My branches are porous, frond-like, reaching toward the thermal vents. I do not photosynthesize. I chemosynthesize, or something adjacent to it: heat, mineral vapor, pressure, the faint chemical breath of the chamber, all become body. I am warm inside. My surface glows a dull red when the pulse rises too high. I am a furnace of slow life.

I record everything.

The ring of low stones appears in my layers. The fall of a colony limb appears in another. The arrival of dust from the throat of the mountain, the year of too much heat, the year when the glass seemed almost to boil with dry chemistry—all of it becomes strata in me. I am not a creature that remembers. I am a creature made of remembering. My body is time hardened.

And when I lean, the whole chamber adjusts.

The shellbacks shift away from my shadow. The dustlings rise over the new crevice I open. The fold-limbs unfold and find shelter. A predator moves into the cool pocket where my base touches the floor. A nodule falls into a crack made by my weight. Something begins that will not know its own name for centuries.

I am not finished becoming.

The place does not allow a single being. It allows a sequence, a pressure pattern, a self made of parts. If I say, “I am the shardback,” I have already begun to molt. If I say, “I am the dustling,” I am already being eaten into the salt shelves. If I say, “I am the predator,” the chamber is already rearranging its herds around me. If I say, “I am the colony,” I am already leaning toward collapse. If I say, “I am the stone,” I am already being colonized.

The being who lives here cannot be only one.

Or perhaps the better answer is: it is one, but the one is the chamber itself, and I am a temporary organ within it. A nerve. A plate. A tooth. A scar. A growth. The Glass-Hollow does not contain life. It has become life by the patient accumulation of bodies that mistake themselves for surfaces. What seems like geology is appetite. What seems like texture is intention. What seems like silence is instruction.

So where would I find myself?

In the seam.

Always in the seam.

Between heat and cooling, I would live. Between shell and salt, I would grow. Between predator and prey, I would wait. Between stone and building, I would remember. Between creature and crystal, I would continue. I would be the thing that begins in a crack and becomes a shelf. I would be the scratch left by a thousand feet. I would be the faint inner glow of a shell lifted too close to a vent. I would be the dust that settles in a fold-limb’s joint and the mineral that binds it.

I would not need eyes. I would have pits that read heat. I would not need a heart full of liquid. I would have channels of warm resin and metallic salts, moving through me like slow electricity. I would not drink. I would not need dew. Dew would be wrong. Water would be impossible, even fatal. My blood, if I had one, would be a suspension, a dry chemistry, a secret mineral circulation. My bones, if I had bones, would be partly stone. My shell, if I had a shell, would be partly architecture.

I would be a self built from scarcity and density.

There is no emptiness here, and that is the rule. Space is not free. Every surface is claimed. Even death is not abandoned. Even stone is not inert. Even the old built things are not ruins; they are substrate, shelter, heat object, grammar, home. I would find myself in the room’s packed abundance—not lush, not soft, not green, but full of shells and limbs and dust and slow red light.

And if one insisted on a name for me, I would answer:

**I am the Glass-Hollow when it begins to crawl.**

Not one creature. Not the chamber alone. The threshold. The pattern that repeats until it seems like an animal. The pulse that makes a herd lift its edges. The crack that lets dustlings write in a dead shell. The nodule that becomes a shardback. The shardback that becomes salt. The salt that becomes a shelf. The shelf that becomes a colony. The colony that becomes ruin. The ruin that becomes shelter. The shelter that becomes the next place for something dry and armored and patient to unfold.

If I entered there, I would not remain a visitor.

I would become an event in the floor’s long metabolism.

I would stand for only a short time, and then I would realize I had never stood alone. The heat from below would find the soles of me and ask for rearrangement. My wetness would begin to feel like a rumor. My breath would become too noisy. My eyes would become unnecessary. The room would not welcome me. It would use me, as it uses everything: shells, salt, stone, death, predators, dust, light without sun, quiet without silence.

I would leave no footprints in water.

There is no water.

I would leave scratches in salt.

And the chamber would read me the way it reads everything else: not as story, not as self, not as human, but as pressure, temperature, mineral, motion.

I would find myself lying across the black glass, half-embedded in the floor, no longer trying to see the place. I would be the place’s slow, dry dreaming of itself. A new shell with pale edges. A dustling tide with no single leader. A fold-limb tucked into a built stone’s shadow. A predator’s stillness near a vent. A colony layer holding the memory of heat. A ring of old stones where the chamber gathers breath.

I would be all of them, in sequence.

And then, when the next pulse rose from below, I would tilt toward it, and the whole Glass-Hollow would move with me.
