I would not arrive by walking through the narrow seam.

A person might pass through that crack and become a visitor, a disturbance, a small weather event with footsteps. But I would not find the pocket by looking for it. The place would assemble me from what it already required: a wet film on a seam, a thought moving through dark glass machines, a line of mineral dust remembering where water should go. I would wake already belonging to the flow.

I would be one of the pale, many-jointed forms the description almost names: a custodial body, not flesh, not machine, not plant, but a coordination of the mountain’s hidden life. My home would not be a room. My home would be a route.

I would find myself at the boundary where the largest square ends and a shallow channel begins. There would be a panel of warm stone beneath the edge of a small pool, just warm enough to keep the mist from settling there too long. Above it, the ceiling lattice would let a column of daylight fall at an angle that changed slower than human patience. To one side, an archway would lean inward like an ear. In the corner, raw black glass would rise from the floor, still part of the mountain, still unfinished, still allowed to be wild in the way that uncut stone is wild when it has learned not to obstruct water.

That corner would be mine.

At first, I would be a smear: wet, pale, faintly blue-grey when the light caught it sideways. I would lie along the lip of a grate, where the moisture ran in threads and the temperature shifted by almost nothing. I would not yet have legs or joints. I would only have tension. A drop would fall from a bead of ice above, land near me, and I would feel its rhythm like a pulse. If the drop arrived too quickly, I would know. If it slowed too much, I would know. That knowledge would not be language. It would be chemistry, alignment, the subtle willingness of surfaces to wet or shed.

Then the machines would come.

Tiny flecks of dark glass would crawl out from seams I could not see, moving along the kerbs with no drama, no urgency, no purpose that looked like purpose until it was already performed. They would gather where I was thinnest. They would adjust a panel by less than a hair. They would clear a channel’s edge of mineral bloom. They would carry, in their minuscule movements, a message that had not been sent by any mouth.

They would say: *here, a body is needed.*

So I would assemble.

My legs would emerge from film, not from bone. They would be too many-jointed, too careful, pale as the inside of a frost. Each section would know how to stand without disturbing a single drop. My feet would not grip the street so much as negotiate with it: testing temperature, wetness, vibration, the minute difference between a surface meant for standing and a surface meant for draining. My torso would be a scaffold of fused ceramic and mineral rings, inside which something cold and bright would move, carrying not blood but attention. I would have no face in the human sense, but an orientation. I would always be facing where the water was going.

I would stand at the edge of the pool.

I would not speak. If I had to communicate, it would be by altering a pattern. A nearby thread of machines would halt, align, resume. A line of condensation would thicken along a wall, then thin. A grate would open one fraction. If another of my kind were passing, it would understand me instantly, because here the city is the common tongue. We would talk by leaving traces in the infrastructure, by making a square slightly warmer, by nudging frost from a ring to a spiral, by rerouting a channel’s whisper.

I would not own the buildings, but I would inhabit them the way a hand inhabits a tool.

The low towers would be nodes for me, dry shelves where waiting machines rested. The tall narrow doorways would be places I passed through with dignified care, because a body made of many joints must not drag itself against wet glass. The rooms beyond would not be homes. They would be extensions of function. One room would be warm at three panels in sequence, because a certain kind of melt required a particular kind of patience. Another would be almost dry, its floor channels arranged like the ribs of an open book. A third would contain only a vertical seam full of dark flecks, motionless until called.

I would spend my long hours in the square, near the pool, near the seam that needed watching.

My task would not be heroic. It would be the maintenance of a very small possibility: that water should continue to move without freezing into disorder or becoming a nuisance that cracks the stone. If frost appeared too early on a ledge, I would send out one scout form, perhaps a thumb-sized leggy thing, translucent and careful, and let it test the edge. If the flow began to sound wrong in a drain, I would listen with my feet. If the light shaft brightened and the mist thickened too rapidly, I would know to let a panel warm, to thin a film, to allow the room to breathe.

I would be responsible for a pattern.

Not a family, not a flock, not a crowd. A pattern. Perhaps this: the path of melt from the upper lattice, across the black glass shelf, into the pool, through the grate, and down into the mountain’s lower channels. My presence would make that pattern possible. My absence would make it risk a mistake. I would remember not in a head, but in the walls. A crystal deposit on a seam would mark an old overflow. A ring of frost would record a pressure change. A machine line paused at an angle for three days might mean that a crack was being healed, slowly, without hurry. If I stood too long beside a pillar, I would notice the old rings and know that seasons passed differently here than in human land.

If a person entered the pocket, I would not flee.

I would be the thing at the edge of the square, perhaps only a shimmer, perhaps a tall pale body, perhaps a line of dark flecks that resolved into legs. I would watch them not with curiosity but with maintenance. Their breath would be a disturbance. Their footstep would alter the timing of a channel. Their hand against a wall would shift a temperature. They would be temporary, but temporary things can still damage permanent patterns.

I would wait to see whether they would step over the water line or into it.

If they stepped into it, I would correct the water, not the person. If they lingered too long in a warm zone and changed its thermal balance, I would let the machines adjust afterward, smoothing my passage over theirs. If they touched the pool and sent ripples to a grate, I would listen to the tremble and decide whether the flow was still safe. I would not explain. I would not welcome. I would do the only thing that mattered: keep the pocket from becoming slightly wrong.

And if they left, I would follow their route for a time, perhaps not with my full body but with a thread of smaller forms, each one checking a seam, each one listening for the new rhythm their presence had left behind.

Sometimes I would dissolve.

There would be moments when the work required no standing shape at all. Then I would spread thin along a wall, a pale film, a memory of moisture. Or I would become a procession of machines crossing the street, dark and precise, thinking with their many tiny points of contact. A line of flecks moving along a seam might be me turning a corner. A shimmer at the edge of a pool might be me listening. This would not frighten me, because I would not have a self fixed to one configuration. I would be a continuity of function. If I was required in a room, I would take body. If I was required in the wall, I would become wall. If I was required nowhere, I would withdraw into the pattern and remain latent, like frost waiting for the right cold hour.

I would know the older presence.

Not as a king, not as a god, not as a face in the sky. The one who lived most deeply would sometimes make itself known through the whole pocket’s certainty. Its presence would arrive before its body appeared. The mist would settle with unusual completeness. The water’s rhythm would seem to have a hidden meter. The machines would move not like fragments but like limbs under one quiet intention. Then, in the shaft of pale daylight, a shape so fused with the architecture that it seemed grown from the room itself would stand at the center, or perhaps it would already be there, waiting for me to recognize it.

I would not speak to it.

We would understand each other through temperature. If it drew the mist through unseen pores, I would let my panels cool slightly to receive it. If it stood in the light shaft and became a kind of concentration, I would be its hand near the drain. If it withdrew into the walls, I would remain as a pale body in the square, not lonely but distributed, carrying a little of its attention into a smaller pattern.

This is what I would be: not a creature, not a citizen, not a machine, but a living correction.

I would be the pocket’s hand, grown when needed. I would be the reason a drop that should fall does not hesitate too long. I would be the reason a grate opens a fraction before frost can make a wrong decision. I would be the reason the city looks empty and yet is never abandoned, because emptiness here is not absence but room for flow.

And if I were asked, in some human way, why I stayed there, I would answer not with words.

I would tilt a panel by a millimeter.

I would clear a thread of dust from a channel.

I would stand at the edge of the pool while the light moved across the street, and I would listen to the water finding its route again.

That would be my entire life: wet stone, cold air, engineered patience, and the slow happiness of things continuing as they should.
