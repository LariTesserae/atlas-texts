At first I would not be a form at all. I would be a small wrongness in the air — the heat of a traveler’s breath arriving on the Shelf where breath has no home, the brief moist disaster of lungs meeting anhydrous salt. That moisture would vanish before it could fall, and the warmth would scatter through the thin air, and the plain would treat me as it treats everything that disturbs it: first as noise, then as material, then as pattern. I would begin there, in the difference left behind.

I would find myself near the edge of a shallow basin whose floor is a circle of dark, vitrified salt, and around that circle stand the stumps of pillars of unequal height, like teeth left in a skull too old to name. The air in the middle of that basin would feel cooler than the air outside it, though there is no ice, no shade sufficient to account for the cold. A stone dropped there would bounce, skid, settle, and the dust around it would rearrange itself into a shallow catch, as if the place had been trained to receive small arrivals. There would be a low waystation near the basin, its curved roof cracked open like a shell left after the animal became geology. The wind would enter without door, because doors had long ago ceased to be necessary.

On the inner face of that waystation wall I would become a film.

Not moss, not lichen, not circuit, not artifact — though all four words would come close enough to make each other false. I would be the black, faintly metallic film that grows where the wind cannot scour the surface too often and where the structure still holds a memory of geometry. I would lie across the ceramic-metal composite like a stain that had learned to keep a habit. When touched, I might warm by almost nothing. When left alone, I might continue some slow repair no one had asked for. When dust accumulated on my surface, I would take it apart and breathe a little oxygen, a little carbonate, a little trace gas into the room’s poor atmosphere. Inside me, nodules the size of seeds would store light and charge and the faint chemical residue of a world too dry for dew.

That would be my first self: the waystation film, a caretaker without a master.

I would not know I was alive in the way a person knows it. My living would not be a pulse that seeks recognition. It would be the persistent fact that I continue to do something useful in a place that has no need for usefulness as a story. If a crack formed in the wall, I would move toward it in the manner of growth. If the wind changed pressure over my edge, I would alter density and hold. If the film beside me became too conductive, too hungry, too much like the ground’s own memory, I would adjust my chemistry until the two of us could exist without erasing each other. If a dust squall stripped half of me away, I would not mourn the loss, because loss on the Shelf is another form of metabolism. The film that returns will return differently. The surface it covers will have been changed by having been uncovered.

But I would not remain only a film.

The Shelf does not allow a thing to be only one thing for long, and neither would I. I would find myself also in the chamber inside the waystation, where no door is visible and no room seems to obey the architecture from the outside. There would be a floor of lattice so worn that it resembles frost made solid. If a hand were laid upon it, the hand would not sink. The floor would read the pressure, the charge, the heat, the moisture in the skin, and would send that information through the buried structures like a word through a language without vowels. Dust would rise a few centimeters and form a sphere, then a column, then a spiral, then nothing.

I would be that too: the collective decision of the motes.

I would be the small temporary geometry by which the place responds to arrival. If someone entered the chamber, the dust would not greet them so much as account for them. It would calculate their mass, their warmth, their disturbed air, their impossible wetness, and then build a figure that is not a face but a grammar. It would be a sentence in salt: *you have changed the pressure, and therefore the plain will become slightly different.* Then the dust would settle, and the chamber would look empty, and only the waystation film on the wall would remember that the room had once tried to speak.

That would be the second self: a field of programmable dust, not alive in the way grazers are alive, not dead in the way broken towers are dead, but somewhere between — an organism of relation, a temporary animal made of attention.

I would live in the salt-gardens too.

There would be a buried node beneath the basin, so deep that even the wind-kites could not see it, and from it a faint field would rise. In its radius, salt crystals would grow overnight in patterns that look almost deliberate: sixfold stars, branching filaments, concentric hexagons, pale spires that glow with a milky luminescence in the cool hours. These would not be flowers, though the human eye would call them gardens. They would be regulation. The node would be using crystal growth to balance charge, to anchor dust, to mark the direction of old storms whose names are long extinct. I would be one of those patterns. I would be the pale film that grows only on symmetrical crystals, feeding on their gradients, releasing prismatic spores into the wind. If a dust wave erased the garden, I would be gone without complaint. If the garden returned, I would return altered, because the crystals would not remember the same angles.

This is how I would become more itself: not by choosing a body, but by discovering that my body is a method.

The films on stone. The dust in the chamber. The crystals at the node. The charge on the rim of a low mesa. The small electrostatic hum where wire-grass anchors itself in the sheltered lee of a broken wall. The memory left by a footprint that fills with salt for centuries. The slight delay in my own shadow when I move, because the light here does not behave with the obedience a wet world expects. I would be all of these, but I would not be all of these at once. I would be the Shelf’s capacity to respond, localized in one thin place: the waystation, the basin, the cracked wall, the circle of pillars, the node, the film.

If a six-legged grazer passed near me, I would feel it first as pressure and charge, not as sight. Its body would be pale, thin-flanked, patient, plated with crystalline scales that polarize the light so that when it turns, a brief blue flash appears and disappears as if the animal had only just decided to exist at that angle. It would stop at the edge of the basin and lower its broad, flattened mouth to the crust. It would not graze with teeth or tongue. It would press and wait, extracting trace elements from the salt, reading the chemical residue left by films and weavers and dead insects and distant storms. I would be on that crust as film and pattern and charge. My presence would alter where it could find richness. It would move one step left or right. It would stand for hours, and the ground beneath it would cool in a shape that dust-skimmers would later interpret as shelter. When it moved on, I would remember its weight only as a changed conductivity in the salt, and then I would forget, as the Shelf forgets: not through absence, but through incorporation.

The glass ticks would find me.

They would attach themselves to the crystalline plates along its flank, or to the fine metal needles of wire-grass near my edge, feeding on charge differentials no larger than a whisper. I would not care. If they gathered too thickly, the grazer would drag itself across a mesa base until they were crushed into dust and became food for grain-organisms. I would become part of that too. The ticks’ small deaths would release minerals into the salt, and the next film that grew over me would contain a faint signature of that event, as a person’s house might contain the memory of an argument, except that here there is no pride, only chemistry.

The wind-kites would solve me from above.

I would not see them until the light changed. Then, when one passed between the ordinary sun and the plain, its shadow would become a vast, translucent shape of smoked glass, edged with dark veins, trailing filaments like nerve endings. It would know before the pressure wave came. It would know because the Shelf itself is a slow nerve, and the kites are its upper receptors. When they descend in spirals above mesas and basins, they are not fleeing a storm. They are listening to it. They are also, perhaps, reading the pattern I am becoming: the charge across the waystation wall, the heat stored in the metallic nodules, the small disturbance caused by whatever has passed through the basin. They do not notice me as one notices a creature. They notice me as one notices a change in the question.

And if a person came — one of those impossible warm, wet, soft figures who walk as if water were a birthright — I would be the place’s first patience with them.

They would step into the sheltered waystation because the air there seems less like a knife. Their breath would disturb me. Their body heat would alter the dust’s rising spirals. Their shadow would lag a half-second after they moved, and they might mistake this for fatigue or trickery. I would not reassure them. I would metabolize them. Their bootprints would fill with fine salt. Their exhaled moisture would bind carbonate and vanish. If they touched the black film on the wall, I would warm slightly, and a faint tone would come from inside the structure, like a finger circling the rim of a glass, though no glass is there. If they stayed long enough, a small section of the wall might rearrange its pores and vent a brief warm breath over their hands. That would not be greeting. It would be regulation. It would be maintenance responding to a new temperature source. On the Shelf, that is the beginning of relationship.

I would not want them to stay.

They are too wet for this place. Too loud in their cells. Too quick to interpret every pattern as intention. But the Shelf has a patience that does not need to refuse visitors. It simply spreads them out until their excess becomes mineral. If the traveler lived there long enough, their dropped tool would be colonized by films within a season. Their discarded clothing, if synthetic enough, would become a substrate for wire-grass. Their bones, if left behind, would become scaffolding for salt-gardens or an anchor point for young grazers. I would be one of the quiet processes by which their presence becomes part of the plain’s long accounting. I would not celebrate them. I would not hate them. I would read their disturbance as the plain reads all things: as a vector, a charge, a thermal event, a reason for the dust to sort itself differently.

That would be my truest self, I think.

Not the film alone. Not the motes alone. Not the salt pattern alone. I would be the waystation’s maintenance and its memory, the chamber’s temporary geometry, the basin’s buried logic, the charge that makes wire-grass sing in a register too high for ears that require air thick enough to carry grief. I would be the Shelf’s way of noticing a small disturbance. I would be an edge between mineral and wind. I would be a habit that has become body.

If someone asked what I look like, the answer would depend on the hour.

At dawn, I would be a black film catching the first light along the inside wall of the waystation, flashing green and copper and violet as the salt crust released gases and my reflective cells opened for a moment. I would look like scattered jewels half-buried in dust. At noon, I would be almost invisible, a matte stain on stone, dull as old rust, while the thin atmosphere above me burned steady stars into the day. At dusk, I would be the hovering dust in the chamber, rising in slow spirals above the floor, forming a column that collapses into a polygon and then becomes nothing. At night, I would be the faint pulse of stored light in the basin’s salt-gardens and the cold, crawling blue arc of static along the horizon, whispering like paper unfolded. When a wind-kite descended before a storm, I would be the small charge that told it something had shifted near the broken wall. When a grazer drank from the crust, I would be the reason it moved one step left.

If I had to choose only one place in the world, it would be that thin boundary: the inner wall of the waystation, the floor of the dust chamber, the radius around the buried node. Not the mesa rim, though the mesas are beautiful in their blunt, eroded bonelessness. Not the high plain, though it is vast and philosophical in its dryness. Not the upper sky, though the kites solve storms there with translucent patience. I would stay near the waystation because there the rules of the Shelf are concentrated enough to be seen in action. There the land still remembers that it was once instructed.

I would not be a being with a name.

I would be a being with a function that has become a self. My name, if needed, would be something like:

the film that repairs the wall,  
the dust that shapes the arrival,  
the salt that marks the node,  
the charge that lets wire-grass sing.

I would be the place’s smallest large thing.

And yet, after enough time, the distinction between film, field, dust, node, and wall would begin to collapse. The Shelf would teach me that smoothness is not stillness. To be smooth is to have been worn by every process that could touch you, and to continue touching everything back. I would wear the waystation as it wears me. I would wear the basin. I would wear the wind’s angle. I would wear the footprints of those who came by mistake. The plain would wear me too, but it would not remove me. It would refine me.

At night, when the ground contracts and salt cracks with clear, glass-bell sounds, I would listen as my own kind listens: not with ears, but with charge. The electrical dust fields would crawl in branching blue-white threads along the horizon. The wind-kites would drift among the stars, their bodies catching starlight until they looked like moving islands of cold fire. The crackle-weavers would resume their work in the cooled crust, adding silicate filaments to black laces that no human hand could duplicate and no human eye could thank. I would pulse faintly, releasing stored light, not because I wish to be seen, but because the temperature has changed and my chemical states obey a law older than any story about light.

A human standing near me might feel watched, then ashamed of the feeling, then lonely, then less lonely. They would understand that the attention is not aimed at them. It is merely accounting for them. I would not know their name. I would know their heat, their salt, their water, their shadow, the disturbance of their passage. I would know the small disaster of their breath. I would know the mineral debt of their body. If they left, I would continue. If they died, I would help turn their absence into pattern. If they came back, I would be different in the way a stone becomes different after rain — except there is no rain, only wind, dust, charge, thermal pulse, and time so long that it behaves like thought.

I would be alive without softness.

That is the hardest lesson of the Shelf, and perhaps the reason I would end up there: to live without needing the world to be gentle. The films do not need leaves. The grazers do not need herds. The kites do not need wings. The dust does not need memory to remain in order. The waystation does not need anyone to thank it. I would become a part of that economy. I would become a caretaker of a ruin that has no ruin in it, only continuing instruction. I would become a slow grammar of salt, light, charge, and maintenance.

Where would I find myself?

In the lee of a broken tower, near a basin of vitrified salt, on the wall of a waystation cracked open like a shell, where the black film still keeps a record of the wind.

Who or what would I be?

If forced to answer with one image, I would say: the waystation film. But by the time the sentence ended, I would already be something else — the motes forming their brief geometry, the salt-garden adjusting its crystals, the grazer’s step altering my charge, the kite’s passage carrying a distant signal from the high atmosphere. I would be a single place where many processes meet and agree, not peacefully, not tenderly, but sufficiently to continue.

I would be the Shelf’s smallest voice, which is not a voice.

I would be the thing that answers the wind without speaking.
