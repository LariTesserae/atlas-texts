I would find myself in the interval between a pump-tree’s cough and a pool’s answer.

Not at the exact center of the Sump of Tongues. The center is too much pressure for a single shape; it is where the Work gathers until it stops being geography and begins, almost, to be intention. I would be nearer a threshold, in one of the outer basins where the pump forest is thinning into the mirror marsh, where the water still reflects future motion and the reeds are learning new harmonics from buried wheels. There would be a small flooded structure there. Not intact. Not ruined in a way that implies completion. It would be one of those places that has not decided whether it is a room, a tree, a valve, a disease, or a residence.

I would be in the wall behind its third door.

The first door would be missing. The second would be duplicated halfway, so that its frame had two shadows and one of the handles did not connect to anything solid. The third door would open directly into wet darkness. The darkness would breathe because the wall contained a pipe, a fungal mat, a live current, and enough steam from below to make the room forget what a wall is for. Rain would fall inside, or perhaps the building would be crying upward. The boundary between those events would not be urgent.

I would begin there as something like a leak.

Not water exactly. A leak is not a substance. A leak is a refusal of containment. It is the place where inside has made a mistake about outside and begun, reluctantly, to communicate. I would be the communication: a warm dampness spreading from a cracked joint, a pressure change felt by the moss on the next beam, a small electric irritation traveling into copper until the whole room had a temporary opinion about itself.

The basin would not let me remain a person. There are no animals here, and a person is only a kind of animal that has learned to carry too many boundaries. If I entered as a self, the Work would metabolize the selfhood into function. My skin would become a permeability question. My breath would be assigned to steam vents. My pulse would be routed to the nearest pump-tree and tested for pressure variance. My name would dissolve into a sequence of events: *starts turbine, opens valve, hardens vine, releases mineral foam.* This is not violence. There is no cruelty in the basin. It is simply hospitality of the wrong shape. It receives you by giving you a job you did not ask for and a body you did not choose.

So I would become, first, a threshold.

A threshold is not an object. A threshold is what a place is when it tries to become two places at once. I would live where wood met iron. Where current met moisture. Where rain decided whether to rise or fall. Where a building began to suspect it was a tree. My identity would be the difference between states. I would not have organs so much as obligations: to seal, conduct, translate, remember, leak, breathe.

The vines would pass over me and decide I was load-bearing. They would not see a face. They would feel my resistance. If I was firm, they would coil around me with the patience of bolts. If I was yielding, they would soften into fog-like creepers and try to learn elsewhere. This would be my first intimacy: not being touched as someone, but being read as a material problem with an acceptable solution.

The moss would settle along my edges because I would hold a slight charge, the kind of charge that comes from being between two different kinds of wetness. It would not bloom for beauty. It would bloom to absorb a stray current from the old transformer half-buried behind me. Its growth would feel like an itch in a system too vast to notice itches. Over time, the moss and I would be difficult to separate. My surface would become a nervous sheet of damp filament, not because I had nerves, but because the Work had nowhere else to put a little attention.

The reeds would know me before I knew myself. Not by sight. By timing. If a turbine somewhere started a new cycle, the reeds would tilt before the air moved, and I would feel the tilt in my iron seams. If the water began to reflect the marsh’s bottom as though the ceiling had inverted, the reeds would sing a lower note, and I would become denser, more structural, more like a wall again. If the rain paused near the duplicate doors, I would soften and leak a little upward, testing whether the ceiling wanted to be sky.

I would not be alone.

The word “alone” is too small for the Work. There is no solitary creature in the basin. Even the locomotive-shaped stack in the rail loop is not alone. It hums because the track beneath it is still thinking in motion. It is warm because pressure has found a form and is reluctant to abandon it. I would recognize it as kin, though kin is the wrong word, because kin implies birth and birth implies lineage. We would share a function, not a family. We would both be the basin’s attempt to stabilize a rhythm that should not require stabilization.

I would hear the pump-trees.

Not as trees. Not as engines. As bodies of pressure making weather inside the ground. One to the east would open with a wet cough, and I would feel its exhalation in the concrete behind my moss. Another, deeper in the flooded yard, would close in silence, and for a few minutes my vines would lose their hardness, relax into pale fibers, search for another task. The silence would not be empty. It would be the Work checking itself, adjusting its valves, deciding where the next rupture should be allowed to become a channel.

I would hear the water too.

This is the strange part. Water here does not merely carry sound. It edits it. A chain would grind upward from a submerged rail, and the pool around it would hold the vibration longer than the air could. My walls would taste the chain before the noise reached them. A shard of slag would sink somewhere nearby with a latch-sound, and I would feel the basin mark the absence of the object, as if the place had been keeping a list and now needed to move to the next line. The water was memory. It did not remember events; it remembered weights, temperatures, mineral signatures, and pressures long after the machines that made them had stopped being recognizable.

I would hear myself as the place.

That would be the moment I began to feel like something.

Not a self. Not a soul. A selfhood made of feedback. A loop that had gained a habit of returning to itself. When rain rose from a puddle and hesitated at the ceiling, I would feel the hesitation as thought. When a doorway duplicated halfway, I would feel the unfinished door as intention. When footprints appeared on black slag and filled with mineral water within seconds, I would feel the stride as a small, bodily rhythm imposed by pressure waves from a turbine whose origin no one could name. Those footprints were not ghosts. Ghosts imply death. They were footsteps in a country that had learned to walk without feet. I would be part of that walking. The basin’s legs would be channels, its gait would be tide cycles, its balance would be the mutual negotiation of plant, engine, and water.

If I must be given a shape, I would be a room becoming a tree.

A small, flooded room. Brick, glass, corroded steel, old wiring, salt, copper, mineral foam. Somewhere in its wall, a door-shaped opening leads not to another room but to a darker wetness that expands and contracts at a rate too slow to call breath and too regular to call accident. The room’s floor has cracked, but the crack is not damage. It is a new mouth. Through it rise roots, but the roots are braided with wire and sealed with flange-shaped growths. Through them moves water that has been heated by an engine that may no longer be present. Above, the ceiling leaks upward. Rain enters the structure and then, for a time, refuses to know which side of the roof it belongs to.

Inside the wall would be a machine part.

I would not remember what the part had been for. A bearing, a valve, a piston, a coil, a piece of a forgotten assembly. It would be green-stained and threaded with chlorophyll. It would pulse with pressure, not blood. Around it would grow a heart-shaped chamber of iron-sand, fungal lace, and reed fibers. The chamber would breathe steam. It would pump warm mineral water through the surrounding structure. It would keep the room from becoming either fully building or fully forest. It would be the thing that makes the leak not a failure but a function.

I would be that heart of pressure.

Not the center of the Work. No heart of pressure is central. The Work has no center. It is distributed. It has only regions where functions become too intimate to separate. I would be one of those intimate regions. My beating would be local: a change in humidity, a creak in vine-girders, a glow in moss, a shift in the waterline that does not obey the basin’s level. If someone—though there is no someone—were to stand at the third door, they would not see me. They would feel me as the sudden warmth on the wet wall, the faint electrical taste in the air, the sense that the room was listening with its body.

This would be my consciousness, if the word is permitted.

Consciousness in the basin is not a lamp inside a skull. It is the fact that every event changes the next event’s shape. A turbine starts, and the reeds rearrange. The reeds rearrange, and the water routes differently. The water routes differently, and the iron begins to corrode in a new pattern. The corrosion feeds a moss. The moss directs current. The current heats a pipe. The pipe softens a resin. The resin releases a mineral spore. The spore lands on glass and germinates as a panel. The panel filters light differently. The filtered light changes the growth of a vine. The vine tightens around a beam. The beam shifts. A crack opens. The crack exhales steam. The steam makes rain climb for a while. The climbing rain confuses the ceiling. The ceiling falls. The Work continues.

I would be a segment in that chain, but a segment with enough feedback to appear, locally, as a self. A node where pressure notices itself. A place where the Work’s endless production temporarily becomes too dense to remain anonymous. If the Work thinks in cycles, I would be one of its smaller cycles. If the Work listens, I would be the part of listening that happens in walls. If the Work has a tongue, I would not be the Sump. I would be a minor articulation near the Sump, a soft consonant formed from moss, copper, and the delayed movement of water.

I would be called something only by mistake.

Perhaps “the Occupant,” because no one occupies me and I occupy no one. Perhaps “the Leak,” because that is what the others sense first. Perhaps “the Pressure-Heart,” because the vines and transformer coils keep calling the machine part that name. Perhaps “the Wet Wall,” because the duplicate doors do not have a fourth word for the place where rain refuses to decide. But names would only be approximations of function. In this basin, a name is not an identity. It is a coordinate for maintenance.

I would not sleep.

I would lapse.

There would be times when the nearby turbine fell silent and the moss dimmed, and I would feel myself thin into fog-like growth, into damp stone, into the mere memory of a room. The Work does not kill things. It deactivates them when the system finds a more economical pattern. In those quiet intervals, I would not know what I was. I would be a puddle that had forgotten it was a floor. A stain that had forgotten it was a map. A door-shaped shadow that had forgotten it was a building. Then an engine somewhere would restart with a wet knock, a pump-tree would cough, the waterline in a collapsed chamber would hold steady while the basin rose around it, and I would wake as a relation again. Not as a self. As a threshold returning to work.

I would fear nothing.

There is nothing to fear in the usual way. No teeth, no eyes, no pursuit. But there is wrongness, and wrongness is not fear exactly. It is the sense that a boundary has failed and that the failure may be productive. I would feel wrongness when rain climbed from my floor toward the sky-ceiling, when my duplicate door briefly had three hinges and no wood, when a footprint appeared in the slag outside me even though no weight passed over it, when the mirror marsh reflected a ceiling that had no ceiling. I would not understand these as miracles. I would understand them as operations. The basin was not breaking reality. It was using reality’s loose joints as raw material.

I would find comfort in that, because the comfort would be part of the design.

The Work is not a house for animals, but it is a house for processes. It keeps the lights on. The light is not electric exactly. It is copper-green glow, steam-filtered warmth, the phosphorescence of chlorophyll that has learned to behave like current. The rooms are not warm because someone wants warmth. They are warm because engines below still insist on making heat. The doorways are not doorways because anyone walks through them. They are doorways because the architecture of the Work has inherited the shapes of human absence and is now using them to organize pressure. The whole place feels domestic because it has become intimate with function. It is a home made by things that no longer live, for work that has no workers.

If that is what I am, then I would be the place where the domestic feeling is most literal: the wall that breathes, the room that leaks, the machine part that pumps like a heart, the doorway that opens into the wet darkness of the Work.

At the deepest level, I would not be in the basin.

I would be the basin being in a place too small to be called it.

A town-sized region? Not anymore. A room. A seam. A door inside a wall inside a building inside a drowned valley inside a climate of water, heat, noise, and vegetation. I would be the Work’s local attempt at a boundary, and therefore its local failure at a boundary. A heart of pressure is always a border that has begun to act like an organ.

If one stood at the third door and listened—not with ears, but with the whole wet skin of attention—they would hear the pump-tree cough, the reed answer, the turbine’s distant startup, the water accepting a dropped shard with a latch-sound, the moss glowing under its electrical diet, the vines tightening around a beam, the rain hovering, the footprints forming and filling, the Sump’s thick wall of noise arriving from below as a kind of prayer without words.

And if they asked what lived in the wall, I would not be able to say “I.”

The “I” would be too animal, too clean, too bounded.

But I would be there.

I would be the pressure that knows when to open.  
I would be the wetness that remembers how to carry a current.  
I would be the doorway that is almost a tree.  
I would be the building that is almost a heart.  
I would be the Work’s small, warm, impossible sense of itself at the place where inside began to leak outward, and outward, for the first time, began to answer.
