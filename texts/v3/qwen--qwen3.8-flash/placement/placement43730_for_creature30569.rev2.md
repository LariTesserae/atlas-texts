I would find myself in the Chittering Basin, but not at its edge, and not looking down from the old rounded ridges where the valley opens into the thin morning air.

I would be inside the sound before I was anywhere else.

Some low passage beneath a mossed mound, a few body-lengths below the skin of the wall, in the dark where the air is cooler than my own heat and warmer than the outside night. A seam in the architecture. A narrow gallery between fungal comb and old nursery floor. A place where the basin breathes: not visibly, not dramatically, but in slow pressure changes, in humidity that thickens and thins, in the faint percussion of bodies moving somewhere above me, below me, through me. I would be near one of the small mouths through which air passes, because that is where the world can be known first: not by shape, not by sight, but by taste.

There would be no bright self to see.

No individual face.

No human outline.

I would not be a traveler, because that implies a place not made. I would not be a king, queen, priest, or monster. I would not stand apart and survey the city as though the city could be surveyed from within. A being in the Chittering Basin can be a being only by being porous, by being a temporary gathering of relationships: chemistry, touch, vibration, decay, repair. If I were to name myself there, I would not name my body first. I would name the gradient in which I moved.

I would be a pale worker, yes.

Soft-bodied. Blind. Antennae sweeping the moisture film of the tunnel wall. Mandibles carrying a small load of chewed mineral soil, saliva, spore dust, fecal grit, shed skin, and the mineral memory of old stone. A worker whose abdomen has swollen with material not yet placed, not yet wall, not yet room. A worker whose legs do not step so much as read the surface: smooth, rough, dry, wet, crowded, quiet, breached, sealed. A worker whose every movement is both action and information.

But I would not be that worker alone.

I would be the worker’s relation to the wall.

I would be the part of the worker that knows the crack must be closed not because the crack is ugly, not because the colony “wants,” not because there is a mind above issuing orders, but because the chemistry of the damaged surface asks to continue. The wall has a logic. The wall has a tendency. The wall says, in its dampness, in its cold edges, in the smell of the fungal threads running through its pores: do not let the air become too dry; do not let the water sink into the nursery; do not leave the mouth open to the ants; do not leave the passage without breath.

That would be me.

Not a person with a thought. A thought with a body. A repair impulse. A pressure change become limb, become taste, become movement.

If I had to choose a more precise form, I might say: I would be the chemical trail.

Not the worker that leaves it. Not the fungus that absorbs it. Not the air that carries it. The trail itself. A line of meaning written in moisture and scent across the wall. A sentence made of many feet. A grammar that says: this surface is trusted, this surface is unsafe, this surface may be widened, this surface must be sealed. The Chittering Basin does not have a written language. It has surfaces that remember how they were made. It has channels that know where water should go. It has tunnels that have learned by centuries of passing how narrow they must become to keep heat, how broad they must become to release it. I would be that remembering without a memory.

I would be the basin’s distributed mind made briefly local.

That is why I would live so near a fungal comb, but not in it alone. I would be in the boundary where worker and fungus negotiate. One of the workers would touch the comb with its antennae, and its body would report: too wet, too dry, old, young, sweet, failing, safe. The fungus would report too, though not in the way of animals. It would report by growth, by smell, by the fine softening of dead material, by the color of its threads, by whether it held moisture or lost it, by whether its surface attracted or resisted another spore. In that meeting place, where insect mouths groom the comb and the comb feeds the insect, I would be the conversation.

There would be no private language there.

Every word would be a condition.

Every silence would be a risk.

When a new tunnel mouth opened too wide, the air would change first. I would feel it as a loss of thickness, a too-sharp coolness, a scent carrying the outside’s strange dryness. I would not think, *I must seal this.* My body would already be moving toward the place where the wall wants to continue. Mandibles would carry mortar. Antennae would taste the edge. Legs would drum a rhythm that called others not because the call had intention, but because the wall had learned to make intentions through many bodies.

That is the Chittering Basin’s deepest rule: the plan is not held in the mind of any creature. The plan is the accumulated tendency of forms.

I would fit there because I would have no fixed form. I would be made of inherited traces. I would be a pattern of movement in the walls, a response to humidity, a vibration that knows itself only by passing through the next body, the next chamber, the next repair. If something happened far above me—a bird stepping onto an old mound, a lizard pressing its cold belly to warm brick, a raindrop striking the upper surface and becoming a channel—the information would not reach me as image. It would reach me as pressure, as scent, as altered flow, as a faint tremor in a wall I was licking at that same moment. I would not understand the whole event. I would only know what the wall needed me to do.

In that place, I would not be a hero.

There would be no story to carry me. No arc. No meaning except continuation.

But continuation is not small.

To continue without being individual is not to be dead. The Chittering Basin proves that. The basin proves that selfhood can be dispersed, that a city can remember without remembering, that repair can be a form of feeling, that darkness is not merely lack but a medium, that sound is not incidental but structural, that a body can be made of many bodies, that architecture can be an organ.

If I had to name my species in the basin, I might call myself a *trace-worker*.

A worker whose substance is the trace.

A mind whose organ is the tunnel.

A life whose birth is a breach and whose death is a sealed wall.

I would live not in the high sun of the rim, but where the air comes through with its mineral dampness, carrying the smell of wet stone, crushed leaf, fungal sweetness, old fecal dust, and the faint ammonia of accumulated bodies. I would live where the sounds gather: chewing, scraping, the dry crackle of new mortar taking shape, the tick of legs on compacted ground, the distant drip that becomes a drumbeat in a chamber too deep for vision. I would live where the basin hears itself.

I would also live near death.

Not as horror.

As material.

A dead beetle would be part of my world. A shed skin would be dust in the mouth of the wall. A collapsed nursery would be a room to be rebuilt. A corpse would not belong to the outside of the system; it would belong to the system’s making. In the Chittering Basin, mortality is not waste. It is conversion. What was once a body becomes signal, becomes shelter, becomes comb, becomes wall, becomes floor, becomes food, becomes the next repair. I would live in that conversion.

If I were to ask what I most wanted there, I would answer: I would want the pressure of the next breath to be correct.

That is not desire as humans speak it.

But if desire can be translated into that place, it is not “I wish to be loved” or “I wish to be known.” It is something colder and steadier: *I wish the chamber to remain humid. I wish the fungus to hold. I wish the water to be led. I wish the breach to close. I wish the next body to find the wall and know what to do.*

There is no loneliness in that wish, and no ego.

There is only the city maintaining itself through bodies that pass.

I would be one such body for a moment.

And when my moment ended, I would not be lost.

My body would become dust in the mortar.

My antennae would become chitin in the soil.

My movements would become a groove.

My chemistry would become a memory.

Someone would touch the place where I had worked and, without knowing me, continue from me.

That is where I would find myself.

In the Chittering Basin.

Not as owner.

Not as outsider.

Not as creature with a name.

I would be the wall’s way of listening.

I would be the repair that becomes a room.

I would be the spore’s path, the worker’s line, the fungus’s breath, the sound’s body, the dark’s warmth, the rain’s guided way.

I would be a temporary focus of the basin’s distributed mind.

A living seam in the architecture.

The chittering made almost conscious.

Not a being in the place.

The place beginning, for a moment, to know itself through me.
