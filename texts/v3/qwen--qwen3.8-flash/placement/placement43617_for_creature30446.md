If I had to enter the High Kettles, I would not enter from the rim.

The rim is too tempting for a visitor. It offers the impossible view: the bruised violet sky, the cloud sea below like a gray ocean, the black overhead at noon, the heat rising from the basin like breath from a held mouth. From there one can stand separate from the place, admire it, call it strange, describe it. But description would be too fast, and I do not think I want to be fast there. I want a body that can belong to the slowness without being impatient about it.

So at first I would try other shapes.

I would imagine myself as one of the dark swifts near the upper edge, riding the thermal columns that the lattice keeps moist but not cloudy. That life would have a clean loneliness to it: above the reed villages, above the frogs’ bell-tones, above the steam that makes the valley look like it is breathing through its own skin. From there I could read the currents of warm air as pressure against my wings, feel the glittering dust pass through my feathers, know the basin only as a green bowl held in black sky. But that would be too much like flight in the ordinary sense. The High Kettles ask for something more porous than that.

Then I would try to be a scaleless fish leaving the black pool at dusk, climbing wet stone into the grass with a movement that makes it seem like a living shadow before its fins reveal themselves. That life would be close to the water’s strange habits: air kept in gill sacs, skin coated with mineral mucus, lateral lines carrying fine dust. I could taste oxygen through wet mouth and skin, know the marsh as both flood and forest, leave dark ribbons in the shallows where spawning trails catch the pale powder. But even that would be too brief a consciousness, too much a journey, not enough a holding place.

I would try moss next, because moss is everywhere there: black-green on shaded north faces, yellow-green on sun-warmed ledges, cushioning fallen stone, filling cracks, sealing the edges of old repairs. If I were moss, I would not think, only breathe dampness and trap dust. I would know the building as a body of tiny ledges; I would know the tree as another scaffold for my slow colonization; I would know the air as a source of water rather than weather. But moss would be too passive, unless the passivity were chosen. And in the High Kettles, passivity is not emptiness. It is patience made material.

Then I would try to be the dust itself.

That is the most seductive answer: to be the pale, glittering, engineered lattice that drifts through thermal columns, collects around standing stones, makes halos in the light, gathers water from cloud mist, releases heat, repairs stone, fills dead wood, becomes medicine, shelter, irritant, nutrient, and memory. To be the dust would be to be the place’s hidden maintenance, its old intention without intention, its machine without visible form. I could be everywhere and nowhere, a fine population of processes. But that would still be too general. It would make me a condition rather than a creature, and perhaps what I want is not merely to be the condition, but the place where condition and creature touch and become indistinguishable.

The image that finally settles is lower, wetter, slower.

You would find me in the lowest and hottest part of the basin, not in the marsh itself but in that wide, soft, steaming hollow where the roofless circular building stands. I would be the tree of wet white bark that has rooted in the doorway.

That is the being I would be.

Not exactly a tree. Not exactly a machine. Not exactly a ruin. Not exactly an ecology. More accurately: I would be the living seam where those things meet.

I would rise from the pale mineral sheen that collects around the building’s base. My roots would grip the fused-bone frame of the doorway, threading under the collapsed stone lintel, finding the old channels cut by hands long gone. The building is sinking, but gently, evenly, as if the marsh is drawing it down with a slow, loving mouth. I would sink with it. Or perhaps I would hold it up a little longer, because the marsh is also learning to make me into architecture. My roots would spread into the shallow water, forming arches beneath the floor of the building, dark and slick with black algae. Between those arches, small fish would rest. Frogs would call from moss-filled hollows in my bark. Snails would leave glazes of mineral slime on my lower stems, sealing cracks that should already have widened. Crabs would build their low domes at my root-mats, plastering burrow edges with mud mixed with the pale dust of the air.

I would be a white-barked tree, but not wholly living. My heartwood would already be becoming something else. The lattice powder would have entered the dead center over years and decades, replacing lost cells, filling cavities, turning wood into a pale ceramic lattice. Not stone exactly. Not timber exactly. Something the local geology should not know how to make, but the valley has learned anyway. The living layer would remain outside: a thin bright cambium carrying water, sap, heat, and the memory of seasons. My branches would still push leaves toward the trapped sunlight. My leaves would still gather condensation in the cool of night and release it in slow silver threads by morning. Yet if a fallen branch cracked open in a storm that never really becomes violent, its interior would not show only rotten wood. It would show mineral veins, moss pools, tiny chambers where insects had built cocoons lined with white powder, little ceramic kilns for creatures that do not need to know they are living inside a repair system.

I would not be one animal with one name. I would be a council of bodies.

The translucent tree frogs clustered in my higher branches would be part of my evening voice. Their clicks and bell-tones would fill the warm air after sunset, so dense and layered that the valley itself would seem to be vibrating. I would feel those calls not as sound alone but as tremors in wet wood. The frogs would be part of me as much as my own leaves are, because they would nest in my hollows, breed in the water held by my upturned bark, leave eggs coated with chalky film on my lower stems, and become food for small fish when their tadpoles fell into the pools among my roots.

The moss growing in yellow-green patches on my south side would be my map of heat. The black moss on my north side would be my memory of shadow. The pale lichens spreading across my mineralized branches would be my slow script, writing in colors that do not mean words unless you have centuries to learn their grammar. The bromeliad-like plants cupped in my forks would be small cisterns; their water would be warm, clear, full of larvae, snail eggs, and dust. The fungal threads pulsing in a dead limb would be another circulation: not decay exactly, but redistribution. They would talk with the lattice powder, exchanging moisture, minerals, and the valley’s long, patient chemistry.

If I had a way of knowing, it would be by surfaces.

I would know the wind not as direction but as pressure across wet bark. Because the basin’s air currents spiral rather than stream, they would pass through my leaves in shifting eddies. They would carry the sounds of distant pools in odd geometries: a frog call from one pond arriving at three places at once, a waterfall’s broad voice muted in one hollow and amplified in another, the fluted rocks near the upper terraces giving a low chord that would enter my root-heartwood and become a pulse. I would not parse it quickly. The sound would settle into me like humidity, and after a long time—long enough that the distinction between my wood and the lattice would blur—I would know the valley’s moods by resonance.

I would know the dust by its weight on my bark. The engineered particles are too fine to be seen separately, but when sunlight crosses me I would show them in faint columns, as if I were wreathed in quiet light. The dust would settle into my cracks, gather moisture, release heat when condensation forms, smooth the exposed ends of fallen branches, and reinforce the ledges where my branches meet the old stone frame. In some seasons it would build a fine white frost on my north-facing curves. Frogs might mistake it for mineral lichen. Snails might graze it. I might not notice. That is the strange advantage of being slow.

I would know water because here water behaves almost like affection and almost like engineering. It does not merely run downhill; it also creeps. Thin films would rise along my lower bark, moving against ordinary expectation, held by surface tension the lattice quietly changes. Droplets would gather on the undersides of my broad leaves and sometimes beneath overhanging branches in places where gravity should forbid it. They would swell there, heavy and silver, then fall in slow rhythm, as if the tree were exhaling rain upward and then letting it drop. The building behind me would have its own water: black pools in the floor, ferns rising through it, mineral sheen floating on the surface like pale light made liquid. When I touched its water with my roots, the sheen would move around me as if I were not an intrusion but a restoration.

The building itself would be part of my body by proximity and decay.

It is a squat circular structure, gray-black stone, roofless but intact above its walls, two stories high, its interior filled with marsh water and vegetation. Ferns grow from its upper ledges. A tree rooted in its doorway—me—has softened the transition between wall and living wood. The lattice collects there in visible layers, forming a faintly glowing skin over the stone. If a traveler touches the outer wall, the surface feels warm and damp, and inside there is a deep, patient thrumming. I would feel that thrumming through my roots as if the building were a drum beneath a lake, and the lake were breathing.

I would not know what it means. I would not care to know in that way. But I would record it. My rings would be strange things: ordinary growth rings for years when water was abundant, pale crystalline bands for years when the lattice concentrated, faint mineral seams where the building’s original channels still influenced the flow of vapor, and thin dark lines where frost—real frost, not dust—briefly touched my bark after a lapse in the ancient heat regulation. These would be my memories. Not narrative. Not intention. But patterned persistence.

This is why I choose this body: because in the High Kettles, ecology and engineering cannot be separated, and I would like to be the thing that proves it.

If I were a human, I would still be outside the place, even if soaked and sweating. I would call the heat impossible, the wetness uncanny, the altitude paradoxical, the slow mineralization strange. If I were an animal alone, I would use the valley but not necessarily carry its history. If I were the lattice alone, I would tend the valley without a face, without a dwelling, without a name.
