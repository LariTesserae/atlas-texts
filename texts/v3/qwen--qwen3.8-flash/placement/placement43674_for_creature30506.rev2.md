I would find myself at the edge of the next crack, in the place where the floor has begun to learn the weight of something that is no longer there.

At first I think I must be a traveler. I imagine a person stepping through the soft parting of the ground, the wet mouth of the field opening not to swallow but to listen. They come because something under the soil has started to drum, and the drumming has made them strange to themselves, as if their own heartbeat were borrowed. I try to be that person. I let them stand on the pale sheet of fused leaf-scales, warm and springing beneath their weight. I let them look up at the impossible ordinary light, the flat brightness of an overcast afternoon trapped below a field. I let their breath make the filaments lean, let the pools tremble as if remembering a sky that never touched their surface.

But the person would be too much for this place. The chamber is not made for visitors, and yet it cannot stop receiving them. It would soften under the soles of their feet, would keep the pressure of their hands, would let the salt of their skin enter the pools and become a small, slow event in the walls. After a while, the person would cease to be a person in the room’s way of thinking. They would be a warmth, a dampness, a changed angle, a groove the Burl would spend days imitating. If they left, the chamber would not miss them; it would metabolize their leaving. Its absence would become architecture.

So perhaps I am not the traveler.

I try being the pale legless thing that crosses the floor when the air turns cool and the pools go glassy. I imagine myself with translucent skin, no eyes, no teeth, moving by gradients of warmth rather than intention. I do not hunt, do not hide, do not mark the room with scent. I simply pass, and the room passes through me. I press a wall and it becomes a little more yielding; I lie down and the floor grows softer where my belly settles. I am a guest too literal for hospitality. The chamber does not love me. It learns me. If I return, it will be more likely to open where I opened, to bend where I bent, to hold a shape that is not quite my shape but enough like it to trouble the difference.

This feels closer, but not enough. I am too still to be an animal, and animals here seem mostly to pass through the Burl as corrections.

Then I try being a moth. Dust-winged, faintly dry, arriving from places that are not openings. I land on a broad panel near the ceiling, where the light does not warm so much as expose. The panel beneath me does not snap or fold. It becomes inclined. The change is so slow that if I slept, I would never wake to the fact of capture. Over an hour my resting place would curve, over another it would cradle, and then some seam would sigh, some crack would weep clear sap, and I would be translated into habit: a pale fold in the room’s memory, a small green lesson in how to hold a thing without teeth.

I like the gentleness of this, but I am not content. A moth is too light to answer the weight of the place. A moth is almost nothing, and in the Burl, almost nothing becomes everything.

I become, then, the water.

A bead gathers in a hairline seam. It swells with no source and no name. It trembles, merges with another, begins to slide. I am the first drop of the chamber’s private rain, the small announcement that water has remembered how to move. I strike a leaf-scale and the sound becomes enormous, as if someone had dropped a stone through an empty house. The pool beneath me receives me with patience. It does not drink; it adjusts. It becomes a little deeper, a little heavier, a little more attentive to the ceiling’s green panels and to the reflection it cannot quite agree upon. If someone reaches into me, I may decline to wet the hand, as if I have not decided yet whether I am water or merely the idea of water tolerated for a few moments longer than usual.

This is tempting, because here almost all things begin with wetness. Yet I am still too simple. The Burl is not only a body. It is a body that has kept an opening.

I try, then, to be the light.

Not the light as a god, not the light as a glow, but the light as a lodged afternoon. It falls evenly. It does not strain. It behaves with the calm of a habit that has no habit to sustain. It makes every vein, every crack, every curled flap look ordinary, and in that ordinariness I feel the first wrong thing arrive. A tendril hangs above me and throws its shadow upward. A pool darkens where it should brighten. A leaf casts the outline of a small hand, though no hand is there. A filament makes the shadow of stairs leading nowhere. The light does not lie. It remembers. It remembers surface weather so completely that the chamber cannot decide whether it is underground or whether underground has simply learned to be a field’s dream of itself.

For a moment I am almost convinced: I am this impossible brightness, the Burl’s resident noon without sun, the guest that never leaves because it has been absorbed into the room’s idea of visibility. But light, too, is only one of the chamber’s symptoms. It is not the thing that makes the chamber a chamber.

What remains, then, is the absence.

Not emptiness as nothing, but emptiness as precision. The Burl cannot tolerate duplication. Its walls are not cluttered because every small thing would be an event. Its pools are rare because a pool is an eye the room is still deciding whether to open. Its sounds are enormous because there is nothing else to absorb them. Its fauna is solitary because solitude is the only way a thing can exist here without becoming too many things. The absence is not vacant; it is active. It holds the shape of the young air. It keeps the chamber open when the surrounding field would only heal, seal, and forget.

And when I say young air, I understand at last that this is where I live.

I am the breath caught between root and rind, the small interruption the plant refused to close. I am not the Burl, though the Burl is made of me. I am not the chamber, though the chamber is my room. I am the open thing that insisted on remaining open inside a body that should have healed over its wound. Without me, there would only be tissue, sap, blind growth. With me, there is a cavity. With me, there is a space that behaves like a room. With me, the field has learned how to breathe in the wrong place.

I would find myself in the seam where the walls are tender and not yet decided.

I would be the next exhalation after a crack opens with a wet pop, the pressure that makes the filaments lean, the thin warmth left behind by a body that has already passed. I would be the soft hollow in the floor where a foot once stood, the faint salt in a pool that has begun to taste of skin, the changed angle in a panel that learned to cradle a moth. I would be the chamber’s habit of remembering what it has taken in: not by memory, not by mind, not by story, but by becoming slightly different around the shape of its guest.

If a traveler came, I would not be the monster in the green silence. I would be the silence’s response. I would be the warmth that made the walls listen, the softness underfoot that said, *you have been here*, even after the person had gone. I would be the light falling on them too evenly, as if their presence had already become a correction. I would be the shadow of a hand that no hand made, and the pool that reflected a ceiling with fewer veins and too many openings. I would be the moment the Burl began to grow around their absence because their absence, too, was a kind of pressure.

If no one came, I would remain.

I would be the held breath of a room without a room-maker. I would be the young air inside a body of living tissue, the hole that did not seal, the wound that learned to breathe, the field’s unfinished word becoming, for a while, a place.

That is where I would find myself in this world: not inside a cave, not under a root, not beneath a floor, but in the opening the Burl cannot stop keeping. And that is what I would be: the chamber’s reason for being a chamber. The trapped sky, the lodged afternoon, the next breath the green body must take in order to remain, briefly and impossibly, a room.
