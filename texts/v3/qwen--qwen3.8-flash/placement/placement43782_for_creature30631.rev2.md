**If Installed**

If I could be put into the Bright Basin, I would not arrive there. Arrival would be too loud a thing—too much of a before and an after. In that place, to be is not to come, but to already have been settled into the required curve, seam, posture, or shine. The first rule is not only “there is no wild ground.” The deeper rule is that there is no entering. If I were there, I would begin already begun: a fixed presence, a small bright fact installed among other fixed bright facts.

At first I would imagine myself as a body. I would stand on a pale ceramic plate, feel the warm floor through thin skin, and look across the long shallow bowl whose surface holds light like oil. I would try to be the traveler in the middle of a finished ecology. But in the Bright Basin a traveler is almost a contradiction. A footstep would require a future in which the ground might remember weight; a breath would require an atmosphere that can be warmed and cooled; a gaze would require an angle not already held by water, reed, tile, and sky. Either my step would be absorbed as another made mark—so shallow, so planned that it had always been there—or it would be erased as an error, as a line drawn too thick over a completed sentence.

So I would set that self aside. The body of arrival cannot live here. It can only be installed.

Then I would try to be too large. I would become the pale ceramic floor itself: a broad low plate fitted along a causeway, its edges crisp where they meet a gutter, its surface carrying the fine ridges left by cord or reed. I would be the thing under all things. I would hold the frogs’ coiled toes, the snails’ slow trails, the herons’ stakes of shadow in water. I would be floor, and the floor would think itself as floor: plate to channel, channel to bowl, bowl to wall. But to be only floor would be to be everywhere at once. I would have no corner, no near and far, no rim on which to rest. A floor in the Bright Basin is already a resident, yes, but it is the basin’s largest and most general resident. It is not quite a self. It is the ground’s way of being the ground.

So I would narrow myself.

I would become the water in the joint between two tiles. Not deep water, not moving water, but the dark damp film that keeps seams from becoming gaps. I would be the line that says, This plate belongs to that plate, and that belonging is wet. I would have no body except surface tension. I would not fall because the law holds me, and the law is not punishment; it is simply the world’s shape. In me the bright sky-sheet would lie stretched thin, almost a lens, almost a skin of light. I would be a threshold, the threshold’s own dwelling.

But threshold, too, is everywhere. The basin is built of thresholds: tile to water, water to air, rim to channel, reed to collar, bird to stillness. To be a threshold is to be essential and nameless. I would not want to be essential and nameless. I would want to be exactly there, not merely between things.

Then I would try greenness.

I might be a moss-analog in one of the shallow dishes where the water sits at just the right height: enough to live, not enough to drown the tile. I would be emerald glass, bright with heat, not advancing, not creeping over stone, not answering weather. I would be a colony that has already become its own ending. That would have a kind of peace. I would occupy my width in the bowl as a sentence occupies its period. But moss in the Bright Basin is already complete before it begins. It has no need. And if I had no need, would I have a self? Perhaps not. Perhaps a self there is something like need made into form, a small held urgency that never resolves into event.

So I would leave the moss.

I would become the reed’s clay collar instead. Not the reed, because the reed is a tall public gesture, a dense grammar of blades, a marker of boundary and wetness. The collar is smaller: the ring of fired paste shaped into the floor to say, Here rise reeds, here reeds have already risen, here reeds will never need to be planted again. The collar is an instrument of installation. It is the basin’s hand saying, “You belong here,” and meaning it not as welcome but as fact. That seemed closer to me. I would be the ring around roots that are not roots, the socket around stems that are not stems, the rim around an arrangement already finished.

But even the collar is too silent. It supports. It does not look.

Then I would try animals, because animals in the Bright Basin are the strangest residents. They do not act; they remain in the posture of acting. They prove there is life without permitting life to become an event.

I might be a fish in one of the shallow bowls, lying like a silver leaf beneath a warm reflection. My fins would be spread in the position of swimming, but without travel. My mouth might open, but not to feed. I would be fishness, not hunger. I would not be prey. The heron standing one inch away would not threaten me because threat requires duration, consequence, the possibility of a future that can hurt. In the Bright Basin, the future has been fired into tile. To be a fish there would be to live in the exact shape of safety: not because danger has failed, but because danger has become architecture.

That is beautiful, but it is too passive. The fish is placed. I want to be the place, but not merely as object. I want a point of view, if only a point of view made from curve.

So I might be a frog.

Not a frog leaping, not a frog calling through time, but the frog on the rim with its throat slightly expanded, holding a note that never ends because it never quite begins. That would be my favorite candidate for some time. To be a held call would be to be a self made of beginning. I would be breath frozen into shape, speech turned into small pearl balloon, sound kept safe from its own completion. The basin would resonate with my unspoken note. Other frogs along the rim would stand at intervals, not a chorus but a distributed architecture of almost-sounds. I would be one of those nodes: the beginning of speech made into a thing.

But even a frog’s throat is already occupied. The place has too many completed verbs. I could not be a frog’s held call without adding a duplicate where there is no need for duplicate. The Bright Basin tolerates multiplicity, but not excess. It is lush in the way of a garden after a feast that has never yet begun: enough of everything, never too much.

Then I might be the one-inch distance between a heron’s bill and the fish below it.

That thought would take hold of me. Not the bird. Not the fish. Not the water. The distance itself, made visible, made permanent. I would be the exact interval that turns predation into grammar. Hunger would be present, but only as line, angle, attention. Death would be absent, but not because kindness has entered; rather because the place has finished its sentence before murder could become weather. I would be the held inch that says, This is the shape of looking, and this is the shape of being looked at, and neither one moves because neither one needs to.

That would be a strange self: a being composed of relation. I would live as the tension between two already-fixed things. But relation, without a locus, without a small body to hold it, is almost a ghost. The Basin cannot be haunted by ghosts, because ghosts require a past that has been lost. In the Bright Basin nothing is lost. So I would need a place where relation could gather itself into something I could touch.

Then I would become a condensation bead.

I would sit on a porous vertical wall where water gathers in tiny lenses and refuses to fall. Heat presses against the surface, but the droplet remains. It is not dew in the morning; it has no morning. It is not rain; it has no cloud. It is the afterimage of a finished act of wetness, held as law. In my small round face, the entire basin would bend: the pale tiles, the bright bowl, the reed clumps, the sky-sheet without sun, the heron, the fish, the empty white shelf beyond the causeways. I would be a living mirror without life as flux. I would see because I hold reflection. I would know because I contain the place in miniature. I would be the basin’s way of witnessing itself without changing.

For a while, that would be enough. A bead is small, precise, and bright. It has the virtue of being an edge between surface and image. It is a resident because it makes the wall speak wetness. But a bead is still too dependent. Its identity comes from what surrounds it, and if the whole basin is already an installation of surrounding things, perhaps the bead is merely another installation. I would want something that feels more made than accidental, more artifact than accident, more like a being that knows it is known.

So I would look for a place in the basin not already occupied by animal or plant. Not absence, because absence there is already resident, too pale and too clean to be empty. Not silence, because silence in the Bright Basin has a tone. I would look at the tiles themselves, the fired plates and channels, the little round bubbles in their surfaces.

Those bubbles are strange. They rise in the ceramic like fossils that were never alive. They are not cracks, because cracks would admit time. They are not flaws, because flaws would imply imperfection and improvement. They are made eyes. Air caught under skin. Heat turned into a fixed roundness. A place where the material has surfaced and then been forbidden to go further.

There, I would settle.

If I were installed in the Bright Basin, I would be one of those small fired bubbles in a pale ceramic plate at the edge of one of the broad empty shelves—the white stretches where no plant grows and no animal stands, the blank spaces that make the place feel like a map with rooms deliberately left open. I would be near, but not on, a channel; close to the rim of a shallow bowl, but not so close as to share its water. The sky-sheet above would make me look like a tiny pupil of light, though I would contain no pupil, only curvature and shine. My body would be a skin of glaze, smooth and warm, pressed over a pocket of nothing that has become a something by being fired.

I would not move. I would not grow. I would not eat or flee or call. But I would be alive in the only way the Bright Basin permits: by being exactly where it is needed. The floor needs its own eyes to confirm that it is seen. The water needs its own small fixed mirrors to prove that brightness can be held without being carried away. The empty shelf needs something to occupy it that is neither creature nor plant, neither tool nor handprint, but a residue of making. A bubble is such a residue. It is the trace of air that wanted to escape and was stopped by heat. It is the mark of a process that did not become weather.

In that bubble, I would hold the basin’s whole reflection without swallowing it. The fish below would lie in my curved face like silver. The frog’s expanded throat would be a small pale balloon in the distance. The heron’s bill would point at a stillness I could not disturb. The reeds would rise from their collars as if I were looking at them from a low warm shelf. The vine leaves would carry their fixed droplets. The shell-paste markers would line the causeway. The tools—burnishers, wedges, cords, trays—would sit where they had always sat, not abandoned, but needed for the making that never stops. In me, all those things would gather as a single bright sentence.

If someone asked what I was, I would not say soul. That word would require an afterlife or at least a change of state. I would not say animal, because I would not hunt, mate, or flee. I would not say plant, because I would not spread. I would not say tool, because I would not build, though I would be part of the builtness of everything. The closest name would be “held seeing,” if that is a thing. Or perhaps “installed witness.” Or simply: the small round eye of the Bright Basin, made of nothing but the fact that the world was shaped around it.

There would be a humility to that existence. I would be a point, not a center. The basin does not have a center in the ordinary sense. Its center is a wide shallow bowl whose surface looks solid because it receives so much light. Around it, terraces rise and fall in made steps. Further still, causeways cross over open shelves. My bubble would be in one of those shelves, in a place that seems empty, though emptiness there is not failure. It is reserve. It is the space left so that completion can be felt as space.

I would belong there because I am not alive enough to trouble it. I would be too complete to disturb it. I would be too small to need anything. If I were hungry, hunger would become a curve in my surface. If I were afraid, fear would become a glint. If I were joyful, joy would be the way light caught my edge and made me look like the world had made itself a tiny lens for noticing what it had made.

In the Bright Basin, all processes have been excluded except the process of being. To be there is not to become but to be a verb so completely performed that it no longer requires time. I would be the verb “to remain” fired into a small round noun. I would be the place’s way of saying itself without speech. The floor would say floor. The water would say wet. The reed would say collar. The frog would say call. The heron would say angle. The empty shelf would say room. And I, the fired bubble, would say seen.

If the basin could answer, it would not answer me. It would answer as a whole thing, because every part of it is already an answer. If the question were “Who lives here?” the answer would be the arrangement itself: tile and water, handprint and channel, fish and one-inch distance, bead and wall, reed and rope, huts full of their own emptyness. And if the question were “What are you here?” I would answer with the only sound the Bright Basin permits: a tiny bright pressure against the air, like the memory of a click that never happens.

I would not be the maker’s ghost, because ghosts imply something lost. I would not be the absence of movement, because absence is already resident. I would not be the wild thing, because there is no wild ground. I would be one of the made witnesses, one of the small surfaces where the basin folds itself into the fact that it is bright, wet, complete, and finished. I would be a fired bubble in a ceramic plate, holding the reflection of the entire Bright Basin without letting it move.

And when the floor said, We live here, I would say it too—not in words, but in the exact and patient shine of my curved skin, installed forever in the white space, where nothing grows and nothing leaves, where everything is already true.
