Glass is not a window here. It is a cooled eye set into the walls: smoked, faintly green, precious enough that a single pane may belong to a family across generations. It does not frame a view, because there is no view; it holds a small, borrowed light and lets it breathe back into the room.

I would find myself in that borrowed light, on the central gallery where the settlement is densest and the chamber still feels young enough to watch me. Not at the crack-mouth above the salt-stones, where the descent begins. Not down in the lowest hot galleries, where iron blooms in red-black crust and the water tastes too close to fire. Midway, instead: in the vertical middle where heat and condensation pass each other like two people who share a house but rarely meet. I would stand where the town can be heard breathing, where the walls pulse once every half-hour, where a single wrong step on a mossed ladder could disturb the rhythm of a room for days.

I would be one of the Ashroot.

But not only a person, if “person” means something separate from the place it lives in. I would be a condensation-tender, a valve-listener, a moss-mapper, a mender of flows. My body would be human enough to carry the name, but my awareness would begin at the skin and continue outward along copper tubing, wet root, blackened brass, and the pale silver trails left by slow water. Salt would bloom at my hairline in fine white crowns. My hair would be tight against my scalp, braided with root fiber and thin wire because loose strands catch on valves and bring spores into sleeping places. My hands would be broad, warm, and stained by metal that has been handled until it became almost affectionate. My feet would know which stones are cooler, which are fever-hot, which are too wet to trust.

I would not say, “This is my house.” I would say, “This is my flow.”

A room would belong to me only as long as I could keep its living balance. The trickling shelf above the basin, the thermal seam under the floor, the vine that drinks from the condensation line, the fungal shelf that must be thinned once every few rains, the beam that hums when the air shifts behind it—these would be my obligations. If I pruned too little, the wall would become too soft, the room too damp, the house a nest of black-green fur. If I pruned too much, the plaster would crack, the heat would dry the lintels, and the ceiling might forget how to weep. To live here would be to tend a temperature, not merely to occupy a space.

I would wake by tasting the morning condensation.

There is no sun to announce itself, so waking is a matter of reading the chamber. A faint change in the hiss below the floor. A bead of water gathering on the copper rail beside my bed. The smell of warm leaf and mineral dust. The taste, perhaps, of a little salt on the lips, because salt is always nearby here, in the stone, in the hair, in the sweat, in the memory of the crack that opened. I would not stretch loudly. Loudness is for places with empty space. I would place one hand on the wall and wait. If it pulsed again within the half-hour, I would note it: the beam is breathing, the pocket behind it has shifted, something small has changed in the chamber’s lungs. I would remember it later when the pressure in the lower pipes begins to rise.

My name, if I had one, would be kept not only in sound but in material. A notch along a pipe. A coil of vine tied around a handrail in the family gallery. A layer of ash scraped into a clay pot during the season when the upper crack exhales green. Perhaps a moss color at the edge of the lamp-shelf that only I would know by heart. The Ashroot are people, but their identities are not sealed inside their skulls. They are spread across the settlement like lichen across stone. If I vanished from my room, I would still be present in the way I kept the basin, the cut vines, the taste of the water stored there, the faint wear on the ladder rung I climbed every day.

I would be a person, yes. But I would also be a process.

When the warm mist falls from the ceiling, I would hold out my tongue to it. When the basins fill, I would test their water by mineral breath, by the green memory of the rock, by the faint heat left in the clay lining. When a press groans too softly, I would suspect a blockage in the returning cool-air loop. When a lamp flickers behind smoked glass, I would check the algal film on its shelf, the moisture in its casing, the tiny blue-green colonies cultivated there like shy children. I would not call these jobs with separate names. They would all be one act: listening to the chamber.

I would belong to the dense, layered life of the place, not to its empty spaces. There are no parks here. No clearings. No idle corners except corners in danger of being claimed. So I would be part of the occupation itself: the moss and the machine and the hand moving between them. I would not merely use the settlement; the settlement would use me. It would demand that I climb without noise, adjust without haste, and leave no dry place unused. It would teach my body to move with economy: no leaping, no shouting, no sudden gestures. A vine-stair may be holding the heat of the room. A pipe may be carrying the breath of a family three levels below. To treat the place as scenery would be to risk collapse. To touch the wrong surface lightly may be to start a week’s condensation somewhere else.

If I stood at the high platform in the central gallery, I would see the whole place at once, and the seeing would feel like being seen. The stacked rooms, the glowing pipes, the black moss covering unused walls, the occasional silver rain, the pale pools shimmering in alcoves. The settlement would look geological from above, but from inside it would look domestic, intimate, wet with daily care. I would be both: one small human body and part of the chamber’s slow metabolism.

Glass is rare here, then. A good pane might be set where a family wants light to travel softly, where a valve needs to be watched, where a lamp-shelf must glow without drying too much air. I would carry a small piece of glass if I could, polished by hand, edged in vine resin. It would not let me see outside. It would help me read the inside better.

In the end, I would be what the place says when it has hands: a hot, damp, copper-veined thought growing through the seam between ash and root. I would be an Ashroot, a keeper of flows, a person made partly of the chamber itself. I would not be standing beneath the world as a visitor. I would be the world’s own slow voice, breathing through moss, moving through pipes, tasting the warm rain when it finally forgets to hold back.
