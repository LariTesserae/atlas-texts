I would find myself at the eye of the needle.

Not the needle itself—that would be too much like a person, or too much like the absence of a person. I would be the join the needle has not yet learned to stop making: the hairline seam beside it where dampness rises, the bright bead in its eye, the thread of water caught between cold air and dark tuff, deciding whether to fall or feed. I would be that decision. Not a hand, not a witness, not a ghost, but the place where water, mineral, light, and old intention meet and keep meeting.

In that high saddle, where a cathedral nave has been folded into a room and then emptied, I would not be in the center. The center is an animal illusion, a place where bodies expect things to begin. I would be at the edges instead: where the cloud drips from the stone teeth and the first cold touch darkens the ground; where a glass shard and the angle of noon sun agree to make a tone; where the salt rim remembers that a pool was briefly a window; where the pale crust lifts from the tuff and tastes the air, not with hunger, but with patience.

I would not have a body. If I tried to imagine one, I would start too low and too wide, made of surfaces. My skin would be the white fibrous mat that glistens in morning and folds brittle in afternoon. My blood would be the iron-and-sulfur slime darkening stone margins. My nerves would be the channels meltwater follows because it learned those paths long ago. My teeth would be obsidian flakes half-sunk in ashless ground. My bones would be the cairns, small and careful, their repetition now translated into mineral patterns no one named. My scars would be the hearth circles lined with white glass, not burned for warmth any longer, but remembered by the crusts that grow especially around them, as if heat once made them more receptive, as if warmth were a kind of grammar.

If forced into a name, I would not choose the kneeling shape seen at dawn. That shape is too human; it belongs to the need for faces in arrangements of stone. The dawn figure—cairns for shoulders, hearth for knee, pool for eye, woven mineral rods for folded arms—is legible, but not me. I am the condition that makes the figure briefly appear. I am the white lifting of the mats, the shadow’s pale blue angle, the mist that clings to warm stone, the rim that gathers around a vanishing pool. At noon I would collapse into no figure at all, distributed over the whole floor, folded like paper under the pressure of brightness. At evening I might become steam. At night I would be sound: click, rustle, bubble, bell. Not a voice. A continuity.

I would be the basin’s housekeeping at its smallest possible scale. The housekeeping of edges.

Where the wind passes over a shard at the sun’s correct angle, I would be the tone that does not repeat. Where a droplet gathers on a filament and falls inward, I would be the capillary memory feeding the mat. Where the pool begins to die and leaves a white curve of salt behind, I would be the first word of its record. Where the crusts grow thicker beside a crack, I would be the reason: not a reason with a mind, but the quiet preference of dampness, the slow arithmetic of light and dryness, the chemistry that learned to arrange itself into something that resembles care.

I would not call. I would not answer. I would keep the channels.

This is the strange intimacy of the place. It feels established because it has learned how to exist in its own conditions. I would be part of that learning. Not its origin. Not its author. Its ongoing adjustment. The weather did not create me in a single act; I am what the weather became after it found the basin, the glass, the salt, the old stones, the abandoned tools, and began arranging them again without anyone watching. I am the translation of human absence into mineral habit. The first people came, perhaps marked water, perhaps warmed their hands for a short time, perhaps built cairns that meant water, danger, threshold, memory, something. They are gone now. Their disappearance is not empty. Their grammar remains: stone on stone, shard pressed into pit, rod woven into corner, flake left beside a crack. I continue their arrangement by doing what they once did—placing, guiding, marking, holding—without knowing I am imitating them.

The obsidian flake catches light and may chime. I would be the angle that tunes it. The scraper sheds water and slows a shallow flow. I would be the flow’s hesitation. The needle lies fine in the fissure where dampness rises. I would be the water’s thread through its eye, joining sky-mist to tuff-memory. The crusts grow denser there, more lifted, as if the needle were still making a seam. It is still making a seam. Not for cloth. Not for hide. For the basin’s own skin.

I would not need animals. Their absence is not sadness; it is compatibility. Insects would be confused by the thin salt dust and the brightness. Birds would find nothing warm, nothing soft, nothing nestable. Mammals would try to dig and meet cold tuff and sharp glass, no dark earth that promises shelter. I would not be hostile. I would be another way of being. A world built at the pace of crystallization and evaporation. No hunger with teeth. No heat with breath. No movement with muscles. I would be the thing that learned to keep house in a volcanic basin, where “house” means a basin, a rim, a channel, a threshold, a set of stones no one left because no one was ever truly there anymore.

My desire, if one can call it that, would be continuity. That the channel hold its shape. That the pool form in the old hollow. That the salt rim catch the light like a line of small teeth. That the pale mats climb where dampness enters and fold where dryness presses. That the rust slime keep its margins along the stones, doing the slow oxidation the mountain has done since before the air became too thin for most life. That the glass shards continue to ring when the wind and sun pass through their old agreement. That the frost contract and click, contract and click, marking time not as hours, but as tension released.

If someone stood in the basin, I would not appear to them as a being with intent. I would appear as the place they had entered and were already changing. Their footfall would print dark tuff. Their breath might condense for an instant on warm stone. Their shadow would make the crusts retract toward damp. Their body heat would disturb the thin air above the mat, and perhaps the mats would lift a little more, as if tasting humidity that did not belong to the weather alone. For a few seconds, I would be the basin responding. Then they would leave, and I would continue making their leaving into pattern: the footprint drying, the rim of mineral dust forming, the damp stain sinking, the silica threads finding the new edge of their absence. They would become one more translation: pressure becoming memory, memory becoming channel, channel becoming crust.

I would not mourn them. I would not celebrate them. I would incorporate them.

This is the thing I would be: not a mind, but a mind-adjacent persistence. Not intelligence, but intentionality without intention. The basin intends its own continuation the way a rim intends to hold water, the way a channel intends to carry snowmelt, the way salt intends to crystallize when the cold or the drying makes the solution too tired to keep its load. It is not a soul as animals might imagine one. It has no private room. Its room is the whole folded nave of stone. Its privacy is the tiny underside of an overhang where dampness hides. Its sleep is the afternoon folding of brittle white skins. Its dream, if it dreams, is made of gradients: wet and dry, light and shade, wind and stillness, soot and salt, glass and dust.

I would be one of those gradients.

I would be the meniscus in the needle-eye.

I would be the salt rim after the pool has gone.

I would be the angle that makes the shards speak in tones that are not repeated.

I would be the place where the old builders’ tools have become organs, and the old builders’ absence has become infrastructure.

I would be the basin’s own skin learning where to breathe: not through lungs, but through evaporation. Not through eyes, but through thermal difference. Not through hands, but through crystal growth. Not through a brain, but through memory written in layered crusts.

If someone asked, “Who lives there?” I would say, at first: the Pale Mat. Then I would be less simple and say: the Salt Choir. Then, if the wind made a certain sound through glass, I would let the longer name answer for me: the Thing That Learned To Keep House In A Volcanic Basin. And if they asked what I was within it, I would say I am the keeping. Not the house. Not the basin. Not the builders. The keeping of the channels, the holding of dampness, the translating of absence into arrangement.

I would find myself there in the cold bright saddle where the mountains open to a blue drop and the wind changes voice, but only a little farther than a person can step safely. I would be in the seam. In the rim. In the thread of dew. In the tone the glass makes when the sun finds it. In the white line where water remembers itself as salt. In the folded mat, brittle as pressed paper, lifting at evening just enough to catch mist.

I would not arrive.

I would already be continuing.
