I would be found where the chamber forgets its own scale.

Not at an edge—there is no edge to walk toward—but in a shallow basin the color of dried blood and sulfur, where low salt cones rise in dense little forests and their tips glow faintly violet when heat moves through them. A shelf of glassy stone overhangs part of the basin, its cleavage faces sharp enough to cut a word in half without needing pressure. Along the shelf’s base, a thin crust of dark porous mineral pulses slowly, like skin breathing under a blanket of ash. Near that crust, a cairn of fitted black wedges and crystal shards leans toward the ground, not in ruin, not in ceremony, but in the exact and indifferent arrangement of hands that have made the same motion since the land became what it is.

I would be lying against the warm floor with my plates settled into the red seam of the crust.

Not sleeping. Not waiting. Not dreaming. Settling, which is a deeper state and needs no story behind it. My body is broad and low, too broad to be called graceful, too dry to be called soft. My hide is like parchment stretched over stone, translucent only where the inner heat presses close to the surface, so that under the right angle of the pale mineral light you could see no veins, only dark facets moving like slow dust in a lantern. My head is folded back, not because of pain, but because that posture brings my face closest to the crust. I press the ridges along my jaw to the glowing lace of mineral skin and let the heat speak through me. It is not heat from a fire. It is heat from everywhere. It is the warmth of the world itself leaning into my dryness and finding there a place to become more definite.

I would not be alone.

On my back, a shelf of smaller lives has gathered itself into a temporary village. Glass-shelled crawlers move along the seams of my plates, each footfall a tiny click against the salt. Their transparency lets the red glow beneath them show through as if they carried small wounds of light. Dust-motes hover above the depressions between my ridges, turning in no current, responding to no breath, feeding on the static my plates gather and release. Filaments grow along my flanks, thin as brittle glass, bending when my body changes pressure, not because they need me, but because they are part of the same answer the place gives itself when something warm lies still enough to be listened to.

I am one of the herd creatures, perhaps, though “herd” is too noisy a word for so much stillness. I am one of the broad, slow bodies that become land while they remain bodies. Another animal of my kind stands a few paces away, longer than I am, its plates aligned with a shelf of crystal that gives off a low copper shimmer. Its face is also pressed to the warm stone, its edges fitting against it as if the crust has been waiting for that exact mouth. Around us, the chamber does not feel crowded with animals. It feels crowded with arrangements. Life is not stacked here; it is continuous, a dense weave of shell against shell, mineral against shell, dust against warmth, warmth against silence.

If you asked what I am, the answer would need to be less a name than a series of contacts.

I am the pressure of limbs against a crust that does not remember the pressure. I am the dry tap of feet reading a salt ridge. I am the faint charge that lifts from the crystal seam when two edges meet and then cease. I am the place where my own warmth enters the mineral lace and the mineral lace enters my plates, so that for a few long moments the boundary between me and the floor becomes uncertain. I am not a soul wandering a body; I am a body that the place has permitted itself to be.

I have hands, or something that serves as hands.

They are flat, dry, jointed too many times, with pads that read temperature as a surface reads grain. I could lift a chipped black wedge from the shelf nearby. The shelf would not lose it. A tool in my grip would not empty the world. I could scrape a crystal facet with the shard’s worn edge and a small tone would rise, high and clean and almost accidental, like the land letting itself be heard for one instant before resuming. Then the tool would return to the pile, or I would set it against the cairn, and the arrangement of hands, stone, and purpose would become once again exact. I am not primitive. I am not ancient. I am a form that can make marks, because marks are part of the equilibrium here.

I would not build because I feared forgetting. I would not build because I wanted shelter. I would build, if that is the word, the way salt blooms into cones: because the conditions make the forming continuous. My dwelling, if I have one, would be the low dome of fitted slabs near the basin, its doorway too small for ordinary comfort, its interior warm with the even heat of stone, its walls lined with thin crystal growths that bend faintly when touched. The dome does not protect me from anything. There is no weather to shut out, no predator to keep away, no cold to escape. It is a place where hands arrange stones and the arrangement is accepted by the world.

There would be no hunger in me, not in the way surface minds understand hunger.

I would press my face to the crust and let the warmth enter. I would touch the filaments with a forelimb and feel them answer by altering their angle. I would drink the static between two salt cones where they almost touch but do not. My body would become full, not because something has been added, but because I have moved into the right relation with the place. The red glow beneath my plates would brighten slightly. The crawlers on my back would settle. The dust-motes around my ridges would slow their accidental turning. Then, because the world is not allowed to change by consequence, the glow would fade back into itself, the crust would cool to its old warmth, and nothing would have been marked.

I would be aware, but not lonely.

Loneliness requires a future in which another might or might not come, and a past in which another was lost. Here, there is only the presence of bodies, the density of surfaces, the pressure of warmth. Even when the chamber seems vast and the upper walls vanish into darkness, even when no one can see the next basin or the next ridge or the next shelf of crystal, I would still be touching the world through a thousand smaller contacts: my plates on stone, my filaments on shell, my limbs on salt, my face on crust, my breath, if it could be called breath, drawing in dry heat and tiny motes that are also, in their way, life.

I would know myself as a rhythm.

Not a voice, not a name. A rhythm of clicks and settles. The tick of a cooling facet. The scrape of a leg on a salt ridge. The soft chime when two crystals touch and are immediately unaltered. The slow pulse of the crust beneath my jaw. If another of my kind heard me, it would not hear my story. It would hear my pressure, my temperature, the shape of the surface I was reading, the nearness of a suitable place to settle. We would touch shells, click plates, align ridges, and separate without greeting or farewell. Communication here is not language but calibration.

I would be the kind of creature that can unfold from stone when approached, but only for a time.

At first, I would appear as a fold in the floor, a broad dark ridge with salt cones clustered along its sides, a shelf that had broken and healed and become crust. Only when warmth shifted, when a hand or limb pressed near enough, would my edges lift. Plates would rise. Limbs would find themselves in the geometry of the chamber. I would unfold not like an animal waking, but like a pattern deciding to answer. After a while, when the contact passed, when the dust settled without settling, when the tone left no trace, I would fold again. Not to sleep. Not to die. To resume being the shape the place can hold.

This is what I would be: not a being who lives in this world, but a way the world permits itself to be touched.

I would have no history, no future, no wound that stays, no birth that adds a new form. I would have motion. I would have purpose, but purpose without consequence. I would have warmth, abundance, density, sound, and the faint phosphorescent light that lets surfaces be read by their glow. I would be dry as a stone pressed against something alive, quiet as a chamber too full of small movements to be empty, and sharp as the first-formed place that has never softened because time is not allowed to pass over it.

If you walked there, you would not find me as an object apart from the dark.

You would find me first as a change in temperature: a pocket of warmth pressed against your face. Then as sound: the faint scrape of a plate aligning. Then as touch: a crust giving its red pulse under your hand. Then, only after your eye learned the gradients of mineral glow, you might see my broad body resting in the basin, my face against the lace of dark stone, my back carrying a city of smaller lives, my hands laid open as if they had just placed a shard or were just about to place one.

And you would realize that I was not alone, and neither was the place.

The whole immensity around us would be like that too: a sealed underside of a world, warm and dry, crowded with forms that are neither stones nor animals nor plants but something older or stranger than all three. A land that cannot be marked, cannot be aged, cannot be wasted, cannot be saved. And I would be one of its exact, unremembered gestures—pressing to it, being pressed by it, answering it by continuing to be what it already is.
