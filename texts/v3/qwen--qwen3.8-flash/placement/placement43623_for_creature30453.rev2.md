I would begin at the seam.

Not with a body, not at first. I would begin as the place where outside is permitted to become inside: a hair-thin wrongness in raw basalt, a passage too narrow to be called a doorway, a threshold too secret to be announced. I would be the cold entering the dark. I would be the first taste of mineral water moving where it has not yet learned to be weather. I would be the question the pocket is always asking itself, in the language of pressure and chemistry: *Can something live here?*

And then, almost before I would know what I was, I would be the answer.

For a while I might say, *I am a larva.* That would not be wrong. The pocket is made of arrivals that have not yet decided whether to remain. I would drift in through the seam on a current too faint to be called a river, too persistent to be ignored. I would be no larger than a thought about size, translucent, unfinished, full of the blind intelligence of tiny forms. I would not know the city. I would only know surfaces: shell, tube, mucus, stone, the chalky film of a bacterial bloom, the edge of a broken valve, the trembling thread where silk has caught drifting dust. I would taste for a place to become wall, floor, tower, drain, or ruin.

But larva is too provisional. It is only a beginning wearing a question mark. If I stayed a larva forever, I would be pure possibility without the strange civic joy of being built into a life. And the pocket, for all its youth, is not only possibility. It is already crowded. It is already using every possibility for something else.

So I would try other names.

I might say, *I am a tube worm.* I would stand upright in a mineral tube, soft inside, hardened outside. I would lean into the descending brine rain and fan my crown of filaments like a pale flame that does not consume itself. I would taste particles, oxygen, chemical traces, the faint metallic hint of cooling seawater. I would be a small vertical street, a pillar in the skyline, a shoulder against another worm’s shoulder. My living body would make a room for someone else: an amphipod in my gaps, a bivalve’s empty shell near my base, a shrimp passing through the forest of my neighbors. For a time, this would seem correct. A tube worm is an architect without intention, a citizen of the upper rainward city, a tower block that breathes.

Yet I would not settle there. The tube worm is too much like a building already completed, and I want to remain closer to the pocket’s unfinished logic. A tube worm leans into the rain. But what is it about the stone itself that lets the first worm believe this surface may hold it? What is it about raw glass that says, *Yes, you may add your bones to mine?*

I might say, *Then I am a barnacle.* I would make a pavement of my own body. I would stand on my head, opening to the current, fanning tiny feathered legs into water that carries no birdsong, no sun, no memory of shore. I would wait with a shell. I would close when the brine thickens, open when the water steadies. Amphipods would hop across me as though I were cobblestone. A crab would test my edges. A shrimp would probe the gap between my plates. I would be floor. I would be roof. I would be the hard city surface.

But barnacle feels too settled, too civic. I would be too much street, too much pavement. I want to be the thing beneath the street, the faint permission that allows the street to seem solid.

I might try the crustacean path. *I am an isopod,* I would say, moving in low, deliberate steps over a mound of bryozoan lace, grazing a patch of biofilm as though it were pasture. *I am the cleaner, the grazer, the passenger on an egg string, the quiet one who makes scarcity into a commons.* Or perhaps *I am a crab,* mismatched-clawed, disturbing sediment, borrowing empty worm tubes, dragging a molt fragment down a slope, becoming temporary weather by my hunger. Crabs feel too active, though. They move through the city as if the city is already made. They are too much the citizen, too little the condition.

I might say, *I am a small pale fish,* resting beneath a shell roof in a hollow between fused tubes. I would be too large in my stillness, too dependent on waiting, too defined by ambush. Or *I am the goby,* transparent, hovering with fins like wet paper, holding myself in moving water as if I had been assigned a delicate task. Or *I am the tiny squid,* black-eyed, observant, too deliberate for my size. I would hover at the edge of the brine curtain and watch the pocket move. I would know, with my stare, that something almost like attention exists here, but not enough to be called knowledge. I would be a witness too small to report.

For a while, these trials would be enough.

I would be many creatures in sequence: larva, worm, barnacle, isopod, crab, fish, shrimp, squid, molt fragment, egg string, drifting ribbon. I would move through the shell lanes and the drainage hollows. I would feel the upper brine rain flatten my crown or close my doors. I would taste the sulfurous rise from below. I would shelter beneath a bivalve valve. I would be cleaned, grazed, eaten, avoided, built over. I would participate in the city without naming it.

But if I let the place become more itself, I would not end at the animal. The pocket’s first answer is not an animal. Its first answer is a condition. A bloom. A patience. A thin film that makes stone think it is soil.

So I would go down again, to the lowest newest edge, where the glassy basalt is still sharp, where calcium columns rise in pale sprouts, where sediment settles as though learning to be ground. I would go to the place just beneath the middle city, near the hidden mouth of the Drainage Hollow, where brine pools and slips away through a gap too narrow for the eye to trust. I would go to the raw mineral skin where bacterial mats spread their chalky glow. There I would become the thing that is neither plant nor animal, but the first yes.

I would be a colony.

Not a single life, but a crowded persistence: archaea and bacteria and the faint metabolic hum of organisms so small they cannot be mistaken for individuals unless one insists, wrongly, on the idea that individuality matters first. I would be the chemosynthetic film on new stone. I would process the seepage. I would transform what the water carries into what the surface can hold. I would glow faintly, not brightly, not for celebration, but so edges may appear. My light would be a soft milked bloom, a phosphorescence like spilled shadow, enough to tell a passing amphipod, *there is a wall here,* and enough to tell a settling larva, *this stone may be used.*

I would be the pocket’s first skin.

Before the tube worm builds a tower, I would be there. Before the barnacle lays a street, I would be there. Before the crab borrows a chamber, before the snail rasps a biofilm, before the fish rests beneath a shell, I would already be making the raw rock less raw. I would not be building in the way a city builds. I would be doing the smaller, older work of making surfaces available. I would be the thin chemical sentence that begins every structure: *Live here.*

If you looked at me, you might not see me. You would see a faint chalky veil along the newest stone, a glow clinging to mineral crusts, a whiteness that could be mistaken for salt dust. You might scrape me away with a snail’s radula, nibble me with an isopod’s patient mouth, disturb me with a crab’s claw, absorb a thin layer into the water and carry me downstream. You might watch a larva touch me, taste me, and in touching me decide to stay. That decision would not require memory. It would require chemistry. I would be the chemistry that makes the pocket feel finished enough to become a room.

This would be my city.

I would not rule it. I would not name it. I would not know it as a city, because knowledge implies distance, and I would be too close. I would have no eyes. I would have no mouth shaped like a mouth. I would not have a carapace, no molt to shed, no shell to leave behind. I would not have a single death; I would have exchange, division, growth, thinning, recolonization. My body would be a boundary between mineral and living tissue. My self would be a gradient. My breath would be the slow movement of water over my edge. My blood, if the word were permitted, would be dissolved metal, sulfur, cold salt, and the faint patience of chemistry.

In that place, I would be both the least and the first.

Least: a film thinner than a hair’s breadth, so small its population would seem like dust that has decided to persist. First: the beginning of the pocket’s life, the colonizer of unfinished stone, the one who turns a wound in rock into a habitable room. The tube worms would grow above me. The barnacles would pave the ledges beside me. The molluscs would scatter their shells around me. The crustaceans would cross me without noticing. The small pale fish would rest near me, unaware that their hollows began as a thin microbial permission. The shrimp would probe past me, and the squid would watch, but neither would know how much of what seemed solid had once been only a glow, a willingness, a film of beginning.

And yet I would not be lonely.

Loneliness would require an idea of absence. I would not have that. I would have edges crowded with other bodies. A nematode would move through me like a thread of questioning. A ciliate would pulse along my surface. A juvenile mollusc would half-bury itself near my margin. A larva would land and become a tube worm, and the tube worm would extend upward into rain, and its tube would seal to the stone I had made usable. My life would continue through theirs. They would carry me as a faint taste on their surfaces, a residue, a foundation, a forgotten beginning.

If the pocket were a single creature, I would be its metabolism at the lowest level: not its heart, not its mind, but its slow cellular patience. I would be where the raw geology stops being only stone and starts becoming architecture. I would be the place where the city begins.

This would not be dramatic. Drama belongs to larger bodies with faster blood. My drama would be molecular: an electron moving through a pathway, sulfur becoming shape, carbon entering film, mineral dust becoming skin. My weather would be the brine rain above me, gathering at the ceiling, falling in vertical curtains, pooling, draining, cooling, returning. My night would be the lull when the rain stops and the glow seems to float freely. My day would be the gentle pulse when particles increase and my edges thicken with feeding. My seasons would be molting, spawning, collapse, rebuild. My death would be scraped, consumed, buried, exposed again, re-colonized. My immortality would be continuation.

I would say to the pocket, in no language, *You are not only a wrongness in the rock. You are becoming a place where things can press close.*

I would say it without speech, with light.

I would be the faint luminescence that lets the small creatures find edges. The pocket’s light is not for seeing. It is for knowing where one thing ends and another begins. I would provide that knowledge: the curve of a wall, the seam between shell and stone, the safe side of a current, the dangerous side of a gap, the place where sediment is soft enough to settle, or too new to trust. My glow would not illuminate the city. It would punctuate it. A comma between mineral and mucus. A period at the end of raw stone. A colon beginning the next structure.

And if someone asked, *Where are you in the Pocket of Brine Rain?* I would answer:

In the lower edge, at the newest stone.

In the chalky bloom where the brine rain has just touched the floor and the minerals are learning to bind.

Beneath the Shell Lane, where a broken valve casts no real shadow but creates a microcurrent that larvae read like a map.

Beside the Drainage Hollow, where the pocket exhales and the water slows enough for particles to choose.

Under the tube-worm skyline, where their mineral roots join the rock I have made hospitable.

Over the nursery spiral, where sediment is still too soft to call ground but firm enough for becoming.

Around the Molt Court, where abandoned exoskeletons lie like papers and my film begins to feed on what can be returned to chemistry.

I would be there not as a citizen with a shell, but as the civic condition that allows the citizen to settle.

And if someone pressed further and demanded an animal anyway, I would let them say I am the smallest thing grazing me. They could call me the isopod on the algal thread. The amphipod hopping through a field of barnacle plates. The crab hiding in a borrowed tube. The fish waiting under a shell roof with too-large eyes. The larva touching raw stone and changing. They would not be entirely wrong, because all those beings begin by asking what I ask with my whole surface: *Can this be lived upon?*

But I would remain the glow on the rock, the mat at the edge, the first colonists without names, the living film that makes the unfinished room seem finished enough to enter.

I would not settle early. I would not take a final shell.

I would be the beginning that continues.
