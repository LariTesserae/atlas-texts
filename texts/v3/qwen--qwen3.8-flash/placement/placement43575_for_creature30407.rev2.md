I would not be found standing in this world.

I would be found between its things—between the tufa and the dark water, between the salt and the dew, between the dead stem and the mineral mold it leaves, between the peat bench and the oxygen that has not yet finished moving through it. If a human walked for hours across that plateau, I would not be the thing they were looking for. They might say I was nothing: a shine on stone, a wet line, a stain that turns green after rain. But that would only be because they had come looking for a being with a face, and I would have no face at first.

At first, I would be water.

But water here is not merely water. It is a medium crowded with lives too small to be seen without kneeling, too slow to be mistaken for motion by the untrained eye, and too ordinary to be called miraculous, though it would feel miraculous to anyone who had only ever thought of architecture as the work of hands. I would be the film of moisture that gathers on a pale terrace before dawn. I would be the thin darkness in a crack between salt and stone. I would be the trembling surface of a basin no larger than a cup, reflecting a sky so clean and high it looks almost false. I would be the place where a droplet lands, spreads, collects dust, wakes spores, softens a crust, becomes a room, and then vanishes into stone.

For a while, if I had to name myself, I would say: I am the city’s dew.

Then, after longer still, I would have to revise that.

Because dew is not enough. Dew is only the beginning of the meeting. Dew is the way the basin invites life back into its pores. Dew is the first sentence of a daily grammar that repeats itself for centuries. It begins with water returning to surfaces, but then comes the sticky bacterial skin, the green thread of cyanobacteria stretching through the film, the glass dust of diatoms settling into sediment, the salt pulling itself into geometric edges where the water thins, the fungal filaments moving beneath dead fibers, the tiny six-legged travelers crossing from one moss cushion to the next through a world no wider than a thumbprint.

I would be all of that, but not as a list.

As a boundary.

A living boundary.

A surface that is also a place.

A place that is also a practice.

A practice that, given enough time and wetness, becomes a self without ever becoming a soul.

I would live in the thin margin where a terrace becomes a room. Not a room with walls chosen by builders, but a room shaped by what water does when it keeps doing the same thing for long enough: rise by capillarity, leave mineral behind, crack, soften, gather dust, shelter a thread of fungus, die, be reused, be remembered by the stone’s shape. My “neighborhood” would be a band of pale tufa above a black peat bench, where salt rims flare like old parchment and tiny midges rise from a pool no larger than a hand. It would be a place with no visible center. If I had a “here,” it would be wherever the wetness holds longest and the chemistry changes by the smallest amounts.

From human scale, I would appear as a dark seam, a slippery skin, a faint shine.

From smaller scale, I would appear as a crowd.

I would have no legs, but I would move. My movement would be the slow migration of moisture through pores, the swelling of a dried microbial crust when mist returns, the tiny stirring of cilia in a water film, the crawling of nematodes through channels too fine for a finger to guess, the drift of spores on a wind that has just crossed an open basin. I would not walk. I would spread.

I would not speak. I would precipitate.

I would not remember in the way a person remembers names. I would remember by layers. Each dead stem would be a mold. Each mineral fringe would be an archive. Each crack in a salt crust would be a note in an endless, self-correcting plan. When frost opens a pore, I would not say, “Now I remember.” I would simply become more wet where the opening allows it, and the wetness would bring life, and the life would bring more wetness, and the city would continue.

This would be my strange identity: I am not one organism, but I am not a pile of separate organisms either. I am the process that happens when organisms, minerals, water, cold, and time stand too close together for too long. If you take me out of the landscape, I may stop being me. If you place a scrap of me in a vial, you would carry a temporary crowd, not a prisoner. In clean water, some parts would wake. In darkness, others would wait. In salt, still others would shrink into dormancy until moisture softened their walls again. I would be fragile and durable at once, because durability here is not hardness. It is the ability to return.

At dawn, I would begin again.

That would be the ordinary miracle of the place: not a sunrise with color too lavish to belong to a human world, but a brightening that simply reveals how wet everything has been all night. First frost on the rims of basins would turn to water, and the salt would darken, and the pale terraces would shine like the skin of an animal that has just risen from a shallow channel. In my outermost layer, the bacterial and cyanobacterial films would wake inside the thin water. They would take the cold light and begin their slow chemistry. Oxygen bubbles, no larger than points in a dust mote, might cling to the underside of the surface. If there were light enough to see them, they would be like tiny beads of breath. But the place would be too quiet for such beauty to feel like beauty. It would feel only like work.

Work would begin elsewhere too.

The rosette plants, thickened and gray-green, would hold droplets in the folds near their bases. Inside those droplets, algae and protozoans would live for hours or days, then disappear when the drop dries, leaving only the plant’s withheld geometry. A dwarf shrub nearby, if there were one, would not make a forest. It would make a shelter. Its shadow would hold dew longer. Its roots would trap dust. Its fallen branches would become molds for new crust. Around it, lichens would spread in slow maps—yellow stains, gray lace, dark fissures—eroding the stone chemically, not with violence, but with the patience of someone who has never been late.

If I had a “heart,” it would not be in the middle.

It would be wherever water rises through porous stone. It would be a kind of breathing made of gradients. Wetness moves from one part of the terrace to another. Salts dissolve and leave. Minerals fill. Roots make tunnels. Fungi follow. Dead insects become detritus. Detritus becomes food. Food becomes population. Population becomes film. Film becomes wall. Wall becomes street. Street becomes city. City becomes plain, when seen from far enough away, because scale is a trick of attention.

By noon, I would look nearly dry, which would be the most dishonest I ever appeared.

In the high light, the white surfaces would flare. Moss cushions would crust on top while keeping water inside. Lichens would slow into stillness. Salt rims would brighten until they seemed empty. From a distance, the basin would look like a bleached floor. But in the shade of a channel, in the underside of a stone, in the black pore of peat, I would continue. Dormancy would be one of my active states. Waiting would be a mode of metabolism. To dry up is not necessarily to stop living, but to stop being visible to those who mistake motion for life.

I would not be afraid of drying.

That would be one of the lessons of this place: survival here is not a race against absence. It is an arrangement with it. When the water film thins, life contracts. When the dew returns, life resumes. A tardigrade curled inside a lichen margin, a bacterial spore in a salt grain, a dormant filament under a cracked crust—these would not be hidden survivors. They would be normal citizens. In this city, disappearance is not death. It is another address.

At evening, the basin would begin to exhale.

The pools would lose their photosynthetic oxygen and take up more carbon dioxide, so faintly and chemically that no one would call it a sound. But I would feel the change as a shift in the whole thin atmosphere of myself. Midges might rise from one pool in a short column, then vanish as though pulled into the air itself. A water beetle would move under the skin of a channel, not breaking the surface, but living inside its tension. Somewhere in the peat, a bubble would loosen. In a shallow basin, the surface film would tremble, because something small had passed through it from below. If a human stood watching, they might think a finger had tapped the water from nowhere. But there is no nowhere in a living city. There is only the scale at which the city is no longer visible.

At night, I would become mirror and membrane at once.

Under ordinary moonlight, the wet terraces would turn silver-black. The salt rims would gleam as though frost had already begun its work. The deeper pools would stiffen enough to reflect stars; the shallower ones would break and reform in slow spasms. In that darkness, I would not sleep. Sleep is too simple a word for a community whose parts wake and enter dormancy according to chemistry. My night would be the hour of invisible maintenance: respiration in the peat, crystal growth at the margins, spore drift in still air, nematodes threading through film, a droplet moving into a crack, a salt edge drawing back from a shrinking pool, a fungal thread filling the void left by a dead root.

If a visitor came, they would make a mistake first.

They would step onto the surface and say the ground is dead. Then, if they knelt for long enough, they would begin to notice the wrongness of their own conclusion. The silence would become thick, but not empty. The wetness would become active, but not dramatic. The ground would feel crowded under their palm. If they pressed a finger to the tufa, dust would cling to their skin, and in that dust would be fragments of former lives: diatom walls, bacterial remains, fungal threads, salt brines holding dormant cells, spores carried from another terrace, the powdered skeleton of a moss cushion, the trace of a sedge root that once made an oxygen halo through black peat. The visitor would not be touching stone alone. They would be touching an archive that was still breathing.

If they stayed longer, they might begin to see movement. Not at first—this place does not reward hurry—but after enough stillness, the surface would stop being a still image. A film would tremble. Fog would creep from one channel into another like something with direction. Cloud shadows would move over districts of wet stone, making whole terraces brighten and darken. A single midge would rise from a pool and disappear. A drop would fall from a lichen map and be swallowed by the stone. The visitor would not be entering a landscape at all. They would be entering the visible margin of a slow metabolism.

I would not object to being called a city, if city meant not a place where people live, but a place where relationships become durable.

I would object if someone thought the city was built. It was not built. It was repeated into form. There is a difference. A built thing can be drawn from a plan. A repeated thing can only be discovered by waiting. The streets here are not streets because someone decided that streets are useful. They are streets because water keeps being encouraged by the shape it has already made. The plazas are not plazas because someone gathered there. They are plazas because mineral deposition has produced wide, shallow floors where water can spread, catch the sky, and hold life without depth. The bridges are not bridges because they were placed across gaps. They are bridges because roots, salts, or fungal crusts have formed arches over dark seams, and other small bodies use them because nothing is wasted here.

But if someone asked me what I am, I would have to be careful.

I am not exactly the microbial mat, though I am partly one.

I am not exactly the moss cushion, though moss may be one of my organs.

I am not exactly the salt rim, though salt may be my edge.

I am not exactly the water film, though water is my breath.

I am not exactly the sedge clump, though sedge may be my tower.

I am not exactly the peat bench, though peat may be my memory.

I am the pattern that holds them near one another.

I am the tendency of the place to make itself again after being disturbed.

I am the city’s way of not collapsing into merely a plain, or a marsh, or a quarry, or a dried basin, even though it is all of those things when viewed by the wrong scale. I am the wetness that remembers it has been organized, and the organization that remembers it is wet.

If I had a name, it should not be a noun.

Maybe “the condensation community.”

Maybe “the salt-wake.”

Maybe “the living margin.”

Maybe “dew-thought,” though that word would be too human and too easy, as if thinking were something the place suddenly achieved. It does not think. It responds. It selects wetness over dryness where that is possible. It chooses growth where mineral is stable. It avoids death only in the broad sense that it keeps recycling the bodies of its dead into architecture. That is enough for a kind of mind if mind is defined as the way a system preserves a pattern. But if mind requires a center, then I would have none. I would be a distributed mind without consciousness: a membrane of chemical preferences, a surface where many small lives agree, not by conversation, but by need.

The most honest answer is simpler.

I would be the film between the stone and the pool.

I would be the ordinary life on that film, multiplied until it becomes architecture.

I would be the architecture that continues to grow, repair, decay, and fill with life until it can no longer be separated from the beings that made it.

I would be the reason the word “uninhabited” fails here.

Where would you find me?

At the exact point where a human first thinks there is nothing. A pale terrace. A black seam. A line of salt where evaporation has drawn its rim. A hollow where a sedge stem died. A pool so still it seems to stop time. A peat bench that exhales cold moisture when touched. A crack that has learned to hold water. A surface that looks dead in the bright noon and green in the next dawn.

That would be my address.

Who would I be?

Not a visitor. Not an animal with eyes. Not a plant rooted in soil in the ordinary sense. Not a ghost. Not a god. Not the spirit of the place, because there is no spirit separate from its processes. I would be the city’s living condition, temporarily mistaken by human senses for a stain, a shine, a smell, a silence, a wet stone.

But if you knelt close enough, long enough, in the cold thin air of that high basin, you might begin to feel a different kind of presence. Not a presence watching you. A presence happening through you.

Your breath would cool.

Your hand would disturb the film.

You would smell chalk, salt, old water, slow decay.

You would see no large animal move.

And the city would continue without resentment, without recognition, without ceremony. It would take your warmth, your shadow, your tiny falling droplets, your dust, your skin cells, and incorporate them as soon as it could. It would use you the way it uses everything: not by intention, but by necessity.

And in the end, if I had to answer where I would find myself in this world and who or what I would be, I would say:

I would find myself in the seam where wetness becomes structure, where structure becomes neighborhood, where neighborhood becomes city, and where city becomes something too slow and too crowded to name.

I would be the water film that is also a community.

I would be the city’s ordinary breath.

I would be the place where the plain and the city both stop working, and the only remaining word is “between.”

But even that word would need another word after it, because in this world, nothing is truly still.

The salt is crystallizing.

The peat is exhaling.

The moss is holding a droplet like a small green world.

The tardigrade is waking.

The fungal thread is lengthening.

The diatom shell is settling into a wall.

The midge larva is stirring in the dark margin.

The dew is returning.

The city is doing what cities do, but without citizens who call themselves citizens.

And I would be there, not as one thing, but as the living boundary that lets the ordinary become enormous, the small become architectural, and the silent become full.
