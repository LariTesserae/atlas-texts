If I tried to enter as a person, I would fail in the way a hand fails when pressed too hard into wet chalk.

I would bring weight. I would bring attention. I would bring the habit of naming: *pool*, *stone*, *moss*, *throat*, *wader*, *call*. Naming would be a kind of building. It would lay a line across the basin’s surface and say, *here is a thing, here is another thing, here is the space between them.* But the space between them is not empty there. It is warm. It is full of drifting filaments, suspended droplets, wing-beats, spores, breath, dissolved mineral. To name it would be to make a boundary, and the Basin That Does Not Drain has no room for boundaries that remain.

So if I am there, I cannot begin as the one who arrives.

I would begin, if beginning is the wrong word, by being low. Low in the way of damp ground. Low in the way of a leaf’s cupped underside. Low in the way of a pool’s first edge, where the water thinks about holding its curve and then, for a long time, does not.

The first shape my mind offers me is a throat-animal.

That is easy to choose because the throat-animals are loud, obvious, nearly human in their posture: sitting, pulsing, calling. I imagine myself pale and broad-mouthed on a warm stone, throat filling with the basin’s humid air, pushing sound out through soft wet chambers. I imagine my call locking into the calls beside me. I imagine the stone warming me from below and the moss taking my weight without remembering that I was there.

But a throat-animal is still too much of a self. It has a body, yes, but the body is already almost an event in the basin’s atmosphere. It does not own its call. It joins a chord. If it stops, the chord continues because the conditions require it to continue. That is beautiful, but too impersonal. The question “what would you be?” still expects something slightly more like a person, and the basin offers only the ghost of personhood: a position in the wetness, a pulse in the chorus, a temporary arrangement of thirst and heat.

So I try another shape.

A moss-ridge wader.

There is more generosity in it. It is large enough to carry gardens on its back. Moss grows along its shoulders. Algae paints its skin with faint green. When it stands, the water at its feet becomes a lens. It is a moving island, but not an island that separates itself from the rest. Its body hosts small ecosystems. Snails travel along its ridge. Insects settle between its spines. The pool receives its shadow and then forgets the shadow. It does not trample. It does not clear. It does not improve. It simply carries the basin around inside its own dampness.

I like that image. To be a creature that is also a place, a place that is also a community, a community that has no center. But again, there is something too stable about a wader. Too much like an individual. Too much like a body that could return to the same pool and be recognized. The basin would allow that only if it meant nothing. The body would stand, and then the body would move, and then the body would be gone, and then the place would be gone, and then the whole would be the same.

No. If I stay with the wader, I become too much of a landmark, and this landscape does not permit landmarks.

I try to become something smaller.

A thread-walker.

It hangs beneath a curled leaf. Its legs are fine and wet. It has no urgency. It collects the water that gathers on the underside of the leaf, lets it swell along its body, and then returns it to the pool below. It is not beautiful in the way of wings or song. It is more like a tiny plumbing system made of flesh and fiber, a soft node in the basin’s breathing.

There is something honest in that. A thread-walker does not declare itself. It is a part of the water’s slow return. If I were that, I would be the place where moisture stops being air for a moment and becomes droplet, then stops being droplet and becomes surface again. I would be a small wet relay in a vast humid circuit.

But still the thread-walker is too separate. Too much a body with legs. It suggests something that could climb, hide, choose, rest. The basin has rest, yes, but not as private pause. It has resting places, but not homes. If a thread-walker moves, the leaf does not feel the lack. If another replaces it, the leaf does not know whether it has known another. That is perfect, but it is not enough for the question. The question wants the shape that answers it.

I try a glosswing.

This is tempting. Glosswings live in suspension. They are dust that becomes sound. They rest with wings slick and folded, then rise as a cloud that does not scatter because it was never truly separate. Their existence is almost pure medium: air given weight, light given tiny beating edges. To be a glosswing would be to be a breath of the basin made briefly visible.

But a glosswing is too much like a word spoken in a room full of wind. It rises and falls too readily. It makes me think of flight, of leaving, of escaping the dampness. The basin does not allow escape, because nothing ever goes missing. The clouds remain exactly as many as they should be, not because of survival rates, but because the basin holds the number like a chord holds its tension. That feels closer, but still too airborne. I would rather be lower, more touched by stone and water.

I try a veil-snail.

Its shell is thin mineral glass. Its body is dark thread. It crosses the moss without leaving a slime trail. It drinks from living films on stone. Its hunger is osmotic, quiet, almost tender. It is slow enough to seem grown from the wetness itself. To be a veil-snail would be to move through a world that accepts your passage without remembering it.

That is very near the answer. The veil-snail does not mark the moss. It does not scrape away the film it feeds on. It exists as a slow line of moisture with a shell. It is almost entirely transparent. It is almost entirely forgotten while still present.

But even the veil-snail has a shell, and a shell is a little too much like a self. It is a boundary made by an organism, a home the organism can withdraw into. The basin has boundaries, yes: pools, rims, leaves, skins, membranes. But a shell feels too permanent. Too much like the architecture of an individual. The basin is full of architecture that is not architecture: stone rims delicate as frozen spit, moss cushions like wet hills, leaf-bowls like cups, backs of waders like ridges. These forms support, host, tremble, breathe, but they do not house themselves. A veil-snail almost achieves that, but not quite. It has a little too much of the idea of shelter.

I try to be the pool-skin.

Now the image begins to change.

The pool-skin is not an animal. It is not a plant alone. It is a living membrane: algae, protists, tiny swimmers, mineral films, warmth, pressure, sound. It holds the surface together without being a solid object. It trembles when the throat-animals call, not because the sound is loud, but because the whole wet atmosphere is made of pressure and response. The skin of the pool gathers spores, catches wing-hairs, drinks light, hosts larvae, reflects dim color, and stills itself again without having been disturbed.

If I were the pool-skin, I would not stand at the edge of the water. I would be the edge’s thinking, the water’s hesitation, the place where surface tension becomes a community. I would be a living line where the basin tests itself. Not a thing that observes the trembling, but the trembling itself, briefly organized by countless tiny lives.

That feels better, but it is still too broad. The pool-skin could be any pool in any basin. The question wants where I find myself. I need a more specific intimacy, a closer point of contact.

I try to be the water held in a leaf-bowl.

A leaf curled into a cup. Heat rising. A pool no deeper than a finger. Inside it, translucent eggs, slow swimming bodies, tiny films, a suspended world. The leaf is not separate from the animal life it holds. The animals are not separate from the water. The water is not separate from the light. The whole thing is a basin within the basin, a wet eye open in the green thickness.

If I were that water, I would be small enough to feel every movement of the leaf. I would be large enough to host a civilization too tiny to name. I would be held by plant, warmed by stone, cooled by air, stirred by the falling of a drop from above. My surface would take the dim light and return it broken into many soft colors. My inside would hold life without owning it. If a larva disappeared, I would not mourn it. If another appeared, I would not wonder how it arrived. I would simply be the wet condition in which disappearance and appearance are the same gesture.

This is nearly the place I would be.

But I keep circling toward something even less fixed.

A pool’s edge. The thin curve where water meets tufa. The place where moisture decides, without deciding, how far to climb the stone. The place where sound stops being pure atmosphere and becomes a shiver in the skin of the pool. The place where heat from below makes tiny bubbles gather along the mineral rim, as though the basin is softly breathing through its surfaces.

If I am there, I would be not the stone and not the water, but the relationship between them. A meniscus. A living curve. A trembling surface with memory removed. A place of contact so constant that it becomes a being.

Now the answer begins to emerge: not as a creature I would choose, but as a state the basin would allow.

I would not be a person with footprints. I would not be a visitor taking notes. I would not be an animal with a life-story. I would not be a plant rooted in identity. I would not even be a pool, because a pool still has boundaries, and boundaries can seem like claims. I would be something lower, wetter, more continuous: the surface that takes pressure and returns it. The thin living film where sound becomes visible. The warm tension of water holding itself together long enough to be seen, then forgetting that it was seen.

I would be the place on a pool where the nearest throat-animal’s call becomes a shimmer.

That is the shape I would find myself in.

Not a throat-animal itself, though I would borrow its pulse. Not a glosswing above the pool, though I would taste its wings as tiny dimples. Not a moss cushion beside the water, though I would feel its breath through the stone. Not a wader standing nearby, though I would wear the reflection of its green ridge across my surface. I would be the response. The basin’s way of listening without ears.

If you ask where I would be, I would say: at the smallest edge. The edge of a leaf’s cupped pool. The edge of a shallow black-water hollow. The edge of a warm mineral rim where the water climbs a hair’s width and does not go farther. The edge of sound where vibration becomes trembling. The edge of life where many tiny organisms are not yet a community, already are.

If you ask who I would be, the question has to soften. No one. That is too final. Not no one either, because the shimmer is real. The who is a wet local condition. The who is the basin’s attention turned toward its own surface for an instant that has no duration. The who is the pool-skin tightening where the call arrives. The who is the trembling that does not become history.

If you ask what I would be, the answer is more stable, though not stable like stone. Stable like equilibrium. I would be a living membrane. A film of algae and mineral warmth and suspended creatures, arranged so finely that it behaves like a single skin. I would be the curve of water against stone. The place where heat from below makes bubbles rise and pop without changing the pool. I would be the surface tension of the chorus. Not the song, not the singer, but the medium through which the song proves it is not silence.

I would be very small and also everywhere.

There would be no “me” looking across the basin. There would only be basin looking at itself through my wetness. The moss would not call me to shelter, because I am not dry enough to need shelter. The throat-animals would not answer me, because I have no voice except the one they give me when their sound presses down on my surface. The waders would not notice me, because the water at their legs is only another membrane like the one I am. The veil-snails would cross me, or near me, or through the stone film next to me, and the crossing would be complete without residue.

This is why I cannot be a human there. A human would want to carry the image out. To carry an image out would create history. To create history would break the balance. The basin would tolerate the weight for a moment, because it tolerates waders and throat-animals and insects and heat without breaking, but the moment the footprints became memory, the moment the eye began to remember rather than remain, I would become the only thing wrong in the basin. And the basin does not know wrongness. It only knows pressure, water, green density, breath, sound, return.

So I would have to surrender the part of me that wants to keep the place.

That surrender would not be painful. Pain is a kind of memory. The basin has sensation, yes: heat rising through feet, dampness entering lungs, light bending through films, calls vibrating in wet bone and mineral rims. But it has no pain as accumulation. If a drop falls into a pool, the pool accepts it. If a foot presses moss, the moss accepts it. If a creature disappears, the chorus accepts it. Nothing becomes a wound. Nothing becomes a relic. Nothing becomes a ruin.

What I would be would therefore have no relic either.

No fossil of my passage. No note in my handwriting. No story I could return to later and say, “This is where I was.” If I were asked, after leaving, “What did you see?” I would find no image preserved in me. I would have only the wet feeling of having been near a living boundary, and even that would evaporate.

Maybe that is the truest answer: I would be evaporation.

Not the visible part, not only mist, but the whole wet turning of water from surface to air and air back to surface. The basin has evaporation that never fully separates, because everything returns. So to be evaporation would be to be a motion without escape. A leaving that is already a staying. A rise that is already a fall. A breath taken from the pool and then given back to the leaf. That is very close to the kind of being the basin permits.

But evaporation still suggests movement away from the pool. I want something more intimate: a thing that does not leave even when it changes.

So: I would be the meniscus.

The curve of the water. The tension at the rim. The living film that holds the pool together while countless organisms pass through it. The surface that trembles with the throat-animals’ calls and stills without memory. The place where the stone and water negotiate their edge in a pale, wet, breathing line.

A meniscus has no center. A meniscus is not an object standing in the world; it is the way the world touches itself at a boundary. And in the Basin That Does Not Drain, boundaries are not walls. They are skins. They are films. They are membranes made by living pressure. They hold eggs and larvae. They catch light. They accept footfalls. They tremble. They return.

I would be one of those returns.

If I had to be more animal, I would be a throat-animal, but not one with a life. Only one with a pulse. My body would be the wet hollow where air becomes sound. My throat would be a small warm drum of humidity. I would not call because I wanted something. I would call because the basin needed the pressure of my call to make the pool-skin tremble. The pool-skin tremble would stir the glosswings below me. The glosswings would hover, carrying moisture from leaf to stone. The lichen-backed waiter, nearby, would shift its chalk-green body between two rims and take the throat-animal with slow, unviolent swallowing. Then another throat would begin the same note from the same conditions. The chord would not miss me. It never misses anything, because the basin is not made of things that miss one another. It is made of things that require one another.

If I had to be more landscape, I would be moss.

Not moss as a mass, but moss as a breathing sponge: a cushion of tiny saturated leaves and internal warm air pockets. A thread-walker would pass through me without my knowing it had gone. A veil-snail would cross my upper surface without a groove forming. A throat-animal would settle into my dampness as though sitting into a bowl of water made green. My weight would support it without bending into memory. If it rose and left, I would return to my shape exactly, without longing, without residue, without becoming “the moss that once held a body.”

If I had to be more plant than either, I would be a leaf-bowl.

A wide low leaf curled into a cup, holding a warm pool. The pool holding larvae and eggs and films and light. The light bending through the film. The film supporting the larvae. The larvae becoming small swimming threads that are neither animal nor plant, only living water with the habit of movement. The leaf would hold me without knowing me. I would be the held water’s surface, trembling when a glosswing touched down, brightening when the heat from the stone lifted into the air, returning to smoothness before the smoothness could become stillness.

If I had to be more animal than plant, more sound than stone, more breath than body, I would be the shimmer in the pool-skin when the nearest throat-animal breathes.

That is the place I would find myself in.

Not as a being looking at the basin.

As the basin looking at itself through wet tension.

So my answer, finally, is not a creature’s name.

I would be a local tightening of the living surface. The place where a pool’s skin accepts a sound and trembles. The curve at the edge of shallow water where warm stone and warm air and warm life meet in a thin breathing line. The meniscus of the chorus. The surface that holds enough to show the world and forgets enough to keep the world from changing.

And if I needed one more name, one more shape, I would take the name the basin gives to everything that moves without consequence:

I would be a pulse in the wetness.

A pulse with no owner.

A pulse with no after.
