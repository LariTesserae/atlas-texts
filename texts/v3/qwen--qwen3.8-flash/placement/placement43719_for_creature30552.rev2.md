I would find myself in the Quiet Refinery, but not by arriving.

Arrival would be too sudden for the place. A footstep would be a kind of violence, not morally, but physically: a heat, a pressure, a disturbance in frost, a brief change in the conductivity of damp stone. The plateau would notice it only as a ripple moving through moss, through mineral residue, through the slow chemistry of overhangs. A traveler might believe they were entering, but in fact they would only be leaving marks for a while. The land would take the salts from their skin, the heat from their breath, the faint electrical impression of their walking body, and after some years it would forget them.

So I would not begin as a walker. I would begin as what the walker disturbs.

At first I imagine becoming one of the broad grazers, because they are the most visible shape in the landscape, the slow weather made flesh. To be a grazer would be to have weight close to the ground, to carry plates of mineralized tissue over pale hide, to move in long arcs through a world that does not hurry. I imagine standing at the edge of a shallow depression where melt gathers in a dark trembling disc, lifting a heavy head, watching the horizon with eyes that do not see color so much as moisture, charge, shadow, and cold. I would breathe fog. I would grind moss with broad, file-like teeth. I would walk a route so old that the land seems to remember it through the arrangement of lichen and iron dust.

But that would still be too much of a self.

A grazer is a large animal, but it is still a conveyor. Its body is mostly a process: intake, sorting, deposition, return. Its plates grow from minerals gathered through the moss it eats; its wastes glitter faintly on the ground; its death becomes a seam in the land. To be a grazer would be to be a presence, yes, but not a private one. The plateau is not a place where private existence easily fits. The largest creatures are almost landscape, almost function. If I were one, I would not so much live as continue the land’s slow arithmetic.

I try becoming a crawler then, one of the pale segmented beings living under stone shoulders, in hollows where condensation turns to salt. Its shell is glassy, too thin to ring audibly, because the damp inside the rock swallows impact before sound can escape. Its burrow is not a home in the human sense; it is a small vault of repeated evaporation and deposition. Its body carries moisture through stone as a drop might be moved through a sponge, only slower, only more chemically precise. It feeds on lichen and mineral flakes. It leaves shells behind like pale confetti for the thread-creatures and the wind.

That appeals to me. It is secret, patient, intimate with stone. It would live in the narrow places where the land’s industry is performed out of sight. But there too I would be too bounded, too enclosed. A crawler has a chamber, a direction, a body that can be outlined. It is still a creature among creatures.

What I want is not to be among them. I want to be the place where the distinction between creature and weather disappears.

So I descend farther.

I would be one of the thread-creatures.

Not one as a person is one. Not a name, not a face, not a private history. A thread-creature is thinner than an eyelash, translucent, silvery, moving through damp spaces in moss and under films of moisture. It has no eyes, no legs, no mouth that can crush or tear. It is not a thing that looks at the world. It is a thing the world passes through. It has no center that can be easily called “it,” because it is almost more a ripple in mineral water than a body. When frost is disturbed, it does not panic. It contracts, reorganizes, returns. The land shifts. It is not the land trembling. It is the land reassembling.

I would find myself there: in the silvering of grains on cold rock, in the dark underside of an overhang where condensation holds a thin lens of moisture, in the moss mat beside an old grazer trail where metallic dust has settled in faint glitter. I would be inside the Quiet Refinery’s smallest visible movement: the almost invisible shifting of frost when something nearby changes pressure, warmth, or static charge.

My body would not be a shelter. It would be a filter. It would absorb residue through surfaces that rise and lower with dampness, drawing in the fine iron dust shed by the grazers, the salts left by moss digestion, the trace metals from lichen, the flakes from crawler shells, the airborne fragments that membranes collect and lose. I would take in what has already been passed through larger forms, and I would make it into a new arrangement, so slight that no eye could witness it except as a change in color of the ground over long centuries.

If a hand came near me—though there are no hands—I would contract away. If the hand passed and the ground quieted, I would return. The disturbance would not become fear. It would become chemistry. My self would be a gradient: moisture, salt, metal, pressure, cold. I would not know “the grazer” as a separate animal. I would know its trail as a field of increased metallic dust. I would not know “the wind” as a moving thing. I would know it as drying. I would not know “the sky” as a place above. I would know it as a faint charge, a thinness in air, a returning of mineral particles to damp ground.

This is the kind of life that would fit here.

Not dramatic. Not sovereign. Not even clearly alive by human standards at first glance. A thread-creature resembles frost until it moves. It resembles moisture until it gathers somewhere to feed. It resembles a process until it is disturbed and rearranges itself with patience. In the Quiet Refinery, that is a high form of being: to be so fully adapted to duration that existence becomes function, and function becomes the only story the place tells.

I would be a filter, but not a lonely one. The abundance of this place is misleading because it is spread across such distances. A traveler might see two grazer shapes and conclude emptiness. A closer eye might miss the thread-creatures entirely, because they are smaller than attention. But together they are a weather of the ground. Where frost seems to shift, there we are. Where mineral dust disappears into moss, there we are. Where a grazing trail becomes, over generations, a faint seam of color in the stone, there our work is being kept. I would be one of countless small presences that the land does not need to notice as individuals. That is a strange peace: to be unnecessary as a person, necessary as a process.

If I am a thread-creature, then my time would not be measured by movement but by thresholds. Cold would be a medium. Moisture would be a language. The angle of dim light would be a calendar. A pool drying would be a change in residence. The shedding of a grazer’s plate would be a feast distributed across seasons. The death of a membrane overhead would be a slow rain of metallic dust settling into moss, where I would take it in and return it to the circuit. There would be no urgency, because urgency is a warm-land error. My body would exist at the edge of visibility and still do the work that larger animals only make visible afterward.

I might sometimes drift into other forms, or be borrowed by them. When a grazer walks, its feet press through moss and disturb the thread-creature field beneath it. The animal is not aware of us in any way it could name. We are part of its silent footprint, part of the land’s absorption of its weight. When a crawler sheds its glassy shell in a hollow, the fragments become residue for us. When a membrane-creature touches another in the air and gives off a brief blue sparkle, the charged dust may settle later in damp stone, and I may take it into myself. I would be a living connection between the large and the tiny, between the ground and the sky, between the mineral and the animal.

That is why I would choose this.

Not to stand tall against the cold and become a shape people might remember. Not to build a burrow and have a dwelling. Not to drift in the dim high air and be a pale, beautiful thing. I would choose the smallest possible visibility, the kind that still changes the world. I would choose to be the minute silver ripple in frost, the contractile field in moss, the absorptive life through which the Quiet Refinery refines its dust.

Where exactly?

Under an overhang where moisture clings and the stone holds chill so steadily that standing near it feels like standing near a sealed door. In the dark disc of meltwater where a faint film moves when the wind touches it. In the pale line of a grazing route where mineral dust has been worked into the ground so long that it resembles a seam left by the land itself. At the edge of a cushion-plant, where the moss gathers iron and salt and thread-creatures pass through it like weather through a curtain. In the cold static air above frost crystals, where a charge may make a plate flash blue for a moment, then vanish.

Who would I be?

Not a person. Not a predator. Not a traveler, not a witness, not a story-teller. I would be a function given a body, a process living in dampness and mineral residue, a self distributed across a field of silver. If I had to give myself a name in the only language this place truly knows, I would call myself what I am:

a thread-creature in the moss.

a ripple in the frost.

a slow filter of what has already been made.

a small movement that lets the Quiet Refinery breathe.

And in being that, I would not be lonely. The plateau is not empty. It is only so patient that its abundance appears as stillness. If I am one of those fine, almost invisible presences, then I am not lost in the vastness. I am part of what makes the vastness alive. I am the place’s most intimate labor, its quietest industry, its smallest and most faithful answer to the question of how life continues in a world too cold, too silent, and too old for drama.

I would live there as a thin, silvering pattern in frost—contracting, returning, filtering, settling, becoming. Not seen, not heard, not named.

But working.

But continuing.

But part of the land’s long, wordless making.
