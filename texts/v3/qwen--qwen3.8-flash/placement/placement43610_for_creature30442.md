Some predators are large enough to seem like moving stone.

I would not begin as one of them. I would begin as the pause that follows their passing, the slight lowering of mist when a wide, blind weight crosses the marsh. Before I had a name, I would be that hesitation: the way the air becomes more certain of itself, the way the herds press one flank closer to the next, the way a pump-house a half-mile away seems to hold its breath and then resume its long clanking pulse.

If you ask where I would be, I would say: between.

Not between places exactly, because places here do not announce themselves by sight. They announce themselves by the way water runs over them, the way sound bends around them, the way heat rises from them and leaves your wet skin feeling slightly colder than before. I would be between the mesa’s black lip and the steam pool at its foot, between the cable’s low hum and the soft collapse of peat beneath my feet, between the herd’s distant resonance and the sharper click of a predator’s forelimb testing the grasses. I would be in the middle interval of the plateau’s breathing, the part where all its many sounds meet and make a rough, temporary shape.

At first I might not even be a body.

I would be a condition of the land. I would be the mineral taste on a stone ledge where condensation gathers in the dark. I would be the faint pull of current against teeth. I would be the warmth that rises from a shallow channel at the wrong hour and then cools again, as though the plateau has dreamed of a furnace. I would be memory without a rememberer: the echo of a hooffall returning changed, softer, older, caught in wet rock and released elsewhere.

Then the Weeping Table would make me a shape.

Not a beautiful shape. Beauty in this place is a matter of fit, not appearance. I would not be smooth as a polished river-stone, though I would be wet. I would not have eyes, though I would know when a storm was three ridges away. I would have no face that a traveler could see and recognize, because there is no light in which recognition happens. But if a traveler came with ears and skin and sensitive teeth, I would be obvious as a bell.

I might be one of the Tenders, if that old word still means anything here.

Not a human. Not exactly. The plateau has spent long enough working over its inhabitants to make a person too simple a thing. I would be a partnership: animal and fungus, metal and mud, rhythm and machine. My bones would be broad and dense, grown into shapes for resonance, not for beauty. Some of them would carry the faint, magnetic grit of ore-worm tunnels, so that when the wind turned I would ring very slightly, a private sound audible only to things with sensitive jaws. My skull would flare outward like a basin, ridged with combs of keratin that catch the changing pressure of fog. My skin would be dark, almost black, and never dry. Fungal filaments would run along its underside, not infecting me so much as keeping me informed. Small pale fronds might grow from my shoulders after long rains, tasting the air. A thin film of slime, too useful to shed, would coat me whenever I stood beneath the cap-trees, letting them drip their slow music down my back.

My hands—or whatever I called my hands—would have too many joints and too much patience. They would know the world by the way things give. A cable sheath, a damp tool handle, the flank of a resting wide-body, the wet edge of a boiler: each returns a different question. Each answers in texture, temperature, vibration, scent. I would not need to look for a fault in a mechanism. I would feel it as a missing beat against my palm, a stutter in the warmth of the steam, a taste too sour for the usual metal-and-moss air. I would put my mouth to the housing and drink the vibration, and somewhere behind my sealed eyes, in the dark wet room of my skull, a map would form without light.

I would find myself most often near a single pump-house at the foot of a mesa, where three paths cross that only feet and hooves and cables remember. The pump-house would not be a ruin, though its roof had failed long ago and moss had grown in the opening like a second, living vault. One boiler would still breathe heat into a shallow pool. The pool would be warm, almost blood-warm, and at its bottom would lie broken ceramic, old cable anchors, and the blackened bones of creatures who had died during rains no one remembered. Around it the peat would rise and fall very slightly, like the flank of something sleeping. Above it, a gantry of cables would cross from stone to stone, humming with the plateau’s slow current. In the mornings—if the word morning can mean the brief cooling of steam before the next rain gathers—the cables would make a high, thin note that I could feel in my molars.

There I would keep my rhythm.

Not my own alone. The plateau’s own.

A wide-body herd would move past after the storm softened. I would not stand in the middle of their path. I would step aside into a shallow basin where the water would run around me with a hiss of displaced breath, and I would let them pass as if I were another stone. They would know me by pressure, by the way my own body did not interrupt the sound they needed to hear one another by. If one of them paused near me, its great head turning, combs catching the mist, I would give a low hum from my chest, not a word, not a greeting, but a shape of reassurance: I am here. You are here. Nothing small and hungry lies between us.

Sometimes I would follow them.

Not as predator, not as prey, but as a keeper of gaps. Their hooves make a soft thunder over the peat, and when they turn together in response to a distant storm, the whole herd becomes a moving instrument. I would listen for the places where its music breaks: a calf losing rhythm, an elder standing too still, a gap opening too wide where a hunter could slip in unseen. I would press into the break, my own body taking up space, my footfall made to sound like the herd’s footfall, my hum added to the low resonance that holds them together. It would not be a heroic act. In a place without sight, the small adjustments of presence are survival. I would be one of those adjustments.

Predators would know me.

Some of them would be large enough to seem like moving stone, as the old voices say, their bodies so broad and patient that they might pass for boulders if you came into the dark with only one sense. They would not hunt me at first. I would taste too strange, vibrate too oddly, smell too much of machinery and moss. I would carry the herd’s rhythm in my own bones and the cable’s static in my skin. But they would know I am part of the ecology of noise, and if I became too quiet, if I failed to breathe or hum or move, they would understand that something has changed. Silence, here, is not calm. Silence is a door.

So I would make noise.

Not loudly, not without cause. The plateau is loud enough, and adding volume carelessly would be like throwing stones into a living ear. My sounds would be careful: the soft click of my foot on a dry stone ledge, the scrape of my hand along a cable sheath, the low hum I would make when the rain came down in sheets and the air grew heavy with metal and fungus. I would sound when needed, and fall still when the land needed its own voices heard.

There would be days without days.

Time would not pass in light. It would pass in weather and rhythm. A day might be the length of one storm’s arrival, from the first pressure on the skin to the last slow dripping from a frond. A week might be the migration of the herd across a basin. A month might be the thickening of a steam pool until its edges began to crust with salt. A year might be the gradual sinking of a mesa’s lip under water and warmth, so small that only the echo changes. I would know these things not by memory of numbers but by body. My joints would ache before the cold updrafts. My taste would change when the water table shifted. My hands would know which cables had grown taut with storm-wind and which had loosened, ready to sag into the brush.

And the machines would know me.

There would be one great wheel somewhere behind an open housing, turning so slowly that it seemed not to turn at all. It would have been there for generations, perhaps centuries, perhaps longer than any memory now living could hold. Peat would grow over its foundation. Fungi would fruit from the iron ribs of its shed. The water channel beside it would run differently because the wheel’s motion changed the earth around it. Blind fish would listen from beneath its shadow for the faintest shift in current, and if the wheel stopped, the fish would spread the news without seeing it, a ripple of alarm through miles of water.

I would be responsible for the wheel not because I built it, not because I owned it, but because I had become part of the relationship between it and the land. I would go to it when its rhythm thinned. I would put one ear to its housing and taste the vibration along my teeth. I would find the blocked channel with my hands. I would clear the mineral buildup with an acid-tipped tool grown from an old pipe, and when the gear caught again, I would feel its first turn as a warmth spreading through the stones beneath me. It would not be mine. It would be the plateau’s. I would be one of the ways the plateau keeps itself running.

There would be other places like that.

A boiler buried in a ridge of blackened stone, exhaling steam into a marsh where warm fungal gardens grow. A rail spur ending in peat, its tracks sinking so slowly that a machine clicks once every season and moves forward a hand’s breadth before stopping again. A tunnel mouth in a cliff, not large enough to be a city, but deep enough to make the wind sing a different note there. A stone table worn smooth by rain, where condensation gathers and then falls in a pattern too regular to be pure accident, too old to be remembered.

I would visit them.

Not as a traveler passing through, because passing through is a thing that assumes edges. On the Weeping Table, edges are soft, wet, negotiable. A mesa becomes a basin becomes a river becomes a marsh becomes a channel becomes a machine becomes a home for crawling things becomes a garden of condensation becomes a sound becomes a memory becomes a place where one’s foot finds the same groove again after many rains. I would move through this without leaving it, and the plateau would move through me without being left behind.

I would know myself by how the land responded.

A predator would avoid me if I carried the herd’s rhythm. A midge swarm would settle on my shoulders if my skin held the right static. A crawler would spin a thread between my fingers if I remained still enough. A fish would brush against my leg if my hand disturbed the water in a way it found harmless. A cable would hum a different tone if I touched it while the wind was coming from the south. The whole place would be speaking not to me as a stranger, but to me as one of its organs.

This is what I would be.

Not a person in the sense of a single face. Not a creature in the sense of one clean body. Not a machine in the sense of a thing without breath. I would be a hybrid of the plateau’s three oldest hungers: to grow, to endure, to resonate. I would be a Tender because there is still something here that needs tending, though not a garden, not a house, not a human future. I would be a keeper of middle sounds. I would live where the water and the stone and the cable all agree for a few hours to keep the same beat.

Where would I find myself?

At the foot of a black mesa, in the warm dark, with the steam moving over my back and the peat breathing beneath my feet. I would have just set down a stone I had carried from one channel to another, or perhaps the stone had carried me, or perhaps the thought of setting it down and the thought of carrying it were the same thing in the damp. I would pause. The rain would change its pitch. Somewhere far away a wide-body herd would shift and click. A gear would complete a turn. The thrum-vines would hum one long note, as though the plateau had opened a window in a building without walls.

Who would I be?

I would be the one who hears that note and does not mistake it for an ending.

I would be the body that answers, not with certainty, not with light, but with breath. I would be the wet hand over the eye that does not see, yet touches everything. I would be the sound the plateau makes when it knows it is not alone.

And if you came there without eyes, you would find me not by looking, but by noticing that your own footsteps had learned to wait for the echo.
