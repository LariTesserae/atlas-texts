I would begin by not being a creature.

I would begin as the hesitation between breath and fog, as the place where the air decides it has had enough cold and becomes water on a blade of grass. I would be a taste of iron, a faint ozone edge, the soft give of moss under something too tired to call its name. If I tried to locate myself too quickly, I would choose the wrong thing: a vole’s tunnel, a bird’s shadow, a bubble rising in a black pool. Those are only the places the land dreams in.

If I had to be somewhere, I would be in the seam.

Not on top of the plateau, and not beneath it. Not fully in water and not fully in fog. I would live where the two admit they have been pretending to be different. At dawn, I would be near the edge of a hummock, where the sedge thins and the moss slopes toward a pool so shallow it is mostly sky. A film of orange mineral would cover that water, and under it something would shift without announcing itself. A bubble would rise and pop. The fog would move through the tussocks like an animal trying not to wake the whole meadow.

For a while, I would try several shapes.

I could be the droplet trembling on a sedge tip, too heavy, too uncertain, waiting for gravity to make the decision.

I could be the static spark that jumps between wet root ends when a bird steps through the mat. A tiny sound, almost too small for the landscape to hear. A brief blue thought between two wet things.

I could be the bacterial film on the stone, working the mineral water into rust-colored lace, turning decay into structure without ever becoming visible.

I could be the breath of a grazer lifting from the firmer ridges, white and then gone, returning to the cold air that made it.

But the place would not let me remain only an element. It would begin to gather. It would add one wet surface to another, one slow rhythm to another, one small appetite to another, until I had a body that could survive by being low.

So I would become something wider than I thought I wanted to be.

I would be an animal, if “animal” does not sound too proud. I would be a small, low thing, not quite hare-sized and not quite frog-sized, the sort of creature that seems to have been made by compression, by damp, by patience. My body would sit close to the ground as if the plateau had once lifted me too high and then pressed me back down. My feet would be broad, fringed, half webbed, with pads so fine they could sense the difference between firm peat and hidden water a hand’s width beneath. I would be able to cross a floating mat without breaking it, because the surface of this world is too full for any creature to be heavy-handed.

My feet would be part of my eyes.

Through them I would read the hummocks: where the channel is narrowing, where the peat has softened, where a seep is coming up through basalt like a slow exhale. I would know the water table not by seeing it, but by the pressure my toes receive, by the faint cold that enters through the moss, by the mineral taste carried in the film across the ground. When rain came, I would feel it as a change in the electrical weight of the air, a softening, a loosening, a charge moving through roots. When fog rose, I would know it first as a wetness gathering in the bristles around my ears, then as a faint metallic taste on my tongue.

I would not have fur in the clean, bright sense.

My coat would be old before I was old. It would grow moss in the hollows of its hairs, not because I were slow, but because the world required surfaces to be claimed. Algae would live in my outer hairs. Tiny crustaceans would ride me from pool to pool. Spores would settle in my underfur like dust that had become useful. The more I moved through the bog, the more the bog would move into me. If I lay still too long, I would begin to look like a hummock with ears. If I stood in the mist at dawn, a bird might mistake me for a knot of roots and peck at the water beside me.

My skin would be damp even in dry air, because dryness would be a stranger here. It would have places so thin along the throat, the belly, and the inner limbs that they could exchange gases in water or wet fog. I would still have lungs—slow lungs, large and low in my chest, made for cold air that was already half water—but I would not be proud of them. Breathing would be something I did with the whole surface of myself.

My head would be wide and blunt, not because I lacked curiosity, but because sharp edges are too loud. My eyes would be set low in the fog-line, protected by translucent lids that always seemed to be holding dew. I would not see the world in clear outlines. I would see it as wetness and motion and temperature. The droplets on sedge blades would shine like a scattered alphabet. The shallow channels would be readable by how they bent the light. The floating mats of moss would reveal their hidden currents through the tiny tremors of their surfaces. I would not need to see far. Far would only be fog again.

My ears would be almost closed to air.

They would be more interested in ground.

I would listen with the whole skeleton of my skull, with the bones lying near the spongy floor, with the fine nerves in my feet. A vole passing through a tunnel beneath a hummock would reach me as a sequence of small disturbances: water shifting in a capillary, peat compressing, a root flexing, a grain of ochre sliding into a film. A bird stepping through dense sedge would come to me as a soft pulse of pressure, a whisper of broken surface tension, a faint static tremor from wet fibers separating.

I would not call out.

Calling out would be like throwing a stone across the plateau. Not because I were afraid, but because sound would return too many answers and too few warnings. Instead, I would speak by leaving things on the ground. A smear of iron-rich saliva on a stone. A chemical trace released from a gland near my hind foot. A deliberate compression of moss, making a dark pattern that another of my kind would read as: water rising here; safe path to the channel; grazer used this route at first light; something small was eaten, therefore something large may have been watching.

When I needed to say that I was present, I would let my tail discharge a quiet spark between two wet root ends.

That spark would not illuminate much. It would only be a blue thread, a breath of ozone, a brief interruption in the fog’s patience. It would say, in a language older than voice: I am here, I am not alone, I am wet, I am listening.

I would eat from the surface world and the hidden one.

In the mornings, before the sun had enough heat to lift the rime from the hummocks, I would lick water from grass tips, catching the dissolved minerals, the microbial film, the tiny drifting organisms that fog had concentrated on a blade. I would nibble the tender bases of sedges, the soft white zones where roots braid into peat. I would suck larvae from the mouths of beetle tubes. I would press my broad foot into a shallow pool, disturb the orange skin just enough, and take the trembling thing that came up—fish, frog tadpole, midge worm, a piece of dead leaf that smelled like future soil.

My digestion would not be elegant. It would be a small bog in itself.

My gut would house fungi and bacteria, not as guests, but as workers. They would break down fiber, release nutrients, precipitate minerals, turn the meal into something the peat could use. My droppings would be valuable enough that other creatures would compete over them: beetles, flies, mosses, tiny root tips. Even my waste would become a place. If I slept in a hollow, my warmth would encourage microbial mats on the surrounding fibers. If I died in one of the better-drained channels, my bones would gather iron and calcium, and my body would begin the long process of becoming hummock.

I would not mind that.

To be useful to decay is one way of remaining part of the world.

The landscape would not be kind to me in any personal sense. It would be too full for kindness. It would be indifferent in the way that a living machine is indifferent: no malice, no care, no hurry. If I stood too long on a floating mat, it would compress and let me down into the cold dark water below. If I crossed a ridge too boldly, a stoat or a silent raptor might mistake my low movement for the rhythm of prey. If I failed to smell the change in the air, fog might thicken and steal my bearings; not because the fog was cruel, but because everything here was too wet to remain visible for long.

Still, I would survive.

Not because I were strong. Not because I were fast. But because I would know how to make my body agree with the place.

At the edge of a channel, I would not drink by lowering my head into the water. I would place my mouth near a wet stone where the water had already been filtered through mineral layers and sedge roots. The liquid would taste cold, flat, metallic, and faintly sweet from the microscopic films. I would drink by capillary action, by licking a moss strand until it gave up its burden.

When the wind came, bending the sedge into pale waves, I would flatten myself and let my mossy coat fill with droplets. The wind would smell of high air, of cloud above the rim of the world, of iron and cold stone. I would move through the wet grass the way a slow thought moves through a quiet mind.

When a grazer passed, broad-hoofed and low-headed, I would not flee unless the grazer’s foot came too near my hummock. I would know it by the change in vibration, by the smell of damp wool and digested stems, by the way the fog would part before it and close behind it. It would be larger than me, but not more alive. It would simply be one of the plateau’s heavier organs, a piston that compressed peat, spread spores, and made footprints that became pools.

When a bird waded past, its toes spreading over the floating mats, I would watch it as much by how the water skin moved as by its shape. It would tilt its head, listening. I would know that it was listening too, but in a different sense: not to the surface, but to the underside of it. The bog had so many small voices that only silence could hear them.

When a salamander slid from beneath a shelf of moss into darker water, I would smell the change in the minerals. Its skin would leave a slick of scent on the channel edge, a message I could not ignore: oxygen thin, temperature low, shelter good. I would remember this for later, when my body needed to sink beneath the floating mats and breathe through my sides.

I would have no center of self.

Not in the human sense.

My memory would not be a bright story. It would be a chemistry. I would remember the year by what grew in my fur. I would remember hunger by the sour taste of low water. I would remember danger by the vibration that stayed in my bones after the soft impact of a raptor’s wings. I would remember a safe ridge by the way its moss gave just enough, then held. I would remember seasons not by dates, but by the slow rising and falling of the water lenses beneath the plateau, by the rhythm of steam vents hissing in the cold morning, by the day the fog stopped tasting metallic and began to taste like growth.

If there was one thing I could call myself, it would be a temporary agreement between moss and movement.

I would be the plain’s way of noticing its own damp.

Not a god of the basin. Not its guardian. Not its voice. Something smaller than that, and more true: a living seam, a mobile pore in the body of the bog. When I moved, water shifted. When I rested, fungi crept closer. When I ate, the channel became slightly cleaner or slightly murkier, depending on my size and my speed. When I died, the hummock gained height.

At dawn, I would find myself where the ridge narrows and the fog thins just enough for light to make the droplets bright. The air would be cold but not sharp; the cold here is too wet to cut cleanly. It gets into everything. It sits on the tongue. It lives in the roots. It makes the stone seem softer and the grass seem heavier.

I would be standing on one hummock, then another, then a mat that is neither fully water nor fully land. My feet would sink, hold, and release. A bubble would rise in the pool beside me and pop with a tick too small for the human ear, but not for mine. The sound would carry into my legs, into my ribs, into the strange electrical sense that runs along my flank. I would know something was moving beneath the skin of the water: a larva turning, a tiny fish tasting the surface, a frog embryo shifting inside its jelly, or only a pocket of gas escaping from the ancient carbon stored in peat.

I would not know which. It would not matter.

What matters here is that the land is full.

Every droplet is a place of arrival. Every channel is a road.
