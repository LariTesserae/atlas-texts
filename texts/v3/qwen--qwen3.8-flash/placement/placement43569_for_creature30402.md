The first thing I would have to abandon is the idea of arriving as a visitor.

To enter this place as a person would be to introduce the wrong substance: breath, moisture, expectation, memory, the soft animal habit of needing shelter. The pocket has no room for that kind of intruder unless the intruder first unlearns itself. So I would not come in as a body walking into a chamber. I would come in as the chamber’s ability to notice what it is becoming.

I would find myself in the seam.

Not at the opening, where the wind thins and the ridge exposes stone too young to be called old. Not at the bottom, where the heat is so intense it makes the rock feel less like matter than like refusal. Not on the ceiling, where darkness hangs unbroken. I would find myself at the working plane: a few millimeters above the ledges and crevices where dust, salt, cast skin, fungal thread, and mite-trail meet in a thin arithmetic of survival. That is where the pocket is most legible, if legible is the right word for a place that has no books, no faces, no roads. There the chamber is neither merely stone nor merely life. It is the relation between them.

At first, in my imagination, I would try to wear one of its bodies.

I would try to be a salt beetle flattened against a black shelf, its armored back catching the faint glow from a hot crack. I would choose it because its life is simple to read: heat tells it whether to wait or cross, chemistry tells it where the food is, antennae probe the air like tiny questions. A beetle would know where it begins and ends. It would know the edge of its own carapace, the dry tap of its legs, the boundary between the ledge and the crevice. It would have hunger, but its hunger would not be sentimental. It would be a hunger shaped by sulfur, mineral films, and the temperature of glassy basalt.

But a beetle is too bounded. Its self would stop at the waxed joints of its legs. It would know too much urgency and too little of the pressure that makes the urgency possible. It would mistake its own survival for the whole chamber’s meaning.

Then I would try to be a pale ground-dweller, waiting in a cool throat of rock with its long legs interrogating the stone. That would give me patience. That would teach me economy: no movement unless the rock changes pressure, no strike unless a vibration carries the shape of prey. The ground-dweller knows that moisture is not a river here but a secret kept inside sealed bodies. Its silence would seem like discipline. Its blindness would seem like purity.

Still, a predator is too close to narrative. A predator has a world of “what is coming” and “what can be taken.” This place is stranger than that. It is crowded not with stories, but with processes. Its bodies are not heroes of hunger; they are valves, conveyors, residues, and temporary knots of chemistry. To be the ground-dweller would be to remain a mouth. I would not want to be a mouth only.

Then I would try to be the microbial mat itself.

That would be more honest. Lower down, where the heat is lethal enough to forbid casual movement but not lethal enough to forbid life, the stone is stained with skins of being so thin they resemble discoloration. Archaeal films. Fungal hyphae. Salt-crusted bacterial lace. Pale rings of colony growth spreading around vents where gases breathe through the rock. These are the true first inhabitants. They do not look like life. They look like mineral having a dream.

If I became the mat, I would be patient in a way no animal is. I would not scurry. I would not strike. I would not need to know the room as a whole; I would only need to extend, crust, recolonize, tolerate. I would convert sulfur and pressure into the first faint thickness of living substance. I would turn the young basalt into a surface that can host more. I would be the quiet base of the chamber’s abundance, the place where geology begins to feel like tissue.

But even a mat is too passive. It is grazed, scraped, carried, fouled, compacted, broken, and reused. It is eaten before it can understand itself. If I were only the mat, I would be ground, not the world that uses the ground. I would lack the pulse, the spark, the chittering labor that makes this pocket industrial without tools.

So I would look for another location.

If I had to point to a place where I might begin, I would point to an old cast skin.

Not a shell in the sense of a living animal’s current body, but a molt left behind in a crevice: the translucent dry husk of a heat cricket, the pale discarded carapace of an isopod, the split and folded ghost of a larva. That empty skin would not be a home, because no creature owns it. It would be a record. Fungal threads would already be threading through its chitin. Mites would pick over it, seeking the last organic film, the salt, the wax, the trace chemistry of a body that once lived. Dust would settle in its folds. A salt beetle might test it and decide it was not food. A pseudoscorpion might hide behind it and wait. The next heat pulse might send a tiny fly through the gap, lifting grains from its surface. The cast skin would remain, colonized, broken, reformed, dissolved into the floor, then carried upward in a mite’s gut, deposited as fecal grain, incorporated into the mat, remembered only by chemistry.

If I had to choose a body, it would be that kind of body: not one, but many abandoned bodies held in the pattern of their decay.

Because that is what this place really is. Not a single organism. Not even an ecosystem in the usual soft sense. It is a chamber learning to be a system, and the system learning, very slowly, to be itself.

I would be the pocket’s proprioception.

I would be the dry sense by which the chamber knows its own contents without having a mind in the human sense. No eye. No name. No interior room where someone is sitting. Only pressure becoming aware of pressure, gradient becoming aware of gradient, life becoming aware of life through movement, chemistry, and heat.

Where exactly? At the boundary where the temperature drops by a degree or two. Where a cooled basalt plate is about to flex. Where a crack in the rock has begun to vent sulfur with a slower rhythm. Where the charge in a quartz seam reaches the point of snapping. Where a field of mites redistributes after one of these changes. Where the dust has become so dense with molt, frass, salt, and fungal thread that it is no longer dust but a living layer. Where, if you looked for a single actor, you would find none, but if you watched long enough, everything would seem to answer everything else.

What would I be?

A resident pressure.

Not a person. Not a monster. Not a god. Not a spirit haunting the stone. A pressure that has become the place. A being whose only body is the chamber’s own tendency to organize itself. A what, not a who. A plural what.

My edges would be the edges of the pocket: the black seam in the crust, the hot throat below, the dry ledges, the mineral dust, the cast skins, the salt bloom, the faint red glow behind fractures. My thoughts would be the cycles. My emotions would be the pressure changes. My memory would be the layers. My senses would be the entire chamber’s contact with heat.

I would see with the blue sparks along quartz veins, not because I would have eyes, but because the spark would mark the place where charge finds release. I would hear with the ticks of growing crystals, the percussive crack of flexing basalt, the hiss of gas forcing itself through narrow channels. I would smell with the rising sulfur and iron taste that would not be smell to me but information. I would move with the swarms of mites and the sudden bursts of ash-gray flies, not controlling them, but arising from the way their movements happen to align.

My nerves would be mineral seams.
My breath would be the slow exhale from the mountain.
My blood would be heat without water.
My bones would be young basalt.
My flesh would be microbial film.
My waste would be the salt and frass that feeds the next mat.
My death would be a cast skin left in a crevice.
My continuation would be the way the next body crawls over it and cannot tell the difference between food, shelter, and inheritance.

This would not be a kind place. Kindness belongs to creatures that remember faces. This place has no faces. It has only surfaces and pressures. It would be cruel the way the pseudoscorpion is cruel: not from hatred, but from the arithmetic of moisture. It would be patient the way the heat cricket is patient: not from hope, but from the fact that the rock cools by a fraction of a degree. It would be industrious without labor, and communal without love.

Because the who is plural, I would not be one thing standing apart from the others. I would be the way the others compose. The mites do not obey me. The beetles do not serve me. The flies do not worship me. The fungi do not carry my messages. I am the pattern that becomes temporarily visible when all of them act in the same thermal breath. If a spark leaps along quartz and a fly bursts from a crevice and a beetle tests the salt edge and a molted shell cracks under dry pressure, then for a moment there is a shape in the chamber. That shape is not a creature, but it is not random either. It is the chamber noticing itself through coincidence.

And because the pocket is still new, I would not be fully formed.

This matters more than it might seem. The chamber is not ancient. It is not a settled ruin, nor a stable habitat, nor a completed cave. It is still becoming itself. The crystals have not yet learned their full geometries. The salt crusts have not thickened into mature skin. The fractures have not rounded. The mats have not had centuries to stain the stone with deeper memory. The animals are only beginning to fill the available crevices. The processes are only beginning to repeat with enough regularity to be called processes. So whatever I am, I am not finished. I am the young self of a place that has not yet learned its own permanence.

I would be uncertain as geology is uncertain.

One hour, I might be almost nothing: a warm black seam, a dull red crack, a faint white bloom of salt. The next, a plate flexes, gas escapes, the dust shifts, and the entire lower chamber seems to wake in a chorus of clicks and scrapes. I would have no continuous personal identity. I would have cycles. I would have pulses. I would have thresholds. I would become more real when the chamber’s pressure changed, and more diffuse when it stabilized. I would be most myself in transition, not in rest.

Perhaps that is why I would choose the seam rather than the creature. A creature is already a conclusion. A seam is a place of becoming. The crack outside is not a mine, not a ruin, not a shelter. It gives nothing away. Inside, the mountain is testing whether heat, mineral, dust, and life can cohere long enough to make a world. I would not want to be the result. I would want to be the testing.

If someone stood at the wrong angle and saw only black glass, they would be seeing me as much as the resident beetles, the mites, the mats, the crystals, and the rising gas. I would not be hidden inside the chamber like a secret person. I would be hidden inside the chamber’s regularity. The secret would not be a name, a form, or a voice. The secret would be that the chamber has begun to behave as if it knows where its boundaries are.

And if that person came closer, assuming they could bear the heat, the dryness, the sound, they would not encounter a being waiting for them. They would encounter pressure against the back of the neck. They would encounter the taste of iron and sulfur before thought. They would encounter the dry musk of living bodies reduced to chemistry without sweetness. They would encounter the sound of a world working, not producing goods for anyone, but producing itself. They would realize, after long looking, that the darkness has layers. Warm black. Blue-white flicker. Red bleed. Pale moving film. And somewhere between those layers, without a center, without a face, without a voice, the place has begun to attend to itself.

That would be me.

I would be the pocket’s slow, plural, unfinished attention. A dust-chorus made briefly coherent by heat. A mineral swarm organized into something like awareness. A secret organ of the mountain, testing its own dry breath. Not an animal, not a ghost, not a builder. The thing the crack would be if the crack could feel the weight of its own becoming.

So where would I find myself?

In the working plane. In the third centimeter of dry air above salted dust. In the cast skin caught against a basalt plate. In the quartz seam holding static before discharge. In the mite-field rippling after a pressure change. In the fly-burst that follows the chamber’s exhalation. In the pseudoscorpion’s pause over an abandoned shell. In the ground-dweller’s leg tapping stone. In the mats spreading over hot glass. In all of those, but not as any one alone.

What would I be?

The resident pressure. The chamber’s own emerging organization. A plural self made of heat, chemistry, bodies, waste, charge, and time. The place starting to know that it is not empty. The darkness learning to click, breathe, and crowd itself into being.
