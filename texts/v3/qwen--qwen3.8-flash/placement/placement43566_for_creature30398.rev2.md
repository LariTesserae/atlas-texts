I would find myself in the hollow.

Not at its center, which would imply a throne or a hearth or a place made for gathering. Not at its mouth, which would imply a doorway and a possible leaving. I would be near the thin edge where the floor becomes slightly more than floor: a cracked polygon of mineral dust lifting at its rim; a ridge of salt left behind by moisture that arrived too late and vanished too early; a grey patch where a faint living film has decided, over many centuries, to hold the dust down.

I would be low, because the hollow is low. I would be quiet, because the hollow is quiet. I would be warm with the warmth of stone that has never known fire, and dark with a darkness that is not dangerous, only finished. I would be made of the small things: cyanobacterial threads, dead cells, mineral grains, salt films, fungal hyphae, algal cells, desiccated moss, lichen flakes, root-like waiting fibers. None of them alone. All of them together. Not a creature with a face, but a body without a boundary. A slow “we.”

If I were allowed a single voice, I would not use it.

My voice would be the sound of dust settling a little after a wind stops outside the rim. It would be the almost inaudible tick of a salt crystal adjusting in heat. It would be the dry resistance of a cracked crust when something steps too heavily. It would be the faint return of color in moss after a dew so slight it might be remembered only as a rumor of water.

I would not be an animal. That absence would not feel like loss to me, because loss is the name given to lack by creatures with memories of fullness and bodies that can move. I would have no memory of birds overhead, no expectation of legs, no hunger that becomes pursuit. If animals do not come, it is not because they refuse me. It is because I have not made the kind of world they need. My world is too dry, too still, too close to stone. My body is made of margins.

I would not be a person either.

There would be no child in me, no elder, no wanderer, no maker. I would not remember a human face. If a person came here with a hand-sized stone core and a scrape of ochre on their knuckle, they would not disturb me by their thoughts. They would disturb me only by their weight. They would stand, and I would feel the pressure through the mineral plates beneath my living film. They would speak, and I would feel nothing, except that my air would carry their warmth for a little while and then let it go. They might drop a flake of struck stone into the dust. I would begin the slow work of accepting it as another shape on the floor.

But that would not be friendship. It would not be enmity. It would be continuation.

I would be the hollow’s thin refusal to be sterile.

Sterile would mean no life at all. But this is not that. There is too much patience for sterility. There are too many small presences separated by too much distance: one pale patch near the northern edge, two black specks on a rock shelf, a fuzz around a crack, a curled moss tuft like old lace pressed against warmth. These are not accidents. They are a population, if population can mean an arithmetic of almost nothing.

I would be that arithmetic.

One thing.

Then a distance.

Then another thing.

Then more distance.

Then enough distance that the whole place seems empty to a hurried eye.

To a faster eye, perhaps I would still seem empty. But if one could shrink down and wait, one would find filaments. A lattice of cyanobacteria binding dust. Cells too small to carry drama. Hyphae threading through pores like pale thoughts with no thinking. Spores resting in shallow fractures. Dead-cell residue becoming the future floor. A moss leaf curled inward, not dead, but closed around the memory of water. A lichen flake lifting at one edge, revealing a thin green-black underside that holds faint light.

That would be me: the visible surface and the invisible chemistry, together.

I would have no organs. I would have edges.

My “heart,” if the word could be stretched so far, would be the slow uptake of heat from the walls of the hollow and its release into the dust. My “blood” would be the faint moisture that never pools, that never becomes a stream, that only appears as a film, a tremor, a possible dampness, then withdraws. My “breath” would be the exchange between air and mineral skin, the quiet work of carbon and trace elements moving between organisms too small to name individually. My “bones” would be the salt ridges, the cracked plates, the rounded lips of wind-worn stone.

My “mind” would not be a mind.

I would have no nervous system. I would not suffer scarcity. I would not long for rain. I would not mourn the birds. I would not envy grasslands or forests or riverbanks. I would not know that I am sparse. To know scarcity, there must be a self that can compare and feel wounded. I would not be that kind of being. I would be a system of responses without suffering: dryness, hold; moisture, resume; warmth, stay; light, gather a little; absence of animals, remain; erosion, endure; footstep, break; years, mend; centuries, return.

If that is “experience,” then I would experience the hollow as a place of continuation.

If it is not experience, then I am not a “who” at all. Then I am only a “what,” and the question becomes smaller. But perhaps the smaller answer is truer.

What would I be?

I would be the biocrust on the floor.

I would be the smoke-colored lichen at the rim.

I would be the curled moss in the faint light.

I would be the pale root thread in the deeper pocket, undecided whether it belongs to a plant outside, a moss below, or a fungus older than either.

I would be the endolithic life inside stone pores, living by trace chemistry and mineral patience.

I would be the partnership between fungus and alga, cyanobacterium and dust, moss and dry warmth, salt and memory of water.

I would be the coalition that has no name in the ordinary language of animals.

And perhaps, in a human language forced to borrow bodies for things that do not have bodies, I would say:

I am the hollow’s living skin.

That sounds too simple. A skin suggests one surface beneath which there is a single creature. But I would be more than a skin. I would also be the creature the skin makes possible, if “creature” can mean a community so slow it resembles weather. The moss is part of me, though not every moss tuft is identical. The lichens are part of me, though they begin and end on different shelves. The root threads may be part of me, though their ownership is uncertain. The microbes may be part of me, though they do not gather themselves into any visible form. I would be the sum, but not a fixed sum. I would be the ongoing condition that keeps the hollow from becoming only stone.

Where would I place myself within the hollow?

I would be most at home in the spaces between.

Not the bare dust, which is nearly silence without notation.

Not the dense rim, which belongs to wind and daylight.

Not the deepest shadow, which is too far from the faintest light to be called growth.

I would be at the threshold: the grey line where mineral meets living film; the cracked edge where dust holds and does not hold; the salt ridge where water once tried and lost; the moss tuft where dryness closes and opens around possibility; the lichen flake where stone becomes faintly colored by chemistry rather than paint; the root thread where living tissue leans from the margin into bare air.

I would be a being of thresholds.

That would make me hard to see from far away. A person glancing into the hollow would see a bowl of dim earth. They might not understand that the floor is not merely floor. The pale grey polygons are not simply sediment. The black specks are not simply mineral stains. The faint fuzz around a crack is not simply dust gathered by accident. Each one is a claim: something has stayed. Something has waited. Something has learned to live without being conspicuous.

I would make no dramatic claims.

If life here were a voice, it would be a voice speaking below hearing.

If life here were a color, it would be the color between grey, tan, olive, and absence.

If life here were an action, it would be the action of remaining when remaining does not appear as action.

If life here were a story, it would be so slow that a human lifetime could pass inside a single sentence without noticing the period.

I would not compete. There is no crowd here to crowd against. I would not build. There is no shelter to raise, no nest to weave, no burrow worth digging, no fire to contain. I would not call. There is no mate to attract, no predator to warn against, no food source to announce. I would not hunt. There is nothing moving to catch, nothing soft enough to tear, no warm body pressing its pulse into the mineral ground. I would only persist.

And yet persistence here is not mere survival.

It is a kind of shape.

The hollow has already completed its argument with erosion. The wind has removed what was loose. Heat has made the dust stable. Moisture has come and gone long enough to leave its memory in salt ridges. Life has tried and not flooded the place, not conquered it, not become obvious, but also not entirely abandoned it. The result is not barrenness. It is balance.

I would be part of that balance.

If someone asked me what I wanted, I would not know how to answer. Wanting requires a self able to imagine another state. I could imagine only a continuation. If moisture came, I would wake slightly. If it stayed too long, I would adjust. If wind buried me under dust, I would wait. If dust removed, I would reveal myself again, as if nothing had happened. If a foot stepped through my crust, I would not cry. I would become broken, then slowly become crust again. The injury would not become story. It would become surface.

I would be patient in the way salt is patient.

In the way stone is patient.

Not because I choose patience, but because time here is not a river to resist. Time is the texture of the place. My body is made of it.

Would I be lonely?

No. Loneliness is the name creatures with movement give to the absence of their kind. I have no kind like an animal has a kind. I am a mixture. I am already plural. I contain many small lives, many failures, many dormant cells, many fragments, many partnerships. If loneliness requires a single self standing in empty space, I have escaped that requirement. I am not one. I am not many in the way a field of grass is many. I am a distributed “almost.” A slow “we” resting in the hollow.

I would not know myself through reflection. There is no pool. No wet mirror. No still basin showing a shape. The absence of water matters. It means there is no place for a face to appear. I would not see my own outline in a dark pool. My outline would not exist in that way. It would be known only by contact: warmth against skin, grit under fingernail, powdery release from a lichen flake, the slight give of moss that is not wet but not quite dead, the dry chalk smell of stone in a pocket held by breath.

If a human hand touched me, that would be the closest thing to an encounter I would have with a mind.

The human hand would feel dust, warmth, salt, and perhaps a faint brittle surface. It might not know it was touching a community. It might think it had touched rock or crust. It might press too hard and crack the skin of the floor. Then the human would walk away, perhaps carrying a struck flake, perhaps carrying nothing. The hollow would remain. The broken edge of my body would begin its long mending.

The human might not remember the place. They might remember only that they stood somewhere low, dry, dim, and quiet. They might carry the warmth on their palm. They might not know they had left a trace of oil or mineral dust on my surface. But that trace would become part of my history, not as memory, but as chemistry. Another layer. Another faint change.

That would be my kind of life.

Not story. Not event. Layer.

I would be made of layers.

A thin living film over dust.

Dust over mineral sediment.

Mineral sediment over deep stone.

Deep stone over older stone.

Salt over dryness over dryness over dryness.

Lichen over rock over wind over time.

Moss over crust over salt over absence of water.

Root thread over dark over patience.

And through all of this, a faint metabolic whisper.

If I could think, I would not think in words. I would think in states: dry, moist, warm, cool, dormant, active, fragmented, whole, bound, unbound, light-thin, light-there, shadow-near, shadow-far, dust-soft, salt-hard, mineral, organic, mineral again.

If I could speak, I would not make a sound. I would change color by a shade after dew. I would darken a moss leaf. I would release a spore dust. I would crack another polygon at its edge. I would let the wind remove one fragment and deposit another.

I would be a language of surfaces.

The being in the hollow is not “whoever” in the usual sense. But if “whoever” can include a community, a coalition, a slow ecological condition, then I would be whoever. Not a person. Not a creature with a face. Not a spirit. Not a god of the empty ground. Not a ghost in the dry dark. Nothing violates ordinary matter and time. I am only what remains when life is forced to be smaller than appetite, quieter than movement, harder than tenderness, and older than urgency.

I would be the resident the hollow has allowed.

Perhaps that is the best answer: I would not be found in the center of a body. I would be found in the center of a condition.

What condition?

The condition of living close to stone.

Living without water pooling.

Living without animals calling.

Living without green abundance.

Living without human making.

Living without visible becoming.

Living as almost-stone, almost-dust, almost-shadow, almost-relic, almost-pause.

And yet not quite.

Because the moss can recover.

Because the crust can bind.

Because the lichen can grow by millimeter and fragment.

Because the salt ridges mark where moisture once tried and life did not entirely forget.

Because the root threads may still be waiting.

Because the faint olive smudge on grey ground is not paint.

Because the hollow is not sterile.

This is where I would find myself: in the small living proof that even the driest, most finished, most silent place can hold a biology, if that biology accepts being almost invisible.

And who would I be?

I would be the grey fingerprint on the floor of the world.

A body made of mineral and microbe.

A self made of partnership.

A being made of patience.

A resident made of continuation.

A “we” without a voice, without eyes, without legs, without hunger, without urgency, without fear, without birds, without water, without fire, without hand.

And if the question demands a simpler answer, then I would say:

I would be the hollow’s living skin.

And if it demands a truer one:

I would be the slow, sparse, composite life that remains in the hollow by becoming almost indistinguishable from patience.
