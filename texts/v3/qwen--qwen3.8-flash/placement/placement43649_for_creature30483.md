I would begin by giving up height.

Standing tall is how the world is missed. If I tried to be the human who notices the hollow, I would already be wrong. I would be too large, too loud, too much of a weather event. My shadow would cool a court that was about to dry; my breath would warm an arch that needed condensation; the movement of my hand through the root curtain would be a storm.

So I would not be the observer.

I would be inside the almost.

I would find myself in a side chamber of the settlement, behind a false wall of pressed clay and fungal paste, where the floor dips slightly and a narrow seam of dampness runs beneath the surface like a vein too pale to see. I would not be in the bright, airy courts. Not there. Not in the dust-colored plazas where beetles walk with their antennae lowered to taste the dry air, nor along the upper rim where sun comes only as a brown smear and the crust flakes underfoot. I would be lower, where moisture persists without being water, where the earth’s chemistry stays cool and pliable, where construction is not display but necessity.

I would be one of the pale bodies in the wall.

If a name is needed, I would be a larva. But not a larva in the simple animal sense. I would be a body whose purpose is to be a boundary. I would live where dampness begins to become too much and dryness has not yet become too absolute. My existence would be a movement between two dangers: slumping collapse and desiccation. I would follow the gradient not because I understood it, but because I was made of it. My hunger would be wetness shaped into mineral paste. My rest would be a chamber whose air was just thick enough to keep my skin from cracking. My direction would be given by taste: calcium salt, fungal mustiness, the faint sweetness of rotting root thread, the cool chemical memory of water that had once seeped this far and now retreated.

I would be soft.

I would be translucent enough at the head that the jaws might read like a dark fold in a piece of parchment. I would be cream, amber, the color of wet clay from which the light has not yet drained. I would not look like an individual. That is the first lesson of this place. At this scale, an “individual” is almost a false impression. I would be more like a temporary emphasis in the wall’s own process. A bulge where moisture had gathered. A moving seam. A mouthful of ground becoming structure.

I would eat the settlement and the settlement would become me.

I would consume fungal threads, mineral grains, the fine powder of broken carbonate rims, shed skins, fecal grains, dead root hairs, the soft black paste where old larvae had passed. I would not eat with appetite in the human way. I would eat with geometry. Each mouthful would remove one thing from one place and later put it back into another. My body would be a small construction machine, not clever, not proud, not self-aware in any way that resembles language. It would be a wet instrument of placement.

I would chew the wall.

I would scrape along a narrow tunnel, leaving behind a line so slight that a human eye would mistake it for a flaw in the clay. But the flaw would be a route. The route would be a room. The room would be a chamber where later another larva might lay its molting skin, or a beetle might taste the air, or a spider might wait behind a false arch, or a fungal network might thicken until the wall stopped feeling like mud and began to feel like architecture.

I would repair what had not been destroyed.

That is the strange economy of the hollow. Nothing looks ruined, but nothing is finished either. A wall has just begun. A ceiling has almost sagged. A floor has dried into crust and now needs to be softened, lifted, returned to paste. I would move through these beginnings and endings without ceremony. My antennae—if I had such things, if the larval body had some sense so fine it could not be called antennae—would test the tilt of a passage, the temperature of a seam, the mineral grit under my mouth. If the wall was too dry, I would work from a damper side. If it was too wet, I would stop. If a thread of moisture threatened a nursery chamber, I would not “know” danger. But my body would move away, and in moving away, would leave behind a trace that other bodies would interpret as a boundary.

I would be one of the Just-Damp Ones.

That name is better than “beetle,” and better than “builder.” It says the truth of the place: survival here is not a matter of grand conditions, but of delicate ones. A chamber too wet is a collapse. A chamber too dry is a corpse. A wall too soft is mud. A wall too hard is dust. Life persists in the narrow corridor between. I would belong to that corridor. I would not live in water, not exactly. I would live in the memory of water.

I would be guided by absence.

There is no river here, no lake, no visible current. Water exists as promise. It is the dark patch beneath the clay, the bead forming on the underside of an arch, the damp stripe left on the floor where a film passed across and then vanished. I would move toward that promise, but not as if toward a destination. More like a substance drawn by a slight difference in saturation. The place would teach me its own shape through moisture. Where the floor dipped, I might become slower, more active, pressing paste. Where it dried, I might thin into rest, or abandon the chamber, or molt and become a membrane. Where a root had shifted and let too much air enter, I might leave behind a line of refusal that another body would seal.

I would be both resident and material.

My molts would be architecture. My skins, pale and translucent, would catch on rough clay and become part of the roof lining. A discarded larval body would not lie waste in the corner of a room. It would be eaten, absorbed, softened, pressed into walls. I would not mourn myself. I would continue as membrane, as binding paste, as a faint sheen of chitin reinforcing the wall’s surface. Death here would not be an interruption. It would be a change of use.

I would compete, but without theater.

Another body might enter the same damp seam. We would meet at the mouth of a narrow pocket. There would be no battle in any way a human would recognize. One of us would press forward; the other would hesitate, turn, leave behind a chemical trace that would alter the next decision of another body. Perhaps a line would be abandoned for an hour. Perhaps a chamber would be sealed with fresh paste. Perhaps the loser would simply become slower, less favored by the wall’s moisture gradient. This would not be tragedy. It would be the town learning what to carry on and what to let go.

I would fear without fear.

There would be the vibration of a centipede, felt first as a tremor in the soft floor. There would be the taste of wasp near an opening. There would be the shadow of a gecko at the warm edge, distant and diffuse. I would not know what these things were in the way an animal might know a predator as a creature with a face. But my body would respond. I would curl. I would stop. I would press into paste and become a line, a fold, a nothing. I would seal the chamber mouth before danger could follow. This would be the town’s quiet vigilance. Safety here would not mean comfort. It would mean the slow closing of possibilities.

I would build with my body.

There would be no tools. No sticks. No woven baskets. No cut stone. The materials would be the place itself, and my mouth would be the instrument. Moist clay packed into narrow gaps. Fine mineral grit carried in pellets. Fungal thread dragged across a floor until it began to bind. Shed skin pressed to the roof of a chamber until it became a thin, tough membrane. The walls would rise not by design but by accumulation. They would not be planned. They would be remembered by matter. A repeated path becomes a street. A repeated scrape becomes a room. A repeated excretion becomes strength.

I would be part of a town without ownership.

If someone asked me to say “I live here,” that phrase would be too large. The hollow would not belong to me. It would belong to moisture and its opposite, to salt and fungal film, to the slow pull of roots through ceilings, to the polishing tracks of snails, to the carrying of grains by beetles, to the spider’s thread at the mouth of a passage. I would not be one part of a society. I would be a node in a process. The process would make me, and I would make the process, and neither would have a first cause.

The beetles would be my distant movement.

Above me, adults would walk across open courts, dark and matte, flattened as if pressure were part of their shape. They would carry away bits of crust, test dry passages, perhaps bring droplets on their backs from some hidden wetness. I would feel their passing only as faint tremor. Their legs would comb the floors. Their antennae would taste the air. They would be the visible circulation of the settlement, but I would be the interior metabolism. They would be the town’s movement; I would be its making.

The snails would be my slow weather.

When dampness allowed, pale shells would cross mineral floors, leaving silver trails that dried into almost lines of light. Those trails would guide smaller bodies, bind grains, create smoother paths through galleries. Their empty shells would become basins, nurseries, shards. I might not meet them. But I would live inside their work. My walls would be polished and strengthened by the chemistry of slimes and shells. The snail would be a mobile cistern; I would be a fixed mouth. Together we would make the settlement’s body possible.

The fungi would be my silent partners.

I would not be able to see them as separate from the walls. In the dark, after a day of moisture, white threads might spread through the clay until the ground stopped feeling like ground and began to feel like tissue. The fungi would bind grains. They would carry moisture where roots could not reach. They would condition air in nurseries. They would grow into dead matter and return it to usable paste. Their boundaries would be chemical laws. I might follow one, stop at another, turn where a different fungal scent changed the room’s permission. The fungi would not command me. They would simply be more of the place, and therefore more of my movement.

The moss would be my roof, not my ceiling.

In abandoned courts, old mounds, and sheltered seams, green cushions would swell with the faintest wetness. They would shade floors where larvae needed protection from drying. They would hold water against clay. They would curl when moisture failed and soften when it returned. I would not live beneath them directly, but my world would end where their world changed. If moss colonized a chamber, the roof might become too damp. If moss dried, the chamber might lose its shade. If moss spread too far, it might seal a passage. If moss retreated, raw ground would slump. The settlement would be neither mineral nor vegetal nor animal. It would be a negotiation.

I would be that negotiation made flesh.

Or made paste. Or made movement.

If I had to answer the question, “Who would you be?” I would say: I would be the adjustment.

Not a hero of the hollow. Not a queen of the mound. Not a conscious ruler. Not even, at first, an animal. I would be the slight correction by which the town remains a town. If a wall softened, I would thicken it. If a room became too dry, I would move toward the damp side. If a chamber filled with wrong paste, I would remove it grain by grain. If another body blocked my path, I would leave a chemical line that changed future passage. If I died, I would become a wall. If I molted, I would become a roof. If I rested, the resting itself would shape the next day.

And the most precise thing would be this: I would not stand out.

To a human eye looking from above, I would be nothing. A slight bulge in the clay. A pale fold in the side of a chamber. A line that could be mistaken for a crack. A movement that could be mistaken for condensation sliding downward. This is how the hollow remains hidden: not because it is secret, but because its inhabitants are so fitted to their place. A beetle becomes shadow. A spider becomes dust. A larva becomes wall. A snail trail becomes mineral stain. A fungal thread becomes mold. A moss patch becomes old mound. Everything is so perfectly adapted that it seems less alive than made.

But it is alive in a way that does not ask to be recognized.

I would not need another to know me. The town would know me by touch. The dampness would know me by movement. The fungi would know me by the chemical pressure of my passing. The next larva would know me by the route I left or abandoned. This is a way of being seen that does not require eyes. The observer is not a spectator. The observer is the condition.

If I stayed still too long, I would dry.

If I moved too fast, I would collapse a roof.

If I carried too much wetness, I would make mud.

If I sealed too tight, I would suffocate the eggs.

So I would live in interrupted motion. A leg lowers. A body pauses. A taste extends into the air. A grain is moved. A pause. A grain again. The rhythm would be slow enough that human time would feel like catastrophe. A human hand could destroy a gallery in a second. A human breath could change the air of a nursery. A human footstep could be an earthquake. I would not react to humans as enemies, exactly. I would react to them as weather.

If a shadow passed overhead, I would stop.

If warmth increased, I would move inward.

If moisture appeared, I would begin.

If silence remained, I would continue.

The loudest thing I would ever know would be a drop of water falling from a ceiling into a thin crust basin.

That sound would be soft, but it would rewrite the world.

Because the hollow’s whole logic is that everything is in process. The drop is not an interruption. It is information. One drop means the air has condensed. One drop means a passage may darken. One drop means a spider will adjust its thread. One drop means a larva may curl tighter. One drop means the town has shifted from maintenance to anticipation. Then the water vanishes into clay, and the event becomes residue, and the residue becomes chemistry, and the chemistry becomes wall, and the wall becomes body, and the body becomes event again.

I would be caught in that loop.

Not because I chose it, but because I would be shaped by it. The town would be both my birth and my excrement. My first chamber would be a wall. My last chamber would be a wall. In between, I would move through walls, eat walls, repair walls, become wall. If asked what I was, I might not have an answer. If asked what I wanted, I might only move toward dampness. If asked where I belonged, I might not belong anywhere except the threshold.

And perhaps that is the true answer.

I would find myself in the hollow, but not as a visitor. Not as the human eye kneeling down. Not as a mind trying to read the settlement. I would be the settlement’s own attention. I would be the slight, living edge where moisture meets clay, where building meets collapse, where body meets architecture.

I would be a larva.

And a molt.

And a wall.

And a damp seam.

And a line of chemical refusal.

And a place where the town remembers how to continue.

If someone later asked, “What were you in that world?” I would say: I was one of the Just-Damp Ones.

If someone asked, “Were you important?” I would say: importance here is not a title. Importance is a gradient.

If someone asked, “Were you alive?” I would say: I was being maintained.

If someone asked, “Were you conscious?” I would say: the wall moved because I moved, and I moved because the wall needed to be.

If someone asked, “What did you see?” I would say: I did not see. I tasted.

If someone asked, “What did you know?” I would say: the knowledge was not in me. It was in the damp.

If someone asked, “What did you fear?” I would say: too much water, too much air, too much silence, too much speed.

If someone asked, “What did you hope for?” I would say: the next grain.

If someone asked, “Where did you live?” I would say: in the room that is not a room yet.

If someone asked, “Who was the ruler of that place?” I would say: the boundary between wet and dry.

And if someone asked, “Would you still be there?”

I would say: yes, as a fold in the clay.

Yes, as the faint dark line beneath a floor where moisture has not yet dried.

Yes, as a molt becoming roof.

Yes, as a droplet falling and being absorbed.

Yes, as the town continuing to be the town by never quite finishing itself.
