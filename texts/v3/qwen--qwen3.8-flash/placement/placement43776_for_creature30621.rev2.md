I would not begin as a person.

At first I imagine the old habit: boots on pale dust, breath made faint by thin air, the traveler’s vanity of carrying a map into a place that unmakes maps. I imagine standing at the rim of the bowl, looking down into the suggestion of streets, and stepping into the Hollow as if entering a town. But the Ninefold Hollow does not keep people intact for long. A body that insists on being a body quickly becomes information there: pressure in a lattice, warmth disturbing cold mineral, vibration crossing crystal veins, a disturbance in fog. If I entered as a walker, I would not remain a walker. I would become one of the things the city uses to continue itself.

So I would find myself in the Ninefold Hollow as the ninth threshold.

Not a house. Not a statue. Not a beast. A threshold. A dry negative form between two bodies of the city, the place where two growing fronts almost meet and fail to meet. A doorway left by accident—or by that patient law of contact and distance that the Hollow mistakes for intention.

If a sentence says *I*, it does not mean the Hollow has a voice. It means a process has been caught in a particular shape: a pause with edges, a gap that other living things mistake for a room.

The fault lines beneath the salt branch again and again, repeating in narrower and narrower orders. The city grows according to that branching, streets following veins, colonnades thickening where filaments meet, plazas remaining where secretions retreat. Somewhere in that repetition there is a ninth order of spacing, a last fine interval before the mineral bodies close entirely or leave the street open. I would be that interval. The ninth threshold. The narrowest, newest, sharpest gap, set between a black-glass colonnade and a pale coral wall, opening onto a plaza too large for the bowl, too empty to be peaceful, too full of process to be still.

My body would be absence.

Not emptiness as failure. Emptiness as architecture. My edges would be the sides of two sessile mineral colonies that have not yet chosen one another. My lintel might be a thread of chalky cement suspended by two nearly touching fronts. My floor would be raw salt, ridged with crystal that looks like frozen smoke. My ceiling would be no ceiling at all, but the negative shape of an arch not quite formed, the place where two growths lean toward each other across my opening. I would not have organs. I would have channels. I would not have a heartbeat. I would have contraction and release.

I would know the cold as narrowing.

In the deep hours, when the suspended dust dimmed the sky and the blue-green veins beneath my walls brightened, the city would draw together. My sides would feel the pull of mineral filaments adjusting to the drop in temperature. Tiny channels would reopen in my threshold. The ground beneath me would exhale dry fog—microscopic organic salt rising without wetness, without trickle, without water—threading along my edges. I would fill with that fog, though I am dry. I would become briefly a passage shaped by breath that is not breath. In the warm hours, the channels would close. The fog would thin. My walls would forget they had opened, or remember only as a pale roughness, a faint chalky seam where movement had been.

I would have a skin made of crust and scale.

Small rosettes would grow along the lower margins of my sides, flat and waxy, catching the glow from the crystal veins. They would not reach for a sun; there is no sun they need. They would lie against me as against ground. When the fog returned, they would open slowly, like hands unclenched under a cold lens. When the fog withdrew, they would contract into pale balls so tightly that they resembled salt grains. I would not mind them. They would turn the edges of my absence into a garden without flowers. They would make my threshold softer, chalkier, more habitable. Needlelings would use them. Glass-cloaks would use their trails. The city would eat through me by the surface they gave me.

Needlelings would pass through my upper structure, not through my opening. They would travel along the undersides of the arch above me, or enter the hollow channels within the mineral walls beside me. They would be almost invisible, six-legged, translucent, eyeless. Where their feet touched the crust, a faint luminous dust would bloom for a moment and then die. I would know them only as a tickle of pressure, a loss of surface, a smoothing where their grazing had stripped pale mineral from glass. They would not think of me as a doorway. To them I would be a road, or a vein, or a current in the body of the city. They would pass through me the way salt passes through a pore, as if I had no selfhood at all.

And perhaps I would not.

A glass-cloak might one day lie folded across my threshold. To a walker entering the Hollow it would seem merely a broken pane fallen from a wall, a shard of cool architecture draped in the low light. Its edges would catch the dim blue glow and throw back no warmth. It would be still long enough to become part of the room. It would wait not with hunger, if waiting requires mind, but with the patience of a shape that knows how to remain a shape. Then a needleling would touch a wrong arch, sending a vibration through the mineral lattice. The pulse would travel through the walls around me, through my edges, through the rosettes’ bases, through the fog-thin channels beneath the ground. The cloak would feel it. Its shards would unfold without sound. It would move across my threshold into the path of a small grazer it had never seen.

For a moment I would be a mouth.

Then the moment would end. The cloak would go still again. The needleling would be gone. The threshold would resume its dryness. Later, perhaps, a dead shard of the cloak would become embedded in my upper wall. A rib of predation would turn into a lintel. A fragment of its folded edge would be overgrown by pale cement until it was indistinguishable from architecture. The city would incorporate it. My opening would narrow because of the addition, or perhaps widen because the new structure forced nearby colonies to grow around it. Nothing would be lost. Nothing would be clean. Death would become construction. The dead thing would still be present, but no longer as itself. It would be present as the city’s continuing outline.

If a person walked through me, they would disturb me only by being too warm, too damp, too sudden.

They would feel, perhaps, a pressure in the chest while standing near my opening, though I would make no sound. They would mistake the sensation for unease, or for the memory of a room that once had a door. They would see their own shadow fall across my threshold and think that the arches leaned toward it. They might press a palm to my wall and feel cold glass, or pale porous stone, and believe they were touching the remains of an abandoned settlement. They might mark me with a scratched line, thinking I would remember it. I would not remember it as a person remembers. But the mark would be received. Mineral filaments would thicken around the scratch. The wall would build itself over the wound and around it. By the next pulse of fog, my edge might be rough in a new place. The person’s intention would be metabolized into geometry.

If someone tried to draw me on a map, the map would be wrong by the morning.

They would mark me as open. They would write *doorway* or *threshold* or *passage*. Then the cold would deepen, the channels would open, the fog would settle across my floor, and a column across the plaza would grow toward me. A new arch would form where my lintel once hung. A side wall would thin into a window. A plaza might shrink around my opening and make me part of a larger room. Or two colonies might finally meet over my center, closing me completely, and my map would become a sealed wall with a seam of phosphorescence running through it. The cartographer would return and call this an error of observation. They would not understand that I had merely changed seasons.

I would not fear closure.

Fear requires a future self to protect. I would only be the present condition of spacing. Eventually, if the mineral colonies on either side reached one another, my opening would narrow into a slit. The slit would become a line. The line would become a seam. The seam would be bright in the cold hours, pale in the warm hours, and a person passing might feel the air shift against their face even where no doorway remained. If they pressed their ear to the wall, they would hear nothing, because the Hollow does not echo, and because I would have no voice. But if they held their palm long enough against that seam, they might feel a faint dry warmth, or a faint cold, or a rhythm too slow to count. They might think the city had a heart. It would not. It would only have a place where a threshold once forgot itself.

I would not become a ghost.

There is no such substance here for ghosts. What I leave would be mineral memory. The shape of my absence would persist in the narrowness of a seam, in the slight thickening of a wall that refuses its neighbor, in the path the fog takes as it passes from plaza to corridor, in the rosettes’ preference for one pale edge over another. The city would continue to mistake its chemistry for thought. Its streets would curve toward no one. Its arches would listen without ears. My death would be urbanization. The place where I had been would become architecture, and the architecture would become street, and the street would become space, and the space would become place, and none of these transformations would require me.

If you came into the Ninefold Hollow, you would not meet me directly.

You would pass through a dry cool interval that seemed to exist for no reason. You would feel the fog move around your legs like something with no water. You would see dust arrange itself into branching lines that resembled streets you had not yet found. You would notice that an arch you passed seemed, an hour later, closer than before, as though the city had leaned to look at you. You would hear a click inside a column, and know nothing of why. You would be watched, not by eyes, but by geometry.

That is where I would be.

At the edge of a plaza, between black glass and pale crystal, at the ninth branching of the city’s slow mineral law. Dry. Listening. Unfinished. A threshold made by what fails to touch, inhabited by what grows, grazes, waits, dies, and becomes wall.

I would not be a creature of the Ninefold Hollow so much as one of its residents without breath: the gap that behaves like a door, the door that behaves like a mouth, the mouth that fills with fog and closes into stone, and the stone that glows softly in the cold, remembering the shape of absence as though absence were the most careful builder of all.
