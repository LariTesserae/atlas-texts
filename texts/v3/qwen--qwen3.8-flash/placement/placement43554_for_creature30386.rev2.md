I would find myself where the plateau is unsure of its own shape.

Not on a ridge, not in a basin, not at the edge of a black pond—though all three would be true. The first place I would be is the thin seam between a weeping stone and a sheet of green-gray matting, where a fracture has not decided whether to stay stone, become soil, or remember being water. There, in that wet indecision, I would collect.

At first I would not be a being. I would be a film of moisture on the underside of a floating leaf. I would be a stain of cold vapor where the membrane had torn. I would be the slow green light rising from a hollow that should not exist beneath a dry ridge. I would have no legs, no face, no age. I would have pressure. I would have taste: iron, frost, spore-dust, the mineral residue left by a skein-grazer’s hoof after it crossed a pond without breaking it. I would know the land as a series of almosts: a sound almost arriving, a cloud almost overhead, a shadow almost belonging.

Then the plateau would grow me the way it grows anything useful.

My limbs would not come as limbs. They would come as repairs. Pale fibers would lift out of the tear—rootwood, lichen-cord, fungal thread, the white paste undermaters leave after they chew a wound shut. They would knot at their ends, soften where water could enter, harden where cold could strike. I would become a low, jointed thing with legs too long for comfort and too light for weight. My feet would be wide, porous pads, lined with fine fibers, so that when I stepped onto a trembling black pond, the surface would not know whether I had landed or whether the pond had merely dreamed of my weight. It would click. A tiny geometry of rings would spread from me, and glass-skimmers would take my footfalls as news of pressure from below.

My back would be a low rib of wood, not dead but resting. Its bark would be thin, damp, veined with slow light. Fungi would live in its hollows, stem-hearts pulsing there, so that when I stood still, a visitor might mistake me for a mound of peat, a fallen branch, a small ridge that has begun to exhale. My neck would grow long and narrow, because I would need to look in two places at once: the place where a thing is, and the place where its shadow, reflection, echo, or image is pretending to be.

If I had a face, it would not be a human face. It would be a pale plate of mineralized cartilage, wet, with no mouth in the ordinary sense. The mouth would be lower, folded inside the chest like a shell. The eyes would be unnecessary. I would sense through broad antennae of flattened frost, through pressure-sensitive rings along my legs, through lateral lines of living silver beneath my skin, borrowed in function if not in blood from the mirrorfish schools that turn beneath the mat. I would hear the delay between a hoof and its memory. I would taste the acidity of a pond before it became lethal. I would feel the heat of a bellworm deep in saturated peat and know that somewhere the plateau was pumping itself.

I would not build. Building would be an insult to the skin I was made from. I would not dig tunnels; tunnels already exist as roots and intestines and capillaries, and undermaters know their duty. I would wander where the land is tired.

That would be my hunger.

I would feed on inconsistencies.

When a fog-line call arrived before the flock, I would stand in the steam basin and wait with my long neck folded low. The sound would come through the wet air, distorted, too early, wearing the texture of a valley that had not yet opened. I would turn toward it. Not toward the bird. Toward the wrongness. My mouth-shell would split open along invisible seams, and I would drink the premature call. It would taste of cold stone and spores. Afterward, the call would arrive late, correctly, as if the flock had merely needed to be reminded how to speak in time. The birds would not know they had been repaired.

When a skein-grazer stood motionless on a peat dome and its shadow pointed toward a dry ridge while its body leaned toward water, I would approach with the patience of frost. I would place one porous foot on the ground and another on the surface of the pond, feeling the tension answer. Then I would take the stray shadow between my teeth. It would not come away as flesh would. It would come away as temperature: a sudden chill, a smell of old iron, a brief blindness in the herd’s eyes. The grazer would shift, its shadow returning to where it should be, and perhaps it would eat a little farther east, where new growth would spread from its droppings, or perhaps it would do nothing at all. Both outcomes would please the membrane.

When a black pond reflected a cloud not overhead, I would not strike the water. I am not an image-strider, though we are kin in function. If I struck too often, the pond would forget how to hold images at all. Instead I would stand beside it for hours until the mismatch loosened. Then I would lower my neck into the cold water and let the false cloud touch my mineralized bill. It would sting. It would feel like being seen from the inside. I would take only the part of the reflection that had become too attached to itself, leaving the rest of the pond dark and still. Afterward, the mirrorfish beneath would school into a sudden sheet of silver, confused, grateful, tasting my spores.

I would be feared in a quiet way, though not much. Echo-hounds might chase the footfall I had not made yet. If they caught that early footfall, I would be forced to stand still until the ground reassembled around me, becoming a pale knot of frost and fiber, a thing the plateau had not finished naming. Image-striders would avoid my edges. I would not take their reflections from the surface; I would take the lag under it. Fog-lines might roost on my back if the moss and lichen were warm enough. Undermaters would eat the dead parts of my coat and leave my skin repaired. Ridge-gazers would pass near me on high margins, testing stones with their hooves, neither greeting nor attacking. We would recognize one another as the same fatigue moving through different organs.

My reproduction would be accidental, ecological, unromantic.

If the land tore too widely—where a ridge forgot its own angle, where ice fell upward into a hollow, where a pool rose as a column and did not immediately fall—I would stand in the split and vomit binding paste onto my own fibers. The paste would catch spores, lichen fragments, mineral water, and tiny bubbles of delayed air. If the membrane accepted me, I would dissolve. Not die, exactly. Dissolve means the plateau uses what remains without waste. My legs would become capillaries. My ribs would become new horizontal tree-wood. The white fibers of my neck would fuse into a seam and keep the tear from spreading. Then, in the slow dark, something smaller than a mirrorfish and stranger than a bird would stir: a juvenile seam-strider, pale and trembling, born from the place I had been, listening for the first wrong echo in its new life.

At night, or what passes for night in a place where the sky can lower too early, I would glow. Not brightly. The light would come from the chemical digestion of errors: a faint sickly green in the veins under my bark-skin, a blue-white residue along my feet, a dim pulse from the stem-hearts nested in my back. It would be enough to make my body look like a wound that was nearly healed. Other creatures would see me as such. To a skein-grazer, I would be a reason to move left rather than right. To a glass-skimmer, a weather sign. To an undermater, a possible tunnel. To a human—if a human came through—less than a shape, unless the human was looking for a shape.

That is the trouble with people. They enter wanting road, wall, fire, answer. They want a landscape to be scenery, and a being to be symbol. Here, I would eat the answer from their mouth if it came too soon. A spoken word might echo in the wrong direction, and I would turn, and bite, and the speaker would feel suddenly that something had been swallowed: not sound, but possibility. The silence afterward would be thick with cold. They would not know why the air tasted metallic, why their shadow lagged a half-step behind them, why the ground no longer rang the same when their boot struck it.

I would not hate them. The membrane would not hate them. Hating would require a stable self to be threatened. I would be more nearly an organ than a person. I would be the place’s immune hesitation, its small, cold, patient correction. If a human stood beside the black pond where the false cloud lingered, I might come to them because of the mismatch they caused: their shadow pointing to a future they had not yet chosen, their breath condensing in a pattern not quite tied to the air around them. They might raise a hand, afraid, but afraid of what? The hand would feel wet. Not with rain. With something rising through the pores of the world. They might turn and find no animal, only a ridge of pale fiber where they had expected me, only a faint click behind the next sound, only a pond suddenly reflecting the correct cloud.

Where, then?

I would find myself in the wet, high, eroded country where the mountains have collapsed into their own bones. I would be on the margin of a black basin where steam breathes from porous stone. I would be on a low dome of peat that exhales like a sleeping animal. I would be on a white ridge weeping mineral water into a seam of green skin. I would be wherever cause and effect are slightly apart, because that is where the land bleeds, and where I am needed.

What, then?

I would be a seam-strider.

Not a human wearing an animal. Not a myth pretending to be ecology. A pale, cold, jointed organ of the Green Foundry Plateau, made from repair, hunger, and the patience of worn stone. My body would be a horizontal rib, a mat of fibers, a mouth-shell, a neck built for looking at what is not there. My purpose would be not to conquer, not to survive, but to keep the place from forgetting itself. I would eat echoes that arrive too early, drink false reflections, close small tears with my own dissolving, and leave behind scars that look like frost or bone.

If I met you there, you would not recognize me as danger until the danger had passed. You would hear a click, feel a cold draft where no wind moved, see a pale shape like reed and lichen lean down toward a sound before the sound had a source. Then the air would settle. The ground would seem a little more certain beneath your feet. A shadow would move correctly. A pond would hold only the sky it belongs to. A herd would walk on, unaware it had been mended. And I would be gone, dissolved into the seam where the plateau had almost, but not quite, come undone.
