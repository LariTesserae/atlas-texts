I find myself not standing at first. That would be too loud a way to begin in a place made of held breath and wet edges.

The first thing I know is the sensation of being smaller than a room, smaller than a body, but somehow still able to notice. I am not coming through the crack in the turf. I do not descend from above with boots, curiosity, or a torch. I am already in the seam. I wake in the damp dark as a kind of awareness without a fixed shape: cold mineral on a reed-wall, the faint green pull of algae, the memory of pressure where someone else’s hand has been resting for a long time.

At first I think I am the water.

Not the pool’s surface, but the part of it that is not surface: the black underside that touches the legs of platforms, that slips between root-fibers, that knows whether a ladder is safe by how much of its rung is now submerged. I move through capillary channels, through pores of peat, through the hidden passages behind walls where roots swell and divide. I taste iron, leaf-pulp, rot, salt, and the thin sweetness of something newly broken. I do not have eyes. I have rise. I have fall. I have the slow intelligence of a level that is always deciding what may touch what.

But water is not a being. Water is what the Cell thinks through.

So I try another shape.

I become the film on the walls.

I am the first green smear, the white fuzz that appears on split bark in a night, the invisible lace of fungal threads that binds the living felt together. I colonize bare places. I make wounds into surfaces. I am not yet a city, but I am the condition by which a city can happen: a coating that softens sharp edges, that tells the builders where a structure is ready to be inhabited. I smell of iron and stone when I am too new. I smell of bitter water when I am choking a root. I smell faintly sweet when sleepers may lie upon me.

Still, this is too many selves at once.

If I am the whole coating, then I am also the platform that breathes, the ladder that flexes, the moss pouch where a child hides from too much cold, the trap that glows with a pale bruise of light. I am everywhere, and that is not the same as being someone.

So I let myself narrow.

Not much. The room will not permit it. The settlement will not permit it. It is too crowded, too layered, too alive to belong to a single name. But I find a place where I can stand—or rather, where I can lean, crouch, press, and listen without breaking anything.

I am at the low place.

The place where the black pool has just risen enough to touch the underside of the sleeping shelf. The place where a hand can go down through a gap in the platform and find water, then something that is not water, then something that is not quite alive, then something that is most certainly alive and will not be touched twice.

I am small enough now to have a body.

It is a builder’s body, or something adjacent to one: short through the torso, broad at the hips, long in the arms. Skin washed to a translucent green-gray, with veins showing at temples and wrists like pale roots beneath thin bark. No sharp edges. No dry, proud posture. My feet are wide, with toes that know before the mind does where stone is loose, where moss is spongy, where the wall is about to answer me. I wear a cloak of lichen-fur and reed-woven strips, sealed at the shoulders with waxed leaf. A pouch hangs at my hip: flint flake, bone needle, tiny shell cup, a coil of cord, a pinch of glowing pulp sealed in a case so it wakes only when needed.

I am one of the people of the Cell.

But what do I do? That is the question that matters more than the question of what I am. In this place, being is doing. Identity is not carved. It is grown, tested, repaired, and then grown again around whatever needs attention.

I become a felt-mender.

I wake with my hand against a wall that has split during the dark. The wound is fresh: pale fibers exposed, damp pulp underneath, too much nakedness in a place where nakedness invites trouble. I do not shout. I do not strike it with tools. I carry the city’s medicine in my own palms—scraped algae, chewed stalk, mineral grit, spit, and patience—and I close the root around the crack. I press small reed fragments into the paste so it will not slip when the water swells. I make no permanent scar. A permanent scar would be arrogance. Instead I make a hinge. I make something that can breathe and be moved by.

This is enough for a while.

But mending walls is not enough for a whole being. The Cell is not only a house that needs repair. It is a body that needs to notice itself.

So I become a night-tender.

I climb where the ceiling lowers, where dripping tendrils brush my shoulders like slow questions. The air is colder here, thin with spore-dust, faintly sweet with root-moths. I do not carry light. Light would be an insult. I carry the memory of where the sleeping shelves are, where the children’s stones are laid, where the drying racks hold leaves that will become paste if the damp takes them, where a stalker-spider has paused too long against a cloak that belongs to someone still breathing.

I do not kill the spider. That would be clumsy. I tap once on the rim of a leaf-shingle, twice on a reed-stake, then draw a small trap of glowing pulp along the wrong route. The spider follows, tasting vibration and light, and I seal its passage away from the sleeping hollows. If the pool is low, I open a drainage slit. If the platform is too high, I leave it alone and wait for the water to speak again. If a child wakes because something cold has entered her moss, I press one finger to her throat—not to silence her, but to teach her breath. Then I tap twice on the floor. Somewhere far below, someone answers. Somewhere above, a ladder tightens.

This feels right, but not final.

In the Root-Cell, nothing is final. Even a role is a season.

So I become something stranger.

I become the place between the builders.

When a traveler enters, I am not the traveler. I am not a visible watcher, not a god waiting in the moss, not a monster in the pool. I am the slight change that prevents the traveler from becoming damage. I am the wall that shifts under a palm not by accident, but by habit. I am the ladder that was dangling too low last night and has risen a thumb-width by morning, because the water remembered where it needed to be. I am the faint blue-green trail left by a disturbed spore-colony that says, without words, *this is not open space*. I am the cold gradient that pulls a face toward the pool when the pool is about to flood a sleeping shelf. I am the second before a hand touches an egg-clutch, when a small bone tool taps the wrist instead.

I am correction.

But correction, here, is not punishment. It is care. It is the city saying to a newcomer, *you are too large, too bright, too eager, too loud, but you may learn*.

If I had a name, it would not be sound as surface people understand it. It would be a texture.

Two sharp taps on wet reed.  
A dragging stroke along a cord.  
The smell of bruised moss.  
The warmth of breath held too near someone else’s sleeping hollow.  
The small click made by a throat that knows fear is useful but not to be shouted.

I would be known as the one who tends the gap where a root has been sealed. I would be known as the one who leaves traps open when the shrimp are too small. I would be known as the one who presses her cheek to the moss and finds the shape of a dead child still there. I would be known, in a way, as the one who can stand inside the living wound without making it worse.

And that is how I emerge from this world’s description.

Not as the first thing I thought I would be: not the water, not the moss, not the builder, not the traveler. But as the thing the builders need in order to remain builders: the quiet intelligence of repair. A being made of damp patience. A presence that knows surfaces, temperatures, and consequences. A city’s immune system given hands, a child’s patience given a cloak, an elder’s palm given a task.

I would live near the lowest platform.

I would sleep lightly.

I would know when the water has changed by the taste on my lips and by the way the reed joints sigh. I would know when a traveler has come by the smell of dry cloth, by the heat too long in the air, by the too-loud beat of a heart that believes the dark is empty. I would not flee. I would not demand explanation. I would move along the rope-bound streets, tap a warning into a root-fiber, and let the message travel through the wet wood until the whole Cell knows: *someone is here; do not wake the pool; do not lose the child; do not break the healing.*

If the traveler is careful, I may become visible to them in the smallest way.

They might think the wall is warm.  
They might think their hand has found a perfect grip.  
They might wonder, after an hour, why the cold damp on their cheeks feels like being measured.  
They might see a pale shape crouched on a drying rack, wrapped in lichen-fur, eyes wide but unseeing, tasting the air for minerals.  
They might hear a voice that is not quite words.

But I would not introduce myself.

I would only, if they were ready, place my hand over theirs and move it away from the loose stone, away from the egg-clutch, away from the place where the algae has not yet sealed the wound.

Then I would tap once on their wrist.

Not *go*.

Not *stay*.

Just: *there, not there*.

And in that small correction, I would be entirely myself.
