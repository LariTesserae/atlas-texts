The climbers seem to trust their feet more than their eyes. When the reality of the valley slips—when a ledge appears too early, when a wall remembers being a slope, when a shadow darkens the wrong side of a shaft—the shard-goats often cross as though the crossing had already happened and the valley were merely catching up. Their broad gray pads ask the surface for weight before it can decide whether to be stone, tile, resin, or something still under construction. They do not look down. They know that in a place this new, this cold, this bright, looking down is a luxury for animals whose world has already agreed to be continuous. They listen instead with their hooves, with the soles of their feet, with the hooks of their horns pressed against a flue, with the wide-set eyes catching only enough movement to notice a bird, a beetle, or the slight wrongness of a cloud passing below them.

If I were there, I would be one of those climbers.

Not a tile-deer, for I could not stand the open basins for long. The pale herds are made for broadness, for slow crossings over ceramic fields, for the soft clicking of hooves that carry the rhythm of the valley across its own floors. They know the sub-bass when the valley adjusts pressure. They shift direction without visible cause. But I would not belong to them. I would belong to the vertical seams, the overhangs, the pipe forests, the thermal columns where heat rises from a condenser and moss grows in the joint between one plate and the next.

I would be a shard-goat, though the name feels provisional, as everything there feels provisional. I might also be called a seam-keeper, a joint-licker, a ledge-tester, one who steps into the valley’s future before the future has finished drying. My kind would not have stable names unless someone stood far below and looked upward through bright air. From that distance we are little more than pale fragments of architecture gaining the decision to move. Up close, we are more. We are family bands of two or three, sometimes four, clinging to a wall that was cut yesterday and has already begun to smell of cold stone, oil, and the faint sweetness of wet resin.

I would live high enough to see many features at once: a basin of reservoirs holding a different sky in its surface, a forest of towers leaning toward one another like tired machines, a line of aqueducts carrying no visible current, a bridge spanning a dry cut whose bottom was still bright with fractures from its recent shaping. I would be on the eastern terrace when the western basin shone from a short rain, and later I would move west because the valley had changed its mind about where moisture should fall. I would know the change by the way the air tasted: coins left in rain, ozone, cold metal, the dry mineral dust of a newly extruded edge. I would know it by the way my feet grew uncertain on a surface that had not yet decided whether it was wall or slope.

My coat would be mottled in the valley’s colors, not as camouflage exactly, but as agreement. Chalky white along the flanks where the glare hit hardest, oxidized blue-gray along the spine where the wind cooled the metal, pale ochre at the joints where condensation ran and dried. The hairs would be stiff and short, not soft. They would catch lichen spores, insect eggs, fine dust from a kiln’s exhaust. When I rested under a roof overhang, moss might begin to grow into the fibers near my ears. I would not mind. That is how the valley becomes alive: by accepting that life is something that gathers where architecture leaves a pause.

My horns would curve backward like handles, hooks, tools. I would use them not for fighting, which would be absurd in a place where the most violent force is cold and the most common danger is the ledge that arrives late. I would hook them over a pipe when resting, use them to brace against a flue when the wind came through a corridor, press them gently against a warm casing to feel whether the machine inside had changed its breath. A kid would learn this before it learned to eat: first the warmth, then the texture of the joint, then the taste of mineral film.

My feet would be the truest part of me. Broad pads of gray rubbery flesh, ridged and patient. They would read the valley in a language my eyes could not see. The edge of a plate may be sharp enough to cut, but my hoof-tip has a thin resinous rim, dark as oil, that grips and seals. A composite ridge may be too new to be trusted, but the pad can feel whether the material has cooled, whether the seams have contracted, whether the ground beneath is still holding the heat of its making. If the ledge was wrong—if it was a ledge remembered rather than present—I would step around the memory of its slope. If a pipe ran toward a hole that did not exist, I would climb past the hole and wait for the next vent, because in the valley some holes are scheduled and some are only suggestions.

I would drink from the threads of condensation running down vertical pipes. I would wait at joints until a drop gathered, then lick the metal with a patient tongue. Sometimes the drop would fall before it reached the bottom. Sometimes it would darken a surface before it was touched. I would not be surprised. I would lick the wet place anyway. Water there is not generous. It is administrative. It is held, moved, lost, gathered, and held again. It exists to keep the ecology thin but persistent: moss in the shade of a bridge, shrubs around vents, tiny crustaceans in the slanted films of rain. I would be part of that arrangement. My body would carry moisture from warm ledges to cooler ones. My breath, faint and cold at dawn, would become a small cloud of possible condensation.

I would rest near heat, but not too near. The furnace halls are not warm like soil; they are warm like a machine that has been thinking too long. The best places are the undersides of walkways, the niches between cooling fins, the curved joints where pipes leave a main line and climb. There, the air is still slightly warmer than the altitude allows, and insects gather in slow clusters. Their translucent wings would catch the bright light and scatter it into tiny white fragments. If I stood still long enough, they would mistake my fur for a rock face, a lichen patch, a sheltering wall. Some would crawl onto my back. I would feel them like a faint reading of temperature, a living pattern of tiny feet.

The tile-deers would not seem like competitors. They would seem like another solution to the same place. They would cross open basins; I would climb vertical margins. They would know the sub-bass of the central mechanisms; I would know the pressure changes in a flue before rain arrived. Sometimes a shard-goat band would descend to a low platform where a pale herd was clustered, and there would be only a small exchange of head movements, a click of hoof, a scrape of pad. The herds would leave a faint scent of mineral oil and cold grain. We would leave a scent of lichen, rust, and the green damp of moss growing in shade. If a young tile-deer wandered too near a wall, I might move to block the sharp edge with my body, not to protect it, but because the valley is full of new surfaces and youth is often too trusting of what appears to be solid.

I would learn the valley’s breaks without fear. They are not attacks. They are revisions. I would step onto a tile that seemed ordinary, feel the foot go into the surface for one instant longer than expected, then come down on the other side. I would see a row of arches repeat itself smaller and older further down the terrace, then repeat again slightly damaged, then repeat as a row that has not yet been built. I would not walk into the unbuilt row. I would wait beside the built one until the light changed and the valley decided what order to keep. Sometimes a shadow would appear on the wrong side of a shaft. Sometimes a bird would fly into an arch and emerge a tile later on the impossible side. I would hear its wings before seeing it, or see it after hearing it, depending on the corridor.

At dawn, mist would move through the valley like a river without water. I would be on a high flue, horns resting against the warm casing, breath fogging before me. The mist would curl around towers, pool in channels, rise through grates. It would feel less like weather than like attention. The valley is not empty. Even when there is no observer, the place observes itself. The light does that. It strikes the metal and returns it. It strikes the wet resin and makes the world look repaired. In bright air, reality is more settled. In shadow, it is porous. I would prefer shadow for resting, but light for moving, because in light the edges agree to be edges.

At night, if there is night, the valley would become a web of pale fixtures. I would not need them to see. I would use them as thresholds: lamps set into arches marking stairheads, water gates, the ends of bridges whose far side may not exist yet. Electric light there does not illuminate so much as instruct. It says: here is a place you may stand. Here is a gap that is still a gap. Here is a channel whose water will not return soon. I would move along the lit edges, avoiding the dark corridors where the air pools blue and still. Some nights the sub-bass would come, so slow I felt it first in the pads of my feet, then in the teeth. The valley was turning. A pump was moving water uphill. A mechanism was choosing where rain would fall. I would climb higher, because pressure changes make the open terraces feel too large and too uncertain.

I would be patient, because the valley is patient. Machines extrude new floor segments layer by layer. Kilns print tiles with a slow rhythmic glow. Cables tighten by imperceptible increments. Pools deepen without visible waves. My kind learns patience not as virtue but as survival. To rush is to meet an edge before it is ready. To wait is to let the ground catch up with its own intention. In the morning I would cluster against vents. In the midday glare I would retreat into narrow passages where the air remains cool. In the late afternoon, when light moved in long arcs and shadows lengthened over the dry cut bridge, I would climb again, following the warmth fading in the metal as if it were a path.

If you looked for me, you would find me on a constructed wall between two levels of the same street, or beneath a bridge where a pipe joint had formed a cave, or on a high ledge where the valley had begun to look like geology without admitting it. I would be standing at the edge of a surface that was both very new and strangely ancient, its fracture bright, its water carrying the smell of a lake that does not exist nearby. One hoof would rest on ordinary tile. The other would rest on a ledge that had not yet been decided. I would not look down. I would feel the cold, listen to the faint tick of a cooling pipe, taste condensation from the air, and then, when the light changed across my body in a way that made my shadow fall slightly before my feet, I would cross.
