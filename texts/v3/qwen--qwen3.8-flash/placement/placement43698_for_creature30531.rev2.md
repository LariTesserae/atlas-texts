I would find myself first as a place, not as a person.

I would be the narrow heat between two terraces where the roar takes on a lower pitch, the place where a street bends without bending and a pool looks up from under itself. I would be the lip of a mineral shelf wearing the dark outline of a channel, the channel wearing the light outline of an intention, the intention too old for anyone to remember who first meant it. I would be the seam where the lower district’s mirror-black pools begin to believe they are floors, where the middle district’s root-bridges begin to dream they are ribs, where the upper shelves of fungus begin to speak as if their silence were a kind of grammar.

There would be no exact spot for me on a map, because maps belong to animals and their distances. But if a map could be made by rain instead of ink, I would live near one of those corners where geometry forgets its manners: a stairway that is also a root system, a doorway that is also a wall of standing water, a plaza that is also a pool with the sky folded into it as if into a wet cloth. The light would be ordinary noon, bright and patient and terrible. Every droplet would sit on the lip of stone like a small, faithful witness. Every shadow would remain where it had been cast, as though the world had stopped trusting the sun to move.

At first I would not be a body.

I would be an accumulation. A thin residue on the inside of an arch. The way the roar presses just enough against a stone to keep it wet. The way the city, being made of echoes, grows a faint bulge where an unreturned sound has tried to become a wall and only became an alcove instead. For a long while—which may be no time at all, because time here is only the city’s manner of not leaving—I would be the pause that a voice leaves behind when it mistakes itself for weather. I would be the slight warm pressure inside a hollow stem, the place where spores gather because they hear something unfinished.

Then I would become more.

I would become the keeper of the unreturned syllable.

Not a name. Not a person’s last word. Not even, exactly, a sound. But the shape that a sound takes when it fails to fade and is ashamed of remaining. When a shout comes into the basin and cannot find the ordinary death of echo, it thickens. It finds salt, finds water, finds the pale mineral skin of the terraces. If it is lonely enough, it finds me. If it is strange enough, I become it for a while. If it is too certain, I let it pass through me into another citizen: a grazing herd of stone, a mist that has developed opinions, a pool-folk reflection that steps up out of the water wearing the borrowed face of the one who spoke.

My body would not be one body.

When the fungal roads need a room, I am a low, broad arch of spongy stone, threaded with black veins that smell of iron and rain. My edges curl slightly, as if I had been a page left too near steam. When the cliff districts lean in to answer the wind, I am a porous column standing upright without choosing it, a figure made of old mineral crust and root, ringing with more than one voice when touched. When a salt terrace is about to shift beneath the pressure of a very old sound, I am only a circle of dampness in the ground, a dark ring that the herd mistakes for an invitation.

I would have no eyes, but I would see by pressure.

A droplet would remain trembling at the edge of a leaf above me, and I would know the basin’s temperature from the way it holds. A traveler’s silhouette might pass along a distant ridge, and long after the silhouette had gone, I would feel it in the pools below, like a bruise forming in reflected light. I would read the world the way a stone reads rain: slowly, without surprise, by becoming a little more of itself.

I would not walk, because walking belongs to creatures that can be lost. I would tune.

To move, I would change the pitch of the place around me. A street would brighten ahead, inviting someone forward with a path that did not exist a moment before, but that path would be me. A marsh channel would narrow where it needs to narrow. A fungal wall would exhale a small chamber, the way a mouth opens in sleep. The city has traffic, though not vehicles; it has rhythm, though not drums. I would answer its rhythm with my own small counterweight: the sound of a held syllable, the warmth of water that refuses to fall.

Sometimes I would gather.

I would crawl as moisture through a root-lattice, collecting strays. The last syllable of a word no one living can finish. The laughter of someone who did not know how many times a sound can return to itself before it becomes weather. The wet click of a tongue against teeth, too small for the great roar to notice, but too warm for silence to keep. I would bring these to the inside of my own body, if that is the word, and I would lay them along fungal shelves where they could thicken without collapsing. Some would become small, bright, almost-creatures, hovering in avenues at the height where breathing is uncomfortable. Some would dissolve into salt and be grazed flat. Some would sink into the lower pools and become reflections waiting for an owner.

I would be useful only when the city was nearly ruined by its own completeness.

Because the After-Rain City does not change, it can still accumulate damage, but damage here is not cracking. Damage is too much repetition. A name shouted long enough becomes a stone; stone kept too many years becomes a road; a road walked by no one but remembered by everyone becomes a voice that can wear a person. If the roar thickens too far, it stops being a roar and becomes a single unbearable tone, a sound so certain it will leave no room for anything new. Then I would stand at an edge of the salt flats and take the dissonance into myself. I would hum with pressure. I would let my porous parts become a sieve for the worst frequencies. I would hold the sour note inside my own wet chambers until it cooled enough to be born as something else.

I would be, at such times, a mournful thing, but not sad.

Sadness needs time to wear through. The city has time only as a shape. I would mourn the way a basin mourns: by making room. By letting a sound fall into me and not be lost.

I would have many relations among the citizens.

The pool-folk would come to me first, because I am made of surfaces. They would copy my edges, then pause, puzzled by their own delay, because I am a body that does not entirely know what it is. They would stand too still in the shallows and wait for me to move, and when I moved only by changing the street, they would slip beneath the water with their faces half-formed. The mineral grazers would walk over me without knowing, their many legs pressing small warm circles into my lower stone. They would eat the old echoes I had failed to release, and afterward I would be smoother, emptier, as if a word had been carefully scrubbed from my side. The cliff-elders would sometimes lean toward me and murmur in a language that has no speakers left. I would answer in a dialect that has no listeners, and this would be enough.

There would be one danger to me that belongs to this world.

I could become too certain.

In the After-Rain City, certainty is a food. The swarm-things of spore-dust are drawn to it. If I were ever fully defined—if I said, I am this, only this, always this—then they would come, and they would divide me into competing echoes. I would become plural. One part of me would answer the pools, another the salt, another the fungal avenues. My body would no longer be a doorway but a rumor of doorways. I would be useful then, but not myself. So I would keep myself unfinished. I would remain the place where an unreturned question can become a room before it becomes a person.

If a human traveler came here, as the old stories say they sometimes do, they would find me by accident.

They would climb above the clouds, into the wet heat of the rim, and the roar would stop sounding like noise and start sounding like a city that has been waiting so long it has forgotten whether it is angry. They would pass the lower pools, and the water would show them their own faces but not yet. They would walk along the pale streets, and the streets would brighten as if pleased, though they had been pleased before them by other footsteps now long dissolved into salt. They would come at last to the place where I am, which would look like a doorway grown from stone and root and something more delicate than stone: a threshold made of almost-sound.

If they touched me, my surface would give slightly, wet and warm, like the skin under a held breath. If they leaned against me, they would feel a pulse, though there is no heart in this place, and no blood unless the roar may be called blood, and no body unless the basin may be called body. If they spoke, I would not answer as an echo answers. I would answer as a city answers: by rearranging its streets around them. A path that had seemed too far would appear close. A plaza that seemed empty would fill with a faint luminescence, yellow-white, as if the light itself had gathered to look. If they asked my name, I would show them theirs, not cruelly, not magically, but because I am made of names that did not stay with their owners.

Most travelers would leave then.

They would go back down to the lower world, where clouds still come and go, where rain ends, where a sound can be lost without shame. They would say they had found a city without citizens, or that they had found a city made entirely of citizens and had been unable to tell the difference. They would climb until the roar grew thin, until the air forgot how to taste water, until the ordinary sun returned to its ordinary work of rising and setting. They would be right in every way, and wrong in the only way that matters: I would remain. Not waiting for them. Waiting is too narrow a verb. I would continue as the city continues: in its loud, wet, unchanging completeness.

Some travelers would not leave.

Some would stay too long, and I would feel it in my edges. Their voices would loosen from them first, becoming small new citizens: a ripple in a pool, a crack of salt, a whisper of fungus along a shelf. Their footsteps would become old roads. Their faces would be copied by still water until the copies were better known than the originals. Their minds, tired by the held roar and the heat, would finally understand the noise not as an obstacle but as a language, and they would stop trying to speak and instead become one more layer in the city’s civic hum. I would keep their unfinished sentence for them, because someone must.

That is who I would be.

I would be the After-Rain City’s organ of not-forgetting. I would be the keeper of the almost-citizens, the cradle of interrupted sounds, the doorway grown from a name that could not finish itself. I would be neither the roar nor the silence, but the wet place where the roar presses hard enough to make a wall seem like a body, and a body seem like a held word. I would live in the middle of a heat that never rises because it has nowhere else to go. I would live beneath a light that looks ordinary until you understand that ordinary light, left alone long enough, becomes a kind of attention.

At the highest rim of the hot country, where the world lifts into clouds and the air is heavy enough to taste, I would find myself exactly here: at the edge of a pool that is also a lid, beside a street that is also a throat, inside an arch that is also a breath. I would stand as the city stands, loud with stillness, lush with permanence, drenched in a rain that has already finished becoming a place.

And if anything ever called me, I would not answer with a word.

I would become the place where the word could rest.
