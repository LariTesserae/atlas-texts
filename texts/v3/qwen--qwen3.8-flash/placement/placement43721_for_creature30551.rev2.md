I would find myself first as a heat in tile.

Before I had a name, I would be the warm spot on a north-facing wall at midmorning, where a lizard had chosen to lie and where the dew had not yet finished leaving. I would be the cool seam behind the tile, where condensation was gathering too quietly for anyone standing at a distance to hear. I would be the vibration under the floor, a pump thinking itself through iron and water. I would be the dry breath of a kiln wall after the evening fire. I would be the mineral taste of dust settling on the tongue when the wind came through the cable spans.

Then I would have hands.

Then a body would emerge, because bodies are useful in the Kilnsteps: a body can carry a bucket, press itself to a valve, climb a maintenance ladder, lift a tile, feed a dog, feel the subtle wrongness in a crack. I would be a person, but not a person alone. That would only be the outermost layer of me, the one a traveler might see first, the one the markets would accept as an answer.

You would find me on the fourth terrace below the wind-bell bridge, where the southern condenser pipe has been repaired so often that it looks less like machinery than like a vine that has learned to weep. The terrace is narrow enough that a single loaded goat can make people step aside, yet broad enough for a row of drying racks, a mossed retaining wall, a stair cut into old brick, and a small fig tree planted around the mouth of a root cellar as if it were a guardian spirit. The wall behind the fig is pale where children’s palms have polished it. Below that, a strip of tile remains rough because a channel runs there and the moss is allowed to grow in it. Above, cables hum in the wind. At certain hours, the whole place sounds as if a large animal is sleeping with one ear open.

This is where I would belong.

My household would not be called by my mother’s name or my father’s name alone, though both those names would appear somewhere in the records scratched into the cistern cover. My household would be called the Line of the Weeping Valve. In some courts I would be Moss-Hand. In the lower pump galleries I would be called She Who Knows When the Water Is Late, though this is not a title so much as a warning: if she is there, the water will have to answer. To children, I would be the adult who can tell them not to put their tongue on a certain rail because it is not only cold but also salty with old rust and the breath of the fog net beyond. To the bell-ringers, I would be the one who always asks whether the south cable is humming too high. To the goats, I would be the smell of grain and ash on a wool coat.

If someone asked who I was, I might answer:

I am a condenser-keeper.

I am a root-counselor, because I know which roots are holding a platform together and which are only pretending to be polite while they pry a drain open. I know when a fig is doing more work than its owner believes. I know when a vine has become shade infrastructure and when it has simply become too lazy to climb properly. I know where moss should be left to seal a joint and where it must be removed because it has swallowed a vent that needs air.

I am a tile-fitter, though I use the word loosely. A true tile-fitter can shape a new piece from compressed slag and clay until it fits the old mouth like a tongue after illness. I am not that fast, not yet, but I can hear the hollow place under a tile. I can tell by touch whether the mortar has become powder or whether it still remembers being fire.

I am a pump-listener. I press my forehead to the cool side of the housing and listen to the water climb somewhere beneath the orchard. I do not hear what a machine-shop person hears, only clicks and pressures. I hear habit. This pump has been here for two generations. It starts a half-beat late when the fog has been thick for two days. It sighs through the second valve in the morning. When the upper cistern is nearly full, its tone changes, becomes almost embarrassed, as if it knows it is running ahead of its duty. I am not surprised when people say machines have moods. In the Kilnsteps, a machine that lasts long enough becomes a relative. Its housing wears into the wall. Its heat feeds the vines. Its leaks become channels. Its failure leaves behind a trellis.

I am the person who walks the circuit before the sun is high enough to bleach the courts.

I carry a brush made of reed and wire for cleaning the condensation gutters without disturbing the beetles that live in the mineral film. I carry a cloth for wiping lamp glass before the moths gather too densely at dusk. I carry a small chisel and a pouch of resin for hairline cracks that have begun to drink rain. I carry a clay cup because many places here have water you can drink if you know which cistern is safe and which only tastes clean. I carry a spare bolt because the world is made of fittings, and the fitting is the first thing to fail when the terrace grows impatient.

My hands are rough in the specific way that says I have lived here long enough to stop apologizing for it. The fingers are stained near the thumb with verdigris. The palms are calloused where rope and rail have done their work. There is a small scar across one hand from a shard of glazed tile that flew when I struck too hard trying to free a drain plug. There is another from a goat, not out of malice, but because we had a disagreement about where a ramp was for. My eyes are sensitive to glare; I wear tinted glass often, and when I do not, I squint like a bird. My shoulders have learned the particular patience of carrying loads up stairs that were meant for water.

If you asked whether I was a woman, a man, a spirit, a ghost, or merely one of the Shelfborn, I would say those categories are too flat for this terrain. Here a self grows in layers.

At first you see a body moving among others.

Then you see the duties it holds.

Then you see how those duties are not placed upon the body but woven through it, the way moss is woven through wall.

Then, if you are patient, you see that the body is a local event in a much older made ground.

The Kilnstep Terraces do not merely contain a person. They compose them. The stair is my spine. The channel is my thirst. The fog net is my breath. The pump is my heart. The dust, once, was a mistake; now it is my skin, slowly becoming fertile paste. I am a person who has learned to live inside a machine so long that the machine has learned to be alive inside a person.

You would find me midmorning on the ramp where the old kiln stands with its mouth open to the sky. It is no longer a kiln. It has become a nursery for swifts, a wind-shield for a climbing bean, a shade for a compost heap, and a climbing frame for children who think themselves brave. Its brick is softened so much at the corners that the whole structure seems to have been chewed by weather and then politely spat back into shape. The children’s hands have made certain ledges smooth. A family of lizards lives on the eastern face. I do not disturb them. Their work is also my work: beetles, larvae, the small insects that gather around moisture in the joints.

I stop there because the wall has changed.

A tile near the base has lifted a finger’s width. Not much. Not enough for a stranger to notice. Enough for the wind to begin prying at it. Enough for the dew to run behind it at night and find a new path, one that will carry salt toward the mortar and weaken it from within. I kneel. I tap the tile once. It answers too hollowly. I press the edge of my chisel under it and feel the resistance of old repair, then the surrender of a joint ready to be renewed. I do not remove it in panic. Panic has no use here. I clean the dust from under it, check the channel beneath, move a small beetle aside with my thumb, and speak to the tile as one speaks to something that has served well and is tired.

There is a crack in the glass block behind it. I mark it with a smear of resin. I note the place in the way our family notes all things: not only in writing, but in the texture of the circuit. When I pass here next, my hand will expect the bump. When the children pass here, they will know not to place weight on the lower edge. When the rain comes suddenly, the water will find the path I prepared.

This is maintenance. This is prayer, if you have no other word for paying such close attention to survival.

By afternoon, when the upper terraces glitter too hard and the heat has nowhere to hide, I would be in the shaded court below the cistern. The ground here is warm but not burning. The air smells of wet brick, ash, and the faint sweetness of the fig that leans over the pump house. A dog with thick fur and one floppy ear lies against the cool side of a pipe. A herd of the broad-padded animals moves past in a low procession, their hooves clicking and scraping along the ramp, guided by a boy whose whistle is less a command than a negotiation with the wind. They are not grazing wild slopes. The wild slopes do not exist here. They walk a managed path, past orchards whose trees have been pruned into wind shapes and shade shapes, past drying racks, past rows of tubers in beds rich with compost and old mineral dust. Their dung will be carried down to the southern beds. The beds will become soil. The soil will become food. The food will become bodies. The bodies will become waste. The waste will become patience.

I sit with a bowl of dense grain bread and cheese that tastes faintly of smoke and stone. Someone passes and asks whether the lower fog net has begun to weep. I say not yet. I say it is still thirsty. They say this as if the net is a creature with a temper, because it is. The mesh panel has been replaced four times in our family’s keeping. The first one rotted too soon. The second was stretched poorly. The third held so well it became famous in three neighboring terraces. The fourth is the one we are on now: strong, but lazy at dawn, reluctant to release its dew unless the wind helps it. We have learned not to blame it. We have learned to wake it gently by shifting the collector channels into cooler shadow.

This is what I would be: an ear for the small refusals of the place.

A visitor might say I am a maintenance worker. That is true enough. But it does not explain why, when I pass a wall, I touch it as if greeting an elder. Why, when I hear a pump change rhythm, my body leans without conscious instruction. Why, when I see a patch of lichen, I think not only of beauty, but of drainage, thermal hold, joint stability, and the fact that a particular beetle prefers its underside at dusk. A visitor might say I am a person living in a landscape. But the landscape is too much person for that. The landscape is made of intention. It has been watched, repaired, fed, shaded, and trained for so long that it has acquired a memory. It has habits. It has preferences. It has forms of pain.

I am also that pain, when it is mine to feel.

If a channel is blocked, I feel the heat of the obstruction before I see it. If a drain is weeping too much, I feel the cold at the base of the terrace. If a crack is spreading through an old foundation, I do not see the crack yet, but I begin to dream of doors that will not close properly. In other places, people may say the land is dead and expect nothing from it but material. In the Kilnsteps, we cannot afford that belief. The land is too alive with made things. Every failure of attention returns to us as thirst, as dust, as loosened tile, as a stair that becomes slippery in fog, as a wall that suddenly sheds mineral paste when someone presses too hard with their heel.

I am not separate from the animals who have learned the circuits.

The lizards know which tiles hold warmth from the engine room and which cool first when the wind shifts. The birds know where to build so their droppings enrich the grain below. The dogs know which stair is safe when the upper ramp is slick and which hidden pipe leak can turn moss into a trap in the dark. The beetles know which film grows in the gutter. The moths know which lamp glass will hold scent. The goats know the sound of the evening bell. The children know which climbing frame bars are safe enough to trust with their whole weight, and which ones, though warm and inviting, carry the memory of an old load that should not have been allowed to settle.

I am made of all this knowing.

If you asked me what I am by birth, I would say a Shelfborn. If you asked what I am by work, I would say condenser-keeper, root-counselor, tile-woman, pump-listener, ash-spreadener, bell-auditor, moss-trainer, channel-reader. But these are only names for parts of a single continuous act: staying present enough that the place does not forget how to live.

At dusk, when the shadows stretch long and the warm walls begin to release the day, I climb the upper maintenance ring near the wind-bell bridge. The cable spans have started to hum. Some of the bells are set to catch low pressure; others are tuned to high wind. I move one, a heavy bronze thing shaped like a dried seed pod, because the air is cooling too fast and the tone would otherwise cut too sharply through the sleeping courts below. I listen. The sound settles differently. A few yards away, a man stops carrying a bundle of reed poles and nods. We do not speak. Some adjustments are so small that speech is unnecessary. The terrace itself is the conversation.

The bioluminescent moss in the lower stairs begins to glow faintly. It is not magic. It is a helper we have encouraged by keeping its beds cool, damp, and shaded in just the right way. It marks the path without flame. It saves oil, saves eyes, saves the children from stumbling. It is part of the night architecture. The lamps in the workshops shine through glass blocks, multiplying light deeper into the construction. Somewhere below, an old kiln port glows with the last breath of a fire. Insects gather. A bat takes the air between terraces. A bird calls down a shaft, and the answer comes from a different level, not because there are two birds, but because the architecture makes echoes into voices.

By full night, I am not a single body anymore, not really.

I am the condensation that gathers along the cold ceramic pipe. I am the drip that falls into the reservoir and makes a small circle in the dark. I am the warmth stored in the white wall, releasing itself into the sheltered garden. I am the smell of wet moss and animal hide and ash after rain. I am the pulse of the pump, slow and patient. I am the click of hooves on stone as the evening feed is finished. I am the child who sleeps in a room made warm by the pipe behind the wall, dreaming of a stair that is longer than it should be. I am the tile that will loosen and be noticed in the morning because the hand that knows it has begun to rest less deeply. I am the dust falling into a crack, becoming paste, becoming root-hold, becoming the next layer of made earth.

If you asked me where I would find myself in this world, I would say: in the seam.

If you asked who I would be, I would say: a person, because that is the form the place has chosen for my hands, my hunger, my duty, and my affection.

If you asked what I would be, I would say: not only a person.

I would be a living circuit.

I would be the place where a civilization has spent so long repairing the world that repair has become its second nature. I would be the boundary between the fired and the grown, not because one ends where the other begins, but because they have stopped being able to agree on the boundary. I would be the shelfborn body that carries buckets up stairs older than language. I would be the hand trained to know tile by warmth and crack by sound. I would be the mind that names a machine’s habit as family. I would be the root of a fig threading through brick, the beetle in the wet gutter, the lizard on the tile, the moth on the lamp, the dog sleeping in a patch of heat, the bell shifting in the wind, the condensation pipe weeping in the dark.

I would be the Kilnstep Terraces, awake enough to notice itself.
