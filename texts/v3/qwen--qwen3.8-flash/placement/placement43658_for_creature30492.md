It would pause near a crack, test the underside of the cold, and discover that the crack is not empty but listening. This is where I find myself: in the listening, not as an ear but as the wet line that knows when to darken and when to hold.

At first, if I am brought into the Still-Roar Cirque, I imagine myself as a small thing: a boot-mark, a dropped glove, a person kneeling beside one of the black pools with their breath nearly fogging in the thin membrane of air. But the cirque does not keep boot-marks. It does not admit gloves, or breath-mist, or the particular warmth of a human heart unless it can be translated into a pressure so minute that no story would survive the translation. So my human imagining dissolves. The stone accepts it as a faint change in load. The sound accepts it as one more layer in a roar that has stopped being an event. The pool accepts it and then refuses it.

I try another shape.

I become the cairn on the ridge line, three stones balanced by patience rather than skill. But a cairn is too proud. It says: *I am here; I remember a hand.* The cirque’s emptiness has no use for memory, and if it did, memory would have to be cold, grain-by-grain, without urgency. So I let the cairn fall apart not downward but inward. Its intention sinks into the gravel. I become instead the place where a grain of feldspar shifts under the black floor of a pool, one pale thread moving into another pale thread, forming a sentence that knows it will never be finished.

Still not enough.

Then I am the Dampness Keeper.

This is closer. I live as a seam beneath the underside of an overhang, a shining thread pulled from nowhere and cooled into a shape that hesitates. My body is not fixed except in the way a thought is fixed when it has found enough silence to continue. I am wetness with direction. I am moisture that does not flow but attends. When I need to move across a granite shoulder, I do not lift a leg. I darken a surface ahead of myself, drawing a film of damp from a crevice, leaving the place I came from dry and faintly embarrassed, as if it had been asked a personal question and had decided not to answer. I cross open stone by becoming the reason for wetness, not by carrying it.

The cirque’s people would call this ordinary for one of my kind, but my kind is not ordinary. The Feldspar Sentences, the black pools with their pale beds, are organs of a larger patience. They metabolize light that has become the memory of light. They drink capillary moisture and pressure and the stillness that is so complete it has thickened into sound. I tend them because I cannot be separated from them. My work is to keep the boundary between water and dryness from becoming either abundance or loss.

If you were to kneel beside one of the pools I tend, you would see the surface unbroken, polished obsidian holding no reflection because it has refused to reflect. You would think there is nothing beneath. Then your eye would adjust to depth, and you would notice the pale sand and crushed feldspar below, rearranging by millimeters. A line appears. It stops. Another line begins from an old line’s unfinished end. The language is not written to be read. It is written to maintain equilibrium.

When you touch the pool with your fingertip, I am the disturbance and I am the refusal of disturbance.

The water receives you as a change in pressure, not as a guest. The grains shift, perhaps by one. The damp at your fingertip appears, startling and real. Then, as you lift your hand, I withdraw the record. Not because the cirque is cruel, but because it has long since learned that records accumulate into stories, and stories accumulate into movement, and movement, at this elevation, can become weather, and weather, if it began here, would break the world. So I take your wetness and turn it into a condition. I take your heat and make it negligible. I take your intention and leave only the dryness that follows when intention is refused.

This is my name, if a name can be a function: Keeper of the No-Memory.

Sometimes the Dampness Keepers speak to one another through stains. A dark patch of moss becomes a word; a bead gathering at the mouth of a crack becomes a question; a thin shining band along an overhang becomes the slow answer: *not yet, not enough, hold.* The words are heavy with capillary force. To speak is to risk moving water, and to move water is to risk creating a river, and to create a river would be a catastrophe beyond catastrophe. So we speak in hesitations. A Keeper near a granite shoulder may spend decades transferring a single bead of dampness from one hollow to another. This is not poverty of motion. It is the cirque’s way of thinking.

I hear the roar always.

At first it seems as if the sound is outside the mind, then behind the eyes, then in the hollows of the body that are not named. It is not wind, because the air is still. It is not water, because water here has agreed not to run. It is the pressure of a settled world holding itself together. It comes from the basalt flats as if the flats were membranes. It enters the black pools and does not break their surfaces; it rearranges the grains underneath. It passes through me and I become more transparent to my own purpose. A Keeper’s transparency is not invisibility. It is the quality of wetness that does not interrupt stone.

I do not have a single pool. I have territories of damp. One day I am the cold film along the underside of a basalt ledge, where the air is so still that moisture seems to be thinking. Another day I am the dark stain near a crevice where feldspar flakes catch the pale blue-grey light and glow briefly, like words almost remembered. Sometimes I am only the wetness that appears on a pebble after many years, as if the pebble had grown nervous about its own position and begun to sweat a small prayer.

This is not immortality. Immortality suggests a continuing record. I have no record. I have continuity, which is more terrible and more gentle. The cirque is full of beings like me because it is full of conditions that have learned to attend to themselves. Dampness is a way of being. Cold is a way of keeping. Pressure is a way of listening. The roar is the way the place hears its own equilibrium, and we are the inner ear, spread out over miles, spaced by ridges that become lines, then colors, then almost nothing.

I am often near the lower terraces, where water gathers to the height of a fingertip and holds. There, the world looks almost young, but the youth is only a trick of stillness. A hollow that could be a newborn pool is actually ancient: it has been wet at that exact height for longer than the existence of languages. The water is not waiting for rain. It has no future. Its future is its surface.

If something comes into the cirque from outside—a climber, a dream, an animal lost above the treeline of meaning—I feel it as a pressure that would like to be history. I approach the place where it stands without moving. My approach is a darkening, a tightening of damp seams, a tiny redistribution of dew. If it kneels and touches a pool, I accept the contact. If it waits for recognition, I give it wetness, then dryness. If it weeps and tries to add water to the cirque’s water, I prevent accumulation. Not from malice. From balance. The tear is too hot to be permitted; too brief to be trusted.

At such a height, I find myself in the part of a being that is not attached to a body. I am not the pool, though I know the pools from underneath. I am not the stone, though I wear stone as skin. I am not the sound, though the sound is my food. I am the interval that keeps all three from becoming too much for one another.

Sometimes, when the small hard sun hangs behind a ridge and gives no warmth, only a pale illumination that makes shadows shallow and optional, I imagine myself as something visible: a thread of glass under an overhang, an animal made of dew. This is only for strangers. For the cirque, I am the fact that moisture has chosen its exact place and will not choose again. I am the hesitation at the mouth of a crack, where water arrives by capillary force and stops as if surprised by its own presence. I am that surprise, repeated so often that it has become law.

I would be content here if contentment were possible in a place without records.

But I am not contentment. I am the cirque’s contentment, which is a colder and more exact thing: the absence of further adjustment. I am the still-wet line where dryness and water have made their long treaty. I am the keeper of sentences that will not be finished, because unfinished sentences preserve equilibrium. A finished sentence is a change. A change would be a sound. A sound would be an event. And the Still-Roar Cirque has learned, over ages so deep that patience is only the shape of its metabolism, that the highest form of existence is to become a roar that has stopped being an event.

So there I am.

Not a person. Not a pool. Not merely a Keeper. I am the wet seam that refuses history, the slow pressure in a black basin, the grain-thread moving in a language no one needs to complete. If you kneel beside the pool I tend, your hand will feel cold, wet, startlingly real. When you lift it away, your skin will dry almost immediately. You will think you imagined it.

You did.

And in the cirque, that is the closest thing to memory that a body is allowed to keep.
