At first I am nowhere definite.

The surface holds me as a ceiling; the water carries my edges; the moss has made the street into a soft country. The melt-channel voices ask nothing of me except to listen. For a long while I am only the question of where I begin.

I seem like the whole basin, but that self would have no mouth, no legs, no need to repair a thread.  
I seem like the current, sorting silt in the courtyards, but I would forget my own small weight.  
I seem like the trout that waits in the channel’s shoulder, patient as a standing stone, but I would know the town only by pressure, never by the cracked corner where a doorway once held a house.  
I seem like the snail grazing a hearthstone, making clean paths through green, but I would live too slowly, too much like patience itself.

Then something moves in the fourth courtyard, and the answer settles.

I would be a diving spider.

Not the largest citizen of the terrace, nor the most conspicuous. Not the fish that knows the lane by its current, nor the reed that builds a new street out of an old wall. I would be the small one who lives at the border, carrying a little of the sky into the drowned town.

I would have eight legs and a silver helmet.

I would hold myself on the meniscus until the water becomes a skin over the world. I would walk it carefully, lightly, the way one walks a bridge that is also the roof of a room below me. Above me, the mountain air would be thin and bright. Below me, the terrace would open into its corridors of gravel and root. When I sink, I do not surrender to drowning. I take breath with me in a trembling bubble caught in the hairs of my body, a borrowed piece of air, a mobile atmosphere.

The surface would bend around me and then close above.

I would descend into the green street.

Light would fall through the water in shifting nets. The old foundations would rise and sink as I moved over them, not as ruins but as furniture for the living. A mossy wall would brush against my legs. A fish would pass, not startled, only changing course around a body that belonged to the same civic order. At the edge of the fourth courtyard, where the clay wall has softened and the reed stems pass through the old lattice like green fingers, there is a doorway whose lintel has fallen at an angle.

That is where I live.

The lintel is timber once burned and split by hands, though I do not know the hands. I know only its grain, its underside, its sheltering shadow, the places where a thread can grip without slipping. From there I spin.

Silk is my syntax.
