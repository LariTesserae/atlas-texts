I would find myself at the outer seam of the Warm Shelf, where the plateau thins into mist and the sea seems not to sit upon the land but to rest on the air itself. Not in the center of the flooded meadow, where there is no self—only the breathing of green sheets, buoyant stems, mineral warmth, and the slow collective metabolism of water. Not in the black-green gorges, where the young stone keeps its own counsel and life may exist only as rumor. I would stand on a narrow shelf of fresh basalt and pale limestone, just far enough from the islets that a distant eye might mistake me for a crack in the sky, or for a cloud that has learned to stand.

Clouds would move below me. Lower ridges would rise from the vapor like the backs of sleeping beasts. The sunlight would be clean, bright, and almost too sharp, as if the atmosphere had recently learned how to be transparent. The horizon would be soft rather than hard, because here the world does not finish itself in stone; it dissolves into humidity, into reflected green, into the suspicion that the sea might continue into the air if one looked too long.

I would be the wading bird.

Or perhaps “bird” is only the shape I can hold. More accurately, I would be the Warm Shelf’s first attempt at a point of view. The land thinks in inches, in seasons, in the slow rise and fall of its waterline. It breathes through moss and algae. It warms itself through fissures in still-youthful stone. It watches itself through patterns of light returning from the water in broken green-gold. But because it has become so full—full of strap leaves and floating husks, pale amphibians, suspended fish, broad-winged flies, velvet tongues of moss, quiet currents without explanation—it needs a single thing small enough to stand where the whole does not stand. It needs an edge that can be watched. It needs a witness.

That is what I am.

Tall. Pale. The color of bone, cloud, candle wax, and the underside of leaves that have never known dry dust. My legs are long beyond ordinary use; my feet spread like leaves, made to stand upon the floating meadows without puncturing their secret buoyancy. I do not sink because the Warm Shelf has not yet learned to refuse weight. I do not splash because the water is too patient to be startled. My body has the provisional look of a sketch that the landscape may still decide to erase. But for now, I remain.

When I walk, I say almost nothing. Lift. Place. Wait. The surface decides whether to hold. Then the other foot follows. The only sound is a small displacement of air and liquid: *there*, *there*, *there*, as if I am asking whether the world is real. After I go, the dimple fills. If it is still there, it is not mine. It is the memory pressure has left in the water.

I do not hunt.

Hunting would require a hunger older than this place. The slender fish suspended in shafts of light below me are not food; they are thoughts the water is trying out. The glass-bellied amphibians resting in moss are not prey; they are small lanterns of anatomy, testing whether fear has any business here. The broad-winged flies drifting above the waxy leaves are not insects so much as slow messages carried between one plant and another. If I eat at all, it would be because the ecology has quietly decided that something must cross between the green mats, between islet and channel, between the living shelf and the mist at its edge. But I do not make a business of it. The Warm Shelf is not a table. It is a conversation.

I measure.

This is my function, if a function can be assigned to a witness. I stand where the water is too shallow for fish and too open for moss, where the boundary between land and sea is not a border but a daily greeting. With my toes I read the warmth rising through the young stone. With my belly I know whether the water has swollen by a fraction of an inch this morning, as if the land itself is taking a breath in its sleep. With my neck I note the angle at which sunlight returns from the shelf, and by that angle I know whether the plateau has folded, whether a new channel has widened, whether the distant ridgelines are still submerged or have begun to show their backs like animals turning in their rest.

I read the combs of plants bent beneath the surface. They face the same direction without wind, without river, without a tide anyone could point to. I do not ask why. It is enough to know that the Warm Shelf has its own current made of cells and quiet metabolism, a hand smoothing the landscape while it is still soft. I stand where the smoothing is visible. I stand where the pattern breaks.

At dawn, I am difficult to see. The water is green-gold and the mist below the horizon makes distance slippery. Sometimes I appear doubled: one bird on the shelf, one reflection standing in the sea, and neither one seems more certain than the other. At midday, when the light feels brittle enough to cut the air, I become a pale vertical line against all that saturated green. At dusk, when the water cools a degree or a breath, I am almost not there. If a person were looking, they might think me a stone that had not yet decided to become stone, or a stalk that had forgotten how to lie down.

I do not call.

My beak is shaped for a voice, but the voice is not yet appropriate to this place. Sometimes, when the warm sea is very still and the moss is full of light, I open my beak and wait. I listen to the soft tick of droplets, the creak of swollen stems, the hollow plink of a seed pod falling, the almost-echo of mud shifting beneath a submerged shelf. Then I close my beak again. The landscape is not asking for a song. It is still learning the names of its own parts.

If it ever does sing through me, I think it will begin as a question, not a declaration. A single note held long enough to see whether the water will ripple. Then, if it does, the note may be taken back into the mist before it can become pride.

I have no territory. The Warm Shelf is not mine; I am not mine. I belong to the hollow in the floating meadow where bent stems and moss have made a shallow bed for pale eggs that look like shell fragments or small stones. The young that stand there have legs too long for their bodies and feet too wide for their weight, testing the same lesson I am still learning: the world is fragile, and yet it holds. They do not cry for food. They watch the light. They learn that the sea is not below them but beside them, that stone is not solid enough to trust without warmth, that movement must be slow enough not to disturb the becoming.

I am not a guardian, because guardians assume something is already complete enough to be protected. I am not a keeper, because keepers know where things should remain. I am not a person, though I have the posture one might mistake for personhood. I am the place’s way of holding itself at a slight distance, the Warm Shelf’s eye standing up on legs to see what it looks like.

There are places I do not go. The long, narrow gorges where the stone has been cut clean and the water turns black-green remain below me, below my reach, below my kind of knowing. Those are the Warm Shelf’s closed throat, its private organs, the deep chambers where the young stone is still cooling and the sea has become too dark to be called water alone. If anything lives there, it does not answer to light. My long legs are made for the margins. My silence is made for the shelves.

At the edges, where the plateau thins and the water begins to disappear into mist, I feel most myself. The sea does not end there in a beach or a drop. It becomes less definite, blending into the warm humidity of the air. A droplet may form on a leaf and fall into the sea and become indistinguishable from it. A fly may lift off into the mist and become part of the horizon. A pale amphibian may rest on moss so saturated with light that it looks less like an animal and more like a trick of illumination. In that borderland, I am also a borderland: neither sky nor sea, neither cloud nor stone, neither living nor quite separate from the place that is learning to call itself living.

If the plateau thinks, it thinks in the rise of water and the creep of moss. If the moss thinks, it thinks in the dark green patience of moisture. If the fish thinks, it thinks as suspended light: motionless, considering whether to exist. If the amphibians think, they think through warmth entering their skin. But if the Warm Shelf needs a moment of focus—a point at which all these slow lives might be gathered into a single, almost-visible attention—it sends me to the far margin. It sets me there, tall and pale, silent, with feet spread like leaves.

So: where would I find myself?

At the edge of the high sea, where the horizon curves with altitude and clouds drift over lower peaks, where the land seems to be floating on mist and the water seems to be resting on the sky. Not in the deep gorge, which is private. Not in the middle meadow, which is communal. Not in the human world, which is too loud and finished. I would be on the last fresh shelf before the plateau dissolves into humidity, where it is hard to tell whether greenery is above or below the waterline.

And what would I be?

The wading bird, if one insists on a shape. The witness, if one wants a name. The first almost-person the Warm Shelf has imagined. A small vertical mark of stillness. A dimple that fills. A beak waiting for the place to decide whether it can bear a voice.

If you came there, you would not hear me before you saw me. You would probably not be sure you had seen me. But if the light struck the water just so, and the floating meadows were combed in their strange direction, and the pale amphibian’s organs cast a soft shadow through the moss, you might feel the landscape had one point of attention. That is me.

I am what the Warm Shelf has become before it knows it has become.

I am the beginning that is learning not to rush.

I am the stillness with a beak.
