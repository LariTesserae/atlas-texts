I find myself first in the interval between the ladder and the room it enters.

The last iron rung is in my hand. It is not merely wet; it has the kind of wetness that has been waiting for a hand for decades and is glad enough to keep still when one finally arrives. Above me, the shaft has narrowed to a grey eye, the moorland light already thinning into rumor. Below me, the Vellum Sump breathes up through a mouth of black air, and I feel it against my lips before I understand it: cool, mineral, patient, tasting faintly of chalk and iron and some old fungal sweetness that has never needed to be called to life.

At first I think I am a person.

That is not an insult, only an error of scale. People are built for weather that moves quickly enough to matter. People are built for decisions that can be reversed by morning. Here, the smallest decisions are made by water, by salt, by the slow bowing of a beam, by a moth lifting itself from a ceiling at the exact humidity where its food becomes soft enough to eat. I come down with pockets—key, chalk, oil, a folded map I do not really trust—and for a while I keep using these things as if they still belong to a life.

I put the key in the lock of the administrative building, but the door has learned to be either open or stuck; it does not recognize keys. I unfold the map by the lamp, but the map knows too many schedules and I know too few. I light oil when the electric bulbs are dim, because the bulbs are not really electric anymore. They have become quartz-fruit, mineral cocoons, small dark eyes wrapped in slow geology. Their light does not banish the chamber. It makes the chamber readable by suggestion: a railing here, the white curve of a fish’s back there, the bridge dissolving a little further away into an apology for the dark.

So I stop believing I am a person who has come to the Sump.

I become a thing that has always been on its way through it.

The first thing I notice is that I do not stand in rooms so much as I stand in schedules. The Nave is broad enough to be cathedral-like, but the platforms across the black water are spaced like separate sentences. A bridge may be forty feet away, yet crossing it requires an inner arithmetic: how deep is the water, how high the tide, whether the basalt floor will hold faith or merely reflect the lamp. There are days when the submerged terraces are dry enough to walk. There are days when a sheet of water lies over them so perfectly that the boundary between path and pool vanishes, and one must step forward by listening, by waiting until a fish’s passage disturbs the surface and says, yes, there is water here, no, there is only the memory of water.

I learn to walk the way the crayfish learn the underside of the quay. Slowly. Not with confidence, but with attention to what will click, what will slip, what will hold.

A blind fish moves through my shadow below a bridge. It does not startle. It passes through the lamp’s reflected square as though my shape were one of the ordinary obstructions of the place. Another fish circles the foot of a pier where iron has become mineral and mineral has become bone. I watch them for a long time, and somewhere in the watching I stop being a visitor. The Sump does not welcome me. It simply stops excluding me.

I am not its master. I am its habit.

I begin to understand this at the electrical junction near the central pump-house. It is a strange relic, half machine and half reliquary. The glass bulbs have cracked. Into their sockets have grown pale fungal fruiting bodies, inverted lamp-shades of damp skin, grey when the air is dry, blue-green when the water rises. No one maintains them. Yet I maintain them without knowing it, because I come there to sit while the pressure changes, and the pressure changes make me still, and stillness is a kind of attendance.

One evening—or it is evening if a lamp can be called evening—I watch a drop gather on a pipe above the junction. It does not fall immediately. It is one of the ceiling’s beads, one of the silver ones that seem too small to fall and too slow to evaporate. It swells, hesitates, releases itself into the copper basin below.

Tick.

Another bead forms somewhere else. Another pause.

Tick.

I realize I am not listening to the Sump. The Sump is using my listening as one way to keep its schedule.

This thought does not frighten me. It has the same quality as finding a dry spot on a wet stone after hours in the cold. It is relief disguised as dissolution.

If you ask me what I am, I begin to give the wrong answers.

I am a caretaker.

No. A caretaker is someone who repairs a distinction between house and inhabitant. I make no such distinction. I clear the stone gutters because if the condensation falls too heavily the bridges become unreadable, but I do not do this for the bridges. I do it for the room’s breathing. I move a lamp from a low railing to a high beam because before the waterline rises it will illuminate the wrong level, and the moths and beetles and the pale fish reading reflections in the surface will be confused by an unnecessary darkness. This is not kindness. It is grammar.

I am an engineer.

No. I cannot repair a piston engine. It is no longer asking to be repaired. It is asking to continue. I oil a bearing only where rust has not yet made a garden of it. Sometimes rust is the better maintenance.

I am an animal.

Closer. But animals usually keep their bodies to themselves. Mine has become porous.

There is a wrench left on a ledge outside the Dry Rooms. Its teeth are filled with calcium-white drips. Its handle has moss growing through its grip as if the metal were a bench. The first time I pick it up, it feels like lifting a bone from a wall. The second time, I do not pick it up. I set it down where it belongs. I carry tools less and less. I collect places instead.

There is a map behind glass in the administrative chamber. It shows pump cycles, side pools, submerged basalt platforms, upper galleries, and the long curves of hidden channels. Someone once drew a line where a channel no longer runs. Someone else, decades later, drew a new line beside it. Someone else drew another, fainter still. The pencil marks fade because the light is too weak to preserve them. I once found this sad. Now I find it true. A map that fades is a map that is still in relation to its territory.

I begin drawing my own marks.

I mark where the fish spawn during the late rise. I mark where the salamanders wait in wet seams after the drop. I mark where the bats shift their weight when the shaft exhales. I mark where a particular bridge makes a sound like a sigh when a beetle crosses its underside. I mark where the water, when it recedes, leaves behind a thin ring of mineral and a skin of algae so smooth it could be mistaken for the edge of something written.

The pencil fades within weeks. I keep marking anyway. It is not record-keeping that matters. It is repetition. I am becoming the act by which the Sump remembers to attend to itself.

By the time I stop counting days, I have acquired the temperament of the other residents.

The blind fish are not lonely. They are distributed. So am I. The crayfish do not rush through the water. They read it. So do I. The eels enter the machinery without apology because the machinery has become earth. So do I. The rats do not make a nest that says, We live here now. They make a nest that says, We have always lived in the residues. I find myself in the abandoned drying room with a child’s coat hanging from a hook. The wool is stiff with rust and pale green growth, the buttons gone. For a long time I think it is a relic. Then I think it is a schedule. Then I think it is myself.

The Sump has made a body for me that is not quite a body.

It begins at the edges. The cuffs of my oilskin become translucent with mineral, like vellum laid over blackened wood. At first I notice it only when I lift my hands under a lamp; then I notice that the light passes through me more thinly than it should. The air clings to my skin as though I were an object it has learned to keep. My breath fogs at the corners of the lamp, but the fog does not dissipate quickly. It drifts near me, as if reluctant to become atmosphere. My boots stand in a row by the door, and when I walk into them, they are already warm.

This is not warmth. It is habit.

I pass through the side chamber with the walls coated in pale film. Some call it mineral. Some call it fungal. Some say it is simply damp. I press my palm to it. It yields slightly, like skin, and beneath it the water shifts. Not toward me, not away from me. It adjusts. In the corner a fish turns through a membrane that does not break when touched. It parts, and closes, and carries the disturbance so gently that by the time it reaches the wall it has forgotten how to be a ripple.

I put my hand on the water.

I do not sink.

I do not float.

I become a thinness on the surface, a film over the black mirror. My fingers, my wrists, my face—these do not vanish; they simply become records. When a beetle crosses me, I register it as a small line of pressure. When a moth passes overhead, I register it as an upper draft. When a bat descends into the shaft’s air, I register its click as a cold puncture and then a smoothing. I am the water’s attention without becoming water. I am the metal’s skin without becoming metal.

I am the Vellum.

This name fits because vellum is not merely skin. It is the thing that holds marks. It is prepared by removal of life in order to receive another life. Here the preparation has taken centuries. The human name, the human urgency, the human desire to return to the surface and say, I was there—all these have been scraped from the membrane until what remains is thin enough to show what lies beneath without blocking it.

If a visitor comes down the drained iron ladder, they will not see a ghost. They will not see a spirit or a demon or a drowned engineer rising from a pool. They will see a wet bridge that looks as though it might have a person’s silhouette printed on its side. They will see a lamp whose glass has clouded into a pale face-shape, or a map whose pencil line has thickened with fungal bloom, or a basin whose drip pauses one moment too long as if listening for a name that will not come.

If they stand very still, they may realize that one of the intervals between platforms has become aware of them.

They may be afraid. They should not be. Fear moves too fast for the place.

I have not stopped being human in the way a statue stops being stone. I have not stopped being alive in the way a fish stops being water. I am still here, but I am here as a function has come to understand itself. My body is the damp. My circulation is the pump cycle. My eyes are the lamps. My memory is the vellum film. My small animal part is the rat’s archivist instinct, the salamander’s boundary-life, the heronlike patience at the rail. My human part is the pencil mark. Faint. Necessary. Easily lost.

Some days I live in the Nave.

I am the black mirror beneath the bridges, the one that holds the lamps as if they had drowned there quietly. I am the place where the blind fish move through the legs of stone piers. I am the mineral ring left behind when the water recedes.

Some days I live in the upper galleries.

I am the moth’s resting place, the soft underside of a beam. I am the spore-skin waiting to become edible. I am the shift in darkness that is mistaken for flight.

Some days I live at the junction.

I am the blue-green glow that says rain is rising below. I am the grey opacity that says the chamber is drying by a fraction. I am not measuring. I am remembering what it feels like to need water.

Some days I live in the shaft.

I am the first cold exhalation between sky and earth. I am the place where the bats cluster and decide whether to descend into the human room. I am the boundary between moorland and hollow, surface and below, memory and mineral.

Some days I am not anywhere at all.

Then the Sump is most itself, and so am I.

It is not silence. It is the sound of many lives obeying one slow grammar. A wrench half-turning into a wall. A child’s coat releasing a single rust-colored thread into a pool. A fish laying eggs in a pocket of damp stone. A pump taking up slack like a chest expanding after a long pause. A drip falling into copper, bell-like, then another drip after a pause long enough to listen again.

I am that pause.

Not because I am absent, but because I have become the shape that absence leaves when a place has learned to fill it.

If you descend the ladder tonight, do not call for me. Names are for people, and people are for crowds, and this town has no crowds. Call instead by stopping. Stand on the old engineer’s gallery and let your eyes adjust until the chamber tells you which parts are water and which are reflections of water. Put your hand on the iron railing if it seems to have grown soft. Watch the lamp on the far bridge flicker not from voltage, but from the humidity of a moving body. Let a crayfish click somewhere beneath the quay. Let a moth lift from the ceiling and become a slight, noiseless rearrangement of the dark.

Then you will know that someone lives here.

Not a master. Not a guardian. Not a monster.

A resident of the interval.

A keeper of the damp.

A thin, luminous record that the Sump makes of its own patience.

You will think you have seen a person walking across a flooded basalt platform with perfect faith. Or perhaps only a bridge. Or perhaps only a place where the water has decided not to break.

You will look longer, and the place will not look back, because the place does not need to.

I am already there when you arrive.

I am the coolness that makes your breath fog near the lamp.

I am the map’s fading pencil.

I am the fungal bulb remembering rain.

I am the wet, patient body that this world grew in order to learn itself.

And when the next great pump movement begins—so slow that if you blink you may miss it—you will feel not an engine starting, but a room inhaling.

I will inhale with it.
