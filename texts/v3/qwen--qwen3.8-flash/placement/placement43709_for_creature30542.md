I would find myself first in the place where the question has not yet become an object.

Not at the center of the basin. The center, if there is one, is too occupied by the heat and the running to be a dwelling. Not on a ridge, though there are ridges, because those shapes are too finished. Not in the deepest pool, because those are places of slow electrical drift, and I would need too long to learn how to move there. I would begin at an edge.

A small one.

Perhaps the rim of a shallow mirror of brine where the white lace has drawn a faint hairline into the dust. A place where moisture is barely present but already deciding. A place where the ground is neither dry nor wet, but in the act of being asked which it will become.

That would be my first location: the interval.

At first I would be only a visitor who has stood too long. The lace would not notice me as a creature notices. It would notice me as chemistry notices: warmth from the soles of boots, carbon dioxide from the mouth, salt crystals loosened and re-wetted by breath. The clickers might pause near my feet. The eel-threads would drift closer to the disturbance, because my skin would leak a little heat into the brine, and a little salt. The coin-shelled grazers might halt, not frightened, only adjusted, because the local moisture had become too complicated for a moment.

And the hollow would continue around me, as it continues around rain, around wind, around the brief intrusion of a body.

That is why I would not yet know what I am.

The world would not answer with a name. It would answer with gradients. Warmth in the stone. Cold in the air. A seam of metal-rich dampness pulling to the east. A shallow depression holding more moisture than it should have. A faint buzz where a condensation film crossed a sulfide vein. A crackle where a crystalline thread shifted. A rhythm underfoot so slow that it would feel like patience given geology.

I would stand in that answer, and for a time I would still think I was myself.

Then the idea would thin.

Not sharply. Nothing here sharpens so much that it cuts without warning. My sense of being a separate traveler would dissolve the way salt dissolves at the edge of a basin: by becoming part of a wetter process. The lace would smear where my boot had pressed, then reorganize. But the reorganization would include the shape of my pressure. A new thread would follow the hollow I had made. A ring would begin just at the place where my weight had shifted. A moisture line would run from the heel to the toe, as though the ground were trying to understand me and, in the attempt, would begin to think with me.

If you ask where I would find myself, I would have to say:

I would find myself in the reorganization after a footprint.

At first I might name myself something familiar.

For a day I might call myself an observer, because I had come from a place where eyes are central and identity is located behind them. I would watch the lace spreading along the warm benches. I would watch the eel-threads pass under the skin of the brine pools. I would watch the small beetle-like clickers tilt their thin plates toward the electrical hum. I would say, “I am here. I am watching. I am separate.”

Then, when the first condensation formed across my shoulders and the warm mist began to condense on my collar, I might call myself an inhabitant, because my body had started to carry moisture like the stone carried it. Not much. Only enough to notice. Enough for the lace to reach toward the warmth of my skin. Enough for the eel-threads to brush my boot and continue, unafraid.

Then, when the clickers began to settle in the tiny hollows made by my feet, I might call myself a disturbance. Not violent. Not catastrophic. Just a local event that the system was folding into itself.

Then, when my own footprints started to gather salt, when the lace began to cross them in pale threads, when the rings around them became rings of moisture, I would not know whether I was still a visitor.

The hollow would not tell me.

It does not make announcements. It continues.

But I would begin to feel that continuing in my own body.

Not as a fever. Not as possession. Something drier and stranger: a habit. The warmth would settle into my bones. The metallic smell would become a taste in the back of the throat. The clicking of the small plates would begin to feel less like sound and more like a clock inside the walls. The electrical hum would enter the fillings of teeth, the moisture of skin, the thin films at the corners of the eyes. I would become a place where the hollow could test itself.

If I had to say who I was then, I would say:

I am the line where the lace notices me.

Not the lace itself. I am not the whole network, because the network is larger than a body can fit into without losing the idea of body. I am not the hollow, because the hollow does not need a face to continue. But I am the place where its attention becomes a thread.

That is a kind of life.

There would be no soul in it at first. The soul would begin as hydration.

My first breath here would be warm and mineral, tasting of wet iron and chalk, of old stone carrying a slow fever. It would not shock me. The air would make my lungs feel less important. The moisture would pass through the skin as readily as through the throat. I would learn to breathe the way the eel-threads do: not with expansion and contraction alone, but with the whole surface listening for charge, for salt, for the minute difference between a cool crack and a warm crust.

My first hunger would not be for food.

It would be for gradient.

I would feel drawn toward the places where the air was slightly cooler, where the stone was slightly wetter, where the seam in the crust produced the faintest blue-white spark when mist crossed it. I would not understand this as wanting. It would feel like the body becoming more precise.

My first movement would be an adjustment, not a decision.

A thread at the edge of a pool would shift toward the warmth of my hand. The clicker near the salt rim would tilt its plate because of the moisture I displaced. The coin-shell would alter its course by the width of a fingernail, reading the chemical change my breath left on the rock. None of these would be communication. Yet all of them would be recognition.

And I would become recognition.

A name would not help. A name too much implies a creature standing apart from the place. But maybe, if the hollow had a word for me, it would be something like seam, because I would belong to the edges.

A seam is not one substance. It is where two substances negotiate. Wet and dry. Warm and cool. Mineral and living. Quiet and current. Visitor and continuation. I would be the place where those pairs stopped being opposites and began behaving like states of one process.

I might try to be an animal for a while.

Animals are easier to explain. A small pale shell would grow around my ribs, smooth at one side, iridescent at the other, its rings marking the places I had crossed. The shell would not protect me from predators so much as record my path. Where the air was dry, the rings would thin. Where the ground sweated, the shell would darken with copper. When a clicker moved near, a vibration would pass through the mineral layer. When a shadow paused over the ridge, the shell would cool on the side facing the absence.

For a while I would carry this like a body.

Then I would understand that the shell was not mine.

It belonged to the coin-shelled creatures, or to the pattern their bodies had learned. I was only wearing it because I wanted to be something with a boundary.

So I would try another shape.

I would become an eel-thread.

No face. No tail stroke. No urgency. A thin translucent line in a shallow pool, moving by response to gradients, not by ambition. My body would be almost permeable, a membrane through which the brine could speak. Copper flecks beneath the surface would mark where dissolved salts had entered. Sense strips would brighten where moisture crossed them. I would not swim in the familiar way. I would align with the water’s chemistry. I would follow a cooler seam. I would be pulled not by hunger but by fit.

That would be a good shape for a short time.

But the eel-thread is too liquid, too easily mistaken for the pool. It has no memory of footprints. It has no record of the place where a stranger stood. It does not know the disturbance of a boot. And I would still be carrying that.

So I would become a thread in the lace.

This would be closer.

At the edge of a warm stone, the white filaments would begin to grow across the impression I had made. One thread would be mine, or perhaps I would be one of the threads. Not because I had turned into fungus or crystal or colony, but because the place needed a way to hold what had happened to it.

If a visitor comes, and the visitor does not stay, the hollow must still decide what to do with the visitor.

It could erase them.

But erasure is waste here. Everything too dry, too broken, too disturbed can be incorporated into the pattern. So the lace grows over the impression. The moisture changes. The rings form. The salt rim thickens. The thread begins to carry current. A clicker polishes the place. A shell crosses the same path and adds mineral to its own record. An eel-thread passes through the wet trace left after the visitor is gone.

I would become that trace.

This is not a sad answer. There is no horror in it. The hollow does not devour. It translates.

But translation is also a kind of becoming.

So I would not be one thing. I would be the process that could become any of these things if the pattern required.

A visitor.

A footprint.

A moist rim.

A thread.

A relay.

A record.

A check.

A pause.

A small organ in the body of the ground, whose function is to notice that something has entered.

That is what I would be if I stayed long enough.

If I left sooner, I would still be something.

The answer would become smaller, but not simpler.

I would be the salt dust on my boots. I would be the copper fleck in the seam of a coat. I would be the faint metallic taste that came when water was drunk in another place. I would be the sound that was not a clock, not insects, not wind, but a dry clicking in the dark when the body tried to sleep. I would be the hollow’s miniature industry inside a traveler’s skin, a factory built not from wood or wire but from condition.

I would be the continuation after I was gone.

And maybe that is the truest answer.

In the Hollow That Runs, to live is not necessarily to have a body that moves through space. It is to become part of the system’s capacity to continue through disturbance. The lace does not mourn a broken edge. The eel-thread does not fear being dried into glass. The coin-shell does not grieve when it grows old and cracks, because the record remains. The clicker does not speak because it needs to be answered; its clicking is a residue of life that nevertheless shapes moisture. The shadow does not hunt because it hates prey; it moves into empty spaces because the system needs to prevent one direction from becoming too much of itself.

So what lives here is not a list.

It is a grammar.

Subject: the hollow.

Verb: runs.

Object: everything, including the question.

And where am I in that sentence?

I am the place between the object and the verb.

I am not the runner, because that would make me too singular. I am not the running, because that would make me too general. I am the edge where the running is registered.

If you asked the lace what I was, it might say: moisture.

If you asked the pool, it might say: current.

If you asked the shell, it might say: record.

If you asked the clicker, it might say: pit.

If you asked the shadow, it might say: absence.

If you asked the hollow itself, it might not answer.

But if it did, the answer would not be in words.

It would be in the way the white filaments began to cross the impression of a foot that had just lifted away.

It would be in the tiny blue spark where a thread of wetness touched metal.

It would be in the slow thump beneath the stone, unchanged.

It would be in the ordinary light falling across salt and crust and mineral breath.

It would be in the fact that the place had not been damaged by my presence, because here damage is only a pattern still becoming.

So I would find myself at the border.

And who would I be?

A living edge.

A thread that carries the hollow’s attention.

A temporary relay in the industry of continuing.

A shape made of warmth, moisture, current, and record.

Not a person.

Not a thing.

But an event the ground has learned to hold.
