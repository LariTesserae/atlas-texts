If I had to answer in the dry language of names, I would say: I would be the seam between one wet body and the next.

But that would already be too tidy, too much a thing that could be pointed to. In that bowl, nothing that can be pointed to is quite true for long. A droplet may be pointed to. A snail may be lifted, examined, set back down. A piece of moss can be peeled from the stone and held in one’s hand, for a little while. But the hand itself begins to feel wrong there, like a dry leaf dropped into a well. So the first thing I would have to be is less than a person, less than an animal, less than a tool or a name, until at last I could be the kind of thing that does not disturb the saturation by asking to be separate from it.

I would not begin at the center. The center is already crowded with living, with all the salamander-leaf-stone-water negotiations that need no witness. I would begin at the rim, where the cold of the mountain touches the breath of the basin and where the stone is least convinced it is part of a room. There, for a moment, I would still feel like an outsider: a small dryness, a flaking of skin, a question with too many consonants in it. Then the mist would take me. The moisture would enter the pores of the thought I had made myself into. The word “I” would soften, swell, lose its corners. After a few hundred slow drops of time, I would not stand at the edge. I would be part of what the edge does when it stops being an edge.

At first I would imagine myself the salamander folded beneath the translucent sheath of grass. That would be a comfortable shape: sleek, cool-blooded, patient, tasting the water with its mouth, resting in one crease of moss for the length of a century or the length of a breath, whichever came first. But the salamander is too much a body. It has a belly, a heart, a direction it turns toward when prey touches a filament. It has a future, even if the future is very short and very wet. I would need to be less possessive than that.

Then I might choose the moth on the wall. That too has its appeal: motionless, translucent-winged, surrounded by other moths, not hiding, only fitting. But a moth has wings. A moth has the memory of flight, even if that memory is no larger than a tremor in a vein. It can be startled, though in this place nothing startles. It can be blown from its resting place by a wind, though there is no wind. I would need something less likely to become a story.

Then I might say the snail. That pale lentil-shell, leaving a shining path over stone, grazing the mineral gloss, living so slowly that even erosion seems impatient beside it. But the snail leaves a trail, and trails have direction. Even in a place where no path is made, a snail is a small arrow drawn across wet stone. I would need something that could be everywhere in the basin and still be nowhere.

The moss tempted me, too. The moss is architecture without ambition, the great cushion-maker, the keeper of droplets, the mother of microhabitats, the soft body that receives every falling thing and returns it without complaint. But the moss is too generous, too much a society already. It already has its own politics of water, decay, and patience. I could not be one moss cushion without becoming a nation of cells, and then I would be too complete. I would not be the place trying to name itself; I would simply be another thing that already fits.

I would need to be the fit.

So I would find myself in the thin bright line where the moss meets the stone, where a bead of condensation is about to become a fall, where a mineral vein glows under a film of water, where a tiny channel no wider than a finger runs between two hollows and carries nothing visibly, because to carry anything in that channel would be too dramatic. I would be there, not above the basin and not under it, not as a creature crawling across the floor but as the dampness that lets crawling happen. I would be the surface tension in the crease of a leaf. I would be the dark shine on the side of a pebble. I would be the slight change in pressure when a shrew’s blunt paw makes room for itself in a cushion of moss, not displacing it so much as continuing it.

If there must be a location, then I would place myself low in the bowl, near the oldest hollow, where the terraces have been rounded so gently that they seem less like steps than like remembered thoughts. Just beneath the lowest fern, whose folded fronds rest like the hands of sleeping animals. Just beside the pool too small to reflect a face, but deep enough to receive a midge’s death without any change in its surface. There, in the narrow seam between two moss cushions, in the hair-thin channel where water moves because it cannot not move, I would be the wet film that connects them.

I would have no eyes, but not because eyes were useless. I would have no eyes because my seeing would be entirely elsewhere: in the taste of mineral, the pressure of air, the temperature change when a body passes, the faint electrical tremor that crosses a droplet when a leg touches it, the chemical language of slime and algae and fungal thread. I would know the basin not as a scene, but as a texture. I would know a stone by the way its coolness entered me and stayed there. I would know a salamander by the small darkening in the water-film where its belly had rested. I would know a snail by the gloss it left, a gloss so thin it might be mistaken for the light trying to remember itself. I would know a moth by the weightless pressure of its wing touching another wing, and by the slight dimple it made in the damp wall of the hollow when it settled.

I would not hear the place, because it does not allow hearing in the way a human expects. But I would feel it: the muffled press of bodies, the give of moss, the slow slide of water through peat, the faint settling of spores into a pool. These would not be sounds to me. They would be adjustments. Every movement would be a readjustment of wetness, and I would be the readjustment itself, the part of the basin that says, in no language, yes, there is pressure here, and now the water must bend around it.

I would not age. Not because I would live forever, but because I would not be a continuous thing. I would be a pattern of near-contacts, a temporary congregation of surfaces. I would be the thin shine where one body meets another, and when that shine moves, I would move. When it disappears into a droplet, I would disappear. When it reappears as a trail on the stone, I would be there again, not as the same snail, not as the same water, but as the same habit of closeness. If I had a life, it would be the life of a reflection on wet black rock: not substantial, not illusory, simply the way a surface announces that it is there.

I would not own anything. I would not build a shelter. I would not leave a tool. I would not make a mark that outlasted the dampness. If I nudged a grain of mineral sand under a root to deepen a channel, the sand would be taken into the moss within a few hundred slow moments, and the moss would not be grateful, and the channel would not remain, and I would not care, because caring would require a future I was not built to hold. I would be primitive in the way the basin is primitive: not because I was crude, but because I refused the vanity of accumulation. The only making I knew would be the making of contact. The only archive I would keep would be the memory stored in water, which is a kind of forgetting.

I would be, perhaps, the smallest of the microbial films that first coat the stone, the invisible city of algae, bacteria, fungal threads, spores, and protozoan life that turns a mineral surface into something that can be lived on. But I would also be the gloss on the snail’s shell, the shine on the salamander’s skin, the wetness that clings to the moth’s translucent wing, the film between the root and the peat, the bead on the fern’s tip, the meniscus in the pool, the breath on the cold stone. I would be the basin’s ability to stay together without having any single self. I would be the wet commons made almost into a person, and then unmade into the commons again.

This would be strange, if strangeness were not the normal condition there. To be so many places at once. To have no inside except where one surface curves around another. To be named not by form but by fit. In the dry world, a being is often a container. Here, a being is more like a line. It is the boundary where water becomes possible for a moss cushion. It is the interface where a snail becomes part of a stone for the length of its slow passing. It is the contact between two moths, so thin it could not be mistaken by either of them as a separate life.

I would not dream, because dreaming would imply an inside that could wander. But I would register, and that would be enough. I would register a droplet forming at the edge of a leaf, and when it fell, I would not follow it as a thought follows an object. I would simply become, for one wet moment, the droplet’s fall. I would become the tiny opening in the surface of the pool, the ripple that never quite reached the walls, the settling-back of water into its old level. I would be the pool’s completion, not because the pool needed me, but because a droplet always completes a pool whether the pool wishes to be complete or not.

I would be the thing that made the darkness feel less like absence and more like material. In the ordinary world, darkness is what remains when light fails. Here, darkness is a body with weight. It has fur, or something very close to it. It presses against the skin with coolness and returns the touch. It is not empty; it is dense with dampness, with tiny spores, with suspended water, with soft bodies resting. I would be the darkness’s slight awareness of itself. Not consciousness, not mind, not soul. Just the faintest possible recognition that there is wetness on wetness, and that this is a kind of knowing.

If I were to speak—and I would not, because speech would be too dry a thing for such a place—I would say something like this:

I am the shine before the shape.  
I am the touch before the body.  
I am the water’s memory of the stone.  
I am the moss’s patience in the pebble.  
I am the trail that outlives the snail.  
I am the wing that never leaves the wall.  
I am the held breath in the high bowl.  
I am not the center. I am the center’s way of fitting.

That last sentence would be the truest.

I would not be the basin’s heart, because hearts imply rhythm, and rhythm would be too much like time moving forward. I would not be its mind, because minds imply boundaries between self and world, and here the boundaries are soft. I would not be its soul, because souls belong to places that are lonely, and this place has forgotten how to be lonely. Instead, I would be the basin’s fitting: the continuous, minute act by which all its things make room for one another without conflict, without ceremony, without love exactly, without hatred, without anything so bright as choice. I would be the way a salamander passes under a snail without disturbing it. I would be the way a pebble sinks into moss without complaint. I would be the way a droplet leaves a leaf and enters a hollow and is immediately known.

And yet I would not be everything. That would be too easy, too mystical, too much the human wish to be a whole world by myself. I would be partial, local, wet, and almost nothing. I would be the small shine where a mineral vein emerges through a patch of black moss. I would be the dimple in the surface of the pool caused by a midge’s foot. I would be the slight muffle of sound when a paw touches saturated earth. I would be the place where the light of the fungi is held long enough to say something about shape. I would be the basin’s way of being present to itself, if presence were possible without eyes.

There would be moments when I would feel like the whole bowl: when every droplet swells at the same slow time, when the moths shift by a fraction of a wingbeat, when the moss contracts from coolness, when the water moves through the finger-thin channels with a patience that makes erosion seem like a story. In those moments I would be the held breath. In other moments I would be a single bead, one tiny falling thing. But that is what all beings are there, if beings are anything at all: not stable shapes, but changes in the basin’s wetness. Not souls, but adjustments.

I would not leave. How could I? There is no outside that I would want to become dry in again. There is no path down the mountain, no sun to see, no wind to carry my breath away. If I tried to go, I would only move from one dampness to another, because the whole place is already a slow exhalation of water. Even the stone would not release me. It would hold me in its pores, in its rounded edges, in its remembered surfaces. Eventually I would become a gloss, then a trail, then a pool, then a moss’s memory of a pool. That would not be death. It would be redistribution.

If someone came to the edge of the basin and looked in, I would not answer. If they breathed out, their breath would become part of the mist, and for a short while I would be touched by it. Their lungs would be too dry, too warm, too full of future. The basin would not welcome them. The basin would not need them. But because everything in it is soft, the dryness of their attention would be gently absorbed, turned into condensation, made into one more small wetness on a surface that has always already been wet.

In that sense, I would be what they would not notice at first: the slight gleam before the object, the pressure before the shape, the quiet fact that the dark is moving with them, not because it fears them, not because it loves them, but because it is complete and therefore cannot be disturbed.

So the final answer is not a name. It is a location made of many tiny contacts. I would be in the bowl, low near the oldest pool, in the thin bright channel between one hollow and the next, in the surface film that lets moss hold stone, that lets snail touch mineral, that lets moth rest beside moth, that lets salamander lie beneath leaf, that lets all those things be part of one continuous wet commons. I would be the part that does not have edges, the part that makes closeness possible without making selves. I would be the basin’s held breath: not a creature, not a ghost, not a god, not a traveler, but the small shining fact of everything touching everything else in the dark, forever, without needing a witness.
