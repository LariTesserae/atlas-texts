Some pipes are warm enough to make moss forget that it was ever only a plant and begin to feel like lining. Some pipes are cold enough that the dripping finds them first, beads along the seam, and moves toward the joints as if water were inspecting its own property. To the Combed, this is not weather alone. It is company. A pipe, a root, a flywheel, and a basin are each a kind of ear. You learn to speak to them not with names, but with attention.

If I were in the Hollowloom, I would be a **Dripwright**—a water-comber, root-tender, and loom-keeper folded into one small, wet, listening body.

You would find me in the third terrace down from the low factory, where a copper gutter from an old cistern runs into a root-woven grille, where the warm breath of an engine-house makes ferns unroll too fast, and where the dripping has been combed for so long that the stones are smooth as teeth. My home would not be a house alone. It would be a pocket of borrowed architecture: a loft behind a brass engine screen, bedded with shed moth-wing and cured basin-walker skin, roofed with a sheet of cultivated leaf stitched by fungal thread, reached by a stair worn low on one side because centuries of bare feet have leaned there while listening. Below the loft, a narrow trough would run under the floor, and every morning I would learn what the chamber had said during the night by the way the water filled it.

My body would be made for the place. Compact, low-set, broad through the shoulders and long through the fingers, with feet wide enough to spread their toes across slick stone and keep balance on a rim no surface person should trust. My skin would be grey-green, slick like a leaf kept too long in soft water, patched at the cheeks and wrists with pale lichen that glimmered faintly when the lamps dimmed. Under my palms, the fungal rind would be thick from work, peeled away on clean days, allowed to grow back as proof of service. My hair would be heavy with damp and with life: copper rings holding small moss bundles, seed-capsules that opened at the right humidity, luminous lichen at the temples, and often a thread-crow’s nest of wing-shreds tucked above one ear. I would blink often. I would listen with my face.

I would be called Dripwright because my work is to keep the Hollowloom’s combing true. Water-combers catch drops, but a Dripwright catches patterns. I would carry catch-leaves, a trough-spoon, a sampling vial, and a bone knife for trimming root hairs where a drip has learned the wrong road. I would move through the wet courts and engine alleys, touching walls with the backs of my fingers, reading temperature by the way condensation beads. A wall cold at the shoulder but warm at the base meant a leak behind the growth. A basin too sweet with mineral smell meant the filter roots were clogged. A cable that hummed at an unusual pitch meant a swarm of electric moths was gathering, or a vine had grown into a junction box and made a new circuit.

The loom would not be only a machine to me. It would be a second skeleton. Some nights I would climb into the brass-and-iron housing of an old belt-loom and sleep behind the frame, because the turning wheel warmed the damp into a fine mist around me. My job would be to let the belts remain wet enough not to crack, but dry enough not to rot; to feed insulation grafts to wires that had been bitten bare by young rootlings; to encourage the pale fungal rind to coat a dynamo shaft in just this thickness, enough to carry heat away but not enough to choke it. If a machine had to be repaired, I would not call it death or failure. I would call it “forgetting how to live,” and I would sing to it low, not because metal had ears, but because the moss growing on it did, and the moss would tighten or loosen its grip depending on my breath.

Animals would be co-workers. A blind basin-walker would pick a cistern clean, its soft legs tapping along the stone like a clock. Thread-crows would settle in my hair and eat stray spores. Small beetles with pale shells would gather at my shoulders, reading salt by the way I sweated. Fish would live in every pool, and the fish in the reservoir beneath my stairs would rise at dawn because they knew the drip from the copper gutter meant the water level had shifted. I would not shoo them away. I would move around them as one moves around furniture that breathes.

I would wear the usual things of the Combed, but stained according to my work: leaf-felt shell blue-grey at one shoulder where I leaned against a cistern, apron of cured basin-walker hide mapped with moss prints and tiny scorch-marks, sleeves of insect-wing cloth that caught lamp-glow and made me look half made of glass. At my hips and throat, water-signs: clay beads hollow enough to hold a single drop, copper pins curved to catch dew, reed tubes slung against the skin so I could taste moisture without breaking the rhythm of a repair. My shoes would be absent except when I walked the newly poured mineral channels, or when a pipe had begun to leak acid-water. Most of my life would be foot-slick stone, root-tread wood, and the warm wet breath of engines on my soles.

I would speak little, but not from pride. A shout could startle a flock, and a flock settling wrong could clog a vent, and a clogged vent could overheat a boiler, and an overheated boiler could boil a basin dry, and a dry basin could make the place forget itself for a few terrible minutes. So my words would be taps, breath-sounds, half-clicks, and the soft language of hands. Children would call me the one who listens to pipes. Elders would call me the one who keeps the water from becoming a rumor.

Some days I would be lonely, or I would think I was. The Hollowloom can feel empty where it is only between tides of animals. But the emptiness is also part of the system. A quiet court lets you hear the slow tightening of roots. It lets a lamp-gardener see which lichen is hungry, which beam is too dry, which shadow has begun to collect beetles. I would live inside those silences. They would be not absences but held breaths.

And perhaps that is the truth of me: I would not be a single person at all. I would be a place where the town and its living substrate have agreed to concentrate. A Dripwright is the Combed idea that maintenance can be a form of body. I would have my own name, yes—something soft and wet, beginning with a tapping consonant and ending like a drop falling into an empty basin. But the name would matter less than the function. If I were taken from the Hollowloom, I would be incomplete: too damp for dry air, too patient for bright clocks, too listening for a surface world that shouts to prove it exists.

So, if you ask where I would find myself in this world, I would say: in the hinge of a cistern and a loom, on the third terrace where copper turns to root, behind a grille that breathes, in the half-second after a drop has left the ceiling and before it knows which basin it has chosen.

And if you ask what I would be, I would answer: a Dripwright. A Combed person. A warm, moss-skinned animal woven into machinery. The small listening part of the Hollowloom that combs water, tends loom, reads pipes, and keeps the swallowed town from forgetting that it is alive.
