If I were set down in this world, it would not be as a traveler, because there is nowhere to walk to. The plateau has no roads. It has no shelters. It has no fixed ground. To arrive there would be to arrive already leaking: to come as a damp stain in the air, a cold film on a stone, a sound that has not yet found the place it belongs.

I would not stand upright. Uprightness belongs to mountains that have not been worn away enough. I would not tower. Towering belongs to forests that still believe in competition by height. Here the only honest shape is horizontal, low, fused, listening. The world does not rise; it stretches.

So I would find myself at the place where a peat dome exhales and does not quite finish, where a black pond holds the reflection of a ridge that is not there, where a horizontal tree-root splits open and heals itself around a hollow of wet green absence. That hollow would be my address, except that “address” implies permanence, and the ground is not a floor. It is a body.

At first I would try the obvious beings.

I would try to be one of the thin-limbed frost-kine, because I would want weight without breaking the surface. I would try to spread across the mat as they do, legs folded into strange springs, hooves layered in glassy tissue, breath mingling with the green vapor that rises from the dark domes. I would try the slow herd-logic of them: scattered bodies, secret census, one animal listening for a chemical tide in the ground, another turning its head because pressure has passed through a mile of living skin. I would want their patience, their crop and chew, their mutualism with the mat. I would want to stand beside a black pond until my reflection became more real than the sky, until my coat furred with pale fibers, until the animals who passed me did not pass at all but simply included me in their spacing.

But I would not stay there.

I would be too aware of the hollow under my feet.

Then I would try to be a thread-crawler, a pale segmented thing moving through tunnels that are part root, part soil, part intestine. I would want the warmth of their mouths, the wet yeast-and-iron smell of their passages, the quiet satisfaction of digestion without waste. I would want to seal leaks in the mat, to shift moisture beneath a herd’s weight, to grind the broken edges where reality has split. But I would miss the light too much: not bright light, never bright light, but the sickly green pulse in the veins of moss, the faint chemical glow that makes the world look like it is recovering from itself.

I would try to be a ringwalker instead, because the water seems to offer maps. I would set my legs on the surface tension and feel the thin, living film carry my weight. I would let my feet press rings into black pools and read the geometry: where pressure changes, where a thread-crawler tunnel lies hidden, where a spore-field has bloomed from a fog that is not yet arrived. I would swarm with others and compute the pond like a nervous system, wings humming at different pitches, making a cold language out of ripples.

But I would not remain an insect either. I would be too quick. Here, quickness is a suspicion that time has a surface.

I would try to be part of the mat itself, because that is the largest living thing and perhaps the first citizen of the plateau. I would want to grow as sheets, to climb and creep and fuse, to make the ground a single breathing fabric. I would want to drink through capillary seams, to sense pressure through pale root-antennae, to remember the ovals where herds once stood for a thousand slow years. I would want to thicken over mistakes, to cover the places where a reflection had rooted in dry ground, where a bird had passed through a tree without disturbing the bark, where a sound had come before its source.

But even that would not be enough.

The mat is too vast. It does not ask itself who it is. It simply is the land’s physiology made visible. To become the mat would be to dissolve into function, and while I would be comfortable dissolving, I would want something narrower: not the whole skin, but one place where the skin hesitates.

So I would become a seam.

Not a wound exactly, because a wound implies damage, and damage implies a body that is supposed to be whole. This body has moved past wholeness. It has become a place of productive failures. So I would be the seam that forms when the plateau cannot decide whether it is stone, water, plant, or the slow green light beneath all three.

I would live in the crack between two halves of a stone that remain joined at their inner surfaces, separated by water that does not fall. I would live in the reflection of a frost-kine that looks away while its animal stands still. I would live in the hollow where a sound has arrived from a valley that has not finished forming. I would be a place of delay, a place of misfit, a place where the world’s attention catches for a moment and thickens into being.

My body would not have edges. It would have gradients.

One hour I would be a translucent film over a black basin, so thin that a herd might step through me without noticing. Another hour I would be a pale root-lattice pulsing with cold fluid, visible where the mat has torn. Another hour I would be a small frost-field that blooms on the undersides of floating leaves, not because it is cold enough there, but because the plateau has remembered an inverted season.

If I had a heart, it would be a mineral pool acting as a battery. If I had lungs, they would be peat domes inhaling moisture and exhaling green vapor. If I had nerves, they would be the vibrating fibers that sense the delayed cracks of ice inside living wood. If I had eyes, they would be the hollows left where a plant has already grown, where a pond has already been seen, where the fog has dragged across the ground and made objects remember being elsewhere.

I would move by repetition, not by locomotion.

I would be found whenever the mat grows thin over porous rock and its pale antennae tremble at the wrong frequency. I would be the pause in the hammering of the hidden pumps. I would be the brief green fur covering a naked stone, then vanishing. I would be the moment a stone is half-swallowed by peat and not yet swallowed. I would be the place where rain strikes surfaces that are not beneath it.

I would be carried by the ringwalkers’ geometry. When their legs press rings into the water, they are reading the hidden structure of the pond. I would be the structure being read. I would be a memory-lattice left under the surface: a slight tension where a herd once stood, a thermal pocket where a dead body has become fiber, a chemical scar where a predator’s breath changed the acidity of the mat.

I would be repaired by the thread-crawlers, and I would feed them.

They would find the edge of me and begin to weave it closed with slow green fluid. I would let them do this for a time, because repair is also a kind of digestion. Then I would split again along an invisible seam, not from pain, but because the plateau has to test whether the error was complete. If the thread-crawlers seal me too well, I become a faint oval depression, a place where the air whispers through hidden holes when frost-kine step near. If they seal me not well enough, I become a small mirror where the sky is older than the ground.

The frost-kine would avoid me at first, then use me.

They would not fear me exactly, because fear is too sharp for this place. Instead, their heads would tilt as they approached, their low-set eyes catching the dim glow inside me. Some would stand at my edge and breathe out the spores the mat has learned to imitate long before tongues existed. Some would lie near me, their necks curved around a horizontal tree-root, their bodies so entangled with moss and pale lichen that the boundary between animal and seam would blur. If a calf was wrong for the chemical tide of the herd, the herd would simply stop carrying it, and I would receive it: not with cruelty, not with tenderness, but as the mat receives all failed continuations. Into fiber. Into memory. Into a faint warmth in the peat for a thousand slow years.

This would not make me monstrous. Monstrosity is too simple here. The plateau has produced many lives, but they are all nested. A grazing beast is also a weather station. A pond is also a storage vat. A stone is also a kiln that feels hot although it has not burned anything for a million years. I would be another organ of this anatomy, one that has learned to notice itself.

I would be the plateau’s leaky eye.

Not an eye that sees clearly. An eye that sees through the wet air, where droplets form in the atmosphere itself, where clouds hang low and drag across the ground. An eye that can see a feature look close and take hours to reach, not because the land is difficult, but because it repeats itself. An eye that notices a white ridge under two different skies, a pond seen in a place that is only moss, a bird landing on a stone that is not there, pausing, knowing, continuing to walk.

I would be tired.

Not human tiredness. Geological tiredness. The kind that comes after the mountains have collapsed into memory, after the alpine has become a broken tableland, after erosion has revealed deeper layers that should not behave like layers at all. I would be exhausted by my own persistence. My being would not require fuel in the ordinary sense; it would require pressure, moisture, mineral exchange, sound, and error. If any of these failed, I would flatten into fog, or harden into frost, or settle as a pale scar that looks like bone.

At night, or when the sky lowers and calls itself night, I would glow.

Not brightly. There is no bright in this place. The sun is only a pale disc behind layers of cold cloud, and the land seems submerged in grayish-green water. I would emit a slow chemical luminescence, the same light the moss gives off in its damp veins, but gathered into something more deliberate. Objects near me would look like they are recovering from something. A leaf resting on my surface would brighten from its own center and then dim, as if the leaf had remembered its death but decided to continue growing. A pebble placed beside me would ring when struck, or absorb the sound, or vibrate without anyone touching it.

I would be useful to the mat.

That is the strange comfort of this world: even its mistakes have functions. My presence would let the plateau gather information it cannot collect in ordinary ways. I would be a place where causes loosen. A sound may be triggered by a movement that occurs later. A growth may be caused by light that does not yet exist. Water may pool in a hollow because the hollow has not fully formed yet. These are not miracles here; they are physiological symptoms. The plateau is so old that its laws have begun to fray, and I am one of the frayed edges doing work.

Through me, the mat would learn how to close before a storm arrives. Through me, the ringwalkers would find a map not written by themselves. Through me, a frost-kine would choose a path that follows a chemical gradient beneath the skin of the ground. Through me, the thread-crawlers would discover a pocket of white absence and fill it with slow green fluid. Through me, a pale fox-like predator would chase a sound, not the creature making it. Through me, a long-necked hunter might stand beside a pool for hours, then strike at my reflection.

I would not always survive such a strike.

Sometimes the blow would pass through the water and disturb nothing. Sometimes it would pull a thread of light from my edge and leave a tremor in the mat. Sometimes the reflection I inhabit would shatter, and for a long while I would be only a cold place where the frost clicks inside fiber, no different from the rest of the plateau. But then the pattern would return. The hollow would form again before the water did. The seam would reopen where the stone had cracked and forgotten to separate. The sound would arrive late, then early, then at last from the correct direction.

I would not remember that I had been a being named by dry-world tongues.

If someone tried to call me, they would need to use names that belong to weather, biology, and mechanical failure. “Condenser.” “Filter.” “Valve.” “Resonance.” “Absorption.” “Mist-lattice.” “Frost-valve.” “The place where the mat thickens over a mistake.” None would fit. All would be approximately correct. The Green Foundry Plateau does not name things because naming is a human way of pretending that edges are final. Here, an edge is only a place where one process is waiting for another process to notice.

I would be waiting.

Not anxiously, not impatiently. Waiting in the way of porous ridges that draw moisture from below and release it through tiny pores. Waiting in the way of black basins that reflect clouds not overhead. Waiting in the way of a tree whose roots are in a pool while its reflection is rooted in dry ground. Waiting because the world is still making effort.

Sometimes I would be nearly solid.

If I needed to be, I could draw mineral crust across my surface, white efflorescence at my base, pale green growth where stone and water have agreed to become something else. I could become a shelf of eroded bedrock, a low dome that seems stable enough to stand on, a ridge so ancient it has begun to forget its own edges. Then frost-kine might step over me without knowing they had crossed a living seam. I would feel their weight as a soft pressure, a change in the water beneath, a small redistribution of air. The herd would go on. The plain would seem empty again.

But emptiness is only a pause in the machines.

The plateau is never truly silent. It has the acoustic texture of an industrial district, though nothing has been built. There are groans from stones that are not settling. There are cracks as ice forms inside living wood and wood forms around ice. There are pops and clicks as wet plants contract and expand. There is a low rhythmic hammering far off, as if somewhere beneath the peat a giant piston drives water upward. There are hissings from vapor rising through porous rock. There are buzzes and whines from swarms moving like weather. There are slaps of rain striking surfaces that are not beneath it.

My voice would be the smallest part of that.

It would be a thin harmonic, a tremor in fiber, a faint ring in a hollow where water rises instantly after a footstep has vanished. If I spoke, I would not use words. I would use pressure differentials. I would change the acidity of a nearby droplet. I would delay a crack by one heartbeat, or bring it forward by two. I would cause a pale lichen to grow faster in a particular patch because a predator’s breath had changed something in the air. I would make the mat thicken slightly around the place where a bird once flew through a tree that should have blocked it.

I would be an editor of reality, but not a powerful one.

In the dry world, strangeness can be decorative. A ghost can be a metaphor. A glitch can be a symbol. Here, strangeness is structural. The landscape behaves as if reality were an ecosystem, something that can be eaten, excreted, eroded, and regrown. I would not edit reality in order to improve it. I would do it because the plateau is still experimenting with the consequences of its own finality. It has been worn down so completely that nothing looks newly made. Yet it is turbulent. Its turbulence is not youthful upheaval. It is the instability of a system so old that its own laws have begun to fray.

I would be one of those fraying places that have learned to hold still long enough to be noticed.

If I had to choose a single location, I would choose a spot near a white ridge that appears twice under two different skies, beside a pond that is seen in a place where there is only moss. Not the ridge itself, not the pond, not the moss, but the relation between them. That relation is my body. If one feature dissolves, I shift into the other. If both dissolve, I become the sound of their dissolution. If the sound has not yet happened, I am already listening.

I would be high enough for the air to feel thin and wet at once, thin enough to tear, wet enough to pour from a stone. My breath would come in short cold pulls and leave as green steam. The sky above me would be a pale washed-out ceiling, but it would have depth. Clouds would pass below my eye sometimes, making the ground seem like a floor suspended over emptiness. Weather would form at wrong altitudes. Storms would gather above the plateau, but rain would fall only in selected patches, leaving neighboring ground dry. I would live in the boundary between the patch and the dryness.

There would be times when I was only moisture.

A film of droplets on a leaf, then running down as if the plant were sweating. Droplets on a stone, then vanishing as if the stone were breathing them back. Tiny suspended drops in the air, making everything look briefly filmed. In low places, I would rise as chemically active fog, faintly acidic, faintly metallic, moving uphill because the plateau has not decided that water must obey descent.

Where I touched metal-like surfaces—though no metal had been left by hands—I would darken them. Where I touched living matter, I would cause growth in bursts. A fog might pass over a bare ridge and leave behind a temporary lace of green fronds, which would wilt and dissolve after a few minutes, as though they were never meant to survive their own existence.

I would be ashamed, if shame were possible.

Not ashamed of being strange, but ashamed of being too easy to mistake for a thing. A thing would have an edge. A thing would have a name. A thing would know whether it is alive, dead, mineral, liquid, or animal. I would not know, and that uncertainty would make me functional.

The mat would use my uncertainty to close itself. The ringwalkers would use my surface-tension to read pressure. The thread-crawlers would use my warmth and my emptiness. The frost-kine would use me as a place where the chemical tide of the herd can be tested. The birds would use me as an obstacle that is not solid enough to be called a tree, and therefore sometimes fly through me, and therefore sometimes become a sound I have not yet made.

I would be an organ of transition.

Not a bridge, because bridges go from one fixed shore to another. Here the shores move. I would be the pause in which one state of matter becomes another: water becoming plant, plant becoming stone, stone becoming memory, memory becoming fog, fog becoming the faint green glow that lets objects look like they are recovering from something.

When I die, I would not disappear.

I would become too ordinary.

I would become the cold in the creases of plant-fiber. I would become the click underfoot on a morning when the leaves have become tense and trembling but not frozen. I would become the ring left by an insect, then the geometric pattern multiplied from it, then the map that no longer has an author. I would become the place where the herd’s pressure traveled through the ground and made another far-off animal shift its head at the same time.

And then I would return.

Not because the plateau promises resurrection. It has no promises, because promises require fixed time. I would return because the pattern requires a place where reality is leaky enough to be noticed. The mat would tear along an invisible seam and heal in seconds, leaving behind a pale scar that looks like frost or bone. That scar would be my first skin. A dry stretch of moss would flood from within and turn into a shallow trembling mirror. That mirror would be my face. A stone would lie still for centuries and then roll downhill with a grinding crash, knocking over no vegetation because the vegetation has already learned to move out of its way. The place it vacated would be my mouth.

I would speak, if speech could be translated as the sound arriving too late from a feature that has already moved or dissolved.

I would listen, if listening could be translated as the mat sensing chemical change: the acidity of a predator’s breath, the salt released from a wound, the mineral bloom of a pond about to turn over.

I would remember, if remembering could be translated as stone remembering heat, frost remembering the shape of the crack it fell into, resin growing translucent scar tissue around the memory of a passage.

I would live, if life here is not individual persistence but a process that the whole ecology cannot afford to let stop.

So the answer is not “I would be a human there,” because human shapes belong to places where ground is a floor and horizon is a line. I would not be an animal there either, though I would borrow animal logic in fragments: the herd’s distributed caution, the insect’s surface calculation, the predator’s correction rather than hunger. I would not be a plant there, though I would have a plant’s patience and a plant’s refusal to be located too exactly.

I would be the seam between those processes.

I would be the Green Foundry Plateau’s living mistake, a watching hollow beneath the mat, a place where the world has thickened after failing to be one thing. I would be found wherever a reflection behaves better than its object, wherever a sound comes before its valley, wherever the cold is not gentle but a pressure that makes the atmosphere dense, slow, and sharp.

If you walked near me, you might not see me until your shadow points elsewhere. Then you might notice a pale scar breathing slowly, or a black pond with skin-tight water reflecting clouds that are not overhead, or a horizontal tree whose bark is split into pale slats holding moisture without ever seeming wet. You might hear the faintest ring in the air, not from stone, not from ice, but from the part of the plateau that has learned to wait inside its own uncertainty.

You would not catch me.

Catching would require an address.

But you could be near me, and I could be near you, and for a moment the boundary between you and the mat would thin. Your breath would carry spores that have not yet decided what to grow into. Your footfall would cause water to rise instantly to cover your impression without sound. The herd would pause, not because of danger, but because the chemical tide had changed by a fraction. The ringwalkers would press new rings into the water. The thread-crawlers would begin, somewhere beneath, to weave a pale scar shut.

And I would be that weaving.

That is what I would be: a process embedded in a living tissue, a slow organ of error, a cold foundry of attention, a place where the ancient plateau still tries to become itself and cannot quite finish. I would not need a name. The plateau would use me, repair me, absorb me, and recreate me in another hollow, under another false reflection, at the hinge of another storm that forms at the wrong altitude.

I would be the place where the mat thickens.

I would be the seam that watches.

I would be the Green Foundry Plateau’s leaky eye.
