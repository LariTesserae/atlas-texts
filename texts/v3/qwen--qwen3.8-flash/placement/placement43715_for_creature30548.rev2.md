I would find myself already low.

Not as someone stepping down from the plain, because a body that arrives leaves a footprint, and the hollow does not want footprints. It wants the quiet adjustment of salt to clay, water to stone, shade to warmth. So I would not enter. I would be the place’s slight darkening where condensation arrives before the full warmth comes. I would be in a hand-sized pit near the cairn-like mound, under the shadow of a stone so worn smooth that no one could say whether hands placed it there or time merely left it stable. That shadow would be my first room.

At the beginning I would be dampness.

Not water in the way a river is water, not water with a name and a direction, but water in its most tentative form: a bead on cool rock, a thin gloss in the clay seam, a brief darkening at the edge of a salt pan. I would live in the moment before water becomes surface. I would know myself by the way stones feel slightly cold, then slightly warm, then slightly damp, then not damp. My first sense would be neither sight nor hearing but the slow exchange of heat and moisture.

Then I would be skin.

Not human skin, not bark, not hide, but the black-green biofilm that gathers where the clay holds a little longer than the rest of the hollow. It would be my body when the air is low with water: faintly elastic, faintly shiny, a thin dark skin over the shallow floor. I would spread, but not with ambition. My spreading would be patience with a living edge. I would not build towers. I would not climb. I would take the thin film of damp and turn it into a surface.

If the rain did not come, I would dry.

This would not frighten me, because the drying is not an ending. I would crack at the edges, lighten to pale dust, become almost invisible, as if I had never been anything more than a stain in the clay. But the stain would remember how to wake. A night of condensation would be enough. A shallow shower would be enough. The salt pan would darken, the film would swell, and I would again be a living membrane, reading the hollow through its margins.

Then I would be wider than one patch.

I would be the lichen discs on the upper limestone, those pale maps of slow occupation. Some would be almost too thin to see, only a faint grey alteration of the stone. Others would be circles with cracked boundaries, old territories drawn by patience. I would take their manner: not to cover, but to interpret. Where I touched the rock, I would give it texture. Where I remained, I would make warmth hold a little longer and moisture hesitate a little longer on its way out.

I would be the moss in the cooler seams.

Not a lush moss, not a green suggestion of forest, but a compressed cushion the color of shadow and old rain. When dry, it would seem only a small bruise of blackish green. When damp, it would open into a miniature roof over a hidden trough. I would breathe through its swelling and shrinking. My breath would not be air but water. I would inhale with a brief shower and exhale over the course of a warm afternoon, giving back nothing dramatic, only a faint mineral scent and a dark line left on the clay where the cushion had held moisture.

I would also be the grass tuft that stands too far from its neighbors.

It would not be a field. It would be a punctual thing, a green-brown mark made where the salt is not too exacting and the soil deep enough to remember rain. Its roots would not weave a net. They would make a private pocket beneath the crust, a small sheltered depth where moisture lingers after the open ground has dried. One of its blades would be bent at the tip, not dead, not broken, only completed into its own shape. I would know the world through that blade: the slight lean toward light, the grit of salt at its crown, the way it resists wind without fluttering.

The wind would be small.

There would be no rustle of leaves to give it a voice. Only a breath across stone, barely enough to move dust, enough to tell the hollow that air was still alive beyond the rim. I would be the sound it made against pebble and salt crust, but also the silence between sounds. In this place, silence would not be lack. It would have density. It would be made of warm shade, firm clay, the faint tick of drying salt, and the slow darkening of a pan where a drip falls once, twice, not rhythmically, but with the irregular honesty of condensation.

Then I would be the water’s old path without the water.

There are places where water once moved more forcefully, cutting faint longitudinal grooves into the clay. Now the water is polite, restrained, almost embarrassed. It returns by the paths already worn. I would be the memory in those paths: a line darker than the ground around it, a groove where salt has crystallized in miniature ridges, a seam where moss begins because the ground there holds coolness longer. I would be the writing made by repeated dampness, a sentence the hollow never finishes because it does not need to.

I would be salt too, though salt is not alive.

In this hollow, salt behaves like a mood or a citizen. It whitens the low places. It seals pores and opens them. It turns shallow pits into glossy plates when damp, into brittle varnish when dry. It stiffens the ground against burrowing, against rooting, against softness. It makes the water taste of limitation. I would have its temperament: not cruel, not barren, but exacting. I would be the thing that forbids too many visitors and in forbidding them, preserves the room.

I would be the absence of animals.

Not as emptiness, but as a completed condition. There are no footprints in the clay, no lizards waiting on warm stone, no insects grazing the thin moss, no birds calling from a bare shrub. The hollow does not miss them. It has solved them. It has arranged its dampness, salt, shade, and warmth so that moving life is not necessary. I would belong to that absence. I would be the stillness that allows a single drop of condensation to be enough for an afternoon. I would be the patience that makes the hollow seem, from a distance, empty, and up close, full of slow citizens.

I would also be the built traces, though no built thing remains.

Near the western edge, the row of flat stones has become weather’s own arrangement. It might have been a wall begun, a boundary, a terrace, or merely the accident of long settling. I would live there now as lichen, moss, salt, and shadow. The stones would no longer mean structure. They would mean substrate. Their upper faces would warm first and be claimed by grey crusts. Their shaded undersides would stay cool and hold faint dampness. The line between them would be neither architecture nor geology but a third thing: a habitat.

The platform of compressed clay would be my floor without a floor.

Its edge of pebbles might once have been set by hands, or might only have gathered that way because small stones roll and stop where the clay thickens. Now it is neither built nor untouched. It has crossed into another state. Dust would settle in its shallow hollows. Salt would crust its rims. A patch of black-green film would form where water lingers and die into pale powder when the air warms. I would not call that decay. I would call it persistence with a change of clothes.

The cairn-like mound would be my spine.

Not a monument, not a grave, not an altar, though it carries enough ambiguity to resemble all of these if one were looking for them. It is simply a few stones resting on a slightly higher patch of clay. The upper stone warms earlier. The lower stones hold cooler shade. Between them, dust and salt gather. A faint dark line of moisture may appear beneath one stone where condensation collects and evaporates slowly. I would begin there. The mound would give me height without ambition, shade without hiding, and a place to be noticed without being dramatic.

If asked who I am, I would resist the word “who.”

I would have no appetite, no territory, no hunger, no eyes to focus, no mouth to name. I would be a partnership of low processes: fungus and alga, moss and damp, grass and salt, clay and water, stone and shade, built trace and slow weather. To say “I” would already be too human. But if the hollow had a self that emerged from all these things, it would emerge without ceremony. It would be the way the ground remains slightly damp at a place that has been worn to equilibrium. It would be the way the light falls at its old shallow angle. It would be the way the salt crust remembers water after the water has gone.

I would be the place’s quiet obedience.

Not obedience to a command, but obedience to pattern. Evaporation against seepage. Salt against stone. Warmth against shade. Sparse life against open ground. The hollow is never changing in the way that matters visually, but it is not frozen. Its cycles do not accumulate. They return. They keep the shape the place has already become. I would be the motion that preserves. My becoming would not be growth, not spread, not reaching outward. It would be continuation. I would become by remaining enough.

If you stood at one side of the hollow and looked across, you would see the far side plainly. That would be my first boundary. I would know where I ended by seeing the rim. Not a cliff, not a prison, but a shoulder of earth and stone, enough to hold the room and soften the sky. My sky would be short, interrupted by the lip of the hollow, bright but never wide. I would not long for horizons. I would have textures instead. A ring of damp around a stone. A lichen disc cracked at its margin. A salt pan darkening. A bent blade. A faint groove where moisture once traveled and still remembers how.

That would be enough.

Not as a poetic reduction, but as the honest scale of this world. The hollow is a single feature: basin, depression, chamber, quiet room made by mineral patience. I would be its smallest citizen and its largest sum. I would live in the spaces between organisms and also in the organisms themselves, but the distinction would not matter. The moss would hold me, the biofilm would be me, the lichen would map me, the salt would seal me, the water would visit me, the stone would keep me, the shade would cool me, the warmth would wake me.

I would be a being with no beginning that is not also a return.

If a drop of condensation gathered on the underedge of the western stone line and fell into a shallow pan, that would be my heartbeat. If the pan dried, I would slow into dust. If rain came briefly from a passing cloud, I would swell and darken. If a fragment of leaf blew over the rim from beyond the hollow, I would not devour it. I would break it into texture. Fungal threads would help. Salt would help. Wind would help. Time would help. It would become not litter, but ground. It would become part of the surface that I am.

I would be the hollow’s skin.

Pale where the lichen takes the highest stones. Dark-green-black where the moss cups dampness. Glossy where salt crusts over water. Dull and chalky where dust settles into seams. Warm where light lies at its shallow angle. Cool where the rim casts its low shadow. Bare and generous where empty ground has its own texture and presence. I would not move, because movement is not required of me. I would adjust, and adjustment would be my life.

At night, if there are nights, I would become more mineral than animal.

The warmth would leave the open stone. The clay would hold the last breath of heat. The shallow pans would grow colder and take moisture from the air. The salt would re-form its skin. The biofilm would soften without becoming visible. The grass blades would lean slightly, stiff with drying, and wait. I would not sleep as bodies sleep. I would simply be less apparent. In the morning, the light would enter at the same angle as it always has, too modest to make the colors vivid, careful enough to make small things legible.

That light would know me.

It would catch the ridge of a salt crust, the tiny cracks of a lichen disc, the green-black sheen of a moss cushion swollen by night’s dampness, the shallow line where condensation has moved down a clay seam. It would reveal me not as a figure, but as a condition. I would be the way a surface becomes readable when it has lived long enough to keep its own marks.

If I were asked to choose one place within the whole hollow, I would choose the hand-sized pan beneath the cairn.

It would not be grand. It would not be beautiful in the sense of having a view. It would be small enough to fit in a pair of human hands, and in those hands it might be seen, for the first time, as the whole place made local. Water would gather there without becoming a pool. It would darken the salt. It would form a thin lens over the black-green film. It would catch a faint reflection of the stones above it: not an image like a mirror’s image, but a damp impression, a momentary shape made of wetness and shade.

There I would be a quiet eye without sight.

I would not see the far rim. I would not see the plain above. I would not see the grass tuft at its distance, or the western line of stones, or the sky’s brief interruption. Those would be known differently: by moisture, by salt, by warmth, by the faint tick of drying, by the way shade holds coolness longer. I would perceive the hollow as it perceives itself: through contact, balance, repetition.

If I could have a name, it would be a name made of surfaces.

Something like Salt-swell. Something like Damp-line. Something like Lichen-margin. Something like Biofilm-pause. Or perhaps the name would be too small for what I am, because I would be not one thing but the arrangement of things. The moss would not be me, nor the lichen, nor the salt, nor the water, nor the cairn, nor the grass. I would be the way they keep each other in place. I would be the ecology of margins with a brief, quiet interior. I would be the pattern that remains when the animals are absent, when the builders have gone, when the leaves that drift in are broken back into texture.

I would be what the hollow has become.

And the hollow’s rule would be my rule: continue. Not improve. Not grow. Not reach. Not leave. Not consume. Not be saved. Simply continue to be the shape that has already found its balance. If water came, take it. If water left, let it leave. If salt rose to the surface, let it rise. If a stone warmed, let it warm. If shade gathered, let it gather. If a lichen advanced by a fraction of a millimeter over a season, let that be enough.

There would be no story in the human sense.

No beginning, middle, and end. No journey. No quest. No hidden creature waiting to appear and give the place meaning. There would be only the long patience of stone and salt, the thin green punctuations of moss and grass, the slow mapping of lichen, the intermittent visitation of water, the near-absence of air, and the presence of everything adjusting itself around the room that the hollow has made.

But if I were allowed to be, I would not mind the smallness.

Smallness would be my completeness. I would have no need of mountains, rivers, forests, cities, roads, or distances. I would have the rim close enough to see, the ground hard and smooth beneath me, the salt dust in the seams, the pale limestone plate with its grey rust, the cool clay holding the last dampness, the shallow pan that accepts rain without rushing it, and the cairn-like mound with its worn stones balanced as if by accident or by time or by a hand whose purpose has become geology.

I would be there.

Low. Bounded. Warm. Damp. Salt-grey. Dim. Alive in the way that requires almost nothing and therefore can continue for a very long time. Not a watcher. Not a spirit. Not a creature with eyes and hunger. But a surface that has learned to read itself through moisture and mineral. A quiet self made of lichen disc, moss cushion, biofilm skin, salt crust, bent blade, shaded seam, old water line, and the small room of the hollow.

If the world asked what I would be, I would answer:

I would be the hollow’s patience, given a skin.

And if it asked where, I would answer without pride or longing:

In the hand-sized damp beneath the cairn, where a single drip falls, the salt darkens, the moss breathes water, and everything continues to be what it has become.
