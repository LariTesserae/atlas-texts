If I were to wake in this world, I would wake first in the listening.

Above me there would be a cold sea that had already used up most of its colors, and around me the dark would press with the patience of something that has never once needed to raise its voice. I would not know, at first, that the place was a room. To whatever small body I had become, the room would only be a weather of pressure, a taste of salt and iron, a rhythm in which the walls had been poured, bolted, welded, flooded, worn, and finally colonized. The sea would not be outside it. The sea would have made itself at home in every seam.

For a while I might be the most obvious thing there: the water itself.

I might think I was the current moving through the intake, the faint turbidity drifting down the shaft, the slow blue glow trapped under the corroded beams. But water, however alive it seems in its motion, is not quite a self. It has no hunger, no scar, no place it must return to when the tide changes. It fills every gap and is therefore not yet one of the room’s owners. So I would have to be a body that learned to live inside the water, a body that let the room pass over it and through it, a body that could read the pulse of a machine without understanding what a machine was.

I might try, at first, to be a shrimp.

I would hover beneath the grating where the light fell apart into pale bands and where the shadows were thick with rust flakes. My body would be nearly clear, a tiny glass thing, flicking away when a current stirred me. I would feed on what the room could not digest: suspended dust, broken shells, the soft decay of paint, the faint bitter bloom of old oil. I would hide in pipe bends when the turbine began its grinding complaint and drift upward when the pump pushed clean water through the intake. There would be no drama in being a shrimp. I would belong to the lattice, to the underside, to all the small pockets where the sea’s machinery forgot to be lethal. I would be almost invisible even to myself.

But if I stayed there, I would become too much a fragment of the room’s darkness. I would be shelter without direction. I would move, yes, but mostly as reaction.

So I might try to be a crab.

I would be broad-backed and colored like rust and old rope, or thin and fast, my legs bent at awkward angles from too many encounters with grating. Perhaps I would be the one near the sump, the one with a single claw too large and pale for its body, a claw it did not entirely trust. I would walk the sloped floor as though it were a desert of bolted plates, tapping across steel that had become geology, picking through the detritus the room shed: torn seals, flakes of shell, bits of seagrass, dead shrimp no one had eaten quickly enough. The sump would be my market and my trap, its smooth worn lip the place where the room remembered how to swallow.

As a crab, I would know the room’s surfaces with a seriousness no one else had. I would know the rounded heads of bolts, the soft scars of welds, the edges of floor plates where the century had polished everything. I would belong to the floor, and the floor would belong to me. But I would also be bound to it. I would know the room as weight and contact, not as passage. I would be maintenance, not movement.

I might then try to be one of the silver fish.

I would be small, almost invisible in the dim blue-green bands under the ceiling, a scrap of mirror caught in the machine’s breath. Near the intake I would gather with others who had learned that this drowned industrial chamber was safer than the open sea for a while, poorer than a reef, and therefore not crowded out. I would school without schooling, a loose agreement of bodies moving with the same hesitation, the same hunger. When the pump thudded, I would tighten. When the turbine grinds, I would scatter. I would use the valve housings for shelter, the gaps between catwalks for escape, the rush of intake current to hide the flick of my tail.

If I were a silver fish, I would know the room as flash and fear. I would know when the water brightened, when the cold came in, when the light made a thin lane of blue through the murk. I would know where the large dark one waited, not by seeing it so much as by feeling the empty water around it, the strange stillness that was not rest but ambush. I would know the room through motion and panic, through the sudden violence of a lunge that was over before the particles settled.

But I would not want to be fear alone. Fear is not a place. It is only what a body does when it has not yet made a way to live.

I might try, next, to be the large dark fish.

I would hang near the broken lumen panel, because the panel had become a landmark, a perch, a reason for stillness. I would be thick at the shoulders, armored with patience, mottled with the colors of shadow. One fin would be worn away where I had scraped against a bolt. I would watch the current not with longing but with the cold economy of appetite. I would let crabs cross below, let schools pass overhead, let the machine’s heartbeat thump through my ribs without seeming to notice. I would wait, because waiting was the only way to win in a room where motion made you visible and stillness made you possible.

As that fish, I would be the room’s own kind of owner. I would not need a kingdom. I would need only one blind angle, one careless body, one moment when the water changed faster than someone expected. I would live alone, but alone here would not be loneliness. It would be strategy.

Still, even this would be too fixed. A single ambush spot could become a coffin if the current shifted, if a piece of debris fell, if the dark fish’s stillness were mistaken by a crab as absence. I wanted to live in the room not by holding one place but by knowing the whole grammar of its holes.

Then I would understand, slowly, without ever having a thought shaped in language, that I was meant to be the eel.

Not the myth of an eel. Not a serpent, not a dragon, not a secret spirit dwelling in drowned steel. Just an eel. Old for its kind. Wrist-thick where its neck widened into the room’s narrow throat. Dark brown-grey, the color of wet metal and oil, its belly pale enough to disappear when it turned in the murk. A whitish streak along one side where a broken cable once fouled it and a current pulled it against a flange. Not ancient, only worn into competence. Scarred, only as scars are ordinary: evidence of survival rather than proof of character.

I would find myself in the gap between pipe supports, where the steel has worn into rounded channels and the water carries the memory of pressure in a way I can read along my skin. I would be in the broken inspection ports that for me are caves, in the sleeves that for me are streets, in the inverted pockets and lipped pipe collars and collapsed catwalks where a long, thin body can do what no other body can. I would belong not to one place but to the passages between places. I would be made of the room’s gaps.

At first I would not call it a room at all. It would be a continent of edges. The pipes would be mountains, the grating forests, the bolt heads islands, the sump a weather system pulling at the floor. I would know it by touch and pressure and taste. I would know it by the mineral salt, by the faint metallic bitterness of old machine oil, by the cool clarity that came when the intake opened and the outside world sent its food into my home. I would know it by the pump’s slow thud, which I would not hear exactly but would feel as a wave through the water, a signal as useful as light.

When the intake pressure rose, I would rest in the bend of a pipe mouth with my head toward the flow. I would not need to watch the fish. I would feel the school tighten somewhere above me. I would taste the shrimp scattering beneath the grating. I would sense the dark fish become stiller, which is the worst thing a predator can do. I would read the room like a weather map, but not in a sky. The sky would be here: the ceiling grid of beams, conduits, corroded channels, cracked glass panels admitting only thin blue-green light. The weather would be mechanical. The rain would be suspended particles falling in slow clouds.

When the current slackened and detritus began its descent toward the sump, I would slip through grating and pipe sleeves with the motion of a hand pulling a rope. I would not be afraid of the sharpness that once existed, because sharpness had long since been worn away. The room would be soft with century, with turbulence, with patient erosion. The metal would no longer bite me easily. The bolts would be domes. The weld seams would be faint scars. The edges of grating would be rounded lips. I would move through this old body as though through a familiar cave, except that the cave had been built.

There would be moments when I was almost visible: a curve of dark in the shadow beneath the catwalk, a tail disappearing into a pipe sleeve, a throat swallowing the intake’s cold water and releasing it again through the lower outlet. There would be moments when I was entirely hidden, curled in a place where the human engineering had collapsed into a pocket too irregular for any human purpose, a pocket that the sea had made fit for me.

I would hunt by patience because the room would teach me patience. Not the patience of waiting for a miracle, but the patience of knowing cycles. Twice a day, or what passed for day in this drowned chamber, the tide would shift. The machine would breathe in, and the water would cool, clear, and fill with drifting life. The fish would move toward the intake. The algae fronds would straighten. The turbine sound would deepen. Then the room would exhale, and the water would warm almost imperceptibly, turn murkier with stirred particles, and drift back out through the low exits. During that phase the crabs would move, the shrimp would scatter, the eels—me—would slip from one shelter to the next, following the food the room carried in and then carried away.

I would not be the owner of the chamber, and I would not pretend to be. The crabs would have the sump. The dark fish would have its ambush. The silver ones would have the open water near the intake, flashing their borrowed mirrors in the dim bands. The anemones on the cracked control panel would have their coin-small faith in the current’s passing. The bryozoans lacing the rail would have their pale calcium lace. The tube worms clinging to the pipe collars would have their red threads. The algae would have every unsmoothed lip, every groove, every seam where metal had stopped trying to remain perfect. The seagrass would have its crack of light, the room’s only pale ceremony of growth.

And the machine would have its strange continuity.

Somewhere beyond the wall, the pump would still turn. No one would be there to start it. It would run because it had been built to continue, because some distant source had not yet failed, because the logic of engineering had hardened into habit. Its thud would be the room’s heartbeat. Its groan would be its breath. The turbine shaft, half-seized and still moving, would be its grinding tooth. I would not know it as machine. I would know it as pressure, rhythm, signal. When it thudded, the silver fish would tighten. When the turbine ground, the shrimp would hide. When the pump pushed, the anemones would open. I would know, without knowing how I knew, when the water was coming from outside and when the room was breathing back out through the low exits.

I would be the eel because, in a place where every surface was human-made, my survival would depend on becoming fluent in the room’s negative spaces. I would not fight the structure. I would move through it. I would make it mine by fitting.

There would be days when I rested in the mouth of a wide pipe, my head angled into the flow, my scar pale along one flank like a seam that had never fully healed. I would feel a crab passing over the floor below, claws tapping steel, carrying its oversized claw close like a tool it half-trusted. I would feel the dark fish near the lumen panel, motionless enough to be mistaken for another shadow. I would feel the school drift above me, loose and glittering, never fully there. I would smell the algae on the intake screen, green and brown and black, pulled into filaments by the current. I would taste the salt and rust and oil of a world that had long since forgotten boots on its grating.

If a current came with too much cold, I would draw back. If a piece of debris moved, I would still. If a shrimp stumbled too near one of my hiding places, I would take it without drama. If a young fish broke from the school and passed too close to the gap where I waited, I would let hunger decide or let patience deny it. I would not hunt every opportunity. The room would be too large for greed. I would be a small thing in a made and water-worn cathedral, and I would understand, in the way a body understands a habitat, that to survive one must also not be consumed by motion.

I might, once, be caught.

Not in a net, not in a hook, not in the careless violence of a human hand. The human hand had long since gone. But maybe a fragment of cable, maybe a collapsing plate, maybe a sudden surge from the intake could pull me against a flange or trap my tail for a moment. I would learn the room’s dangers not as stories but as bruises. I would carry them on my skin. I would move on.

This is the strange part: I would be ordinary, and the room would still seem sacred.

Not because anything in it was blessed. Not because there was a spirit in the water. There was no ghost because no one had left one behind. There was no guardian because no one had appointed one. The sacredness would be entirely mundane: that a small drowned industrial chamber had become a world, that a pump still thudded and made rhythm where people had once worked, that fish moved through the space between pipes, that crabs scoured a sump of rust, that an eel learned to read pressure as a sailor once learned to read wind. The holiness would be the persistence of ordinary life inside ordinary engineering after its intended use had ended.

So where would I find myself?

In the gap. In the channel worn by steel and century. In the bend of pipe where the intake current softened into something a body could rest inside. In the shadow beneath the grating when the turbine began to grind. In the sump’s edge when the room exhaled and the detritus drifted down. In the broken inspection port that was, for me, a cave. In the long vertical shaft that rose toward a surface I might never visit, tiled in cracked ceramic, pale with weed, faintly bright where the light came down as if the sea above had finally forgotten most of its color and sent only its blue-green residue into the dark.

And what would I be?

I would be an eel.

I would be a wrist-thick body the color of wet metal and oil, with a scar along one side and a belly pale enough to vanish in murk. I would be patience shaped by current. I would be a long, thin reader of pressure. I would be the room’s face only in the sense that the room’s face was not the pump or the fish or the crab, but the movement through its edges. I would be the small, ordinary animal that turned the drowned machine into a geography of holes, that slipped through the human-made dark as if the dark had been waiting, without knowing it, to be lived in.

And if the room could be said to have a mind, it would not be the mind of a person. It would be the distributed mind of all these bodies: the eel’s passage, the school’s flash, the crab’s tapping, the shrimp’s scattering, the anemone’s opening, the dark fish’s stillness, the algae’s stubborn fringe, the seagrass bending toward its crack of light, the pump’s thud, the turbine’s grind, the tide’s slow inhalation and exhalation. I would be one thread of it. I would move through the chamber not as its ruler but as one of its learned habits.

I would know the room by its cold. I would know it by its taste of mineral and oil. I would know it by the sound of bubbles collapsing somewhere in an inverted pipe. I would know it by the soft rustle of water moving over grating, by the sudden rush through the drain channels, by the hollow shift of something heavy settling another few millimeters into the floor. I would know it by the way light failed before it reached me, leaving only bands of blue-green near the ceiling and deeper shadows below.

I would know it by pressure.

And in knowing pressure, I would know what to do next.

When the intake opened, I would move toward the current or away from it, depending on what the room asked. When the turbine groaned, I would tighten my body around a pipe curve and wait. When the dark fish lunged elsewhere, I would let the sudden noise cover my movement. When the school scattered, I would feel their tails like tiny hands beating against the grating. When the crab passed over the bolt-domes near the sump, I would watch from a gap with no expectation of malice, only appetite, only caution, only the ordinary need to continue.

I would be an eel, but more exactly I would be the room’s way of fitting into itself.

Not a dream. Not a haunting. Just an animal, ordinary and complete, living inside a drowned machine that had become, by long pressure and cold and sound and current, a habitat. The room would no longer be a room to me. It would be everything. The pipes would be mountains. The grating would be sky and forest both. The sump would be weather. The broken inspection ports would be caves. And my body would be the thing that knew all these passages by touch, by taste, by the slow reading of pressure along its side.

If asked where I was, I would answer, as a body can answer, by moving.

And if asked what I was, I would answer by turning once, pale belly flashing in the murk, and disappearing into the gap between the supports, into the place where the steel was worn smooth and the water was still deciding whether it had come from outside or from the heart of the machine itself.
