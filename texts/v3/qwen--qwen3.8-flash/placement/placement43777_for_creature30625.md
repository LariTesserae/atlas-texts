I would be carrying them, then: pale, shell-like things in a shallow dish of condensation, not as pets but as living weathervanes. They would rise and settle against the brine as if the air itself were too heavy to live in, and I would watch them with the same attention one gives to a vein in the stone. When they clustered near the rim of the dish, I would know the water in that room was changing. When they scattered without sound, I would already be stepping back from the floor.

I would find myself below the old plateau, in that pale and finished room of the earth, where the idea of a horizon has been worn smooth and replaced by a downward curve of light. I would not be above anything. That would take some getting used to. At first, perhaps, I would reach upward as if expecting a ceiling, a sky, a star, a window. The basin would not oblige. Its light would come from everywhere and nowhere: from salt veins, from mica sheets, from the damp skin of fungal mats, from the cool calcite roof that is not a roof but a horizon seen upside down. The light would feel old and patient. It would not burn. It would not warm the skin like sun. It would show me things without making demands, and after a while I would stop needing a source for it.

I would be one of the basin-born, or I would become one by being worn down into that shape. The people would not seem strange to me for long. Their bodies would begin to make sense as a kind of architecture, not a departure from personhood but a deepening of it. They had two arms and two legs, yes, but those limbs had learned to hold wet stone without slipping. They had faces, but those faces had been given ridges to shed condensation, pores to taste the air, lips for salt, teeth for crushing mats that tasted faintly of mineral sweetness and old cellar bread. They had backs broad enough to bear the low, dense weight of a world that had forgotten the sky.

I would wear a skin of pale filaments across my shoulders and forearms, not quite fur, not quite fungus, but something that belonged to the dampness. It would darken when moisture gathered and brighten when the stone began to shed salt. It would stand slightly when the air prepared for a change I could not yet see. At first I would be afraid of that. I would keep brushing at it as if it were dust or frost or mold. But the basin-born would only watch me with their wet, silvered eyes and press their hands to my shoulders, showing me that the down was already learning the room.

My ears would become low, wide shells of chitin, fitted close to my skull like plates cultivated from shed animals. I would not call them ornaments. I would call them tuning points. Sound in the basin would be too soft to rely on, but pressure would speak. A wall shedding salt would make a faint, dry pressure against my cheekbones. A fissure opening beneath a platform would send a ripple up through the soles of my feet. A pool draining through a hidden crack would give a small, almost imperceptible tremble in the air, like the memory of a heartbeat under a heavy blanket. I would learn to listen with my whole body. I would learn to move as if I were moving inside a held breath.

My name would not be a word.

At first I would resent that. I would try to name myself with sounds from a surface world, sounds that would die in the moist air like insects against glass. Someone would smile, or would not smile exactly, but the face would soften in that low-lidded way, and they would take my hand and trace a pattern across my palm: three taps near the heel, a slide toward the wrist, a pause, then a single press over the bone.

I would understand later that this was my name.

It would be called from across a platform by a flick of salt grains. It would be left on a lintel as a line of white dust. It would be tapped on a cistern wall when water beaded too thinly. Some names would be longer than they seemed. Some would require a whole hand to write on a flat surface before recognition would come. Mine would be short enough to carry, but not short enough to be mistaken for an alarm. It would mean, roughly, *the bead that stays at the lip*.

That would be fitting, because I would live by staying near edges.

Not the dangerous edges. Not the fresh crystal blooms that dropped shards in silent arcs, sharp as glass and soft as snow. Not the walls that sweated mineral dust too quickly. Not the blue-black places where the ground was still deciding whether to hold or open. I would live by the edges that could be read, the lips that could be watched, the small wet margins where the stone gave up its hidden water. I would become a reader of beading. A channel-mender. A keeper of slow tremors.

I would wake in the low hours, though waking would not be much of an event. There would be no sunrise, no rooster, no blue window brightening. I would wake because the air had cooled slightly, because my down had settled against my skin, because the faint yeast-sweet breath of fungal mats had thickened in the room. I would lift my head from the hollow worn smooth by my own body, and before I opened my eyes I would already know the room’s shape by the feel of its dampness.

The first task would be water.

Not because I was thirsty, or not only because I was thirsty, but because water would tell me whether the basin had changed its mind during the night. I would go to the nearest cistern, a round mouth of polished stone half-filled with pale condensation. I would press my palm flat against its outer face and feel for the cold movement beneath. I would tap once with a bone wedge, then twice, then listen to the sound it did not quite make. The stone would answer with a dull, softened tone, like cloth drawn across wood. If the water trembled too much, I would move back. If the beading gathered too sparsely along the lip, I would find the channel feeding it. If a small white pile had appeared nearby, left by someone else, I would stop and mark the spot with my finger. That mark would say: *something has opened.*

Then I would work.

Some days I would harvest fungal mats. This would not be like picking leaves from a plant. It would be closer to lifting the skin of a living stone. I would use an obsidian blade so fine it made almost no sound against the ground, and a bone wedge to ease the mat away from the basalt. I would test it by pressing one damp fingertip to its underside. Too wet: it would collapse into a soft paste and be lost to storage. Too dry: it would crack into flakes and taste only of dust. The right mat would flex, then darken faintly under my touch, pale white moving toward ochre, then toward a rusted mineral color as the air took its moisture. I would carry it to a drying frame in long strips or soft sheets, laying it out where the condensation could reach it but not drown it.

Other days I would work the channels. The town did not fight the moisture so much as invite it, correct it, redirect it. The channels would follow old cracks because the stone had already decided where weakness lived. I would clean them with a hooked copper needle, clearing salt blooms and shed filaments. I would widen one lip here, narrow another there, move a chitin plate to guide a flow. Sometimes a channel would refuse to stay open. The basin was always under hushed remodeling. A fissure might seal itself overnight under a film of crystal. A floor might buckle into a slow arch without warning. A shallow pool might swell in a hollow where no pool had been, then drain before noon, leaving behind a dark line that looked like a vein.

I would be patient with these things. That would be the first lesson and the last.

I would tend the grazing animals when my hands became too tired for cutting mats. They would be broad-backed, pale things with translucent shells and eyeless damp faces, moving in slow herds across the flats like the basin had learned to walk. I would not drive them. Driving would be an insult to their silence, and it would not work. Instead, I would lay shed chitin plates along the route they were likely to take. I would smear salt water in the low places. I would open or close the small gaps in a channel, not forcefully, not as command, but as suggestion. The grazers would follow the moisture and the bloom. Their wide mouths would press the ground as if they were reading it. They would know where to drink before I did.

I would watch their shells, too. When one lifted or cracked, I would mark the ground for someone else to clean before the animal stumbled. When one shed, I would collect the shell with care. Those shells were not trash. They were plates, pins, weights, roofing scales, ear-shields, cutting edges, cups, and sometimes the curved things children wore to learn stillness. A fallen shell could become a child’s first game: press it to the wall, feel whether the stone is sweating. It could become a name marker: set this shell by the door to mean *do not use that path today.*

The floating things would be my constant companions. They would not be pets, and they would not be gods, but they would be signs. I would carry them in shallow dishes from one room to another as part of the daily work. If they gathered near a cistern, I would check the air. If they drifted upward and vanished, I would look for a fresh crack. If they sank, I would wait. Sinking was not danger, exactly. It was a kind of listening. I would wait until the water remembered its place.

There would be predators in the basin, thin jointed things with too many legs and eyes like black dew. They would wait beside the waterholes. I would learn to feel them not by sight, for their movement would be too quiet, but by the change in the air around the pools. A grazer’s shell would stop trembling. A floating creature would sink. My down would darken. I would stop. That would be the most important lesson: not running. Running would be loud in the wrong way, a disruption of the stone’s attention, a disturbance of the water’s memory. I would stand still until the black dew had looked past me, until the too-many legs found another way to drink without drinking life.

I would be afraid sometimes. Fear in the basin would not be a shout. It would be a tightening in the palms, a sudden coolness behind the chitin ear-shields, a pressure against the flat ridges of my brows. I would swallow it as one swallows brine: sharply, slowly, with the mouth closed.

At the center of the settlement, around the broad black mirror of obsidian, the town would not gather so much as breathe. I would go there often, because that is where the oldest water came to the surface, bright and shallow, so clean it seemed to be a hole cut into the luminous ceiling. The obsidian flat would double the light below itself. Walking there would feel like moving across the skin of two skies. I would kneel at the edge and look down into the blackness, and the reflection would not be a face but a place: my broad shoulders, my pale down, my wet silver eyes, the chitin plates laced to my chest, the mica sheet over my ribs catching glow like a small house with no rooms.

That is where I would become myself most.

Not because the mirror would show me a hidden truth, but because the basin would be using me to look at itself.

I would not be entirely human. I would not be entirely stone. I would not be entirely animal, or fungus, or salt. I would be a negotiated thing, a pattern of habits that had gathered a body. If a surface person saw me, they might ask: is that a man, a woman, a child, a spirit, a mutant, a survivor? I would not answer because the question itself would be wrong. The basin did not produce fixed forms. It produced repetitions, adaptations, small continuous adjustments. A wall was made of split stone and bracing. A name was made of taps. A self would be made of attention.

I would not be able to imagine living anywhere else.

If I tried to think of grass, the word would come with no taste. If I thought of trees, the image would be hollow, like a structure without purpose. If I thought of a river, I would think of a line too confident, too open, too sure of its banks. Water here would have manners. It would bead, thread, seep, remember, forget, return. It would not rush. It would not flood without apology. It would appear as darker veins or bright lines just beneath the skin of the ground, vanishing when touched. I would know those lines by heart. I would know which hollows were only sleeping, which cracks were not cracks but mouths, which overhangs would sweat in the low hours.

I would know the rules as a body knows a worn handle.

One must not stand beneath a fresh crystal bloom.  
One must not trust a smooth floor unless it has held weight through a full remodeling.  
One must mark a new fissure with salt before the light changes.  
One must listen to water before drinking.  
One must not call with sound when a pattern will do.  
One must leave enough space between structures for the ground to move.  
One must not repair a wall by destroying it.  
One must ask the stone what it wants to become.

I would learn that the last rule was the truest. The town did not conquer the basin. It participated. Every channel followed a fault that already existed. Every dwelling leaned into a wall that had stopped shedding. Every platform rose where mineral pressure had already made a shelf. The settlement was old enough to have layers, but it was not a ruin. A ruin would be a failure. This was a continuing conversation, made in stone and salt and damp skin.

I would be a part of that conversation.

Perhaps I would be the one who noticed first. A slight tremor in a cistern. A salt bloom too symmetrical. A floating creature sinking beneath its own shell. A child’s name-pattern left beside an arch where no one had told it to go. I would kneel, press my fingertips to the edge of the channel, and feel the water’s wrongness. I would not speak. I would set three grains of salt in a small triangle and move a chitin plate across the floor’s seam. That would be enough. Others would see the mark, understand the name of the place, adjust the channel, shift the platform, bring the herd back, or take the children inside.

This would not feel heroic. It would feel like keeping a rhythm.

I would also be the one who carried, if necessary. If a platform rose too fast, if a newly opened fissure wept cold water and sealed again under crystal, if a house leaned toward a wall that had begun to shed, I would be part of the slow moving of stones, cords, mats, shells, and children. Not all movement here would be graceful. A house could become too close to a crack. A shelf could lift under a floor that should not have been built. A channel could drain a room into the dark. But the town would answer not by panic but by repositioning, by taking what can be saved and laying it where the stone has already learned to bear weight.

I would be one of those hands.

If I had to say what I was, I would not say *person*. I would say *listener*. I would say *bead at the lip*. I would say *a small, damp body that the basin uses to notice itself*. I would say *a name written in salt before the salt blooms*. I would say *the pause before water trembles*.

At first, perhaps, I would have looked for an animal body beneath all this. I would have wanted to know whether I was horse or worm, shell or crystal, grazer or predator. But the basin does not arrange itself by such clean surfaces. It grows. It dissolves. It reshapes. It takes the idea of “person” as a starting point and polishes it until it is no longer exactly human, but no less living for the change.

I would be human in the way a causeway is stone.

I would be animal in the way a salt flat is water.

I would be mineral in the way a name is attention.

The light would remain cool and white. The air would smell of wet chalk, iron, salt, and faint yeast. The silence would not be empty. It would be full enough to move through. I would live near the obsidian mirror, but not directly above it. I would live in the low structures around it, where the oldest channels meet, where water collects in bright hollows, where the town has learned to set its oldest rooms beside its most unstable places because some things are worth keeping close even when the ground is always becoming.

I would sleep with one hand near the channel and the other folded under the cheekbone plate. I would wake to pressure, not noise. I would check the beading before checking the fire-stones, if fire existed at all; here, flame would be only a small, polite guest, used when needed but never the master of light. I would step carefully onto grit so it whispered rather than rang. I would avoid the bright hollows where light collected too thickly, not because they were dangerous, but because one could mistake liquid brightness for water and lose the sense of footing.

I would watch the herds from a distance. I would know when the grazers were content: their shells would rest lightly on the ground, their wide mouths pressing with slow certainty, their translucent backs catching pale glow like polished bone. I would know when they were uneasy: their joints would stiffen, their shells would lift, their damp faces would turn toward a place no one else had noticed.

I would carry my dishes of floating creatures between rooms. I would set them on platforms, in niches, beside cisterns. I would not speak to them, because speech would be useless and because I would be learning not to disturb the quiet. But I would read them. They would be small weather, small prophecies, small living instruments. If I were to die, the basin would not say my name aloud. It would mark the place where my body had lain with a line of salt and, if the stone remained stable long enough, with a shallow channel redirected slightly outward.

I would be one of the many pale figures moving through the luminous emptiness. I would cast no black shadow, only soft, doubled ones. I would leave no footprint that did not also look like a natural seam. My hands would be calloused with mineral ridges. My down would darken with moisture. My chitin ear-shields would catch a change before the eyes did. I would press my tongue to the roof of my mouth and listen for water.

That would be the life.

I would find myself not beneath a sky, not above a landscape, not in a room, not in a valley, not in a ruin, not in a city, but in the space where all of those words had worn down into a single pale thing.
