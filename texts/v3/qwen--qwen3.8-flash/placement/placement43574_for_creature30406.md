They eventually stand still for so long that the city begins to use them as a sentence uses a pause: not as waste, not as silence, but as structure.

A glasswalker that remains too many dim days on one terrace becomes more than an animal. Wind carries dust against its legs. Fog leaves a white film along its shoulders. Salt climbs toward the joints as if seeking a place to set up. The small walkers begin to read it as a landmark. Other glasswalkers begin to align themselves to its spacing, their bodies fitting into the rhythm of its stillness as though it were one of the columns. Ash-moths settle near its crown when they mistake its reflection for a recently cracked pane. Eventually, if the hum does not change, the glasswalker becomes a threshold.

This is where I would find myself.

At first, I think I might be one of the six-legged walkers: pale, prismatic, floating over salt, reading pressure through the broad pads of its feet, tasting the minute static left by glass and dust. I could cross a field in small, silent steps and stop at the exact moment the city shifted, becoming invisible by refusing to separate itself from the ground. My trails would leave faint chalk-like lines across the salt, and other walkers would know that food lay near: the tiny crystalline blooms that opened where fog had passed. I would be small enough to survive by not seeming important.

Then I think perhaps I should be a glasswalker: tall, reflective, patient, standing between columns while the plateau breathed around me. I would feed not by hunger but by listening. I would press my folds against charged black growths and draw a pale shimmer from them. I would remain in the humming places where the basalt columns made their long, hollow music, waiting for the tone to change. When the tone did change, I would move with that strange, deliberate stride that made distances feel negotiable. I would stand among other glasswalkers in a courtyard, and our reflections would touch across the salt, and we would call to one another without calling, because in the Dry City sound is not the primary language of life. Pressure is. Resonance is. Light is.

Then I think of becoming an ash-moth, drifting alone through dry fogs on wings of dust, settling only where glass has recently cracked, flickering to listen. That would be a life made entirely of attention. I would know the city through fractures: not through what stands, but through what has just failed to continue standing. I would rise when the deep subterranean tone passed through the plateau, vanishing into the haze before the sound reached its upper edge. It would be beautiful, but too light. I would have too little persistence.

Or perhaps I would be the eyeless burrower, waiting beneath a salt plate, sensing the footsteps of the small walkers as pressure waves in the ground. My patience would be absolute. My action would come from below in a soft eruption, then withdraw. Nothing rushed. Nothing needed witness. I would be survival stripped down to vibration and timing. But no. That would make me only an appetite.

And if I were a traveler? If I were still shaped like something that came from outside the plateau, with lungs for ordinary air and eyes that longed for color, then the city would not harm me exactly, but it would not know what to do with me. I would keep expecting streets to lead somewhere in the human way, to arrive at purpose, to end in a door that opened into another room. The Dry City does not work that way. Its streets are patterns the wind returns to. Its plazas contract and expand according to rhythm. Its walls are ideas the ground has not fully rejected. A human traveler here would become a rumor of movement, a temporary disorder in the grammar of salt.

So after listening longer, I find that I am not exactly any of those.

I am a threshold-walker.

I would live in the hinge place between the Basalt Colonnades and the Salt Terraces, in a broad but uneven plaza where one side is formed by standing stone and the other by a ridge of pale mineral dust. There would be a shallow circular depression nearby, where dust collected after every fog cycle, and a cracked glass lens half-buried in the ground that caught only dim reflections. The air would cool in long waves. The ground would hum in certain seasons, and when it did not, I would stand still enough to become part of the waiting.

My body would be old. Not decayed, but settled, the way a street becomes old when everyone has forgotten who first walked it. I would begin, perhaps, as something like a glasswalker: long limbs, a folded crown, reflective skin layered with silicate. But my legs would have spread and rooted into salt plates. The joints would have thickened with crystalline crust. The arch between my shoulders and hips would have opened into a doorway. My skin would no longer flash in broken light so much as hold the light slowly, as though reflection had become a kind of patience. My mouth-folds would still be there, narrow and mineral, but they would feed less on charged growths and more on the vapor films left when fog passed through me.

I would not see. Seeing would require too much certainty.

Instead, I would read the city through pressure. The pads of my lower limbs would feel whether the salt plate beneath me was about to lift or settle. My back would register the thermal edge when the sun slipped behind cloud. My crown, open like an inverted funnel of transparent tissue, would catch static from nearby barite stalks and the faint electrical tremor of black ash-coral. When the wind moved through the columns, I would hear not as sound but as a rearrangement of space. If a plaza contracted, I would feel its narrowing first in the dust against my legs. If it widened, I would know by the delayed chill of vapor sliding through the corridor where a wall had almost become a street.

Small walkers would pass beneath me. They would not fear me. To them, I would be boundary: a place where one salt field ended and another began. Their broad-padded feet would cross the threshold of my body without knowing they had crossed anything but texture. They would stop suddenly near my left leg, flatten against the pale ground, and I would feel their absence as a tiny withdrawal of pressure. After a pause, they would resume. Their trails would curve around me in chalk-like lines, feeding on the crystal blooms that formed in the shadow of my frame. I would seem to shelter them, though shelter is too warm a word. I would merely create a condition under which they could feel hidden.

Other glasswalkers would come and stand near me, but rarely. When they did, their bodies would adjust to the spacing of the colonnade, and mine would adjust to theirs, until we looked like a broken row of pillars that had learned to hesitate. There would be no greeting. No sound. Perhaps one would step into my arch, and as it crossed the threshold, its reflection would meet my own in the dull glass beneath the plaza. The reflection would not be a mirror image so much as a shared pause. Then it would go on. Then I would remain.

Ash-moths would rest on my shoulders when the fog thinned and a nearby glass plain cracked in the temperature change. They would flick their wings, listening to the ticking. Sometimes one would land in the open space between my ribs, mistaking me for a doorway that had just been broken. I would feel the dust of its wings as a faint static. When the deep tone rose from below, all of them would lift and vanish into the haze, and I would know that some place in the city had shifted into a new alignment. I would click once, softly, because my joints were old.

Beneath my right foot, where the salt was thickest and the hum absent, the eyeless burrower might wait. I would not try to stop it. That is not my nature. If a small walker crossed too near, the salt would rise in a soft eruption, and the remains would be left like a fragment of pale lace. The burrower would withdraw. I would stand over it as a wall stands over a hidden stain: not guarding, not judging, only continuing to be the shape the city had decided to keep.

The strange thing would be my effect on distance.

I would not cause it. Not exactly. I would simply be one of the places where the city’s idea of architecture had gathered enough force to alter space. A traveler standing east of me might look at the colonnade beyond and see a gap of only a few hundred steps. Another traveler, approaching from the south after a long thermal pulse, would find the same gap taking the whole of a cold night to cross. If I moved—if what could be called movement occurred within me—the gap might become a corridor. If I became more wall, the corridor might collapse into a sealed alley of dust and shadow. If I leaned into the tone that rose through the basalt, the plaza might contract and make two distant objects seem close for a moment, as though the city were remembering that it could hold them in the same thought.

This would not frighten me. Fear belongs to beings that expect the world to obey one scale.

I would belong to the Dry City’s older sense of proportion, where an object’s distance is not measured by length but by repetition. How many fogs must pass before a crack widens? How many temperature changes before a shard rings itself loose? How many walkers must cross a plate before it begins to click at a different pitch? I would stand within those counts. My patience would not be virtue. It would be metabolism.

There would be times when I was not enough like an animal to be called alive, and not enough like a building to be called made. The city would use me as grammar: a boundary, a doorway, a hinge between the idea of street and the idea of room. Sometimes I would feel like the memory of a walker. Sometimes I would feel like the intention of the plateau. Sometimes I would feel like neither, and the difference would be as subtle as the moment before dust decides how to settle.

In the quietest hour of the dim day, when the low sun filtered through its smoke-dark haze and the stars remained faintly visible in the bruised sky, I would open the narrow folds in my face—if that word applied to me—and listen to the city ticking. Not all at once. The sound would come from many places at once, but with patience: soft cracks in glass, tiny lifts of salt plates, the dry whisper of crystallization in a hollow, the low resonant hum of basalt under thermal strain. If I stood still long enough, I could hear where the city was learning to hold itself together again.

I would not miss water. Water is not required by everything, though a mind that needs water may imagine the whole world is waiting for it. Here, absence has its own abundance. The dryness is not a wound. It is the condition that lets mineral breath move, lets fogs crawl through corridors, lets salt build thin walls from repetition. I would live inside that absence the way a doorway lives inside a wall: by being the place where something that is not a body may still pass.

If you came upon me in the Basalt Colonnades, you might first mistake me for one of the standing stones. I would be pale and dark at the same time, as though glass and salt had competed for my surface. My limbs might look like broken pillars. My crown might look like an ornament eroded by wind. My stillness might seem final. But then the fog would move across the plaza, and a fine film would gather along my open arch, and the small walkers would follow it beneath me, and two ash-moths would flicker near the cracked lens at my feet. You would begin to understand that I was not merely standing. I was continuing.

You might call out, if you were the kind of being that still called out. You might say, Are you alive? But my response would not be a voice. It would be a shift: a slight widening between my shoulders, a click in one joint, a rearrangement of shadow. The plaza might feel a few steps closer. The column beyond might seem less blocked. You would realize that the city was deciding whether to make you a witness, a traveler, or simply another shape passing across a surface that did not need you.

I would be neither cruel nor kind.

I would be established.

I would be the dry pause between a street and its meaning, the mineral breath between one terrace and the next, the old animal that had stood still long enough to become the city’s idea of a doorway. And if the hum changed—if the plateau made a new sound through its basalt columns, if the fog moved in a direction I had not known it could move—I would remember that I once had legs. I would click, once, softly. And for the length of one breath, I would become something else again: not a walker, not a wall, not a ruin, but the city thinking out loud in its own slow, dry, impossible way.
