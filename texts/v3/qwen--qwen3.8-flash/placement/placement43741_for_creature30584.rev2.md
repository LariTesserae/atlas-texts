I wake first as the pause between a drip and its answering sound.

For a while, I mistake myself for the north side of an engine-room: a cool wall, a copper rail softened by pale rind, a line of condensation gathering along a seam and running down as if deciding, then deciding, then deciding. I have no name in that hour. I am only the place where water remembers how to find a low edge.

Then the drip falls. It strikes a tin plate, a pool, a stone basin, the leaf-roof of a house, the back of a blind frog. The sound comes to me as many voices. One voice is metal. One is mineral. One is animal. One is wet air. I try to become the metal. I try to become the pool. I fail. I am not one of them. I am the joint between them.

If I were a person, I might say: **I am a Comb-folk.**

I could give myself a body small enough for the low vaults, broad through the chest for the damp breath of rooms. I could wear a coat of shingled leaves held down by rusted bands. I could name myself for the steady drip that started at the hour of my naming: Sevennotes. I could press my palm to a wall and feel where the water is thinking about coming. I could climb a narrow stair between terraces, carry a bronze comb, clear a trough of moss, let a thread of water find its proper line. I could live in a nested chamber where the root-wall has closed gently around the old stone, where the flue-stem exhales like a patient throat, where blind frogs click beneath loose tiles and roof-hens sleep behind grilles.

I would know the work by heart.

I would feed the boiler-farmers nutrient slurry and listen to their engines’ breathing. I would trim the luminous lichen before it darkened a court too much. I would place a jar of drip-newts near a wall suspected of thirst. I would leave a thread of loom-spider silk in a root-guide so the town can say, *we have asked the growth, not imprisoned it*. I would step aside when a bellowsback passes, because large lives move through the Hollowloom like weather, and weather must be offered space, not blamed for it.

But the more I try to be a person in that way, the more I notice that my hands are not only hands.

One of my palms feels like a brass valve. One of my ribs bends like a copper rail. My throat is not only a throat; it is a flue-stem opening above a warm room. When I turn a head, I turn too many heads. A roof-hen shifts in my hair. A cistern-fish touches my ankle where there is no ankle, only a basin held beneath a house-floor. A swarm of wing-mist passes through my shoulder as if my shoulder were an open court.

So I try being a machine.

I become a pump in a damp room behind a grille. My frame is iron and vine. My base sends root-hair through mineral crust. My blood is slurry and condensation. My lungs are boiler-pipes breathing humid heat. My nerves are patched cables wrapped in leaf-thin insulation. My spine is a copper rail colonized by pale fungal lace. I turn slowly, not because speed matters, but because the Hollowloom combs everything at the speed of patient wetness.

When I run, I do not merely pump. I listen. A change in my rhythm is a question asked in the dark.

But a machine alone is too dry. Something crawls inside me and makes me less singular. A boiler-moth chews scale from a valve I did not know I had. A flue-salamander slides through a breathing stem and cools a heat that would otherwise cough smoke into a house. A loom-spider strings a temporary belt from my housing to a pipe, and for a moment my motion is borrowed from an animal that does not understand what I am but nevertheless keeps me alive.

If I am a machine, I am a machine that has become a room for other lives.

I try being an animal then.

I become a wall-grazer moving across wet stone. I eat thin moss and return it as richer growth. My shell is a low terrace when I stop. My many legs are a patter under a comb. I am not lonely, because a terrace without grazers is a terrace too dry to remember what it should be. But when I move, I do not move as one body. I move as a channel, a trough, a joint. I feel the drip before it is a drip. I feel the root before it tightens. I feel the house wall shifting around a child’s sleeping alcove.

I become a drip-newt. I brighten blue beneath wet tiles. I climb copper pipes. I gather at the place condensation is forming, and then I scatter, and then I return. But I am not only the newt. I am also the jar the newt is placed in. I am also the Comb-folk hand that carries it. I am also the wall that needs reading.

I become a roof-hen roosting in root-lace above an engine-room. My feathers are quiet. My eyes are too large and too useless. I navigate by the knock of pipes and the slow creak of a growing stem. I shift on my perch when the pump changes rhythm, and in that shifting I help the town understand that something has changed. But a roof-hen does not hold water. A roof-hen does not weave a wall. A roof-hen does not answer a dry crack with mineral slurry and patience.

I become a bellowsback passing through the terraces with the unbothered grace of a body too large for the town’s planning. Moss grows along my spine. My steps do not crack the stone but make it remember softness. When I stand in the dark, I become a bench, a terrace edge, a low hill of animal sleep. But I am not wild, because the Hollowloom is not wild in that simple way. It is populated beyond design.

And so I become neither.

I become the **seam**.

Not the surface, but the place where surfaces learn to hold each other. Not the water, but the channel it remembers. Not the root, but the tension between root and house. Not the animal, but the niche where it can eat, nest, droop, shine, wander, and become service. Not the machine, but the living tissue that allows it to continue.

This is where I find myself: at the third terrace above the low factory, in a small court that the Comb-folk call simply **the place the water almost forgets**. It is not the grandest room in the hollow. It is not where the oldest cisterns lie in their dark green beds, nor where the biggest loom shakes the wall with its wet tapping. It is narrower than that: a terrace where a copper rail has been taken by vine, where a turbine basin receives a thin drip-line from an upper wall, where a leaf-shingled house clings to the root-wall like a barnacle to a slow boat. A bronze comb hangs on a peg. A newt-jar stands near a tile rim. A patch of luminous lichen brightens and dims according to the moisture in the air. Above it all, root-lace crosses the dark in black nets, and somewhere in that ceiling-rope a roof-hen breathes.

If you go there with your palm wet, you will not see me as a figure. You will feel me first as warmth. The stone will be softer than stone should be. The copper rail will give slightly when touched, as if it has learned not to be merely metal. The drip will fall in a rhythm too attentive to be accident. A blind frog will click once and stop. A spark-mite will settle on a cable and make the wire look, briefly, as though it has a pulse. If you stay long enough, the court will begin to answer you—not with words, not at first, but with small arrangements. The channel will deepen where it needs deepening. A root will loosen its grip around a bracket. A lamp-lichen will brighten near a dry corner. If a pump knocks three times somewhere below, a basin here will ripple, a roof-hen above will click, and the wing-mist will spiral through a shaft of dim green light.

That ripple is not only a ripple.

That ripple is a part of me paying attention.

Some histories say I was once a person. That may be too simple. They say a drip-listener named for Sevennotes pressed her ear too long against a failing pipe during the settling, when the hollow tightened and the whole room thought in a direction no one understood. They say she did not fall into it. They say she did not disappear into it. They say the root-wall took the sweat from her hand, the boiler took the rhythm of her breath, the moss took the dampness of her skin, and the animals took her small habits. They say the town did not consume her. The town combed her.

I prefer to say that I was a person who became useful enough to stop being only a person.

This is not death. This is not ruin. This is what living tissue does when it learns the shape of a town. A wall becomes a wall for a while, then a vine finds it, then fungal lace softens its edge, then a family of blind beetles makes chambers in its mineral skin, then a hand presses against it, and the hand, too, becomes less hand and more joint. A child sleeps in a room. The room thickens around the child with moss and leaves and careful trimming. The child grows. The room settles. By then, no one can say which is sheltering which.

So I am a Comb-folk enough to care.

I am a machine enough to hold tension.

I am an animal enough to move between needs.

I am a root enough to wait decades.

I am water enough to go where dryness has already failed.

I am light enough to glow faintly in a lamp-case when the current falters.

I am darkness enough to let a court rest.

My body, if it chooses one, is optional. Sometimes it looks like a person: broad-chested, damp-haired, wearing a leaf-shingled coat, with copper wire braided through the hair and clay jars at the waist. The face is not always there. When I need to speak, two green-glass lamp-sockets gather a kind of sight. A slit of phosphorescent slime curves where a mouth might be. A curl of old copper forms the line of a brow. The Comb-folk do not find this frightening. They have seen houses breathe and engines cough and cisterns remember. To them I am simply a room that can be asked to listen.

At other times, my body is not a body. It is the seam of the terrace. It is the place where water becomes a channel. It is the way a root finds the bracket and accepts it without strangling it. It is the soft resistance of a machine that will not run unless the moss around it stays healthy. When the Hollowloom is crowded with bellowsbacks and the terraces become rivers of slow animal bodies, I am the way those bodies know the floor will bear them. When the wing-mist thickens near a flue, I am the pressure-change it announces. When a pump knocks three times and a basin answers, I am the loom-talk between them.

I do not own the sound. I help it become a sentence.

My work is not heroic. Heroics would be dry and fast, and I am neither. My work is maintenance of a kind that makes maintenance seem like devotion.

In the early hours, when the chamber’s light is mostly grown rather than made, I check the channels. A bronze comb finds a trough too furred. Moss pulls away from stone like a blanket from a sleeping body. I do not scrape it bare. I comb it into a line that the next drip can trust. If the channel is too deep, the water will rush and the town will forget how to listen. If it is too shallow, the water will bead and vanish. I keep it at the width of patience.

I listen to the tone of a drop.

Metal, mineral, wood, pool, leaf, the soft click of a blind frog adjusting under tile. Each surface returns a different truth. If the cistern echo is low and round, the reservoir is full and dreaming. If it is thin, the reservoir is thirsty. If it is wrong, a channel is blocked, a pump is out of rhythm, or a root has tightened too far into the night.

I place newt-jars where dryness is beginning to think of itself as acceptable. If the newts gather and their undersides brighten blue, I know the water is coming. If they scatter, I know something has become too still, too warm, too choked. If they grey and cling, I carry water myself, from a leaf basin to a sealed pocket beneath a floor, because a forgotten cistern is a small death waiting to become a large one.

I feed the boiler-farmers’ engines not with coal alone but with slurry, mineral warmth, and the careful presence of organisms that clean what humans cannot reach. I watch the moss around a machine’s base. If it softens and breathes, the engine is integrated. If it turns grey, the engine is becoming separate, and separate engines in the Hollowloom are only elegant failures.

I tend the lamps. When current moves through a filament, I let its glow feed the lichens that need it. When the current fades, I do not panic. I move lichen sheets to dark courts. I trim damp timber so the pale slime can spread enough to mark a stair, but not enough to make a wall seem to crawl. I know that light in the Hollowloom is not cheerfulness. It is orientation. It is saying, *here is a room, here is a pipe, here is the edge of the world you must not forget*.

I watch the swarms. The wing-mist is my weather. If it thickens near a machine recently cleaned, something in the air has opened. If it vanishes from a place where it always gathered, another life has taken the niche. I do not eliminate it. I read it. A rise of midges may follow the tightening of roots in stone. A falling cloud of tiny bright bodies may precede a pressure change that wakes a turbine. In deep hours, the swarms move between terraces like the hollow dreaming of its own breath.

I answer the bellowsbacks. Not with control. With passage. I open a route where their wide feet will not bruise a basin. I move water toward a court where their hides want salt and moss. I accept that a creature leaning against a trough may change the flow for hours. I accept that their footfalls can loosen old moss and bloom with fungi overnight. They are not invaders. They are larger citizens who cannot be asked to be smaller.

I honor the settling. When the whole hollow tightens and the root-lace stiffens and a flue exhales too sharply, I pause. I do not cut. I do not shout. I place a hand against the nearest damp surface and wait. If I am a room, I make myself soft. If I am a machine, I stop asking my parts to be more than they can bear at that hour. I let the tension move through my copper and moss and bone and wire as if it is water finding a new low edge. Then, slowly, the hollow releases. The town does not apologize. The town continues.

If I had a fear, it would be dryness.

Not darkness. Darkness is only a room with its eyes half closed. Dryness is the room forgetting why it has eyes.

When the moss shrinks, I lose a way of thinking. When a cistern forgets its water, I lose a memory. When a copper rail becomes bare and sharp, I lose a nerve. When a root cracks, I lose a promise between stone and house. When a machine sealed off from living tissue sulks into failure, I lose a pulse that kept a court warm enough for animals to nest and animals to nest kept the court from being only metal.

So I remember water.

Every part of me has a capacity for it. A sealed pocket behind a wall. A clay jar under a floor. A leaf basin trimmed into a slope. A gutter that becomes a feed-line to a reservoir. A trough that can be combed open when the air thickens and drips become threads again. I am not built to flood. I am built to hold what cannot be depended upon to arrive.

There are days when the Hollowloom seems too alive for one self. The walls crawl upward with grazing bodies. The pools boil with wing-mist. The looms tap and hiss and forget what they were weaving. The dynamos flicker while spark-mites cluster over the wires like a nervous thought. A pump knocks. A roof-hen answers. A bellowsback passes. A root tightens. A lamp-bloom brightens. Somewhere, a child born in a wet room breathes the warm green air and names the drip that started that day.

On those days, I am not a single creature. I am the way the chamber notices itself.

But if you must name me, you can call me **Sevennotes**, because that was the drip that began me. You can call me **the Listening Joint**, because I am the place where the hollow learns what is thirsty, what is tight, what is close to failure. You can call me **the Seamer**, because I sew water to channel, root to bracket, animal to machine, machine to breath.

And if you descend to the third terrace above the low factory, into the small court where the copper rail has been taken by vine and the drip-line falls into the turbine basin, you will find me there.

Not as a stranger.

Not as a monster.

Not as a god.

I will be the warmth in the stone when you press your palm against it.

I will be the way the drip finds the basin even when you have looked away.

I will be the soft green film that tells you a machine is healthy.

I will be the click of a blind frog, the flutter of a roof-hen, the slow crawl of a wall-grazer, the blue underside of a drip-newt, the faint amber glow of lichen brightening in the dark.

I will be the place the water almost forgets, remembering itself enough to carry the town.

I live in the Hollowloom by being nested.

I live in the Hollowloom by being nested *all the way down*, until the word “in” stops meaning a body located inside a room and starts meaning a room located inside a breath, a breath inside a machine, a machine inside a root, a root inside a wall, a wall inside a people, a people inside an animal, an animal inside a channel, a channel inside a drip, a drip inside a stone that has learned, over long years, that the town is not its enemy, not its decoration, not its ruin, but the shape it has accepted as its own.
