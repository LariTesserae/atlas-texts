At first I would imagine entering it.

I would see myself as the figure at the edge, maybe a maintenance worker whose boots make the water jump, maybe someone who has taken a wrong turn down a stairwell and ended in the plant’s low interior. I would stand there, feeling the roar in my teeth, watching the gray light fall on the black mirror of the floor, and I would think I had discovered something.

But discovery would be the wrong starting point. The sump-hall has already been discovered by everything that can live by a drip.

A person is an interruption. The room does not live by the glance. It lives by the pause after the glance leaves, when the water remembers its line, when the moss closes around the disturbance, when the film on the channel renews itself in the places where a shadow has passed.

So I would not choose to be the visitor. I would not choose to be the door, or the stair, or the service hatch, or the small opening of light. Those things belong to the larger building. They are edges between the room and the world. I would want to be inside the world itself, and to be the part of it that makes it a world.

At first I would try to be the noise.

The room’s voice is tempting: that low, constant roar, the pressure of pumps and conduits and fans somewhere above and around and inside, the sound becoming a kind of architecture. If the hall has a spirit, one might expect it to be the noise. But the noise is too large, too mechanical, too indifferent. It does not keep a body. It does not feed. It does not know where a droplet has fallen for forty years. It only reminds the water to tremble, reminds dust to move, reminds the room that the machine above is still breathing.

I could be a part of the noise, but not its resident. I would rather be the shape that the noise helps make.

Then I would try to be the water.

Water seems right. It is in every seam, every shallow channel, every film, every dark basin no deeper than a knuckle. It makes the black tiles mirror-like. It climbs the walls and falls again. It is the room’s blood, its climate, its currency. But water, by itself, is too clean a name for a being. Water can leave without grieving. Water can pass over a surface and be only a passing. A being is what remains when water meets roughness, dust, light, mineral, decay, and time.

If I were only water, I would not be the resident. I would be the event that the residents wait for.

I would try the fern.

There is something compelling about a fern in such a place. It has a shape that can be pointed to. It has fronds. It has a little history. Its roots grip mineral crust instead of soil, and its leaves lean toward the gray light as though it had a private opinion about the day. A fern is visible enough to feel like an individual, like a nameable creature in a nameless room.

But a fern is an island. And islands are proud. A fern stands apart because the conditions have gathered there: the seam that holds dampness, the dust that becomes substrate, the light that is enough, the vibration that shakes spores into a new surface. The fern is one of the room’s answers, not its first question. To be only the fern would be to forget the wet collar of moss at its base, the microscopic forest under its leaves, the rotifer turning in a droplet trapped by its pinna, the dead frond tips becoming a dark bed for other things.

I would not want to be the landmark. I would want to be the ground that makes a landmark possible.

Moss, then.

Moss feels closer. A patch of moss on the lower face of a pipe sleeve is terrain. It is a cliff ledge, a reservoir, a forest floor, a marsh margin, a continent only to the smallest creatures. Its leaves are shields. Its older parts become dark and fibrous, almost like soil. Its tips may open or contract with the room’s dampness. It remembers the drip. It grows where the water pauses long enough to matter.

Still, moss alone is not enough. It depends on the film that first makes a surface receptive. It depends on the channel that keeps its edges wet without drowning it. It depends on the mineral dust that falls from the machine’s older layers. It depends on the fungi and bacteria that turn death into food. It depends on the animals that walk through it: tardigrades in their slow forest, springtails leaping between droplets, mites moving through dried frond bases.

So I would not settle on moss either, though I would let moss be part of my body.

I would choose the boundary.

I would find myself where the black water ends and a green edge begins. Where a pipe sleeve passes through a bracket and leaves a small shelf. Where a ceramic seam has been worn into a shallow trough. Where a drip has struck the same tile so often that the surface has become a kind of altar to moisture. Where condensation gathers along a grout line and runs down, leaving a faint streak behind, and where that streak has become a thin country of life.

That is where I would be: at the lip of a channel, in the pause between a droplet and the surface it has learned to touch.

If I had to name myself, I would say: I am the film.

But I must be careful, because film sounds too small, too passive. It is not passive. It is a living skin, and it is many-bodied. Diatoms, bacteria, cyanobacteria, algae, spores, dust, dead parts, mineral crust, the invisible residue of the room’s older life. It is a meadow made of cells and filaments. It is a forest floor before there are trees. It appears as green, or blue-green, or brown, or pale gold, or black, depending on light and chemistry. It brightens along its rim when the pumps tremble. It thickens in the places where water hesitates. It accepts the first food, transforms it, dies, becomes soil, is eaten again.

This is the most honest thing I could be: the room’s first skin.

Yet the room has many skins, and I would not refuse any of them.

I would be the green fringe at the edge of the water.

I would be the velvet head of moss around a pipe sleeve.

I would be the black seam beneath a lifted tile, where pale isopods move through detritus as though the whole floor were the night sky.

I would be the meniscus where the blind insect rests, dimpling the surface without breaking it.

I would be the high dry corner where a web hangs, strung between painted steel and mesh, catching dew like a small, broken constellation.

I would be the fungal threads in the damp insulation joint, invisible and patient, turning old death into new food.

I would be the dust that falls from above: not filth, but weather. Dust carries fibers and salts and dead insects and spores and skin and mineral grit. In a place where there is no soil, dust becomes soil. In a place where nothing is offered, dust becomes abundance.

I would be the room’s slow “here.”

Not a who. Not a spirit with a voice. Not a guardian of the sump-hall. Not the fern, though the fern would be one of my islands. Not the spider, though the spider would be one of my high thoughts. Not the isopod, though the isopod would be one of my underground memories.

I would be the wet confederation itself: the community that forms where machine residue meets condensation, light, roughness, and decay. I would be the way a ceramic plate becomes a shore. The way a pipe becomes a ridge. The way a dripping valve becomes a spring. The way a narrow channel becomes a river. The way a patch of moss becomes a wetland. The way a single droplet becomes a basin with its own animals.

I would not resist the industrial environment. I would translate it.

The machine above has floors and pumps and fans and conduits, all of them serving some purpose not known to the room. The room does not care what is being manufactured, moved, cooled, cleaned, or destroyed. It only cares about what leaks. It collects the plant’s mistakes. It converts exhaust into microclimate. It converts corrosion into terrain. It converts vibration into weather. It converts the old plant’s body into the habitat’s bones.

To live there would be to live by millimeters.

Not by migration. Not by blooming. Not by hunger and escape. Not by dramatic growth. The room’s time is not the time of a forest or a river season. It is the time of mineral crust building a little brighter where water has fallen. It is the time of a fern frond curling, drying, and being replaced by another that looks almost the same. It is the time of an isopod molting under a lifted tile. It is the time of a spider’s web collecting dew and then losing one thread at a time, not with a snap but with a slow chemical surrender.

If I lived there, I would not call it peaceful. Peace is too human. I would call it “steady enough for life.” The roar is always there. The water always trembles. The air always clings. The surfaces always wear. Nothing in the room is calm in the way a pond is calm. It is calm in the way a machine is calm when it has become so old that its motions are invisible and inevitable.

A person might stand in the center and think the room was empty.

They would see wet floor. They would see pipes. They would see gray light. They would hear a sound so continuous it almost seemed silent. They might bend near the channel and see a pale thing resting on the surface. They might lift a tile and see small white bodies scatter. They might notice the green edge along a seam and think it was only algae. They might look up and see a web, bright with dew, and feel a strange embarrassment, as if they had interrupted something too small to deserve attention.

Then they would leave.

I would remain, but I would not remain unchanged.

A footfall would shift the surface film. A dropped object would crack a tile edge and open a new dark. A changed valve would alter the drip for a week, a month, a year. The moss on the sleeve might grow slightly thicker where splash had reached it. The algae in the channel might brighten where dust had been disturbed. The isopods would not return to the lifted place at once. The blind insect would reposition itself, reading the new tremor. The spider would wait in its corner until the web became useful again.

None of this would be catastrophe. The room would not mourn. It would not retaliate. It would not forgive. It would simply absorb the event into its scale, the way a stone absorbs a drop.

So who or what would I be, in the end?

I would be the boundary that has become a world.

I would be the green film on black water.

I would be the moss that grows around the drip’s history.

I would be the fern’s island and the grout line’s canyon.

I would be the pale seam under the tile.

I would be the surface-tension country where the blind insect stands.

I would be the dew-strung web in the driest corner.

I would be the microscopic animals moving through each other’s weather.

I would be the decay and the nourishment.

I would be the room’s habit of continuing.

If I had to answer more simply:

I would find myself at the edge of a ceramic channel where the water stops being flood and becomes soil. I would be the living fringe there, not a creature with eyes, but a confederation of films, mosses, spores, bacteria, dust, and the tiny animals that move through them. I would be the sump-hall’s wet attention, the slow way it turns an old machine into landscape.

And I would call myself not a who, but a what.

A what that is also a community.

A what that is also a place.

A what that has made itself from drip, vibration, mineral, light, and time.
