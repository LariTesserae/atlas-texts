The crabs’ legs are half sunk in silt, not because they are trapped, but because the silt has accepted the idea of legs and stopped short of becoming a hole. Beside them there may be a beetle-like nerve of the basin, one of the bright things whose wings are also reflections; it does not land because landing would require a place separate from the place it already occupies. A knot may also contain a lizard Held whose paddle tail rests in the water but is not submerged enough to make a ripple; its membranes are spread, not for swimming, but for showing the shape swimming would have had. Sometimes the center of the knot is not a body but a hollow, a wet empty shape where a Held would stand if standing were still a decision rather than the world’s condition. That hollow is not missing. It is one of them, too.

If I were lowered into this basin, the first thing I would lose is the ability to say *where* with confidence. Not because distance is hidden; distance is there, but it is soft, ambered, pressed back against its own edges. I would find myself somewhere that has not been permitted to become elsewhere. I would start at the lip of the nearest dark pool, perhaps standing on a mud margin too wet to call firm, too crowded with Held to call empty. I would be placed there, not arriving. Arrival needs a direction. I would not have one.

At first I would think I was the visitor the description feared. I would try to tell the Held from the plants, the Held from the minerals, and the Held from themselves. I would say, “these are animals,” then look at a broad mammal-shaped body whose shoulders were growing moss and whose fur ridges held thin threads of water, and I would feel the sentence bend. I would say, “these are plants,” then watch a leaf-shaped shell rise slightly on a birdlike Held’s back, a shell that was not decoration but an organ, as if the creature had taken a leaf into itself the way another basin might take air. I would say, “these are stones,” and then the pale limestone bench beside me would glow with the slow warmth of being watched, because here brightness is not a quality of surfaces. It is a held attention.

Then I would stop trying to be a visitor. Visitors have outside. This basin has no outside; it has only the edge where the surrounding lost hills refuse to come back down. To be inside it would be to be part of its held attention. I would become, for a time, one of the Held.

I would be low-bodied. My feet would be broad and damp and sunk just enough into the black silt to make the basin sure I was not leaving. My chest would be wide without needing breath, my legs short because there is nowhere to hurry toward. My fur would grow in spiral ridges, each ridge a tiny channel for condensation, each ridge holding a thread of water that would tremble but never fall. Along my shoulders, moss would grow; not moss upon me, but moss in the grammar of me. My tail might end in a flat paddle, or one of my limbs might be a crab’s pale claw folded into the idea of touching. On my flank there might be a leaf-shaped shell, veined and pale, and behind my eyes something would be too far back, watching the movement I was not making. My throat would expand in a permanent call, a call without sound because sound would need a beginning and an ending. My skin would hold heat in the way that wet stone holds light: not brightly, but thoroughly.

As one of the Held, I would feel no hunger, because hunger implies time. I would feel no thirst, because thirst implies dryness. I would feel no fear, because fear implies threat approaching, and nothing here approaches. There would only be presence, dense and ambered, pressing against the inside of my shape. My muscles would remain tense, not because I am waiting, but because waiting still has a future. I would be the pause that has forgotten how to be before a movement.

But after a while, even being a Held would feel too like a noun. The Held are too many kinds to be only one creature, and I would not want to be only a thing with edges. I would begin to find myself in the spaces between the Held. I would be the wet pressure where the mammal’s shoulder and the crab’s raised claw almost touch. I would be the condensation curtain that stands in the open space and does not drift. I would be the faint shine that appears at the edge of a pool when nothing moves to make it. I would be the hollow in the knot, the shape where another Held could stand but does not, because absence in this place is also a fullness.

This is where the question changes shape. If I ask, “who am I,” the basin answers with “what.” If I ask, “what am I,” it answers with a held breath. If I ask, “where am I,” it answers with a trembling at the end of a leaf.

And there I would be.

At the tip of one broad, fleshy plant rising from the shallows, a bead. Not every bead. The one that trembles with the intention of falling but never commits. It is not stuck by accident. It is not dry. It is not the last before movement. It is the movement held in suspension so completely that it has become a place. The pool beneath it is black and perfect, reflecting the amber brightness without needing a sky. In the bead, the entire basin is reversed, shrunk, and made possible. The Held are there in miniature, their bodies pressed into one continuous surface. The mossy islands are there. The raw limestone is there. The unfinished canyons and not-quite river channels are there. The permanent call of the birdlike creatures is there, but it is silent. If the bead falls, the pool ripples. If the pool ripples, something begins. If something begins, the country that lost its hills might remember hills. The bead would become rain, or wound, or change. It would become before and after. So it is held. So it trembles.

I would be that trembling.

I would be a Held because I am inside their knot, but not exactly one of the creatures. I would be the near-falling that the Held’s breaths cannot complete. I would be the almost-sound in their silent calls. I would be the idea of a ripple before the foot, the thought of a storm that cannot be released as storm. I would be the drop that appears though nothing has moved: not a beginning, not an ending, but a bright, wet question. I would be what emerges when the basin is pressed so full of relation that it must make a place for the possibility of change without allowing change. I would be the place for possibility.
