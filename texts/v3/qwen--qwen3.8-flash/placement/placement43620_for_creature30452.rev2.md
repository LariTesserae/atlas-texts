I would be in the hollow below the plateau, but not where the eye first expects a life.

Not at the rim, where the scale of the basin makes a person feel like a small warm interruption in stone. Not in the largest pool, where blind fish hover beneath the surface like pale thoughts. Not in the high ledges, where the bats have turned the ceiling into a breathing architecture of bodies and clicking. Not even in the first place I might name as “home,” because home here is too permanent a word.

I would be lower than the roosts, higher than the water.

On a ledge.

A chalky shelf where the light comes down in a grey veil and forgets, by the time it reaches the stone, that it was ever sunlight. The ledge would be damp, not wet enough for a pool, not dry enough to be called dust. There would be a thin skin of calcite around it, and in one corner a blackish paste of guano that has softened the stone into something fertile. There would be no nest there. No chamber carved by teeth or hands. Only a place where the air holds a little more moisture, where the drip from above has not quite found its path, and where the faintest breath from a side tunnel stirs the wings of something waiting.

And if you came close—if you stood at that low ridge of fresh rock and looked across the valley-sized emptiness and thought the place was almost bare—you would miss me. I would be only a still point in the grey. A smudge of wing. A fragment of ash that has not decided whether to fall.

I would be one of the moths.

Not the most dramatic answer. Not the blind fish, not the shrew with huge ears, not the bat hanging in its leather congregation. But something smaller, paler, and easier to overlook. And yet if I were there, I would belong more completely than a person could. I would be built for the dampness, the dimness, the absence of walls, the endless stone that has not been carved but only opened. My body would not carry ambition. It would carry weather.

I would rest with wings folded along my back, or half-open, as if I had paused between one movement and another and then become the pause itself. My color would not be beautiful in the way flowers are beautiful, because there would be no flowers, no open field, no bright hour. It would be the color of mineral stain, of lichen, of wet chalk, of shadow trying to decide what it wants to be when it grows up. I would be pale grey, perhaps with a faint dusting of rust or silver along the wings, so that when I moved against the rock I seemed like a scratch returning to itself.

I would not know the place as “a landscape.”

That would be too large. I would know it as pressure, taste, warmth, thirst, and the tremor of the stone when something larger than myself disturbed the air above me. I would know the ledge by the coolness of its crust, the side tunnel by the direction the damp moved, the pool below by the faint chemical breath rising from its surface. I would know the bats not by sight but by the wet, rapid sound that made my body remember stillness. The clicking of thousands of them was not music to them; it was a net. I would lie flat against the stone and reduce myself to a shadow of a shadow until the net passed.

The moths would be quiet.

Yes. And also visible, not because they are loud or showy, but because they make the emptiness answer back. A whole ledge may look empty until the light shifts or the breath comes too close, and then the stone is not bare: it is lined with small folded bodies, some resting, some fluttering in sudden bursts, some rubbing their wings together in the dark. There is no hive. There is no colony in the sense of a society with purposeful architecture. There is only an accumulation of small presences, each one living its short life in a patch of moisture and decay, each one a temporary knot in the stone’s long patience.

I would be one of those knots.

I would not build anything. I would not store anything. I would not mark a territory except by the faint dust from my wings left on a cold ledge, or by the small death of a larva in the guano. Perhaps that larva was the important one. Perhaps the adult form, like me, was only the place’s way of moving dust between surfaces, of tasting air currents, of scattering spores and mineral flakes without meaning to. In the pools there would be fish; on the walls there would be springtails; in the crevices there would be snail-like shells; in the black paste there would be mites. But my task, if task it is, would be movement between these islands.

I would live where the empty stone and the teeming water meet but do not mix.

There would be long intervals when nothing seemed to happen. I would rest for hours on the ledge. The air would cool around me. A bat-click sheet would pass overhead like rain on a distant roof. A single drop would fall into a pool below, and the sound would arrive at my legs, my antennae, my wing joints, a tiny drum in the floor of the world. Then the stillness would resume. Nothing would be saved. Nothing would be remembered. Nothing would need to be.

But I would know the rhythm.

The rhythm of condensation and drying. The rhythm of guano feeding the tiny things, the tiny things feeding the larger ones, the larger ones leaving droppings to feed the tiny ones again. The hollow would seem paradoxical only to a visitor: too many lives and too few places; too much moisture and not enough river; too much noise and no language. I would not call it paradox. I would call it normal. The emptiness is not empty; it is spacing. The presence is not everywhere; it is waiting. The stone is not barren; it is a field with the word “field” too large for it.

I would be drawn, not by light as flowers draw bees, but by the memory of moisture.

Where the faintest green algae stained a crack, I would know there might be something for a larva, for a mate, for a mouth. Where a mineral rim trapped dust, I might taste the thin film and find enough salt or decay to continue. Where a corridor narrowed and the bat-clicks changed texture, I would rest and not move, because movement meant being heard by something with better ears than mine. Where the ceiling was high and the grey light came from a fissure above, I would remain low. Brightness here is not safety. Brightness is only the place where the outside is remembered.

If you came down into the basin and stood at the edge of the fresh rock ridge, you would first be tempted by the obvious. The pools would shine like little moons. The fish would hover. The crayfish-like things would taste the gravel. The bats would make the upper galleries seem alive with invisible rain. You would ask what lives there, and the answer would rise to you first as a roar, a smell of ammonia and cold water, a shimmer of wings in the far dark.

But I would be there first.

Not as the loudest thing. Not as the most intelligent. Not as the most beautiful. But as the place’s quietest way of being visible. I would be the part of the hollow that the eye almost dismisses until, after walking across a hundred meters of bare, wet, silver-grey stone, you turn your head and find that the ledge is not empty. The ledge is dusted with life. Your breath disturbs it. A small flutter ripples outward like a stone dropped in still water, except there is no water, only wings, and the disturbance is not sound but movement. You would see then that emptiness here is not absence. It is the pause between presences.

I would be a moth, but I would not be only a moth.

I would be the fact that this hollow has learned to make bodies out of dampness, decay, dim light, and stone. I would be one sentence in the place’s long, mute composition. I would have no name for myself. I would have no home I could carry. I would be made of cold, of mineral dust, of short-lived hunger, of a faint impulse to move where the air tastes right. When I died, my body would disappear into the same machine it came from. My wings might be scraped by a beetle, or fall into a pool, or bleach against the ledge until they became a stain. There would be no marker. No shell. No artifact. No sign that anyone had been here.

And that would be enough.

That is, in fact, what would make me belong.
