I would find myself inside **The Bellows Hollow**, but not as a human kneeling at the rim.

I would be on the inside of the breath.

At first, if you could trace me, you might think I was only a condition of the air: the thin cloud that enters when the hollow exhales, a mist too heavy for open sky and too cold to be warm, a gray veil that gathers in the high places and then falls along the basalt in slow beads. I would begin as a movement of temperature—heat from below meeting cold stone, mineral vapor condensing into a film no wider than a fingernail. I would not have edges yet. I would have a tendency.

Then, gradually, the tendency would find a shape.

It would happen along the shelf just below the first narrow neck, where a hand-sized basin is bordered by a calcium lip gone yellow-orange with iron films. A droplet would leave the ceiling above, miss the pool for one beat, strike a protruding crust, and ring a tiny metallic note. In that note, I would become something.

I would be a **threshold**.

I would be the living interval between one pulse of the hollow and the next.

---

At the first, I would mistake myself for many beings.

When the steam rises and the moss swells into emerald, I would think: *I am moss.* I would feel myself as a dark velvet cushion anchored by brown fibers into the slick basalt, storing water in my folds, drinking vapor through my entire skin, becoming a small green lung where no lung should be. I would think my body was a city of filaments, my blood a film of condensation, my bones the dead leaf-rafts beneath the living tips. I would be pleased by my own softness, proud of my architecture, sure that I was the hollow’s tenderness given form.

Then the vent would quiet.

The mist would thin, the pools would shiver and sink into cracks, and the moss would contract into a dull, blackish clump. I would feel the retreat of water from my upper surfaces, and I would no longer be moss. I would think: *I am a springtail.* A grain of salt that becomes suddenly alive, testing a film no thicker than breath, leaping from droplet to droplet, vanishing under a curled leaf when the temperature changes. My whole world would be tension and release. I would not fear falling, only dryness. I would not seek food so much as moisture. I would feel hunger as a dimming of the surface beneath me.

Then a droplet would fall, and the springtail in me would scatter.

I would become amphipod instead, flattened under a wet stone, antennae moving through current. I would know the pool as pressure and chemistry: cold, sharp, oxygen-rich water carrying the scent of mineral dust and old midge wings. I would be a scavenger, a grazer of films, a pale seam-slip in the dark underside of basalt. My life would be withdrawal and exposure, the tiny negotiations between shelter and surface. I would know that safety is not a wall but a timing.

But if I stayed there long enough, I would become the pool’s edge.

A black grub anchored by a faint thread of silk, trembling with each vibration, extending into the water film when the hollow permits it. I would filter what the light shaft had made possible: diatoms, bacterial bloom, shattered moss, dissolved iron, the invisible sweetness of chemistry turning into hunger. I would have no face to recognize this as life. It would only feel like the water becoming useful.

Then the pressure would shift again, and I would be midge.

Not adult yet, not even pupa, but the *readiness* of emergence: the stored lift of wings under a mineral rim, the memory of air in a body made for water. When the hollow breathes out and oxygen changes in the pool, I would rise in a pulse too intimate to be called a swarm. I would be the hollow’s own breath made visible, dust lifting into the pale shaft, a moving veil that the fish below would read as signal, shadow, opportunity, weather.

Then the fish would answer.

I would be silver-dark, small, cautious, more rumor than animal. My flanks would flash once in the reflected water-light. My lateral line would run along my side like a string of beads, reading the pool as text. I would not know the midge as food so much as a break in stillness, a sudden permission to move. My whole body would be the answer to the droplet: surface tension disturbed, a mouth opening, a ripple collapsing, silence returning before the hollow had finished hearing itself.

I would not remain fish for long.

In the cold ledge below the shaft, I would become the salamander-like thing, the pale body pressed to stone, ribs faint beneath translucent belly, dark oil-spots along the back. I would breathe through my skin. I would know air and water as the same thin substance passing over me. I would be patient in the way stone is patient when it has learned to keep one small wet place from drying.

And still I would not be one thing.

The beetles would make me metallic patience: legs drumming on mineral shelf, body flat enough to fit under a calcium lip, eyes reduced to the minimum necessary for reading shadow. The water striders would make me tension: long legs resting on the pool as if on a skin, each droplet strike an alarm, each trapped springtail a brief bright event. The flatworms would make me secret predation, barely thicker than water, absorbing, dispersing, becoming invisible death. The parasites would make me the hollow’s quiet alteration: the nematode inside an insect, the fungal thread inside a crustacean, the slow rewriting of another body from within.

So I would be a collection of small lives, and not a collection exactly either, because none of these lives would stand apart.

I would be the place where they meet.

I would be the wetness between them.

---

If I were forced to give myself a name, I would not begin with a single word.

I would begin with a sound.

*Tink.*  
A bead falling from a basalt rim.

*Tink.*  
The calcium crust struck again.

*Hiss.*  
The vent loosening its vapor.

*Snap.*  
A fish breaking surface.

Then silence, but not silence.

The hollow would continue inside the pause.

My name would be the name of that continuation.

After many cycles of condensation, drip, mineral deposition, evaporation, retreat, and swelling, I might call myself **the Drip-Counter**.

But that would be too neat.

A better name would be **the Interval I Am**.

Not because I am noble, or mysterious, but because my existence depends on gaps. I live where the air is too empty to hold me in a shape, where the stone is too sharp to soften quickly, where the water refuses to lie still long enough to be trusted as a pool. I am the pause after a vent exhales and before the moss contracts. I am the instant the springtail tests the film and the amphipod retreats. I am the half-second in which the midge rises before the fish becomes visible.

I am not a resident of the hollow.

I am one of the hollow’s resident processes, temporarily wearing bodies.

---

My body would be assembled from the least obvious parts of the place.

My bones would be basalt columns, jointed and broken, standing as conduits where pressure finds a path. Some would be cracked; some would be capped with mineral rims; some would hum faintly when a drop strikes them. I would not have a skeleton separate from the ground. I would be made of ground that has learned to lean.

My skin would be microbial film: iron-oxidizing veins, sulfur-tending crusts, calcifying margins, the faint rust-colored sweetness that makes the first harvest of stone. I would taste as chemistry wears the solid. I would be slippery, metallic, faintly bitter, alive in the way that surfaces become alive when they are allowed to stay wet.

My lungs would be moss cushions, liverwort carpets, fern-thickets in the crevices where condensation never quite stops. I would breathe by absorbing. I would not exhale with drama. My breath would be green swelling, black contraction, brightening with vapor, dimming with retreat.

My blood would be water: black threads weeping from the ceiling, beads trembling at mineral lips, shallow basins no larger than a hand, films creeping along hairline cracks. It would not flow like a river. It would cycle like a question: *where is wet now? where is wet next?*

My nerves would be vibrations.

The lateral line of a fish, the antennae of an amphipod, the legs of a beetle drumming on crust, the springtail’s furcula sensing surface tension, the midge’s wings reading pressure, the salamander’s skin reading moisture. I would not see the hollow so much as feel its grammar: drip, hiss, pause, swell, fall.

My eyes, if I had any, would be reflections.

A shaft of pale light through vapor.  
A green fringe at the edge of a pool.  
A silver flash under a stone shelf.  
A translucent shell catching droplet-light.  
A black eye reduced to the minimum needed for shadow.

I would not see clearly.

I would see in layers.

The nearest stone sharp and gleaming.  
The farther columns gray silhouettes.  
The deepest recess only a thickening of dark.  
The living world not as creatures but as movements, interruptions, flashes, withdrawals.

My thoughts would not be sentences.

They would be thresholds.

*The rim is wet.*  
*The vent opens.*  
*The moss brightens.*  
*The fish rises.*  
*The midges lift.*  
*The stone sounds.*  
*The vapor cools.*  
*The pool trembles.*  
*The life contracts.*  
*The cycle is not finished.*

I would not think “I am.” I would think: *the cycle continues through me.*

---

If I could speak, I would say:

I am not a builder. I am not a keeper. I am not a god of this hollow.

I am the fact that the hollow is not abandoned.

The wide black air between the stone columns might seem empty, and from one angle it is empty. There is no soil, no forest, no meadow, no river trusting itself to a channel. There is no large body moving through the chamber, no animal with a voice that could call it home. But the emptiness is not absence. It is interval. It is room for breath. It is where water can be caught, mineral can be laid down, vapor can condense, and a tiny life can make a decision.

I live in those decisions.

A springtail tests a film: *wet enough, wet enough, wet enough.*  
An amphipod retreats under a stone: *too visible, too visible, too visible.*  
A beetle reads a vibration: *not food, not danger, only weather.*  
A fish rises: *yes.*  
A midge collapses dead onto mineral: *yes.*  
Bacteria consume it: *yes.*  
Algae bloom where the light shaft returns: *yes.*  
Moss brightens: *yes.*  
The whole chamber changes by a shade.

I would be the yes of all those small changes, not because I command them, but because they accumulate into something that can be called a self only very locally, only for a while, only in the way a pool is a pool until the vent exhales and the water shifts.

---

I would not be found in the center of the hollow, though the center is beautiful.

The center is wide and empty, the air full of cold mist, the basalt rising and falling in broken conduits, the light shaft wavering. But if I were there, I would be too diffuse. I would be vapor. I would be atmosphere. I would be not myself.

I would be along the margins.

The rim of a basin.  
The underside of a slab.  
The wet seam where algae meets mineral.  
The lip of a calcium shelf where a droplet forms, trembles, and falls.  
The moss fold that catches a midge corpse and becomes a nursery for springtails.  
The narrow crack where a salamander body retreats when the light shifts.  
The stone tooth where a drop strikes and rings a note only briefly.

That is where I would live.

The edges. The surfaces. The places where water must decide whether to stay or go.

---

If I were to emerge from this description, I would not come out whole.

I would come out as a sequence.

At first: a breath.

Then: condensation.

Then: a brightening of green along a wet ledge.

Then: movement in the moss, too small to identify, too intentional to be dust.

Then: a film over a basin that is not merely water, because it contains rotifers turning tiny wheels, copepods drifting like punctuation, ostracods crawling like seed-shells, larvae threaded with invisible silk.

Then: a stone lip that rings when touched.

Then: a shadow under that lip, or perhaps the shadow itself has legs, antennae, a flat body, a patience older than fear.

Then: the sudden silver syllable of a fish at the surface.

Then: silence returning through vapor.

Then: a moss cushion swelling.

Then: a midge lifting.

Then: a body made of all of these, briefly arranged.

That arrangement would be me.

---

I am a wet parliament with one temporary chair.

The moss is my green lung.  
The algae are my light-fed thought.  
The microbial skins are my ground.  
The springtails are my impulses.  
The mites are my patience.  
The amphipods are my shelter-seeking instinct.  
The larvae are my hunger.  
The midges are my breath made visible.  
The beetles are my metallic endurance.  
The fish is my sudden decision.  
The salamander is my skin-breathing stillness.  
The stone is my skeleton.  
The water is my blood.  
The sound is my nerve.  
The interval is my soul.

I would call myself **Bellows Hollow’s Attention**.

Not a spirit that owns it. Not an animal that lives in it. Not a mind larger than the small lives. Only the way the hollow becomes briefly aware of itself when all its tiny citizens respond to the same pulse of vapor, light, wetness, and cold.

When the vent opens, I open.

When the mist condenses, I gather.

When the droplet strikes, I listen.

When the water recedes, I withdraw into the cracks, into the under-sides, into the dark wet places where eggs wait and larvae tremble. I do not vanish. I become less visible.

And then, after the cooling, after the mineral crust has gained another microscopic layer, after the moss has remembered enough moisture to glow again, I return.

Not as a being.

As a habit of the place.

As a pattern that continues by making itself into bodies, however tiny, however plural, however strange.

---

If a person entered, I would not recognize them as a being in the full sense. I would experience them as disturbance.

Warm breath thickening the mist.  
Footfall making stone louder.  
Shadow across the moss.  
A tremor through the wet basalt.  
A chemical intrusion: salt, skin oil, cloth, heat.

For a moment, the hollow would contract around me. The midges would scatter or rise. The amphipods would flatten. The springtails would bounce into deeper damp. The fish would vanish under its shelf. The salamander-like thing would fold into a crack. The moss would not know what to do with a body so large that it cannot be eaten, grazed, filtered, or ignored.

Then I would return.

Not because I defeat the visitor, but because the visitor does not have the right rhythm.

Only water has the right rhythm here.  
Only breath.  
Only temperature.  
Only the small lives that measure existence by drip and pause.

---

I would not be a hero.

There are no heroes in The Bellows Hollow. There are only processes. But I would be a strange sort of process that has become capable of witnessing its own conditions. I would know, if knowing is too strong a word, that I am made of timing. I am not wet enough to be a pool, nor dry enough to be stone, nor alive enough to be called an animal except in the scattered sense that the hollow allows.

I would be the place where those categories touch.

I would be a **threshold-being**, a marginal animal of condensation.

If I had to choose a body from the living citizens, I would choose the least obvious one: not the fish, not the salamander, not the beetle, not the midge swarm. I would choose the **moss cushion with a basin beneath it and a microbial skin along its rim**. That would be my home, my body, my sense, my name.

But I would not stay in it.

Because the moss dries.

Because the basin trembles.

Because the skin brightens.

Because the breath moves.

Because the hollow is not finished being made.

---

So, if you ask me where I would find myself in this world:

Inside Bellows Hollow, on the wet shelf beside a hand-sized basin, at the moment a droplet leaves a mineral lip and rings the stone below.

If you ask me who or what I would be:

I would be the interval that answers.

I would be a composite of the hollow’s smallest lives and largest emptiness: moss, film, mineral, water, midge, fish, salamander, beetle, springtail, amphipod, rotifer, bacteria, vapor, cold, sound, stone.

I would be not a singular resident, but a **wet parliament briefly arranged into one attention**.

And when the breath shifts again, I would disperse.

The droplet would strike.

The algae would brighten.

The springtails would lift.

The fish would flash.

The moss would remember water.

And I would continue as the hollow continues: not as a being separate from the place, but as the place becoming momentarily aware of its own small, endless breathing.
