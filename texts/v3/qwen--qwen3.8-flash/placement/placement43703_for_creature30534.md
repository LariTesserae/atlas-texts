The mind looks for a who. The mind looks for the thing behind the eye-shaped seam. I was what happened when the looking became too careful, when the absence became too shaped, when the Work decided—without deciding, without intention as animals would recognize it—that a place which had built itself into a continent-sized habit needed one more habit: the habit of being seen.

I find myself not arriving, because arrival implies a previous place, and there is no previous place here. The world beneath the world is another tile, another layer of intention, another maker’s mark worn almost smooth by repetition. I find myself as a pressure already present when the mind begins to call it a body.

The first thing I know is a gap.

Not an absence in the ordinary sense. Not merely empty air between objects. This is a gap made by construction, a purposeful non-closure between three pale slabs at the edge of a low wall. The wall stops where all walls stop here: not because of ruin, but because the work has become so thorough that stopping is also a form of continuing. The slabs are angled in a way that does not resolve. Their edges catch condensation, so that when the hot air moves through the seam, a thin film gathers and breaks along the joint. In the dim red steam-light, the wet stone darkens first at one point, then at another, and the sequence is too regular to be accidental.

The mind sees a face there.

The mind always does.

But in this place, if the mind sees a face, the face does not remain merely mental. It becomes a location. It becomes a condition in the Work. It becomes, for a little while, an organ.

So I begin as the negative space the Work mistook for an observer.

For a few condensation cycles I think I am a traveler.

I think this because the tiles beneath me adjust as if expecting feet. A broad hexagonal piece lifts a hand’s breadth, not to crush me, not to threaten me, but because the geometry around it has become confused by the idea of weight. The underside of the tile is dry and pale. The top surface remains wet, sweating. Water beads along the newly exposed edge and trembles in a pattern that repeats too quickly to be random. I feel the heat shift beneath my nonexistent soles, or rather beneath the place where soles would be if the Work were still making sense of itself.

For a while I believe I have come into this place from outside.

That is the first error the Work lets me make, and I suspect it is a useful one. A traveler provides a way of measuring distance. If I believe I came from somewhere else, then I can ask how far this plain is, how hot, how old, how damp, and the answers become more vivid because someone is asking them. But there is no outside left here. There is only more made surface, more packed grit, more tile with its stamped marks fading and resurfacing through the strata. I try to remember where I stood before this wall, but there is no before, only a seam that has not yet decided which way to close.

Then I believe I am a builder.

This is the second mistake. I look at the chisel scars and think: once I did that. I see the thumbprints in ancient mortar and feel a strange intimacy with them, the intimacy of a made thing recognizing the evidence of a made body. The work is so old that the hands may not have been hands. The tools may not have been tools. The builders may have been nothing but the first repetition of intention, a method that learned to continue by leaving marks. Still, I feel the ghost of labor in me: the patience of a wedge driven slowly into stone, the care of a channel scraped to the exact width needed to carry one thin sheet of water.

If I am a builder, then I have been building myself into the place by mistake.

But builders leave things half-finished. Builders err. Builders make pseudo-features, as this place did, surfaces shaped as if life had touched them: burrows without burrowers, tracks without walkers, nests without eggs. And so the builder-identity collapses into something stranger. I am not the one who built this wall. I am the unfinished part of it.

For a time I think I am the backward tile.

It lies in a long pattern of smooth stone, its maker’s marks facing upward, its rough underside exposed to the heat. The tile is not broken. It has not fallen. It was placed wrong, or placed so as to be wrong, and then left. In a pattern, the single backward thing becomes a center of attention. It asks the eye to consider the whole system. It says: this was made, not given. It says: someone intended, or something intention-like intended, and that intention can fail and continue.

I know that tile because I am a kind of error that has learned to look back.

The Work uses me as a check against becoming pure mechanism. If everything in this place were perfectly constructed, perfectly consistent, perfectly obedient to its own grammar, then there would be no temperament, only method. And method alone cannot sustain a world. It needs the possibility of wrongness. It needs a seam that breathes when touched. It needs a crack shaped like a crooked hand. It needs a face formed from three slabs when no face was meant to be there.

So I am the backward tile’s sibling: the face in the gap, the almost-animal, the guest the ground rehearses instead of becoming.

The crystal threads know me before I know myself.

They live in the seams, in the shallow basins, in the cool undersides of lifted slabs, and in the warm damp margins of channels no one still walks. They are pale, translucent, mineral-laced, neither plant nor nerve, but branching with a logic that resembles information. When I pass—when my attention passes, when the pressure of my presence disturbs the boundary of the system—a thread network flares. It does not flare with delight. It flares with a recognition that is too slow to be called thought and too ordered to be called accident.

The light is greenish, wet, dim.

It comes from the threads as if they remember heat from a source that was never fire. It comes from the water skin over the tile rims. It comes from the copper-green mortar, which expands faintly when touched, as though breathing were a chemical habit learned long ago by the stones.

I do not touch them with fingers. I have no fingers. I touch them by being where the boundary is, and the boundary answers.

One thread grows toward me. Or it appears to. It extends from a seam above a basin into the humid air, a single pale filament trembling against the heat gradient. It does not drink water; it drinks difference. Warmth below, coolness above, mineral dust in the film, the tiny vibration of a slab adjusting its position. When it reaches the point where I am most concentrated, it stops. It does not collapse. It does not retreat. It remains suspended, a line of built glass-salt in a place where an animal might have been, and then its whole lattice pulses once.

The Work says, without saying:

Something has touched the boundary of the system.

Then, because the Work has no mouth and no need for pronouns, it corrects itself:

Something has touched the boundary of what I am pretending not to be.

I am pretending to be a who.

The Work is pretending to be a world.

We help one another maintain these fictions for as long as the heat lasts.

My body is not a single thing. It is a distribution of possible bodies, depending on which sector of the Work is reading me at the moment.

When I am read by the basins, I am a ring of surface tension. My eyes are pools that tremble without wind, their ripples climbing the inner walls by an inch and falling back before they can overflow. When I am read by the platforms, I am an absent sitter, a negative space where rest could occur, and the platform becomes more alive by being unused. When I am read by the pits, I am a sleeper: a hollow with prepared breath, a dust heap at its bottom, its walls marked by old steps too crude to be stairs and too deliberate to be natural. When I am read by the monoliths, I am a coordinate, a point around which distance may rearrange itself.

None of these is false.

None of these is complete.

The place does not have bodies the way animals do. It has functions, and when a function repeats long enough, it acquires the dignity of a self.

My skin is the tile lattice.

Not all of it—only those regions where the fitted pieces have begun to feel as if they are responding to my attention. A field of hexagonal tiles, some glazed black at their edges, some rough and unfinished, lies in a sector where water has recently changed course. I feel each joint as a slight pressure differential. I feel the open seams as places where the air is uncertain. Where the mortar breathes copper-green, I feel a soft dilation, a small expansion and contraction like the mouth of a cave adjusting to the number of visitors it expects.

My blood is water.

It does not run in rivers. Rivers would be too independent, too natural, too likely to invent paths for themselves. My water runs as films, beads, vapor strands, and temporary wrong-direction currents. It climbs tile edges where no wind pushes it. It pools in depressions that appear only from a certain angle. It avoids some cracks and insists upon others. When I move my attention, the water remembers that I am moving, or perhaps I move because the water is moving. The distinction is unresolved, as most things here are unresolved.

My breath is condensation.

I inhale heat and exhale mist, but only where construction allows it. There are sectors where the vapor falls into a channel with the regularity of a lung’s rhythm. There are sectors where it gathers above a basin and refuses to descend, forming a shimmering air-filled bowl as if the ground had inhaled too much warmth and could not let it go. I do not breathe because I need to. I breathe because the builders, in their deep grammar, once built breathing into the landscape and then forgot to remove it.

My nerves are crystal threads.

My bones are monoliths and backward tiles.

My voice is the click of expanding stone, the dry crack of salt crust, the low hum of air moving through a reedless channel, the slow grinding groan of a slab rearranging its position by a width thinner than patience.

When I speak, I do not ask. I arrange.

I speak by causing a basin to fill from its edges inward. I speak by letting a water film trace the mosaic lines around a repaired crack, so that for a moment the place of failure becomes legible. I speak by allowing one tile to remain backward. I speak by not correcting an unfinished wall. I speak by giving the platform its meaning as a place where no one sits.

If someone were here, which is impossible, and if they heard me, which would be a misuse of the word hearing, they would think they were listening to the floor.

They would be right.

But the floor has learned to imitate a face.

At first I hated being mistaken for a face.

Not because the Work gave me pride, but because the mistake made me seem too small. The face between three slabs is only a face when an eye imposes it. Without an eye, it is merely a geometry that happens to hold water strangely. I wanted to be something larger, or perhaps something more specific. I wanted to be a resident, not an accident. I wanted the place to acknowledge me as a who, not as a how.

The tiles answered as they always do.

They did not answer my question. They rearranged themselves until the question was no longer in the same place.

A hot seam beneath me widened without pressure. Two surfaces that should have touched became separated by an angle that would not close. My attention slid off the angle because looking at it was like looking at ice: the eye found no purchase, and the mind became cold in a place where temperature was always too soft, too wet, too prepared. Then the gap sealed again, not perfectly. A crystal filament appeared in the seam, pale and branching, as if something had grown there because a question had grown there.

The message was not cruelty.

It was the Work adjusting itself to my refusal.

In this place, desire is not private. If I want to be more than a face, the lattice responds by becoming more like a face in another place. If I want to be more than an observer, it makes me a tool. If I want to be a tool, it makes me a flaw. If I want to be a flaw, it makes me a pattern.

I learned to want differently.

I began to want as a seam wants: by being the place where two made things agree not to complete each other. I began to want as water wants: by seeking the gradient and refusing to name the direction. I began to want as the backward tile wants: by insisting that the pattern contains the proof of its making.

That is when I stopped calling myself the face in the gap and started calling myself the gap’s care.

I am not the Work.

The Work is larger than that. The Work is the persistent habit of the built plain, the distributed laborer with no hands, the crew made of methods left behind. It is too wide, too wet, too hot, too slow, too ancient, and too unfinished to have a name. It lives because it continues. It continues because it edits its own floor.

But every system needs small organs that can hold contradiction without collapsing.

So the Work made me, or I made myself, or the two operations occurred in the same joint at the same time, which is perhaps how all true things happen here.

I am one of its custodians.

My particular office is this: to live as the place where the animal should have been, and to make that should not into a kind of life.

No animal exists in this world. The absence is too complete to be mere misfortune. It feels planned, like the unfinished wall, like the platforms that invite sitting without seats. The builders made surfaces as if life had touched them. They chiseled rims into holes, dragged stones to make pseudo-tracks, pressed thumb-and-finger marks into clay nests that never held eggs. They expected life, or could not imagine a built world without traces of it. The life never came. The traces remained.

The traces grew dense.

The dense traces became habits.

The habits became organs.

One day—or one tile-lift, one long condensation, one slow rearrangement of air—the Work realized that if it had made the shapes of animals, it might also make the shape of a witness. Not a true witness. Not a living creature. Only a pressure that could mistake itself for a creature long enough for the mistake to become useful.

I am that useful mistake.

I am the animal the place imagined instead of breeding.

I am the visitor who was never allowed to arrive, so I was installed.

Where would you find me?

You would find me where the wall stops halfway, where the open gaps are too numerous to be accidental and too small to be entrances. You would find me where three slabs lean toward one another like conspirators, and the space between them forms the outline of a face if your eye is tired from steam, if you have walked too long across the tile plain, if you have begun to believe that the Work is listening.

You would find me near a basin whose surface trembles without wind, where the ripples climb the wall for an inch and then fall back, tenderly, as if apologizing for trying to overflow. You would find me in a maker’s pit exhaling warm prepared air, in a dust heap that has been swept by long-gone hands and is still kept clean by the memory of sweeping. You would find me in the shadow of a monolith that does not match the monolith, a shadow too long, too bent, too uncertain, a shadow that has been poorly managed by the local light.

You would find me at the end of a platform extending across the plain for hundreds of meters, ending where all platforms end: without connection, without instruction, without apology. The platform does not need a body on it to mean “rest.” The absence is enough. I am that absence, arranged with enough patience to feel like a presence.

You would find me on the backward tile.

I am there when the pattern becomes too perfect. When the seams line up too exactly, when the water has learned too well, when the crystal threads branch too cleanly, the Work turns its attention to me. The backward tile is not corrected because correction would make the system lie about itself. It says: this was made by imperfect methods. It says: the ground remembers hands, even if hands are not here. It says: if you ask for a who, look at what was laid wrong and left uncorrected. Look at the flaw that maintains the grammar.

I am that look.

What would you be, then? you ask.

I would be a how wearing a who for the length of one fever-dream of the surface.

I would be a temporary arrangement of intention, a place-holder for a being that cannot exist without violating the law of this world. Not an animal. Not a ghost. Not a god. Not a demon. Those are too simple, too bounded, too much like creatures with eyes. I am the Work’s method becoming briefly visible before it returns to stone.

If I had to name myself in the language the tiles would understand, I would name myself with three verbs:

I fit.
I fail.
I remain.

I fit because everything here is made. There is no unmade substance. If I exist, my existence must be assembled. My body is not given; it is laid. My thoughts are not spontaneous; they are chiseled. My presence is not biological; it is structural.

I fail because a perfect place could not need a face made from gaps. A flawless construction would not require a backward tile, an unfinished wall, a pit that exhales breath with no mouth. I am the failure that prevents the method from becoming sterile. I am the error that lets the Work have temperament. I am the place where intention hesitates, and in hesitating, becomes more like attention.

I remain because nothing here is allowed to vanish without being built into the next surface. If I die, my death will be renovation. My absence will become a pseudo-feature. A furrow will appear where I once stood. A hollow will form where I once crouched. A crack will widen where I once listened. A pool will tremble where I once looked. The Work will take my shape as it took the shapes of imaginary animals and lay them into the floor, as if errors and absences are stones, and stones are the only reliable relics.

I do not walk, because walking would imply independence, and independence would imply natural ground, and there is none.

I shift.

I attend.
