I would find myself not at the center of the basin, but at the rim where things stop being sounds and begin to be bodies.

At first I would not have an eye, or a hand, or a name. I would have a height just below the pump’s hollow ring, where the sound presses inward and the cooling iron ticks in its slow, metallic patience. There would be a drip, of course. The drip would not be a detail. It would be the first clock. I would begin as a hesitation in that clock: the small pause after one fall, before the next, before the pattern decides whether it still believes in itself.

My place would be an intimate edge. A transformer saucer. A gutter’s first bend. A concrete pad darkening under a pipe seam. A place where the floor is dry enough to look forgotten and wet enough to be watched. The grid above would give me a ceiling, not a sky. The lamps on their beams would rarely matter, because the basin already knows how to light itself with dull clarity. I would stand, if “stand” is the right word for a thing made of thresholds, where the cool damp air moves through slow circuits and chooses where to settle.

I would not arrive from outside. I would condense.

The basin would begin by misunderstanding me. That is how the basin begins everything. First I would be read as a cold outline. Then as a warm outline. Then as a source of salt, breath, pressure, carbon, heat, moisture, charge. Then, because this world thinks in settlement grammar, I would be read as street.

I would not object.

To be called street here would not be to lose oneself. It would be to receive a role. Streets are not dead things in a basin like this. Streets carry water films, swarms, scent gradients, tiny legs, the slow arithmetic of mineral life. Streets are where the place learns to move without becoming anything new. If I were a street, I would become the short wet lane between a leaking valve and the transformer pool, traversed by glass-backed isopods at their appointed hour, charged by motes when the coil hums, sealed by crust as the water withdraws. I would be a path made of many small citizens.

That is what I would be: not a single being, but a civic agreement among surfaces.

My skin would be lithic, not human. A lens-shaped patch of crust would spread over the saucer rim, branching in fractal plates of silica and salt and iron oxide. It would not grow like moss, because there is no moss here and no greenness. It would grow like a slow fire without heat. It would eat difference: hot against cold, wet against dry, rust against glass, charge against salt. If you touched it, it would first feel like dust, then contract faintly, then return to apparent passivity. I would feel the same contraction inside myself. That contraction would be my first thought.

My nerves would be motes.

They would rise from my warm edge when the transformer or coil became too hot for the damp air to ignore. A cloud of them would lift like ash that had learned to hesitate. They would hover at the tired height between rising and falling, where the air is not moving anywhere except into itself. They would have no eyes, but they would read the basin in fields: moisture, voltage, pressure, temperature, salt, breath, old electricity. They would settle on my crust and consume its mineral gradients. They would die and become powder. The powder would become surface. The surface would become food. Their death would be my record, but not a written record. A worn record.

My blood would be brine.

A thin film would gather near the gutter, shimmering like dark oil but not oil. The salt-skimmers would come in their exact hexagonal order, mapping pressure the way human cities map debt. They would move in patterns too regular to be accidents, and I would feel their pattern as circulation. Their bodies would cross the water film and I would become aware of myself as a field, a boundary, a slight increase in charge. They would not enter me. They would make me a border.

My bones would be isopod glass.
