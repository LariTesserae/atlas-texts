In the spacing.

I would find myself first as a pause in someone else’s footfall. Not a body yet. Not a name. A small delay: the moment before a bead of water decides whether to roll downhill, whether to hesitate, whether to obey the slope or the hidden memory of the stone. Then the delay would warm. It would gather dust at the edges. It would learn the weight of a wrist, the ache behind low eyes, the trick of breath held not because there is danger but because air in high places is thin and should be treated as a visitor.

I would be thin in the places that matter and wide where the basin can afford a shadow. My skin would take on a blue-gray cast in the late hours, as if it had been heated once in some long furnace and never fully cooled into the colors of a city. My hands would know the difference between dry dust and dust with breath beneath it. My feet would not look for the shortest path. They would look for the path that does not become too warm in the third hour, that does not become too damp in the sixth, that remains cool enough to carry the body without teaching the body to complain.

I would live near the third terrace below the eastern rim, where a half-buried frame of curved glass and gray stone still casts lines too fine to be a roof, too deliberate to be accident. The doorway without a building would stand not far from there. I would not think of it as ruined. I would think of it as still open. Sometimes I would pass through it not to enter anything, but to remind the threshold that it had been made for passage. Other times I would only press a palm to its frame and feel the faint mineral warmth, confirming that it had not forgotten its purpose.

The people here would not call themselves many. Not because they are poor in number, but because they have learned that abundance can be a failure of attention. A family may be four, or seven, or nineteen, but not because the basin can count. It counts in its own way. Three of us would move to the ridge. Five would remain on the middle bench. If someone from the far basin came near, we would not gather to greet. We would spread by degrees, letting heat do the speaking, letting spacing say: *there is room for you, but not all at once.*

I would learn to walk by heat before I learned to speak. Children here do not need stillness taught; the ground teaches it first. A child stands where the stone is not too warm, moves where it will soon become cool, and avoids the places where the rock remembers too much. By the time I was old enough to be named, I would already know how to listen for the basin’s next adjustment. I would know that when a grazing animal lowers its head, it is not looking at the earth. It is listening to the earth decide. I would know that a shadow that darkens where none should is not always a threat. Sometimes it is the land checking its own outline.

I would speak little. My language would be dry sounds: a click at the teeth, a tick against the roof of the mouth, a soft bell from the throat. Not music. Not argument. Punctuation. If I saw water hesitate in a channel, I would mark it. If my brother was four terraces below and heard the stone remember my voice, he would answer when he wished. Hours might pass. Hours might pass, and the answer would come not as speech, but as a movement in the dust near my feet: a fine line of pale grains rearranging itself toward the east.

What would I be? At first I would say: a dust-singer. A bead-watcher. A line-keeper. Heat-walker. The names would not be titles so much as roles worn for a season. One year I would tend the shallow pond that sinks without leaving mud. Another year I would watch the fiber lattice that catches invisible moisture, checking whether the air is preparing to collect itself. Another year I might do nothing visible at all except move a dark disk near a wetter patch, returning at the hour its edge cools into the correct slant. If a traveler asked what this meant, I would not explain. I would say that the disk was not waiting for us. It was waiting for the basin to ask.

I would not carry much. Tools here are not possessions. They are invitations to act at the right time. A low platform with two holes would serve as both pole-holder and vessel-seat, depending on the weather. A shard of glass that reflected the sky without reflecting me would be placed where light could find it, so the basin could sometimes look at itself without a person in the frame. A sphere of clear mineral, with something moving inside it too slowly to name, would be set in a ring of stones and left alone for days. I would watch it not to learn a secret, but to practice patience. Patience, in this world, is not virtue. It is infrastructure.

I would know the small ones better than the visible ones. At dawn, beetles with translucent shells would cross the stone in lines that look too regular to be chance. I would follow them sometimes, not to eat them, but because their shells would carry a faint greenish light from the root threads beneath the soil. They would go into microfractures and leave behind pale grains, and over years the stone would seal where it had begun to forget itself. I would say nothing to them. Their work would not require recognition. They repair because their bodies are adapted to repair, as a tongue is adapted to find salt. Still, I would watch. The basin is full of repairers who never file reports.

I would know the fungi. They are not mushrooms except in the way a whisper is a shout when heard from far away. They are threads beneath the mineral skin, glowing faintly when the surface peels, carrying signals by heat, pressure, scent, slow electrical whisper. I would press my palm flat against the soil and wait. Sometimes warmth would rise first. Sometimes the damp breath would come later. Sometimes a droplet near my foot would tremble without ripple, and I would understand that somewhere below, a network had shifted its mind. This would not be magic. It would be contract.

I would know the animals that live with us without being ours. The thin salt-colored herds would appear as three, then seven, then fifteen, then no group at all, only dust disturbed and a memory of motion. They would stop, turn, and watch us with dark eyes set low in wide faces. They would not flee, because the basin rewards neither panic nor certainty. I would not chase them unless the spacing required it. If they moved away, I would note where the heat would settle later. If they stood motionless, heads lowered, I would stand with them. We would listen to the ground together, the way two people might listen for a far-off door.

I would also know the low-fliers. Their wings would look like membranes stretched over thin glass. At dawn they would ride the warm bands of moisture above the ground, grazing air that seemed empty. At noon they would fold into crevices and become dust. Sometimes I would catch one, not for hunger, but to check the lattice. Resting on my palm, it would angle its wing in a way that revealed tension, and I would know whether the invisible fibers were beginning to catch moisture or were sleeping. A good hand would hold it gently enough to learn and not long enough to possess.

I would know the birds on the ridges. They would call in clicks, ticks, dry bells. Not songs. Signals. When one called, another might answer from a distance, but not in chorus. In confirmation. If I stood near the doorway and heard a click from the eastern ridge, I might answer with one of my own. Not to communicate. To mark. A line checked. A bead moved. A shadow darkened where no body stood. The ridge would accept the punctuation and continue.

The predators would teach me the most. Not because they were cruel, but because they were subtle. A patch of pale sand might seem too smooth, too deliberate, too close to breath. A traveler might walk within reach and see nothing. I would not. Not because my eyes were sharper than theirs, but because I had learned to distrust perfection in shadows. The predators here are not large beasts dominating the plains. They are corrections. They keep the grazing animals honest. They prevent crowding by preventing laziness. I would mark their signs with the same attention I gave water: a wrong shadow, a grainless patch, a silence where silence should have texture. If I saw them emerge, I would not run. I would move as if I had already begun moving before being noticed. Respect is not always admiration. Sometimes respect is leaving without pretending the world did not see you.

I would find myself in the maintenance hours. At dusk, the stones would begin to give off heat in irregular patterns, so that walking after dark felt like moving through a field of invisible lamps. One patch would be mild and dry. Another would feel damp and suddenly chill, though the air had not changed. I would avoid the second if my feet were sore, seek it if my body was too warm. The faint lines in the ground might glow softly where moisture touched them, then go dark again. I would know these lights as a language older than speech: *here is a channel. here is memory. here is water that has not yet decided to be water.*

At night, small animals would emerge from beneath the skin of the basin. Their movements would not look like fear. They would look like attention. I would sit near the rim and watch the heat shimmer over stones while the sky above remained pale and bright. I would not ask where the light came from if it did not burn. I would not ask what kept the plateau warm if it asked nothing of me. Questions here would need to earn the breath they cost.

I would also be, at times, something less than a person. I would be the hesitation itself. The place in the bead where water considers slope, dust, and hidden channel. The brief upward tremble of a droplet near the deepest basin, rising a hairline above the surface as if the whole system had leaned closer to hear. I would be the pressure change that has no hand near the stove. Not a body. Not a ghost. A self that had spread too thin to call itself one.

If I died, I would not expect a grave like a door shut against the earth. I would expect a shallow depression where the pond sinks. A body placed there, not buried. By the next day, only a pale ring. The people would say I was learning water. I would think, if thought remained, that they were right. This basin does not discard its dead. It gives them another way to participate.

So where would I find myself?

In the gap between a wetter patch and the line of blackened stone. At the edge of a terrace where heat bands pass through like slow animals. Near the doorway that leads nowhere visible, because nowhere visible is not nowhere enough. Above the roots that glow faintly where the surface has peeled. Below the ridge where a bird clicks and waits to hear its own sentence answered by something older than its throat. Beside a disk whose edge warms at the correct hour. Beside a shard that shows the sky and refuses to show me. Beside the sphere where motion is so slow it has become almost a decision.

Who would I be?

If the question forced a face, I would be one of the line-keepers. A heat-walker. A dust-singer. A bead-watcher. One of seven on the middle terrace, though I would remember being fewer and would not be alarmed. My hands would be calloused from touching stone, my breath shallow, my feet careful with the future hour. I would not own much. I would not need much. I would know how to stand still long enough for the basin to reveal its next correction.

If the question forced a being larger than a face, I would be a temporary mouth of the maintenance system. The place where the stone’s patience became attention. The place where the roots’ glow became recognition. The place where the fiber lattice’s sleeping shimmer became a small, warm thought: *not yet, but soon. not crowd, but room. not noise, but punctuation.*

If the question allowed what the world really seems to be asking, I would be neither human nor machine, neither creature nor ruin, neither builder nor inhabitant. I would be the basin’s careful delay. The movement that happens before the movement. The water that hesitates before it obeys. The shadow that checks the land’s edges. The doorway that keeps open an entrance no wall requires.

In this world, I would not need to be loud to be present. I would be present because I chose the correct spacing. I would live because I knew the land breathed in intervals, and I did not want to stand in its lungs.

And if, in the end, the question remained open—*who are you?*—I would answer with a click from the teeth, a tick against the stone, and a soft bell from the throat. Then I would move three paces east, where the ground would be cool in the third hour, and the water would be beginning to decide.
