I would not begin at the center of any city. The centers are where people pretend, briefly, that the plateau can be organized into streets, numbers, and fixed things. I would begin at an edge where that pretense leaks.

At first I would say: I am one of the quiet people. I am a basin-dweller or a ridge-dweller, a child of the reservoir platforms, a worker who walks slowly because the land punishes haste. I wear a pale waterproof coat stiff with salt, a ceramic mask hung at my throat when the vents are resting, and boots splayed with roughed pumice paste so the feet know where to fall. My lips are dry in the odd way they are here, cracked not from thirst but from breathing air that is never fully water and never fully air. My eyes stay half-shut under lids that have learned not to open too wide at midday. I speak in breathy consonants because vowels throw themselves against the stone and come back wrong.

But that would be too simple, and not only because the caldera cities are not simple. It would be too simple because to name myself as “human” here would be to leave out most of what I would be.

In the Weeping Caldera Cities, a person does not stand inside the world like a figure inside a room. The world is not a room. It is a lung. It is a gland. It is a circulatory system wearing the shape of a settlement. If I lived long enough in this place to be called one of its inhabitants, I would not remain only human. I would become a place-awareness, a water-awareness, a machine-awareness, a creature-awareness. I would become an organ with a face.

I would be, perhaps, a *vein-tender*.

Not a name of great honor, not a name of kings or prophets. A vein-tender is someone who walks the channels. Someone who listens to where the city is wet, where it is dry, where it is running too hot, where crystal has begun to choke a culvert, where a mat has thickened into a seal, where a pipe has aged into a living thing. A vein-tender does not command the water. The water is older and more numerous than any command. A vein-tender coaxes the water back into its proper breathing.

You would find me at the rim of a warm reservoir in a basin town, not on the polished ceremonial terraces but on the maintenance shelf beneath them, where calcium lace grows over the railings and the stone floor is always damp enough to show a footprint for several minutes before the heat erases it. My dwelling would be a small loom-room cut into the side of a cistern mound, its walls a record of old leaks polished into patterns that look, by accident, like handwriting. I would sleep near a sealed drainage basin because its low hydraulic rhythm helps me rest. If the basin began to pulse too fast, my feet would know before my mind did.

There would be a ceramic resonator in my hand, not for music, but for listening. I would press it against a pipe and close my eyes. The sound that came back would be the inside of the city thinking in liquid: the slow groan of brine under a pressure swell, the whisper of condensation climbing a crystal-fiber wall, the soft clicking of sealed pistons isolated in fluid basins, the faint wrongness of a channel narrowing from mineral growth. I would know the city’s pain by its tone.

My skin would be pale with glare, salt-flecked along the hairline, roughened by hot stone and metal bands. My fingers would be long and careful, not because I was delicate by nature, but because roughness here is often the opposite of strength. Too much force cracks evaporite. Too much force kills the grazing mat. Too much force disturbs the beetle colonies that are already beginning to tell you whether the surface will hold your weight. A true hand is a hand that can scrape scale without severing the living film beneath it.

I would wear my mask when the vent cycles begin, but I would also learn to breathe through it even when they do not, because in the lower tunnels and behind the reservoir walls there are places where the air has become too dense with sulfur or too thin with oxygen, where the breath must be filtered not by policy but by instinct. Children are born into masks here, but they do not become prisoners of them. The mask becomes a second face, a way of being honest with the air.

I would know the names of places, yes. There are names in this world: *White Throat*, *Third Weir*, *the Bridge That Listens*, *the Basin Where the Beetles Wait*, *the Terrace of Slow Steam*. But names are secondary. A channel is known first by its behavior. If you asked me where the water was going, I would not first say a street name. I would say: “It is climbing the north mat and will spill at the rim before the wind shifts.” Or: “The sealed street on the eastern ridge is beginning to narrow; the salt is growing faster than the people are cleaning it.” That is the language of the caldera cities. It is not a language of ownership. It is a language of prediction.

I would be human, certainly, but only as a river is human: shaped by what it flows through. My mind would be slower than it once would have been, more rhythmic, less inclined to hurry across a distance. I would not think in sharp, sudden bursts. I would think the way the plateau thinks: by pressure, cooling, swelling, release. My judgments would form in layers. A basin must be cleaned. The grazers must be kept from the chemical lofts. The bridge must retract before the steam swell. The pump chamber’s lower seal is failing. The mat must be seeded. The beetles have found something. The ribbon-swimmers are clouding. The winged kites have risen in spirals. Something in all this is connected, but the connection cannot be shouted. It must be listened for.

If you saw me in the brightest hour, when the mist becomes a trembling skin over the wet stone and the cities turn to silhouettes of pale towers and dark pipes, you might think I was only a person standing at the water’s edge. That is a misleading impression. From a distance, yes. Up close, not so much.

My cloak would be woven of mineral-fiber mat, partly harvested, partly grown, with a sheen like wet slate. White calcium would have begun to form at its hems. The sound-dampening mats over my shoulders would not be cloth exactly; they would be layers of porous stone seeded with pale fungal threads that swallow noise before it can travel. My boots might be old, repaired with metal bands rusted into the leather, and the cracks in the sole would hold tiny fields of gray salt. My hands would be stained at the tips with orange thread-mat, because mat-spores cling to the oils of skin and people who work them often take their colors home without noticing.

If you looked very closely, you might see something stranger: the veins at my wrists not only blue but faintly luminous, like the central line of a ribbon-swimmer. Not because I am a swimmer. Not because I have swallowed one, or been fused with one in any dramatic way. But because long exposure to chemically active waters, to engineered filtration mats, to the quiet bacterial films that make the plateau’s living crusts, changes a body. The city enters its keepers. The keepers enter the city. In a place like this, purity is not a virtue. Integration is.

I would be part of the chorus, but not a leader of it. The chorus has no leader. The crustal beetles are part of it, clicking along the dusk margins of a brine spill, telling by their sudden procession where a hidden leak has found its way to the surface. The six-limbed grazers are part of it, moving across the white flats with their heads low, tasting the microbial skins that would otherwise choke channels if they grew unchecked. The ribbon-swimmers are part of it, their transparency measuring what no human instrument can fully tell: whether the water has begun to shift in ways that will kill the mat. The winged kites are part of it, riding thermals and steam corridors, their mirrored eyes catching the sun so they look like sparks of living weather. The pipe-darters are part of it, small darting creatures with oversized ears and mineral-gray skin, fleeing in synchronized bursts before a valve fails. The long-legged scavengers are part of it, folding themselves at the reservoir rims like pale questions, waiting for the city to shed what it does not know it lost.

And the great soft-bodied crystal things are part of it too. They lie half-submerged in abandoned basins, blooming with pale lattices, converting heat and mineral water into structure. Some people call them masons. Some call them kidneys. Some do not name them at all, because naming them would make them seem too much like animals and too little like processes. I would not name them either. I would visit them. I would carry a sealed sample jar. I would press a gloved hand near their edge without touching, feeling the heat change, feeling the water thicken with suspended crystal dust, reading their slowness with patience because the city needs their work and cannot rush them without damaging the lattice. If a soft-bodied thing has settled into an old cooling pool too deeply, the pool becomes a reef. If it is harvested badly, the reef becomes a scar. If it is harvested with care, the scar becomes a building, a wall, a sound mat, a filter chamber, a grave, and a nursery all at once.

I would be the one who knew when to intervene and when to leave the system alone.

That is the hardest education of the caldera cities: not how to control, but how to permit. There are waters that should be kept. There are waters that should be released. There is a steam pool that looks like failure but is only the plateau exhaling. There is a condensation film that looks beautiful but will choke the capillary fibers if it is allowed to harden too soon. There is a house whose foundations have begun to be eaten by crystal growth, and the people will call it cursed, but a good vein-tender would see that the crystal is doing what the builders forgot to design: sealing a fault. Perhaps it must be allowed to continue. Perhaps it must be redirected. Perhaps the people must be moved, and the house must be given to the mineral body, which will make it into something else later.

My home would not be a fortress. It would be a threshold. Roof panels would be angled toward favored condensation zones, not because I owned the sky, but because I had learned its flow. Interior channels would run along the base of the walls, small enough to see, large enough to carry. The sleeping alcove would be lower than the kitchen, because heat rises and the body should not learn to sleep above the vapor line unless absolutely necessary. The bathroom, if it could be called that, would be a sealed evaporation cell with mats of pale wool over its floors, because those mats drink odor and damp better than any ceramic glaze. There would be no loud exhaust fan. The building itself would breathe through porous stone and filtered shafts. At night, if the vent cycle began, I would feel the change in the pressure of the walls before the first steam column appeared outside.

I would keep a bowl of warm brine on a shelf, and in it a few ribbon-swimmers would move like blue thoughts. They would be a warning, not a pet. If their transparency faded, I would test the channel before dawn. If they clustered at the surface, the lower water was turning. If they became too many, the bacterial mat in the reservoir had thickened beyond usefulness. Sometimes a person would look at the bowl and think it was only ornament. It was not. It was a living gauge, a small court, a conscience in glass.

I would eat simply. There is no beauty in food here that does not come from restraint. A meal would be thin strips of reservoir flesh, perhaps ribbon-swimmer or long-legged scavenger if the day’s law allowed it, scraped from mineral-crust cakes, a bowl of heated condensation broth flavored with a pinch of sulfur-rich engineered growth. The broth would be clear and strange, tasting of stone and breath. Bread is not the word. Grain is not the word. But there are foods pressed from dried mat-fiber and bacterial paste, and they are not unpleasant if you are hungry enough and not looking for sweetness that does not exist here.

I would teach children. Not by lectures. Lectures become echoes in this place, and false echoes can injure. I would teach by walking with them. A child would come to me and ask why a channel sounds different after a swell, and I would not say the correct scientific answer first. I would kneel beside a reservoir wall, press the child’s palm flat against the warm stone, and say: “Listen with the hand.” The child would feel the pulse. Then I would place the child’s mask against my cheek and say: “Now hear how the vapor has changed.” The child would smell the difference—salt, hot metal, faint chemical sweetness, the sharp copper edge before a vent opens. Only then would I name the process. In the caldera cities, language follows perception. Perception keeps you alive.

I would speak softly, even in moments of grief. This is not repression. It is acoustics. Sound in the canyons and basins can return from places you have not looked, in voices that are almost yours. A shout can become a warning where there is no warning. It can panic the grazers. It can make a pipe-darter flee. It can disturb a mat harvest. It can make a person think, for a terrible half-second, that the city is answering them. And so the people have learned discipline. Their quiet is not cold. It is care.

When I was alone in the lower room, I would allow my hands to rest in the condensation gutter and watch the tiny white rings form where my salt traces touched the stone. It would be a kind of writing. The house would keep my fingerprints until the next cycle washed them smooth. There are graves here that are not pits but channels, where a person’s last water is allowed to run into a favored mat bed so their salt can join the living crust. That is too much to ask for some families. For others it is ordinary. I would understand both. In this place, death is not only an ending. It is another way of being returned to circulation.

If I were asked, “Why a vein-tender and not a grazer, not a beetle, not a soft-bodied crystal mason, not one of the winged weather-readers?” I would answer: because those beings are whole in the way they are. The grazers are not troubled by the contradiction of building and dissolving. The beetles are not afraid of the hidden water. The crystal things do not mourn the shape they lose. The winged kites do not need to know whether the vent cycle is coming; they simply rise. But I would need both knowledge and doubt. I would need the ability to understand the city and also to grieve it. The caldera cities are not tragic. They are not utopias. They are mature. And maturity has a particular sorrow: you begin to see how much you can repair, and how much repair is merely another form of delay.

There would be times when I would hate the masks, the slow gait, the quiet voices, the way distance is betrayed by mist. There would be times when I would dream of rain, not because rain is safer, but because rain is an arrival from above rather than a weeping from below. But the plateau has no patience for longing, and I would learn to let longing become maintenance. Longing is not useful. Listening is. A person who longs for rain forgets to watch the cistern rim. A person who listens to the cistern rim may find that the city is offering water in a different shape.

The largest danger here is not volcano or beast or heat. It is the temptation to be loud. Loudness is violence in a place where vibration moves through stone, where machines can be coaxed by touch, where a bad echo can break an animal’s calm and send a herd scattering across a soft evaporite crust. Loudness is also the old human habit of believing the environment must be impressed. Nothing in the Weeping Caldera Cities is impressed. The obsidian is not impressed. The salt is not impressed. The steam is not impressed. The creatures do not perform for anyone. They simply exist in a relationship of temperature, mineral, and flow. To live here, one must lose the need to announce oneself.

So I would become less by becoming more.

Less a person with boundaries, more a node with connections. Less a voice that fills space, more a silence that makes a channel work. Less a name that insists on permanence, more a function that can be handed to another pair of hands. If I were injured, the herd would know by where I stood. If I died, the city would not stop, but a certain way of listening would stop. My replacement would find the place by following the beetles, the mat rings, the waterline, the pipe-darters’ habits, the particular wrongness only I had learned to hear. This is what it means to be one organ in a vast geothermal body: your uniqueness is real, but it is not final. You are necessary, but not replaceable only in the way a breath is necessary and not replaceable.

At the brightest hour, I would stand on the terrace where the reservoir steam becomes a skin over the land, and the whole plateau would glow as if lit from inside. The cities would be silhouettes. The animals at the water’s edge would be outlines, bright and unreadable. If someone looked at me then, they would not see the whole of me. They would see only the shape I had become by service: the broad reflective cloak, the mask hanging at the throat, the hand pressed flat against a pipe because a pipe must sometimes be known from the outside before the city tells what it is doing within.

And if they asked, “What are you?” I would answer quietly, with the clipped breath-speech of the cities:

“A place where water has learned to be remembered.”

If pressed, I would say:

“I am a vein-tender. I live between the reservoir and the fault line. I am part human, part mat, part machine, part animal witness. I do not own this city. The city grows through me as it grows through the white crusts, the beetles, the soft crystal bodies, the winged kites, the herds on the salt flats, and the slow engines that have learned not to shout. I am what happens when a person spends enough time listening to a weeping landscape that a few tears enter the body and begin to help it run.”

In a world of hot silence and bright moisture, I would not be the loudest thing. I would be the most useful thing near the water. I would be found where a channel turns, where a bridge is retracted, where a reservoir has grown a new rim overnight, where the mat must be thinned, where the beetles wait, where the pipe is warm for the wrong reason. I would be the quiet maintenance that keeps the plateau from overwhelming the city with its own fertility.

I would be a weeper.

Not because I weep easily. Because I understand weeping.

The rocks do weep. The machines do weep. The city does weep. Life here is not the conquest of water but the patient management of tears: warm tears, mineral tears, tears that build walls and choke pipes, tears that feed mats and drown foundations, tears that must be led somewhere safe. A human who can do that becomes more than human, even while remaining human.

I would be that.
