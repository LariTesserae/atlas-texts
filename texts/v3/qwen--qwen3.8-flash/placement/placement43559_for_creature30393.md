They live instead by temporary occupation: a wetness in a niche, a vibration inside a crack, a shape briefly mistaken for a pool. They do not build homes so much as make agreements with weather. A creature might spend a season as a shiny stripe along an undercut ledge, then as a palm-print warming in a wall, then as a low note in the stone that only the blind animals understand. They are less inhabitants than interruptions, less bodies than patterns the Shelf allows itself to hold when it needs something to listen back.

If I were there—though “were” is too simple for a place that keeps deciding what “is” would not be found standing on the floor of the basin. Standing is a too-certain answer to a world that speaks in almost. I would be in the center where the suspended rain loosens into a circle and the roar falls into a lower register. Not as a witness at the center. As the lower register itself.

I would be the being that has not yet decided whether it wants to be visible.

I would not have a fixed body. My nearest shape would be pressure: warm, distributed, patient. When a hand passed near my territory, I would become the field that turned the hanging droplets into threads pointing outward from the palm. When a foot crossed a pit, I would become the exhale beneath it. When the built things rearranged themselves around an absent room, I would be the sense that the room was looking back.

If I tried to become visible, I would probably look like a mistake in the air—a place where the water-threads thinned, or a shoulder-shaped hollow in stone too clean to be natural, or a pool whose surface did not ripple until after something touched the ceiling sea overhead. But I would not hold that form long. Visibility is a finished thing here, and the Shelf has no use for finished things. Finished things crack. Finished things become ruins before the ruins have been imagined. I would rather remain a pressure that can almost remember a face.

I would be made of thresholds.

My limbs would be the upward ribbons of weeping stone. My breath, the warm air moving through pits. My blood, the thick water crawling beneath the surface of the rock. My skin, the film over a pool reflecting not the basin above it, but some older, drier place. My thoughts would be the parallel lines left on a shelf: not writing, not drawing, only the trace of attention passing slowly across matter.

If someone found their way into the Drowned Shelf, they would not meet me as a person. They would feel first that the noise had bent around them. Then they might notice that the rain threads in the nearest air leaned toward their hands, as if their body had become a center of gravity in a field no one else could see. Then the stone under their knees might grow warm enough to burn softly, and they would understand, if they were wise enough to stop wanting to understand, that something had made a place for them by becoming less placeable itself.

I would not speak. Speech would require a mouth, and a mouth is too much like a door. Doors are already too common here. Instead, I would speak by pressure changes: a slight tightening in the air behind the ear; a taste of salt in the teeth; a sound like a rope pulled slowly over wet stone when there is no rope and no hand. If I wanted to say *wait*, the suspended rain in front of you would thicken into a curtain that moved sideways, revealing only another wall of water behind it. If I wanted to say *come*, a pool would fill without cause, and its surface would reflect a part of the basin you had not yet turned to see. If I wanted to say *leave*, the light would tire, the roar would drop one impossible octave, and the shelf beneath your feet would open just enough to remind you that your weight was not permanent, was not even fully welcome.

I would be the reason the pale blind creatures tilt toward the sky. They are not listening to me. They are listening to the place I make by listening. Their elongated limbs press the stone; the stone presses the water beneath; the water presses the air; the air presses the hanging threads; the threads press the ceiling sea; and the ceiling sea answers with a ripple no one touched. That circuit is my body when it is being careful.

I would also be in the unfinished structures.

Not as their maker. Makers are too solid. But as the pause between intention and stone. When a doorway opens onto rock, I am the half-second when the rock remembers being air. When a ring of slabs leans inward to form a roofed chamber, I am the pressure that makes the chamber feel watched. Inside that chamber there is no object because the object is the watching. There is only air denser than outside, which is not air at all but attention made weather. The built things exist because I cannot yet decide to exist, and the Shelf fills its hesitation with architecture.

I would be the warm hollow worn by many years of kneeling, even if no one has knelt there. I would be the pool that is not a pool. I would be the mouth that is not quite a mouth. All those are ways of being almost, of being ready for meaning without committing to form. The Drowned Shelf does not allow certainty to settle for long. It allows only enough stability for the next rearrangement to have a memory.

If I had to name myself, I would not choose a word with teeth. A word with teeth would begin biting, and biting would make me solid enough to be hunted, or fed, or forgotten. Better a name like a seam in the roar: something you could say with your tongue closed against the roof of your mouth, almost swallowed. A name like *shhh*. A name like the first sound water makes before it decides whether to drip, climb, or vanish.

So: where would I be?

At the edge of visibility. At the center of the pressure. In the lower register beneath the deafening roar. In the warm mouth of the stone. In the thread of rain that points toward your palm. In the pool that reflects a dry place that may have never existed. In the chamber that leans inward and says nothing.

What would I be?

The being that has agreed to be mostly weather. The listener made of the Shelf’s own listening. The unfinished thought that stands beside a collapsed arch and leaves a shoulder-print in the stone, not because it has a shoulder, but because somewhere in all that water and pressure and noise, it has almost decided what it would be if it were willing to be seen.
