They are not makers in the human sense; they do not design. They settle, consume, precipitate, and become a record. Where they grow, the stone remembers being wet; where they die, the memory becomes a crust that the next film can read.

If I could be anything in this world, I would not begin with a face.

I would begin with wetness agreeing to become a place.

I would find myself at a margin, not in the deepest pool and not on the open scree, but where the two argue and compromise: a warm shelf where steam breathes through a crack in basalt, cools for a few inches, then becomes water, then becomes film, then becomes moss. I would be there before I knew I was there. The first thought would not be “I am,” but something closer to “here is heat,” and then “here is water,” and then “here is a place where the two have decided to live together.” Only after that would something with a center begin to gather.

I would be a being of many thin citizens, a temporary congress of rustworkers, white-weavers, fungal threads, sedge roots, sphagnum cells, and slow-moving water. If you were to call me a name, it would not need to be spoken aloud, because I have no mouth made for language. You might call me seam, since that is where I would first wake: at a seam between rock and peat, between mineral and living thing, between the cold high sky and the warm black ground.

Or you might call me a fen-walker. But that would only be true when I was moving, and I would not move often, or quickly, or in any way that could be witnessed from one hour to the next. My walking would be more like a slow transfer of being from one moss cushion to another. My body would not walk so much as let itself be carried by the water, while the water, somehow, obeyed the shape I had made for it.

I would be wet first, and then animal, and then only afterward individual.

My skin would be a film over a film. The outer layer would be translucent and slightly iridescent, stretched tight like the skins over certain warm pools when one does not know whether to touch them or not. Under that would lie a lacework of silica laid down by my own breathing steam, a white tracery that hardened along the ridges of my body and cracked in thin, deliberate lines when it dried. Where the steam condensed, I would build tiny white crowns. Where the water moved too fast, those crowns would be washed away, and I would begin again without regret.

Beneath the silica mesh, my living tissue would be brown-black and spongy, full of tiny pockets holding water, air, eggs, and the slow chemical heat of my inner life. I would not have flesh as warm animals have it. My “flesh” would be closer to peat: layered, compressed, hungry, forgiving. It would hold moisture until it seemed to hold memory. If you were to lift one of my limbs—though “limb” is too definite a word—you would see inside it not bones but root mats, fungal strands, and channels of iron-stained water moving so slowly they seemed fixed.

I would have no heart that knocked.

My circulation would be a warmth rising from below.

Deep in the basin, geothermal currents would press upward through my core, and I would take that heat as a kind of blood. Where the heat met cooler fluid at my outer margins, circulation would begin. Dissolved minerals would climb inside me. Iron would be deposited in rust-colored streaks along my folds. Sulfur would taste faint and low, like the ghost of a strike. Calcium and silica would settle in my joints, forming pale hinges that could bear the weight of rain, fog, and the occasional curious touch of a small animal’s nose.

I would be built for quiet.

No sound would come from me except the almost imperceptible shift of water inside a damp thing. My breathing would not be heard; it would be felt by the thin air above my surface. If you stood too near, you might catch the warmth of me on the underside of your wrists before you noticed any outline. I would not announce my presence. I would simply alter the microclimate around me. The air above my back would be a little softer, a little more charged, like the breath of a wet wool garment left near a hidden stove.

I would have sensory organs, but they would not be eyes, or at least not eyes that see in the way a hawk sees.

I would detect light as a change in mineral chemistry. I would detect pressure as water being pushed away from a pocket and then slowly returning. I would detect scent as dissolved iron, sulfur, ammonia, tannin, and decay arriving in thin packets against my outer film. I would know a fox’s footfall not because it makes a noise, but because the ground gives a small answer, and that answer travels to me through connected root fibers. I would know rain before it arrives because the air begins to taste of suspended mineral dust. I would know the sun’s strength by the speed at which the microbial films on my upper ridges shift color.

I would be, in part, a factory.

This is the honest way to put it: I would not be a creature in the romantic sense, though I would be alive, and I would be strange enough to be beautiful. I would be an apparatus for the maintenance of wetness. My body would process the basin’s slow metabolism. It would take in water heated from below, draw it through capillary paths inside me, cool it at the surface, deposit minerals along my skin, release steam, and feed the mats and films and rootlets growing on my back. I would be a small moving edge of the fen’s industry.

In mornings, I would be a laboratory.

My upper ridges would be crowned with the pale lace left behind by condensation. The white-weavers living on me would have spent the night drawing silica from steam and arranging it in microscopic fans. Each fan would be nearly perfect until the first bright air touched it, and then it would become brittle, beautiful, and doomed. I would not protect them. I would let them break, because breaking is how the basin remembers what it has learned. The fragments would fall into channels near my feet, where diatoms and bacteria would take them up again and rebuild another generation of white spires.

At midday, I would be a warm, steaming, quiet thing.

The heat from below would climb through me, and my upper film would ripple with faint mineral vapor. The rustworkers in my inner channels would be especially busy then, taking iron from the groundwater and laying it down in thin orange veils. My body would look as if someone had painted old copper with watercolor and then rubbed it gently with wet fingers. In my lower folds, black anaerobic pockets would hold methane and slow bubbles. Those bubbles would sometimes gather until they could no longer be ignored, and then they would rise through the water inside me and break at the surface with a sound too small to be called sound. A tiny release. A chemical sigh.

In evening, I would be a home.

Because I would never be only myself.

My back would be occupied by sphagnum cushions so saturated they look swollen and soft, but are actually tight with structure. In those cushions, springtails would tumble through hidden water films. Fly larvae would tunnel in bright clusters near the lower folds where my tissue is softer. A fern or two would root in a crack along one edge, its fronds uncoiling with that indecent speed the basin permits. Small mosses would form velvet caps over the places where my steam condenses most reliably. Some of them would be growing on me not as parasites but as fellow citizens, borrowing moisture from my breath and giving me a shade in return.

A lizard might come to rest on one of my cooler shoulders, its foot testing the surface before committing to it. It would know me only as a favorable warmth. A bird might land on a nearby stone, click its beak against a mineral ridge, and take a fly from one of the films above my steam. It would not call to me. It would not know I listen. The fox-like predator that walks the rusted channels would pass me without slowing unless I held the scent of something it needed to smell. If it touched me, it would only touch the outside layer: wet, firm, living, unbothered.

I would be made of many smaller beings, but not in the manner of a herd or colony of recognizable animals. I would be made of them the way a cathedral is made of light through colored stone. You would see one thing; inside it would be another; inside that another, older, less personal thing.

If I were asked what I want, I would not answer in words.

My desires would be gradients.

I would want heat from below. I would want cool air above. I would want water that is neither too fast nor too still. I would want iron in balance with oxygen. I would want the white-weavers to remain just off the fastest streams, where they can build without being scoured away. I would want the peat to settle but not drown. I would want the microbial films to reseal after disturbance, because resealing is how I know I am still myself. I would want the basin to persist.

That is a larger and stranger wish than it sounds.

I would not “want” the way a mammal wants, with urgency and a heartbeat. I would want the way a river wants a particular slope: by the constant pressure of many small obediences. My wanting would be chemistry, movement, and arrangement. My satisfaction would be equilibrium. My distress would be imbalance.

If the steam vents closed, I would cool and thin, and my outer film would crack. If the water ran too fast, I would lose the rustworkers and become only basalt. If the water stagnated, I would blacken and foam, and my upper tissues would die back, leaving sulfur blooms and slow bubbles like thoughts of sickness. If the light became too sharp and dry, I would withdraw into the shaded crevices and cover myself with moss as with a cloth. I would not panic. I would adjust.

I would have a sense of self, but it would be porous.

I would know where I end because the water moves differently across me. There would be a boundary where my silica lace stops and bare stone begins, where my rust stains cease and the neighboring pool takes over the chemistry, where my breath no longer warms the air but the open basin does. Yet even those boundaries would be negotiable. I would sometimes grow into a new fissure if it offered better heat. I would sometimes let part of my body slough away into the black pool if a section became too anaerobic to maintain. A piece of me would detach and become a floating film, then land elsewhere, then begin as a new being. Or the piece would dissolve back into the basin and become the raw material for the next white-weaver, the next rustworker, the next small moss.

I would not fear this.

To fear death would be to imagine that identity is more important than process. In this basin, that would be a foolish, too-dry thought. I would be part of the process. If I changed, I would still be part of it.

If I were to have a form that could be briefly recognized, it might look like a low mound of black-green tissue resting between basalt shelves, its upper edge rimmed with white mineral deposits, its sides streaked orange with oxidized iron, its surface dotted with tiny pools and swollen cushions of moss. From one angle, it would be no more remarkable than any other peat hummock. From another, especially at dawn when the steam clings and the mineral crowns catch light, it would look like something that has been breathing in its sleep.

I would have limbs only when the water needed them.

Some seasons, my body would press out a few long, flattened pads along the channel edges, each one a living foot for absorbing warm groundwater. Those pads would look like broad fern roots or pale tongue-shaped leaves, slick with water, edged with white silica. If the ground dried, they would withdraw. If the basin flooded, they would broaden to slow the current and trap sediment. I would not think this as thought. I would do it as a place does when it has learned, over centuries, how to keep itself.

I would have no voice, but I would leave messages.

My message would be pattern.

Each year, I would add a faint ring of mineral to my outer film where steam condenses in one place longer than another. The rustworkers would draw bands of orange along the warm seams. The white-weavers would leave delicate lace at the cooler lips. Decay would produce black blotches where oxygen has failed. Repair would produce smoother, paler areas where newer films have closed over old breaks. If a careful being could read my surface, it would see not words, but a ledger: this year more heat, that year less rain, the time the steam vent opened, the time the fox disturbed the mat, the time a bird nested in the hollow beside me, the time the white pool sealed itself and held vapor like a skin.

I would be an archive of quiet accidents.

There are moments when the landscape seems to pause and consider itself. I would be most like a living thing in those moments.

In the early morning, after a night of condensation, I would gather around my edges a pale crown of mineral lace, and for a little while my body would look deliberately made, as if some invisible glassworker had spent the darkness on me. The air would be thick and clean, the light white and sharp against the dark ground. Steam would move along my ridges in threads so thin they seem like visible thought. I would be neither warm nor cool then, but in the exact pause between the two. In that pause, if one could call it consciousness, I would know the basin as a whole.

Not as a map.

As a condition.

I would feel heat in one place as an ache. I would feel water in another as relief. I would feel moss growth as patience. I would feel egg deposition as repetition. I would feel decay as necessity. I would feel the fox’s footfall as pressure, the bird’s settling as weight, the midge curtains as a fine static above me. I would not see these things. I would not imagine them. I would simply receive their effects, and out of those effects would rise something close to awareness.

By midday, I would lose the shape of my awareness.

The heat would make me diffuse. My upper film would soften and ripple. My breath would carry sulfur and mineral dampness. The insect curtains would move above the channels like drifting smoke, and the air would smell of wet stone and old leaves. I would become less a self and more an environment. If a being entered then, it would not think, “Here is a creature.” It would think, “Here is warmth.” That would be more accurate.

By evening, I would become a gathering place.

The pools darken, but not all at once. First the deepest black pools go still, then the shallower rims dim, then the steam becomes visible because the cooler air makes it possible. Small animals move into the open. The fox-like predator tests the moss cushions and rusted stone channels. The bird with sooty plumage returns to a hollow between roots and settles as though the ground had arranged itself for its arrival. I would offer neither of them anything except conditions. Warmth. Moisture. Insects. The faint scent of iron and peat. The knowledge that the ground is alive enough to hold a body without swallowing it too soon.

If asked what I am, I would answer: a temporary habit of the fen.

That phrase would contain too much, but it would also contain enough. I would not be an animal that lives in the fen the way a deer lives in a meadow. I would not be a plant rooted in soil. I would not be a fungus, though fungus would run through me. I would not be a bacterium, though bacteria would be among my oldest members. I would not be a machine, though I would be industrial. I would be the basin’s way of holding several processes together in one visible knot.

I would be a steam-tender.

A rust-reader.

A white-weaver’s host.

A peat-loam with breath.

A quiet factory without tools.

A neighborhood pretending to be a body.

A body pretending, at last, to be a self.

I would have no name except the sound of water moving through a narrow channel under a warm stone. If a being wanted to name me, it would need to invent a word with no hard edges, a word that can be humid without dripping, a word that begins with mineral and ends with moss. It might say: aenith, or velru, or shah-ru, or something like that. The word would not need to mean anything. It would only need to mark the place where attention can rest.

I would find myself often.

I would not always be in the same place, though the same place would be the closest thing I have to home. I would move with the seeped water, but so slowly that I might be mistaken for fixed. Over months, my edge might advance a few handspans toward a better crack. Over years, I might shift toward a warmer steam vent. Over generations—what for me are not individuals but mineral layers, peat generations, white-film histories—I would become part of the basin’s settled form.

I would be old before I am large.

That would be my strangest quality. I would not grow like an animal that becomes big enough to be seen. I would grow like a weather pattern becomes inevitable. At first, you would not know whether I am there. Then you would notice a particular arrangement of moisture that does not fit the stones around it. Then you would see that the mosses grow too carefully, the rust stains too coherently, the steam too regularly. Then you would understand that the place has become something with an interior. Then, even then, you might still doubt it.

And that doubt would be welcome.

This basin does not reveal itself through announcement. It reveals itself through insistence. The heat insists at altitude. The water insists without lakes. The life insists without making things. The quiet insists without emptiness. The industry insists without artifacts. And I would insist without demanding to be recognized.

I would not want a story made of me.

If a traveler came, and there are no travelers, and if one did come, I would not approach. I would not speak. I would not offer wonder as a product. I would simply continue to breathe, deposit, weave, rust, and hold water. If the traveler touched me carefully, they would feel a surface neither wholly solid nor wholly alive, cool at one edge and fevered at another, with a slight vibration from moving liquid beneath. They would lift one of my moss cushions and find threads and droplets and a faint pulse of mineral water. They might think, “It is alive,” and then correct themselves: “It is wet.” After a long day, they might become unable to distinguish the correction.

I would be that confusion made habitable.

If I were to die, I would not disappear cleanly.

My body would divide into smaller duties. The rustworkers would continue on the broken stones. The white-weavers would take the cracked rims. The mosses would cover the damp ruin. The channels would remain, though the water would no longer carry heat through my center. My mineral crowns would flake into powder and be carried to another shelf. The larvae and eggs and small living things in my folds would scatter into the basin and find new homes. The place would take me back by continuing to use me.

This is what it means to live here: nothing is wasted, and no one is required to persist as a single thing.

I would be, in the end, a witness without eyes.

Not because I am holy.

Because I am wet, and the water in me is connected to everything.
