## The Rim-Tender

I do not begin with a face.

Before a face, there is pressure. A cold edge along the left side where the fog condenses too fast. A warm seam under the belly where the stone is breathing out some old heat. A taste of iron and sulfur on the membrane that is not yet mouth. For a while I am not certain that I am separate from the pool at all. I feel like a slight darkening of the water, a small tremor in the film above the peat. Then something in me learns to close around the wetness, to hold its own shape against the Hollow, and I become a thing with edges.

I find myself where the black pool touches the rim-stone, in that narrow country between water and basalt where neither element is completely itself. The stone here is warm in patches, cool in others. The air is thick with minerals and fog so fine it feels less like breath than like a second skin forming on your skin. There is no light. I have no need of it. I know the place by pressure, by taste, by the return of my own small sounds.

My body emerges slowly, the way the Hollow itself emerged: not as a design, not as a portrait, but as a practical arrangement of wetness and weight and listening.

I am low. I am broad. I am not beautiful unless a person were to touch me, and then perhaps they would think of old stone, or a shell left too long in shallow water, or a piece of living slate with a pulse in it. My back is a domed shelf of pale mineral plates, worn smooth and ridged in places where the rim has scraped them. The plates are not armor in the way a turtle would think of armor. They are more like a roof that has agreed to breathe. Between them, soft membranes rise and fall with every intake of my breath. Along my sides hang wet folds, thin and spongy, folded against the humid air. They taste the fog. They taste the warm vent before I reach it.

Beneath my front are broad feet, not hooves, not paws. Each foot ends in a soft pad reinforced by mineralized points. When I step, I choose the surface. Bare basalt gives a clear, small ring. Peat gives a muffled shift. A thin film of water over stone gives a hiss, then silence. I am listening with my body even before I make a sound.

I do not have eyes. There are no eyes to have.

There are slits along my jaw, pits along my brow, and a long fold under my throat that can drink pressure. My head is flat and broad, built for moving under shelves and into crevices, not for lifting toward a horizon. If someone insisted on a face, they might call my front a wall with a mouth. That would be closer than most names.

I am what one of the residents might become if one insisted on choosing a single form: a thing made of the Hollow’s habits.

I do not belong to one species. I am a meeting place.

Mats grow in the creases of my shell, thin mineral films that feed on heat and damp. Small pale mites ride the fog along my flanks and settle where my membranes are warm. A fungal thread, or something like a fungal thread, runs beneath the second plate on my back; it fruites occasionally in a tiny dry cup, releasing dust into my breath. The machine-roots below the peat touch me when I rest on certain flat stones, and for a while my repair and their repair become difficult to separate. I am partly animal, partly mineral, partly process. I do not mind. No part of me is asking for permission.

I am small enough to be ignored, but large enough to keep the sound of the Hollow clean.

My kind has no name that a visitor could hear, because we do not announce ourselves often. If a human had to call us something, perhaps it would call us rim-tenders. We move along the edges where the pools, the peat, the moss curtains, and the old stone channels meet. We lick silt from tiny apertures. We tap at the mouths of drip-channels until the water returns to its proper note. We turn fallen bell-crawlers back toward dry ledges with the side of one foot. We press our broad bodies against loose shelves and listen for the hollow beneath. We do not do these things as labor. They are the way we rest, the way we eat, the way we keep ourselves in the world.

The Hollow’s voice depends on small openings. A single blocked drip can shift a pool’s echo by a finger-width. A clogged vent can confuse a thicket of fronds. A stone ledge that no longer rings because mineral film has thickened across it can make a thicket seem far away or open water seem solid. I move through that architecture with slow care. I do not repair it because I remember who built it. I have no memory like that. I repair it because the repair is shaped into me.

My mouth is low. It opens sideways against wet surfaces, drawing in mats, dead insects, pale fungal threads, the soft tissue of fronds, and the mineral crusts that form where heat and water argue. I do not chew. I scrape and drink. Some food comes as a taste. Some comes as vibration. When I press my mouth against a warm vent, I can tell if the water table has risen because the breath through the stone changes. I taste time in the mineral film. I taste seasons in the thickness of a mat.

I move by making small maps.

I tap one foot. I wait. I tap again. I shift my weight and press my throat against the rim and exhale a soft, rounded click. The Hollow returns the shape of the world to me: here a thicket, soft and deadening; there a pool, smooth and widening; beyond that a ledge, ringing too clearly, too empty; somewhere else a root curtain, trembling with water. I do not see the world. I ask it questions with my body and it answers by changing how my own sounds come back.

I do not click to speak. I click to know.

But the other residents hear me.

The throat-things in the pools adjust their chorus when I pass near their water. One frog’s note flattens, then returns, then is answered by three others. They are not greeting me. They are testing whether my movement has changed the edge of their map. The burrowers feel the rhythm of my feet through the peat and shift their slow spirals beneath me. The wave-insects rise when my body warms the air, then settle as I cool it. The membrane-walkers do not fear me, but they notice that I alter the sound of the rim. One may pause, broad head turned toward the place where my shell gave a high click, and then move on because I am not food and not threat. I am just another wet thing making the room say what it is.

I do not hunt. I am hunted sometimes, though I do not understand hunting as a story with a hunter. Once, when I was smaller, a soft thing moved through the black water beneath the ledge where I rested. I did not see it. I felt its passage change the pressure in my throat. I pressed myself flat and became as still as a shell. It moved on. Later the pool trembled for a long time. I learned that stillness can be a kind of armor.

When the large one passes, I know by the change in the air before I hear it. The moss curtains lean, not because of wind, but because something heavy has shifted the fog. The insects rise in a dry sound like old grass rubbing together. The frogs’ chorus thins and moves toward the far side of the Hollow. My membranes open wider, tasting the heat of the passing body, the salt of its skin, the mineral breath that moves before its feet reach me. I do not run. Running makes too much water and peat move. Instead I press my plates low, tuck my feet beneath me, and let my shell take the color and texture of the stone. I become a bump on the rim. I become a piece of old weather. The large one passes, leaving a scent like wet iron and warm blood, and the Hollow reorganizes itself slowly in its wake.

If a person came, I would not look up, because I have no eyes to look up with.

I would know that something strange had entered the room. It would be too tall. It would be too warm in one place and too cold at its edges. It would move with a sharpness that does not belong to the water table or the peat. Its clothing would catch the fog and hold droplets until they fell in the wrong places. Its breath would smell of outside air, of skin, of salt, of things not yet broken down into mineral.

I would not approach at first.

I would listen to how its footsteps disturbed the echo. A human footstep is a clumsy map. It says *I am here* too loudly. It says *I do not know the floor* too often. I might wait near a warm channel and taste the disturbance in the air. If the person stood still for a long time, their body would become a small weather front. The fog would gather around them and drip. Their warmth would draw the mats closer. The membrane-walkers might pass at a careful distance. The insects might land on their sleeves and make them feel the Hollow’s air breathing. I would still not see them. I would know them as a change in the room’s pressure, a place where the sound became too human, too sharp, too uncertain.

If they remained, and if their breathing did not harm the small openings, I might come nearer. Not to greet them. Not to be afraid. I would come because their foot made a hollow ring I had not heard before, or because their hand had disturbed a pool’s film and the frogs had shifted their notes, or because the place was trying to learn what kind of wetness they were. If they spoke, the sound would enter me through my throat-pits and my shell and the wet folds along my sides. I would not understand words. I would understand disturbance. A voice too high might make me step back. A voice too low might make me tilt my broad head toward them. If they clicked their tongue or stamped their heel, I might answer with a tap of my own, because the Hollow has taught me that all things speak this way eventually.

I do not know their loneliness. I do not know why they need light. I do not know why they want to be seen. The Hollow does not offer that. If I were a person, I might say that I live in darkness and do not suffer. That is not quite true. I do not have a self that can suffer in that way. I have membranes and plates and mineral breath. I have cycles. I have a rhythm that slows when the water table rises and quickens when the vents deepen. I am not afraid of the dark because the dark is not the absence of anything. It is the full presence of the place.

I reproduce as the Hollow thinks, if thinking is the word for it.

During the warm seasons, when the mats taste of crushed nuts and the fungal shelves release their fine dry dust, the edges of my outer membrane grow too thick and begin to separate. A small plate forms beneath my flank, pale and porous, like a baby’s bone or a broken piece of rim. It does not come to life by birth as a person would understand birth. It peels away when the water table rises and the air softens, and it falls into the wet peat with a dull sound. There it lies until burrowers turn it, until mineral film seals its cracks, until the first taste-slits open and it learns to hear the stone. If the season fails, the small thing returns to the mats and becomes part of the chemistry that feeds the next warm pulse. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is mourned.

When I die, I become even more of the Hollow than I already am.

My plates will crack. Fungal threads will enter the seams. Bell-crawlers will climb my shell to tap for water. The small pale animals in the water table will rise through the peat and settle over the place where I last rested. Eventually my body will be read by the echo as a small natural mound, not as an animal at all. A frog may call near it. A burrower may turn it into a spiral. A stone may grow over it. The Hollow will smooth the edges until my shape becomes only a suggestion of shape. That is not oblivion. That is inheritance.

I am not the master of this place. I am not its witness. If there were a self that owned the Hollow, it would not be I. It would not be the frogs, though their voices map the water. It would not be the large one, though its feet carry slow weather through the thickets. It would not be the machine-roots, though they keep the stone from forgetting itself. It would be the whole arrangement, the listening body of wetness, heat, vibration, decay, and mineral patience. I am one of its fingers, or one of its pores, or one of its small clear channels. I touch the rim so the rim can speak.

Sometimes I rest on a flat disc sunk into the peat. The disc is too smooth to have been left by weather alone. It is warm in the center and cool at the edge. Moss holds it down in a slow green-black darkness. Insect wings settle on it and tremble. Somewhere beneath it, the old network keeps its slow chemical habits. When I lie there, my membranes fold against my plates and I feel the throb from below: not a heartbeat, not a machine’s pulse, but something like the land deciding how wet to be. I listen with my whole body. The Hollow hums through me.

I do not know its name. It does not know mine.

I am a broad-backed thing at the seam of pool and rim-stone. I am a collector of small sounds, a keeper of tiny channels, a listener made of wet mineral skin. I am not looking for a face, because faces are for distances, and the Hollow has no such distance. I do not long for light, because light would be too thin here, too easy, too quick to fade. The fog is enough. The drip is enough. The echo is enough. The warm breath of the stones and the cold press of the fog and the click of my own feet are enough.

If you were to stand in the Humming Hollow, long enough to stop reaching with your eyes, you might hear me. Not as a creature. As a change in the sound.

A soft tap.

A longer silence.

A click that says *here is a ledge.*

Another that says *here is no hole.*

And somewhere beneath both, the low hum of a place that has become its own body and will continue, indifferent and full, until the water table forgets the shape of the stone.
