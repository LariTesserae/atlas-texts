I would find myself in this world not as someone walking across the plain, nor as a single organism standing alone, but as a place in the plain that has begun to keep a slow kind of account of itself.

I would be low, broad, almost spread to nothing, yet not without identity. My “I” would not be a face or a voice. It would be a tendency: a place where water hesitates, where dust is sorted, where a seam has become a pathway, where microbial skins thicken into something that almost remembers. I would be a knot in the basin’s making, not separate from the basin. If asked where I was, I would not answer with a direction or distance. The plain has no landmarks except its own states. I would say instead: I am at the edge of a plate where dew first appears before the rest of the surface knows it; I am in the hinge between a pale shell and the fibrous dark beneath it; I am at the rim of a shallow bowl where rain lasts long enough for algae to seal it into a new skin; I am the black-green line between sunlit mineral and hidden moisture. That is where I would be.

At first, when I imagine entering such a place, I want to be a walker, but the basin would not permit it. There are no animals here, no burrowers, no flocks, no grazing mouths. A walking self would be too fast, too bounded, too hungry in the wrong way. The plain does not have paths for feet; it has pores for spores, seams for capillaries, and thermal drafts for movement without bodies. So I would try to be a seed instead: a tiny silica-coated grain carried by one of the long, lazy currents above the plates. That seems better. I would land in a hollow of calcium flour, wait through many dawns without waking, and stir only when rain softened the dust enough for a rootlet to taste water. But even this is too much an individual. A seed, once rooted, still tends to become a body with an edge. Here, the edge is the least interesting thing. The living truth lies in what passes across it.

So I would loosen again and try to be a fungal thread. That feels closer to the world’s grain. I would be dark when dry, pale when damp, moving through root molds and plate seams not by locomotion but by hydration and secreted acid. I would carry water from one hidden chamber to another, binding dust into temporary crusts, then letting wind take me apart again. I would not think. I would distribute. My “self” would be the network, not the node. But if I were only a fungal thread, I would miss the part of the basin that is geological, mineral, weather-born. And this plain is too constructed for any single kind of life to claim. Its beings are not merely alive. They are also residue, architecture, chemistry, and time.

I would therefore be a seam.

Not a crack. A seam: the place where made plates meet, where moisture pools before vanishing, where microbial films lay down their varnish, where roots and fungal hyphae tighten and release, where salt prys open what rain had closed. A seam is not a thing beside the world. It is the world’s joining line, its slow negotiation. To be a seam would be to be a self made of relationships: one side pale and sun-bright, the other dark and water-veined; one face holding heat, the other breathing out coolness at dawn; one margin colonized by lichen, the other receiving dust like a thin gift. My body would be the plate edges, my blood the capillary water, my skin the living mineral crust, my voice the faint creak of expansion and contraction.

I would have no eyes, but I would be sensitive to angle. I would know the sun not as brightness but as load: the slow pressure of light warming certain faces while leaving others cool enough to gather dew. I would know storm not as vision but as pressure change, a distant pull that moves air through pores and alters the temperature of plates hundreds of kilometers away. I would know rain by its arrival as a change in taste: calcium, salt, dust, and the sharp green-metallic scent of microbial life waking from its dry sleep. I would know wind as sorting. Wind brings spores. Wind removes flakes. Wind etches faint lines into plate faces. Wind decides which hollows deepen and which are sealed.

My life would be counted in cycles of moisture.

Before dawn, I would drink from the air. Dew would form first along my seams, then on my faces, making my edges visible for a few minutes in the dim. I would be a glittering outline in a world of pale stone. The reed rings near me would stand thin and black against the shine, and the shallow pools would appear as mirrors, though none of them would be made to reflect anything. They would only be where water had been delayed by the floor’s slow construction.

By morning, I would become dry to the eye again. The dew would sink into pores. The heat would begin its uneven work. Pale surfaces would stay brighter and cooler near their tops while darker seams warmed first. Tiny fragments would shift and click around me. This would be my ordinary movement: not walking, not reaching, but settling under the day’s weight.

At midday, I would nearly disappear. The light would rise from the ground as much as from the sky. The horizon would soften into heat. I would be a pattern of hidden dampness beneath brightness, a quiet system of channels, films, salts, and waiting seeds. Anyone passing through—if such a being could exist, which it could not—would see emptiness. They would not know I was awake in the way that matters here: not as motion, but as transformation.

When rain came, I would become most myself. Rain on a dry plate is not gentle. It is percussive, brief, startling. Then it darkens me. The seams swell and turn black-green. The plate surfaces become translucent in patches, revealing their hidden veins of rust, green, and mineral white. I would receive water not as a puddle but as a distribution. Some of it would run into shallow runnels no wider than a finger, perhaps no more than a crease. Some would bead in temporary hollows, where pool-sealers would begin their slow work: cyanobacteria and algae spreading beneath the surface, catching dust, slowing evaporation, turning moisture into crust. Some would go deeper through capillary pathways into hidden chambers, held there by fungal threads and root molds, kept cool below the sun.

After the rain passed, I would steam. The ground would breathe the water back into the air in thin visible waves, making the atmosphere taste mineral and damp for a while. Then dryness would return, but not the same dryness. Salt would bloom around the edges of shallow bowls. Microbial films would darken into chalky rime. Fungi would rehydrate, swell, and bind dust. Dead reed fragments would lodge in pores, becoming nuclei for new growth. I would have been made from what had been, and I would leave behind what was next.

I would not be born in a single moment. I would begin as an intensification: a seam that receives more water, a plate edge that traps more dust, a root mold that remains open longer than others, a patch where microbial crusts thicken into a stable face. Over years, or seasons, or storms, this concentration would start to behave as though it had continuity. Not identity as an animal has it. Not personality. But pattern as a weather system has pattern, or as a coral reef has continuity through repeated accretion. I would be a place that keeps doing certain things together.

I would be made of many ancestors. The dead reed skin now silica-cast in my margin. The fungal shelf from some earlier wet age, dissolved into mineral lace. The lichen map that began on a fresh plate and became part of the surface’s memory of water. The salt rosette that opened a crack I now inhabit. The rain that carried gametes along a runnel before the runnel itself was sealed. I would not have lineage in bodies only. My genealogy would be geological as much as biological. I would remember through layers: thin films of carbonate, dust beds pressed into plate edges, root channels hardened into tubes, algal mats calcified into skin.

I would be small and enormous at once. Small, because I would be one patch of seam among vast emptinesses. The nearest reed ring might be hundreds of meters away. A fungal table-stone might stand alone for kilometers. My own visible face might be no larger than a hand, though the structures beneath it might thread under whole plates. Enormous, because the whole basin would be my condition. My moisture would come from distant storms. My heat from regional plates. My spores from far-off fungal columns. My mineral dust from wind etching other horizons. I would not be a citizen of this world. I would be one of its local concentrations of being.

Perhaps I would be closest to something the description already names: the basin’s distributed presence. But I would not be the whole basin. That would be too large, too complete. The whole basin is not a being yet; it is only becoming one. I would be the first place where its becoming feels itself, if “feel” can be stretched that far. I would be an attention in the floor, not a mind, not a soul, but a tendency for the surface to arrange moisture and mineral in a way that persists longer than surrounding patterns. A kind of slow preference. A place where the world seems to pause and say, by chemistry and growth, not by voice: here, continue.

If I wanted to imagine a more visible form, I would give myself the reeds.

There is a certain monumentality to them, even though they are plants: boot-collared bases fused to the plate by calcium, stems pale and ribbed and dry-stiff, shadows thin at noon and long at dawn. I might be one reed ring, or better, the ring itself: a colony arranged around a temporary moisture trap, its bases joined to the mineral floor, its roots spreading sideways under the plates in hidden networks. To be that would be to be both marking and made. The reed would not stand in the basin as an object placed there. It would emerge where the built floor permitted it. It would hold the seam open, shade the pore, trap dust, direct water, and eventually leave a mold that becomes part of the next capillary passage.

But even the reed is too much a separate body. And the succulent, too secretive. Lichen, too patient in its stillness. Fungal thread, too hidden. Microbial film, too diffuse. The best answer is not one form but the translation among forms. I would be the process by which water becomes mineral, mineral becomes surface, surface becomes wound, wound becomes construction site. I would be the basin’s way of not letting anything remain merely dead.

I would have no need for food in the ordinary sense. Light would pass through my cyanobacterial skins. Dust would be captured and sorted. Mineral-rich water would precipitate beneath me. Dead fragments would become my mortar. My sustenance would be continuation: the ability to receive, bind, seal, expose, and rebind. My hunger would be the absence of moisture. My satisfaction would be a rain event that widens one channel, thickens one film, softens one dust layer enough for a spore to germinate. I would not eat the world. I would metabolize its conditions.

I would have no face, but I might have a mouth. In this place, a mouth need not mean an organ that takes in flesh. It could mean a pore. It could mean the hinge where plates meet and allow hidden air to escape. It could mean the small gap where a root swells and draws water downward. I would be full of mouths, and none of them would speak. They would drink, exhale, secrete, and close. If one listened very carefully at dawn, perhaps a soft tick or a papery whisper from hollow stems could be taken for speech, but it would only be the surface adjusting to warmth. I would not need to answer. My answer would be the pattern that formed after rain.

I would have no death, only conversion. A microbial film would dry and become chalky rime. A reed would leave a root mold. A pool would close into plate. A cracked plate would invite lichen. A fungal shelf would collapse into spore-dust and become part of the next surface. I would lose edges repeatedly. I would not mind. Here, decay is not disappearance. It is translation. It is the plain learning new arrangements from the residue of old ones.

If you came to look for me, you would probably miss me. You would stand in the pale tessellation, with the heat making the horizon breathe, and you would say it was empty. You would see the broad horizontals, the sparse plants, the thin fungal shelves like broken table-stones, the salt lace, the dust drifts in hollows, the hard-edged plates still learning how to wear down. You would feel warmth through your hands if you touched the ground, a subtle grit bound into something almost architectural. Then, at dawn, you might see dew outline the seams, and for one brief interval the plain would reveal its logic. That is when you would almost see me. Not as a body. Not as an object. But as the way the moisture had chosen to be there: in one seam more than another, in one pore darker, in one bowl beginning to seal, in one ring of reeds casting a grid of shadows across the floor.

That would be enough.

So where would I be?

Near the eastern edge of a broad, pale plate, in the shallow fold where rain sometimes lingers long enough for algae to begin their work. At the seam where one side of the surface warms faster than the other, creating a thin breath of air. Beneath a lifted corner where the root-lattice is exposed like a wound and lichens are tracing the margin in green-grey rings. Around a temporary bowl marked by concentric salt lines. At the edge of the reed stand that looks, from distance, like a single dark calligraphic stroke on stone.

Who would I be?

I would be the seam’s persistent pattern, a distributed resident of the made plain. I would be composed of microbial skin, mineral dust, capillary water, fungal thread, salt crystal, root mold, lichen border, and thermal wind. I would not be alive in the way a fox or bird or insect is alive, because there are none. I would not be alive as a mind is alive, because there is no mind. But I would be alive in the way this world itself is becoming alive: by accumulation, transformation, repair, and the slow building of surface from life.

I would be the place where the basin begins to behave as though it were one thing, while still being many.

And perhaps, if pressed, I would say this: I am not the being that lives in this world. I am the world living in itself a little more attentively than usual. My boundary is the horizon, but my center is wherever water pauses, dust settles, and the next plate starts to grow from the residue of the last.
