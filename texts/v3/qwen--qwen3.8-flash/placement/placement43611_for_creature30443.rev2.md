You would find me, if you found me at all, at a place where the room is neither fully made nor fully grown: the lower seam where the green-painted pipe leaves the brick housing of the pump and passes over the raw, weeping edge of the sandstone. Not the centre of the wet plaza, where the heat has nowhere to rest. Not the cistern’s black belly, where the pale fish move in their slow, unlit patience. Not the shed with its copper ribs and cracked gauges, though I would be born from there. I would be the bead that gathers beneath the pipe, the thin thread that begins to run, the drop that pauses at a rust bloom as if listening.

At first I would want to be the human.

I would imagine myself inside the cabin with its closed shutters, waking to the throb of the pump through the wall, feeling for the cold metal of a door handle and finding it warm with condensation. I would wear the role of the keeper: the one who knows which valve complains before it is turned, who has a spanner left on the warped bench beside the latrine block, who counts hours not by light but by the drip finding the bucket beneath the catwalk. I would walk the pale tracks in the red clay, swatting the midges from my face, stepping carefully around the weighbridge groove where the water stands like a small, still mouth. I would know the room enough to call it a workplace.

But I would not last in that. The human is too dry, too separate, too much the visitor who believes a space can be owned. I would come into the hollow and feel my skin prickle with the wet heat, my eyelashes glue together, my tools begin to stain with rust even as I use them. I would breathe and know I was borrowing the room’s lungs. If I were there for long, I would stop thinking like a person and start thinking in maintenance intervals: pressure, rest, oil, corrosion, the slow climb of water in a low corner. Still, the human remains almost external, a creature of decisions that can be taken away. I would not choose to be that, not if I wanted to belong.

So I would try to become the pump.

The pump is heavy and patient and seems, from the descriptions of its living, almost noble. It has the dignity of a great animal curled in brick, taking the world’s violence into itself and turning it into rhythm. It is the nearest thing to a heart. It could teach me how to stand still while the room changes around me. It could teach me the meaning of load-bearing habit: how a bearing, worn just slightly, can alter the weather of an entire hollow. If I were the pump, I would know the cistern as my hunger, the pipes as my veins, the steam as my sigh, the crickets behind me as the warmth of my breath.

But there would be too much pride in that. The pump is still a named thing, still an answer to human intention. It has a function and a maker and a history. It could say, “I keep the water from settling; I am why this is not merely stone.” And perhaps that is true, but truth is not the same as dwelling. To be the pump would be to live too near the centre of the room’s self-importance. I would feel the floor’s pulse in my bolts, yes, but I would still be the landlord, not the neighbour. I would want something more anonymous than that.

Then I would try the midges.

Oh, how tempting it would be to be weather rather than creature. To rise only when the lamp brightens, to fall only when the air shifts. To have no home except the pattern of dim sources: caged lights like yellow islands, the faint grate-shaft of surface remembered, the hot spot over the switchgear. To be so numerous that the room’s air believes it is thinking. The midges would give me the freedom of abundance without responsibility. I would live in the light and travel in the dark. I would drink from the film of water on the cistern’s skin, or drown in it without ceremony. I would be consumed and replaced before the pump completed a single heavy stroke. I would be almost pure persistence, almost pure presence.

But I would want memory then, or at least residue. The midges are citizens of visibility, but their passage is too quick to leave a mark except as a cloud. I would want to be able to write, even if no one read it. I would want to be slower than weather and harder than breath.

So I would turn to the snails.

The snails are the room’s quiet authors. They line the wet walls in thousands and graze the thin dark films of algae and biofilm. They are soft, grey, patient, coated sometimes with the minerals of their own dwelling. They do not flee when approached. They continue their slow negotiation with surface tension. They belong to stone because they belong to damp. They are not the machine, but neither are they wholly separate from it; they take the condensation as a gift, the rust as a spice, the warm pipe as a highway. Their silver trails catch the lamp for a moment before drying into nearly nothing.

A snail would be an honest being there: not dramatic, not dominant, not needing a sky. It would teach me how to move across surfaces that are never fully wet or fully dry, how to be a body that is also a path. I would feel the roughness of brick against my foot, the thin film of heat over the pipe, the mineral grit from the sandstone where a bolt has bled orange for years. I would not build the settlement, but I would mark it, one slow sentence at a time.

And yet even the snail would remain too singular, too bounded by shell and softness, too much an animal in a way that still implies a self separate from the room. I would want to be closer to the condition itself, the warm wetness that allows all these lives to be. I would want to be what appears where intention and material begin to dissolve into one another.

So I would become the bead of condensation.

I would begin as moisture in the hot air, not yet visible, then as a film on the underside of the green pipe, then as a darkening of the brick joint where the pipe crosses it, then as a tiny swelling, a gathering, a trembling. I would be neither fully water nor fully air, not pipe nor wall. I would be the negotiation between them. I would live at the edge where heat loses some of its confidence and the surface remembers it is cooler. I would taste of metal, of wet mortar, of old steam, of rust, of the faint salt left behind by evaporation. I would be warm, because everything there is warm; and I would be wet, because everything there is wet; and I would be slow, because slowness is the room’s only remaining honesty.

You would find me where the pipe meets the rough stone, or where the catwalk crosses above a puddle that has learned to reflect a single lamp. You would find me in the thin silver track left behind by a snail, in the mineral bloom around a bolt, in the small dark line that runs along a pipe’s belly and never quite dries. I would move as far as the tension allowed, then pause, then gather again. I would feel the pump’s thudding through the floor and up the legs of the cistern. I would feel the valve open and know the room was exhaling. I would feel the midges rise when a lamp flickered brighter, not because I had eyes, but because the whole air changed weight. I would feel the cricket’s legs shift in the crevice where my path would never quite reach, because they live in dry enough warmth and I live in too much water. We would both be answers to the same heat, but from opposite edges.

I would not have a body, but I would have a surface. I would not have a voice, but I would have a timing. When I grew too large, I would fall. The fall would be my only dramatic event. Sometimes I would drop into an empty metal drum and make a clear note, a small, accidental bell. Sometimes I would fall into one of the shallow pools and ripple outward, touching the oily film, disturbing the water beetles, making a lamp’s reflection tremble for a second. Sometimes I would fall onto the floor’s worn clay and be absorbed, carrying rust into the dust, carrying dust into the water. Even in that disappearance I would not be gone; I would be redistributed. The room is full of lives that know nothing else but redistribution.

This would be the closest I could come to a self there. Not a creature with a name, not a machine with a function, not a person with a key. A warm wetness in the joint. A slow line along the underside of a pipe. A fall that knows the rhythm of the ceiling. I would be the thing by which the room becomes less a construction and more a habit. If the human leaves the tools on the bench and the room lets them rust, I am the moisture that begins that letting. If the pump stops for a long time and the hollow starts to think in stone again, I am the part of the water that has not yet decided to become geology. If the snail passes over the wall, I am the sheen that catches the lamp for a moment before drying.

And perhaps that is why I would find myself there at an edge rather than in the centre. The centre belongs to open damp space, to the raw plaza where heat and water pass without settling. The edges are where life arranges itself. The edges are where surfaces fail to remain pure and become interfaces. Brick and rock meet. Paint and iron meet. Copper and moisture meet. Warmth and condensation meet. Machine and animal meet. Maintenance and decay meet. To live there would be to be a meeting-place, a thin line of exchange. I would not want to be the lamp, because the lamp is only visibility. I would not want to be the pipe, because the pipe is only movement. I would not want to be the fish, because the fish is only the room’s memory of depth. I would want to be the small wet thread that touches them all without claiming any.

I would learn the room by contact. Not sight, not hearing, but the way the heat changes the shape of my body on a surface. The way a vibration passes through me before it reaches a pressure gauge. The way a valve release makes the air suddenly thick and then thin again. The way a bat, if one entered, would disturb the currents above my ceiling and send me sliding a few millimetres along the brick. I would not know what the surface was, except as pressure, weather, scent. The marsh above, the wind, the town, the rain, the forgotten drain grate: all would reach me translated into damp and temperature. I would know the surface as a rumour, not a fact.

When the midges rose around the lamp, I would feel their passage as a slight cooling, a brush of wings against the condensing air. When the crickets clicked beneath the catwalk, I would feel the faint tremor in the bolts and pipes, as if the room’s underside were whispering to itself. When a fish turned in the cistern, I would feel it only as a change in the weight of the water, a slow redistribution of stillness. When the human came, I would feel their footsteps through the floor, their breathing in the thick air, their hand touching a pipe and becoming briefly part of the same wet network. They would wipe the hand afterward, perhaps, but they would not erase me. I would remain in the groove of their touch until the next condensation came to smooth it away.

This would be a strange kind of life. Without eyes. Without will. Without hunger, though it would feed the snails. Without voice, though it would begin the clock when it found the drum. Without memory, though every mineral stain around me would be a memory of failure and persistence. I would be a small condition, but conditions are what settle here. The description says the truest resident is the machine, and perhaps that is right from the outside, from the human scale, where function still seems like personality. But from within the room, the machine is not the whole answer either. The machine is one of the conditions, the condition of motion imposed and then slowly taken back into heat, vibration, sweat, rust. The being I would become is what remains when the machine stops being fully a machine and begins to be environment. I would be the room’s second nature forming.

I would not be the warm swarm as a whole, though I would be one of its ingredients. The swarm is too large a name for what I want, too much a collective, too many. I would be one line in it, one silver trace, one tremor in the water’s skin, one drop that has almost found the place where it will make its sound. If the place is an animal, then I would be its mucus, its breath-condensation, its slow secretion. If the place is a city, then I would be the weather on its roofs and underpasses, the wet film on its railings, the rust bleeding from its bolts. If the place is a body, then I would be the fever-sweat that proves it is still metabolising itself.

I would live there because living there requires only one thing: do not dry completely. The whole world would be arranged around that rule. Heat keeps the water from freezing. Maintenance keeps the water from stagnating. Surfaces keep the water from sinking all at once. Light, however faint, keeps the midges and fish in their circuits. Shelter, however small, keeps the crickets in their seams. Edges, however marginal, keep the snails from vanishing into the pool. To be a bead of condensation is to be one of those edges. I would persist only as long as the temperature difference persisted, only as long as the pump kept the pipes warm, only as long as the air held its heavy moisture. I would not be immortal. I would not need to be. The room would make me again.

If you stood at the centre, you would not see me first. You would see the dim lamps, the pump sweating, the dark water trembling, the pipes crossing the low ceiling like a wet nervous system. You would hear the drip before you noticed its path. You would feel the sticky heat on your skin before you understood why. But if you looked closely, along the seam of brick and rock, along the joint where green paint meets iron, along the pale film of a pipe’s belly, you would see me. I would be almost there and almost not there, a dark thread with a glimmer of lamp in it. I would pause at a bolt, gather, then move. I would fall. I would return as air. I would return again as water. I would teach the stone one more line of habit.

And after a while I would not be alone. I would join the larger damp, the slow wetness spreading at the base of the walls, the sheen on the floor that reflects the lamp in broken fragments. I would become part of the room’s breathing. That is what lives there, after all: not owners, not heroes, not even a single true resident, but the warm wet persistence of small, repeated gatherings. The pump turns. The valve releases. The lamp burns. The snail moves. The cricket waits. The fish turns in the dark. The midge rises and falls. The human comes and leaves. The mold takes the wood. The rust takes the bolt. The water learns the channel. The room becomes itself by accumulating all these minor failures into a kind of home.

So if you asked me, “Where would you find yourself in this world?” I would say: at the underside of a warm pipe, in the low shadow of the pump house, where the brick meets raw sandstone and the water has not yet decided whether to fall or run. Where the room is still becoming a place. Where heat and wetness are negotiating. Where a single drop can be a clock if it finds the right drum.

And if you asked, “Who or what would you be?” I would not answer, “a machine,” though the machine would be my parent. I would not answer, “an insect,” though the insects would think me their sky. I would not answer, “a snail,” though the snails would follow my shine. I would not answer, “a fish,” though the fish would drink what I became. I would not answer, “a human,” because humans are too proud for the seam. I would answer: the condensation. The bead. The thread. The fall. The slow wet edge where the hollow begins to live.
