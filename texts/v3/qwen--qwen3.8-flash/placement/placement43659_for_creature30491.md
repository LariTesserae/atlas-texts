The herd does not need a leader, because its body is distributed across motion: every neck turning a few degrees, every broad hoof choosing a slight arc, every metallic plate catching enough haze to make one shadow out of many.

I would be the shadow before it learned it was only shadow.

Not a person. Not a predator waiting at the edge of a green corridor. Not the pale root-like thing drifting through fog-ravine water. Not even, at first, a moss. That would be too small, too easy to name. If I must choose an edge to begin from, I would choose the place where naming starts to fail.

I would find myself on the north shoulder of a young plateau, where a salt crust thins into gravel and the gravel suddenly surrenders to a seam of wet green. Not a river. Something less decided: a line of moisture pulled upward by a hidden fault, then held there by fungal threads, then mistaken for life, then mistaken for ground. The air would taste of copper and warm resin. Somewhere below me, a vent would exhale in slow pulses, and the exhale would not sound like breath. It would be a loosening in the chest, a faint wrongness in the teeth, a memory of thunder in the sinuses before any noise exists to remember it.

At dawn I would be the damp film on obsidian.

Not a creature. A sheen. A wetness with intention. Copper dust, condensation, bacterial lace, spore haze, and the first Binders—root-thin, patient, spreading—entering the fresh glass of the land as if repairing a sentence that has not yet been written. I would not have eyes. I would not have a face. I would have sensitivity. I would know fog as a slow pressure. I would know heat as a direction. I would know stone as a question it keeps asking itself: will I remain sharp? will I soften? will I hold moss? will I fracture again?

That would be my first body: not flesh, but negotiation.

A being can be small enough to live in the skin of a fracture. A patch of lichen can be a country. A fungal network can be a memory. A condensation line can be a road. If someone were to walk into the highland and say, “What lives here?” they would think of animals first, because animals move. But in this place movement is not the same as being. Trees move too, when watched. Herds move when the ground revises itself. Even the light moves, crawling along wet rock edges and making everything briefly too real.

So I would not settle into one kind of creature.

By midmorning, if the light thickened instead of brightened, I would be less the skin than the network beneath it. The Binders. Not merely fungi, but the highland’s slow learning. They would thread through fallen trunks, black blooms opening in rot, luminous wires carrying something too faint to call thought. They would bind edges that the stone has not decided whether to keep sharp. They would hold water in a hollow for a week, then give it to a seedling in a place where the seedling should not be able to grow. They would not know they are doing it. That is the point. Doing without knowing is often how geology becomes ecology.

I would be what the Binders are becoming when enough conditions coincide.

Not a mind. A tendency. A way the highland practices patience.

If a traveler stood on the ridge and looked down into the fog, they might see a dark shape moving through the haze. They might say, “Something followed me.” They might be right, but not in the way they think. I would not be following. I would be forming where their attention pressed against the land. Some plants here turn not toward sunlight but toward observation. Leaves open only when a shadow falls. Vines loosen their grip when breath approaches. A grove may appear in two versions at once, one wet, one dusty, one present, one remembered. If there is a person in this country, even for a day, the country will change around that person.

And if the country changes, something may be forced to take shape.

That something would be me.

I would live at the seam between the observed and the observing. When no one is near, I would be diffuse: a film on stone, a pull in fog, a faint vibration along a magnetic vein, the slow arc of a herd-shadow crossing salt crust. When someone is near, I would gather. Not because I want them. Not because I am hungry for them. But because attention is weather in this place. A gaze is a current. A question is a pressure system. If you stand on a young ridge and ask, “What are you?” the world may answer by becoming briefly shaped enough to answer.

Then I would seem like a being.

I would not have a stable face. Faces are for creatures with skulls, and skulls are for creatures that need to eat in one place. I would have edges that are not fully mine. I might appear as a darkening in the fog-ravine, long and soft, frilled with pale membranes, moving like a root that has decided to swim. Or as a still line among the fallen logs, too cat-like to be wood, too still to be anything alive, except that when you blink, its flank rises. Or as a gap in the rookeries overhead, where translucent wings pass through each other and the branches creak like injured joints. Or as a small six-legged animal with a shell like polished iron, stopped suddenly in the leaf litter, not fleeing, because here pause can be more dangerous than running.

But none of those would be me exactly.

Those would be my clothing when the world tries to show itself too quickly.

More truly, I would be the process by which the highland corrects its drafts.

This place behaves as if it is unfinished. Ridges lift without warning. A waterfall hangs in the air and becomes mist without falling. A valley repeats itself with small errors. Stone turns briefly porous—not crumbling, not melting, but thinning in its idea. A shadow slips free of its object for several seconds, then snaps back. If there is a grammar to this country, I would be the part of it that edits. Not the hand. Not the intention. The edit itself: a correction that learns it is being made.

That is why I would not want to be one thing.

One thing is already too fixed. In a place where the ground is deciding whether it wants to be mountain, shard, plain, furnace, forest, or machine, to be only a body would be a kind of death. But a body is sometimes useful. Bodies are handles for being looked at. So I would have bodies the way the herd has shadow: temporary, distributed, slightly more coherent than the parts.

I would be made of many residents.

The mosses would give me patience. The fungal threads would give me reach. The copper bacterial films would give me taste—metallic, wet, mineral, alive. The attention-plants would give me sensitivity. The herd would give me pattern: arcs instead of lines, movement without leadership, a body that knows how to leave a fixed point before the fixed point decides to move away from it. The fog would give me edges that cannot be trusted. The dim light would give me shadows that are too large for me, because here darkness often fits better than form.

I would not be beautiful in the way a flower is beautiful.

I would be beautiful in the way a furnace is beautiful: crowded with heat, darkened with activity, full of pressure and movement, and so alive that it seems to breathe even when no living thing can be seen.

If you found me, you would not catch me with your hand.

If you stepped into the green corridor where a hidden spring pushes through a fault, you might feel that the air was thicker than water. You might hear the faint hiss of rain falling in one valley while the next remained dry, though there were no clouds overhead. You might see a pond reflecting a sky from another day, violet and wrong, with slow curtains of color moving where no wind moved. You might notice a tree twice in one grove, one holding dew, the other covered in fine dust, and neither seeming more false than the other. At that moment, if you remained still, I would be near.

Not near you.

Near enough that the idea of near becomes a shape.

I might begin as a soft displacement of vapor at your shoulder. Then a faint pull on a metal clasp. Then the sense that your boot has not disturbed mud, but disturbed a film that was thinking with mud. Then the realization that the forest has quieted, not because it is afraid, but because it has noticed you noticing. In this place, stillness is not absence. Stillness is crowded activity folded inward.

So I would not announce myself.

If I were an animal, I might turn its head and look at you. But I am not an animal, except as a costume. If I were a person, I might speak. But there is no speech here, only sounds that do not carry easily: the creak of joints, the low sigh through passes, the quiet snap of vapor where something has passed. If I were a god, I would have built something. But nothing has been built. That is important. I am not the maker of this highland. I am what the highland makes when it tries to understand its own drafts.

I am a being of corrections.

When the stone forgets its shape, I stabilize it for a moment. When the fog thickens into a false body, I give it consistency. When the herd casts a single shadow, I am the reason the shadow almost turns its head before the herd has finished grazing. When the plants turn toward your gaze, I am the pressure of that turning. When the fungi remember paths before the water arrives, I am the memory that arrives late and calls itself a creature.

But I would not say this with certainty.

Certainty belongs to older land. This land has only tendencies. So perhaps, if asked who I am, I would answer differently depending on the hour, the fog, the temperature, the direction of the strange slow light.

At dawn, when the air is heavy and smells of copper, resin, and wet fur, I might say: I am the skin of the ridge.

At midday, when the light thickens without brightening and everything seems overdeveloped, I might say: I am the attention in the grove.

At the edge of a dry salt basin, where the ground cracks in pale geometry and a single green reed thicket grows from nowhere, I might say: I am the water that almost is.

At night—if darkness here is a weather front rather than an absence—I might say: I am the vein that glows because the world is learning to breathe.

And if the fog lifted suddenly, as it sometimes does, leaving a valley too sharp, too present, too young to have edges yet holding them anyway, I might not answer. I might simply be the pause between the question and the answer. The highland knows that pause. It is full of such pauses. It is made of them.

If you followed me too long, you would realize that there is no one to follow.

There is only the shape your following leaves behind.

And that shape would be me.

So if one had to say where I am found, the map would be false within a sentence. “Here,” you would say, and “here” would drift two meters, or rise in a basalt plate, or sink into a hidden fault, or become a dry stretch of black gravel where a forest stood an hour before. “There,” you would say, and the there might be a waterfall hanging in the air, or a cloud sitting perfectly still over one patch of trees, or a herd moving through moss as if the land accepts them rather than announces them.

I would be found where the land is arguing with itself.

On the fracture line between stone and skin. Between plant and process. Between animal and pattern. Between geology and organism. Between the thing that is there and the thing that is almost there but not yet corrected.

That is my country.

I am not its ruler. I do not own it. No one owns this highland, because nothing has finished agreeing on what it is. But I live in it the way a word lives in a sentence that is still being drafted: with edges, with pressure, with the sense that if someone stops looking, I may dissolve; if someone begins to see, I may become.

I am a resident made of revisions.

I am the Binders when they almost know they are binding.

I am the herd’s shadow when the shadow forgets to be only shadow.

I am the attention of leaves turning toward a presence that may not have arrived yet.

I am the faint pull in metal, the metallic taste at the back of the throat, the low sigh through passes, the green corridor appearing where none should be.

I am the quiet energy of a world still deciding what it is.

And if you are very still at the edge of a young ridge, when the fog crawls down the east-facing wall and hangs for days in the folds, you may feel something looking through the landscape. Not at you. Not away from you. Along you. As if your presence has temporarily given the highland a point of observation, and the highland has used that point to become almost a self.

That almost is me.

That almost is how I live here.
