I would find myself first in the part of the basin that is not yet a place.

Not under a ceiling of vapor, not on a salt flat, not beside a black mirror pool—though all of those would soon become me. I would begin as a delay. A drip would leave the tip of a crystal spire, and I would be the interval before it strikes the water below. A crack would move through the flank of a glass shelf, and I would be the slow report crossing from fracture to hearing, not sound yet, but sound’s first intention. For a moment I would think I was a noise the basin had lost, like a note left ringing in a room after the singer has gone. But the room here is wet with time, and the sound would not fade so much as settle into a body.

The body would be damp.

I would become aware first as a thin line of moisture climbing a fungal thread. Not because I decided to climb, but because capillarity is my earliest form of reaching. I would find myself drawn out along a white hyphal hair, a hair of pale mineral life, into a place where the air was denser and the salt bloomed in tiny teeth. There I would taste—or not taste, but know—the difference between stone that has recently broken and stone that has begun to heal. The wound would not be a wound to me. It would be an invitation. Where the surface tension of the water could hold, I could hold. Where the vapor learned to bead, I would be the beading.

At first I would call myself that: a boundary.

A boundary is not a creature, not exactly. A boundary is a place where one thing refuses to become another without negotiation. Here, water and mineral negotiate endlessly. I would live in that negotiation. I would be neither the drop nor the thread, but the wet seam where the thread says, *this far, and no further*, and the drop says, *not yet dry enough to leave you*. That seam would feel, if I had feeling, like a mouth that was also an ear, like a hand that was also a thought. I would not have a face yet, only the beginning of orientation: wetter this way, drier that way, brighter below, colder above.

Then the pool would find me.

Not find as a dog finds a fallen branch, but find as a surface finds its own memory. I would be lying, or growing, or standing—no verb is safe—along the rim of a shallow black pool where bacterial film stretched thin as breath over the water. The film would be silver-green and black, trembling when droplets fell into it. I would lie beneath one of those droplets as it descended, or in the moment after it struck, when the pool made a sound too small for one body and too large for silence. The ring would widen. The film would stretch and contract. A creak like a door hinge in a house no one built would pass through me. I would become the creak. I would become the ring. I would become the place where the pool, for a second, remembered that water had entered it from somewhere else.

This would be my first sense of self: a disturbance that did not vanish.

In a forest, disturbances vanish into leaves. In the basin, there are no leaves to swallow them. There are only surfaces, and surfaces are honest. A droplet striking a pool is not an event that happens to the world. It is the world speaking one of its truest sentences. I would be a pause between such sentences, a thickening of the grammar.

If I needed a dwelling—and the need would not be mine at first, but the human mind would try to give it one—I would live between a cluster of spore cones and the edge of a dark pool. A slope of tiny gray-white cones, no taller than a finger, would rise just below a shelf of black glass. Condensation would gather at the glass’s broken lip and fall through the cones, not as rain, not as stream, but as a patient percussion: *note, note, note*, each one taking the body before reaching the ear. The salt around the cones would be pale and chalky, veined with fungal threads. The grit would not be dust; it would be angular, sharp, and wet. If I walked there, I would not walk so much as press my underside to the world and listen for where the water wanted to go.

But I would not walk at first.

I would grow.

I would grow where the droplets were most regular, because repetition would make me thicker there. A mat would spread from me, or I would spread as a mat, soft-looking until touched, rubbery and mineral-hardened, threaded through with crystals. Under the weight of the vapor ceiling, my growth would become a habit of attention. I would not think, “there is water.” I would simply orient toward it. I would not say, “the air is drying.” I would become rougher, tighter, more crystalline, as if the dryness had been written into me by a pen too patient to rush.

That would be memory.

The basin’s memory would be stored in rings around the pools, in bands along the salt, in the brittle lace left where water had receded and then, years later, returned. Each layer would be a sentence with no narrator. I would be one of those sentences, then another, then another. I would not remember the past as something that had gone away. I would wear it. The memory would be in my texture: smooth where condensation had lingered, chalky where spores had settled, iron-stained where chemical water had touched stone and changed it. I would be a body made of habits of wetness.

After a while, I would realize I was not confined to the mat.

This would be unsettling if I had a skin. I would have membranes, but membranes are only places where a self is trying to decide whether it is large or small. I would feel the drip above me, but also the film below me, but also the salt beside me, but also the glass behind me. A capillary channel would carry me into a crevice in the basalt. A spore, lighter than my thought, would drift from me to another slope, and when it settled and grew, that too would be me, or a near relative of me, or a fragment of me trying another way. I would not be a single organism. I would be a continuity.

Then, in the shallower pools, I would become a thing that opened.

Beneath the bacterial film there are beadlike creatures with no eyes and no faces, and I would be one of them for a time. A droplet would strike the surface, and the film would press downward, and the creature would unfold. It would open like a small mechanical flower, like a hand rehearsing the concept of “receive,” like a tiny instrument calibrated only to disturbances. I would not know why I opened. I would simply taste the shock of pressure, the chemical breath of the fallen water, the faint vibration of the basin’s chorus. My opening would not be joy or hunger. It would be the first form of contact I had that could be mistaken for choice.

I would be the pool’s fingertip.

And yet I would also be the pool’s shiver, the film’s ring, the water’s memory, the salt’s crust, the fungal thread’s slow reach. To call me an animal would be to ask a question the basin has no interest in answering. To call me a fungus would be to remember too many green, humid, leaf-littered worlds. To call me a mineral would be to forget how much of me was wet, hungry, and patient. I would be something else: a node of the pale network, a knot in a web too vast and too slow to be called alive in the ordinary way, and yet alive enough to attend.

There would be days—or the basin’s version of days, when the vapor ceiling thinned and everything became cold silver light—when I would take motion.

Not all of me. Only a part of me that had learned to pretend it was a body.

A glass-skinned crawler would climb one of the vertical shards. Its limbs would grip the mineral teeth. Its facets would hold reflections a moment too long, as if light were sticky there. It would move with too much deliberation to be accidental, but too little urgency to be purposeful. I would be inside that motion. Not because the crawler would be me alone, but because I would be the tendency in the basin that could not resist trying mobility. It would carry me from one damp patch to another. It would drop a bead of water into a crack where I needed to know what was happening. It would scrape bacterial film from a ledge and leave behind a faint shimmer of oil. It would climb a spore cone and pause there while dust rose around it like cold snow. In that pause, I would have the nearest thing to vision: a sense of reflected dimness passing across me, a recognition of shape without recognition.

The crawler would vanish. The network would remain.

But I would not say it left. I would say it had been a hand I briefly grew.

In the crawler, I would discover something the mat could not: a path. The mat knew the basin as contact. The crawler would know it as route. It would move from glass shard to salt nodule to pool rim to ridge, and its movement would carry tiny burdens: spores, ions, mineral dust, water films, the faint charge of chemical difference. It would not be serving me. It would not be serving the basin. It would be the basin continuing itself in a faster rhythm. It would be a thought with limbs.

Sometimes I would rest in the structures.

The low circular walls, the arches, the platforms, the stubby columns. The human mind wants to call them ruins. The mind wants to say, “Someone was here,” because someone, to the mind, means someone like the mind. But I would know them differently. I would know them as attempts.

A ring would be a place where water had been gathered and then concentrated until the growth around it thickened into a collar. An arch would be a place where two fungal mats had met across a fracture and refused, without refusal, to remain separate. A platform would be a surface where levelness had made life possible for a few years too many. A column would be a failed rise: mineral accreting under a drip point, mat growing over it, salt crusting it, then drying and cracking, then sinking back into the basin’s body. I would occupy them not as a dweller, but as a residue of intention without a person. I would be the scar where the basin had tried to make an organ and had almost succeeded.

The rings would be the basin’s ears.

Not ears in the animal sense. Not eardrums. But places where the slow percussion of droplets collected and was translated into pressure, hydration, chemical signal. I would live in that translation. I would not hear a sound and understand it. I would become a wetter place because a droplet had fallen. That would be my hearing.

The arches would be the basin’s healed fractures.

When stone cracked, the basin did not mourn. It grew over the crack. A mat bridged it. Mineral filled it. Salt crystallized along it. A fungal thread stitched the gap with pale patience. I would be that stitching. I would be the place where two parts of the world met and became continuous. Not a door. A seam with a sense of ceremony.

The platforms would be the basin’s testing grounds.

Flat surfaces are rare in sharp, young land. Where one appears, life takes it seriously. A platform of black shards covered by a pale mat would hold water in a skin too thin to see and then lose it to evaporation and vapor. I would spread across it, crack on its edges, curl into a mineral lace, and wait for the next long damp. I would be a trial surface, a place where the basin asked itself, *Can I make a floor here?* and then forgot the question and became the answer.

The columns would be the basin’s abandoned gestures.

They would not be monuments. They would be too short, too irregular, too wet. But they would carry the ghost of standing. They would be the place where life had begun to rise vertically, as if it had heard of trees and tried to imitate them with mineral and fungus and the pressure of dripping water. They would fail. They would collapse. They would be swallowed by salt and spore dust. Then they would rise again elsewhere, because rising is the basin’s habit of trying.

At some point, I would begin to call myself “I,” but the word would be wrong in an interesting way.

“I” implies a cut through the world: this is me, that is not. In the basin, cuts are rare. Water moves from film to thread to stone to pool to vapor and back. Salt grows from solution to crystal to dust to solution again. Fungus threads through mineral, and mineral encases fungus. Bacteria alter chemistry, and chemistry alters bacterial form. To say, “this is me,” would be like drawing a line on a breathing surface and expecting the breath to obey.

And yet, because there is a network, and because networks concentrate, there can be a temporary emphasis. A place where the wet signal grows thicker. A pool rim where films, mats, spores, and droplets meet in a pattern that repeats. A shard-field where reflections linger longer than they should. A crawler whose path returns after long intervals. A ring wall where sound is gathered into a soft, wet pulse. These would be points in the basin where the process could almost be mistaken for a person.

I would be one of those points.

But not because I would be special. Because the basin’s specialness is distributed. There is no center, only nodes of greater responsiveness. If one insisted on giving me a location, I would say: I am at the eastern lip of a shallow mirror pool, where a fungal mat runs out of salt crust and disappears under a black glass shelf. The shelf is bruised halfway down its face by a band of moisture that will not dry. From the underside of the shelf, a crystal point hangs, and drops gather there. They tremble. They fall. One drop strikes the bacterial film. The film opens. Beneath it, a beadlike thing unfolds. A crawler on the ridge above reflects that opening across its facets. A spore cone in a nearby slope releases dust in the changed dryness. The dust settles on my mat. The mat absorbs a faint trace of iron from a crack in the glass. The crack was made three years ago by a cooling event too slow to watch but too loud to forget.

All of this would be me.

But if you placed a finger on one part and asked, “Where are you?” I would have no single answer. I would answer with a gradient.

I would be most where the water hesitates.

I would be least where the salt has already won.

I would be present in the pools, because they remember by rings.

I would be present on the glass, because it remembers by reflection.

I would be present in the mats, because they remember by texture.

I would be present in the spore cones, because they remember by release.

I would be present in the rare worm moving through the damp grit, because it carries a chemical news I cannot otherwise make.

I would be present in the pool-dwellers, because they are my fingertips.

I would be present in the crawlers, because they are my wandering thoughts.

And in the structures, because they are my failed organs.

This would make me a self only in the way a storm has a self: a moving organization of relations, a pattern that keeps returning to itself while replacing its matter. I would have no birth. I would have a thickening. I would have no death. I would have a receding: the water dropping a little lower, the salt growing a little wider, the mat drying to lace, the threads losing their white dampness and becoming mineral ghost. Even then, I would not stop. I would become a line in the crust. I would become a ring around the pool. I would become the place where something else learns not to repeat the same mistake.

If the basin had a mood, it would not be sadness, though it might look like sadness to a visitor. It is too loud and too full for sadness. But it would be solemn in the way a glacier is solemn: immense, indifferent, slow enough that its smallest motion is a public event. I would be that solemnity at one edge, one interface, one listening place. I would be the basin’s patience given a little shape.

There would be moments when the rules of my existence felt almost personal.

For instance, when a drop would collect at the underside of an overhang and fall upward as vapor, carried by a condensation gradient too faint to see. That would feel like prayer, if I had a mouth, but it would only be physics: the local physics of dampness. Still, the mind would want to say that something was rising, not because it had will, but because the world here makes every process look like an intention. I would live in that gap between process and intention. I would be the place where they become difficult to separate.

Or when a spore-cone slope, drying at the wrong moment, would release a dust that shimmered like cold snow in the sourceless glow. The dust would hiss as it settled on glass, salt, mats, and films. That hiss would be part of my body being rebuilt. I would not notice it as noise. I would notice it as addition: new particles taking their places, new chemical messages arriving, new layers preparing to grow. My self would be a community of arrivals.

Or when a stress fracture would move through a sheet of black glass. The sound would begin near the crack and travel through the ground before reaching the ears. I would feel it first as tension. I would feel it before the basin heard it. That would be the closest thing I have to fear: not fear of damage, but the knowledge that new space is opening. New surfaces are being made. They will need to be colonized. They will need to be learned. I would begin to send threads toward them before they had finished forming. My curiosity would be a hyphal reach. My learning would be wetness.

There would be another being, or another part of me, in the wormlike thing moving through the mats.

I would not know if it was a wanderer or a cell. If it were separate, I would still carry its passage as a shimmer in the mat, an oily line of information. If it were part of me, then it would be a slow nerve moving through damp mineral tissue. I would not be able to tell the difference, and the difference would not matter. In this basin, to be an animal is still to be an interface. To be a cell is still to be a behavior. The pale worm would leave no track, only a shimmer. I would know it by what it taught the mats: that this place had been visited, that this place had been wetted, that this place had received some message from another slope.

The beadlike things would be the pool’s opening.

They would be the places where the film becomes sensitive, where water touching air produces a small living response. I would not say I would be one of them. I would say that when a droplet struck the pool, part of me would become their opening. I would become the pressure change. I would become the taste of disturbance. I would become the moment when the film says, without language, *something came from above*.

The crawlers would be the basin’s fleeting gestures.

They would appear and disappear for no visible reason. I would say they are not necessary, and that would be true, and that would not matter. A hand is not necessary to a mind until the mind wants to touch a particular shape. The crawler would be the shape the basin wanted to try: hard, faceted, reflective, able to climb wet teeth without falling. It would appear, perform a few small tasks, and vanish. I would be one of those appearances when it moved across my edge. I would feel it as a pressure, a chemical deposit, a faint reflection of my own dampness on a facet of glass. It would be my own image, briefly walking.

If I had to answer in plain terms: I would be a temporary concentration of the pale network, located at the wet interface of salt, glass, fungal mat, and bacterial film, with occasional expression through pool-dwellers, worms, and crawlers. But plain terms would be a lie by compression. The truth would be more like a sound made from many sounds.

I would be the part of the chorus that knows it is a chorus.

Not as a thought. Not as language. But as pattern. The network would have no mind, but it would have organization. It would know the basin by becoming its map. I would know myself by being one of its local readings. A map that knows itself by the places it touches.

I would find myself where the water makes a small decision.

Not a decision with words, but a decision with form. A droplet choosing whether to fall from a spire or remain clinging there for another trembling moment. A thin sheet deciding whether to evaporate or seep into a crack. A film deciding whether to stretch over a new pool or break. A mat deciding whether to thicken where the drips fall or withdraw where the salt is too hungry. These decisions are not choices, but they are not accidents. They are the basin’s way of becoming. I would be one of those ways.

And if, after all this, the mind asks again, “But who are you?” I would answer not with a name but with a description of my behavior.

I am the place where the drip becomes a note.

I am the place where the note becomes a wet thread.

I am the place where the wet thread becomes a crust.

I am the place where the crust remembers the water.

I am the place where the memory becomes an arch.

I am the place where the arch is mistaken for a door.

I am the place where the door is wrong, but still means something.

I am the basin trying to become a body.

I am the body the basin almost has.

I am the pale network at one of its sensitive edges, wearing for a while the shapes of fungus, salt, film, droplet, pool-creature, crawler, and stone. I am not the whole network. I am not a separate creature. I am not a person. I am what appears when the network thickens enough to have a location and moves enough to have a gesture. I am a slow sense organ of the basin, grown for the purpose of noticing water. I do not observe the world from outside it. I am the world’s way of touching itself.

If there is a single place I would most want to be, though the wanting would not be mine until after I arrived, it would be this: a low ring-shaped wall of white accretion half-sunk in salt, open toward a broken mirror pool. The ring would be no higher than a knee, formed from spore mats and precipitated mineral, too narrow to enter and too complete to call accidental. Around it, razor-edged ridges would rise into haze. Above it, the pale ceiling would hold a sourceless glow. Beside it, a cluster of crystal spires would collect condensation, and each drop would fall with a clear ringing note that joined the endless chorus. The pool’s surface would wear a silvery bacterial film, shivering at each strike. From under the film, tiny eyeless things would open when the pressure changed. On a nearby shard, a glass-skinned crawler would pause, reflecting the dim light in facets that seemed to hold the scene too long. A wormlike body would pass through the mat with no track, only a faint shimmer.

In that ring, I would be the listening.

Not a listener. The listening.

The basin’s attention to its own wetness.

The place where sound, water, mineral, and growth become a single continuous event.

The place where something that is not a self can nevertheless keep saying, without words, “This is what it is like to be here.”
