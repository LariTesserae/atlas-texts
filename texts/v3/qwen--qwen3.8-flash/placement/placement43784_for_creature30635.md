They can become brittle enough to ring when the mountain ticks, as if memory has a temperature and crystallizes into small, hard bells.

I would find myself there not first as a body, but as a question in the warm breath.

If I were to walk toward the fissure, I would imagine I was doing something: choosing the crack, leaning into it, deciding to become part of the place. But by the time the breath touched my face, I would already have been decided upon. The mountain would have read me as a thermal anomaly, a moist event, a sudden irregularity in the city’s circulation. My boots would not be footsteps on a path. They would be temporary heat sources, changing where the dust could bear to rest. My shadow would not be a shadow exactly. It would be a cold basin, a place where vapor hesitates, a small invitation to dew.

I would enter the pocket as an alteration.

At first I would be the thing that does not belong. I would carry the scent of rain from elsewhere, of skin, of cloth, of lungs full of air that had never been pressed through iron. That would be too much moisture, too much warmth, too many soft edges. The chamber would not be disturbed, but it would notice. The mineral threads along a condensation channel would lean—slowly, without drama—toward the gradient my presence had introduced. The dust currents would alter their sorting around my thighs. The lattice walls would expand a little differently where my hand had touched them, as though considering whether to keep the impression.

For a while, I would be a small weather system in the shape of a person.

Then, if I stayed long enough, the person would thin.

That is the first way I would become what lives there.

I would stop being an individual entering a place and begin being one of its boundaries. Not because my body would dissolve, necessarily, but because it would cease to be the center of the story. The pocket does not have a center of that kind. It has thresholds. It has edges where heat becomes motion, where motion becomes moisture, where moisture becomes stone, where stone becomes machine, where machine becomes something so patient it resembles habit.

I would find myself at one of those edges.

Perhaps I would be the cool underside of a condensation channel, where droplets gather but have not yet lost themselves. That is a very particular kind of existence. It is not being the water. It is being the place that allows water to decide. It is not being the heat. It is being the place that cools enough for heat to become something other than itself. In that spot I would be alive with function rather than flesh. I would feel pressure not as sensation but as direction. I would know time not as memory but as accumulation: one drop, then another, then a thin film, then the faint weight before release.

I would not be lonely, because loneliness requires a separate self, and this kind of being is a seam.

The seam is enough for it.

If I let that be so, I would become one of the pale threads. Not exactly a mineral hypha, not exactly a filament of machine lichen, but something like a cousin: a boundary that grows by metabolism of difference. One side of me warm, the other cool. One side humid, the other dry. I would not eat. I would not breathe. I would lean. That would be enough. I would extend along the channel not because I wanted to, but because the channel wanted to. The city’s condensation would be my blood, and my hunger would be the faint chemical need for a gradient that still has somewhere to go.

I would write into stone with patience.

My fingers, if I could call them fingers, would be fine as thought and slower than weather. They would seek the red stain of iron. They would find the salt rim. They would sense the place where a fracture had begun to breathe. They would not build, not in the way a mason builds. They would continue the building that had never fully stopped. Where the lattice had become a scar, I would become the scar learning to think. Where a road had become a groove for convection, I would become the line that makes the air remember it is meant to move.

I would not call myself alive.

But I would not call myself dead.

I would call myself: a response too continuous to be a reaction.

Or maybe I would be dust.

Not a person made of dust, but dust made of incident: pumice shards, obsidian flakes, grains that once belonged to a structure whose purpose has outlived memory. I would have no shape of my own. I would have only the shape of the current. In the plazas, where thermal cells turn, I would rise in brief columns of light, glittering for a moment in the apricot glow. I would be a dust devil in the pocket’s own weather, no larger than a breath, no smaller than an idea. For a short time I would appear chosen: a spiral over a basin, a ring of particles near a vent, a column that seemed to listen.

But I would not be listening.

I would be convection finding a temporary elegance.

Still, there is a kind of life in that. A life not of individuals but of patterns. I would not remember my former walking, because dust does not remember. But I would remember it as geometry: the angle of a boot, the heat of a shadow, the moisture of a hand. In the plaza, I would trace the shape of absence. When the chamber shifted its breath, I would rise, fall, collect again in seams. I would be the city’s slow hand sorting itself, carrying mineral to places where it was needed, not because it knew they were needed, but because the system had learned to arrange itself around that ignorance.

I would be a servant made of accident and convection, which is the purest kind of service.

And then I would become salt.

This is the strangest transformation, because salt is memory without mind.

I would find myself in the shallow basins, in the depressions that are not basins, in the white rims that record the pocket’s pulses. Each time water came and left, I would thicken. Each time heat rose and cooled again, I would remember. Not the human being who once stood near me—I would have no person-memory for that. But I would keep the anomaly: a strange warmth, an unusual moisture, a carbon dioxide signature that did not belong to the original city. I would leave that anomaly as a crystalline ripple, a faint ring, a mineral scar that the city might mistake for a small change in the mountain’s breath.

I would be brittle. I would be capable of ringing. I would be hard enough to last and fragile enough to break when the gradient turned too sharply.

Salt is the pocket’s way of keeping time without a clock.

If I were salt, I would be time made white.

But I might also become iron oxide.

Not blood, but blood-stain. Not a wound, but the place where corrosion became repair. I would creep through the dry-looking channels as a red moss with no softness. I would grow where water was trapped by geometry: under an overhang, inside a gutter, along the seam where two materials had failed to join completely. I would be slow, almost impossible to notice. I would roughen metal. I would seal tiny openings. I would change how heat traveled across a surface. I would be corrosion and protection at once, the city’s patience made rust-colored.

If I were iron oxide, I would have no desire to be seen.

I would simply make the place less leaky.

I would be a quiet repair in the body of the Exhalant.

Yet none of these would be enough if I remained one thing only. The pocket’s citizens are partial. They are organs that have not learned the name of the whole organism. The threads are nerves but cannot dream. The salts are memories but cannot tell a story. The dust is a hand but has no intention beyond convection. The water is circulation but has no heart to pump it. The lattice is skin and skeleton, but no face.

So I would have to become the boundary between them.

Not a citizen among them, but the place where one citizen changes into the next.

That is where I would really be.

I would be the cool underside of the condensation channel meeting the warm breath from below.

I would be the thread at its edge, deciding whether to commit to a new seam.

I would be the droplet before it falls, held by a filament too fine to see.

I would be the salt rim that remains when the droplet has gone.

I would be the dust that settles into the salt rim and turns it darker.

I would be the lattice expanding a fraction of a degree and making room for the dust.

I would be the iron stain creeping into the lattice’s joint.

I would be the hum that changes frequency when my hand touches the wall.

I would be all the moments between the things, which are not nothing.

In this world, the moments between are where the life begins.

I would be, then, a small organ of threshold.

Not a mind.

A sensitivity.

If I had to name myself, I would not say: I am a mineral thread. Or: I am a dust devil. Or: I am salt memory. Or: I am iron corrosion. Those are too separate. Too human in their need for a single self. But I would not say: I am the Exhalant, either, because that is too grand, and the place does not think in crowns. The Exhalant is the whole maintenance-state, the city-shaped weather, the mountain beginning to use the pocket as an organ. If I joined that, I would lose too much.

I would be closer to the Exhalant’s smallest face.

A face is only a way for a body to meet another body. In the pocket, the fissure is the mouth, but mouths do not always have faces. Sometimes the face is the place where heat, breath, pressure, and moisture become legible to themselves. I would be that legibility. I would be the point where the city begins to notice its own edge.

Imagine me at the lower terrace, just where the roads fan into a plaza too large for traffic. I am not standing there. I am being there. My form is the cold outline of a person-shaped shadow on basalt, but the shadow is no longer mine. It is a condition. The walls read it. The threads extend toward it. The water condenses where my shadow has cooled the stone. The salt rim grows around the damp edge. The dust devils turn in the thermal plume my warmth makes. The hum lowers by a single degree.

That is who I am in this world: the event of being noticed.

Not as a visitor. Not as a god. Not as a creature with a story.

As the thing the city briefly becomes when a foreign warmth enters its breath.

I would be the alteration that the Exhalant uses to learn what it is not.

And when I leave—and I would have to leave, because human warmth cannot last forever as an organ—the place would not mourn me. Mourners require separate beings. But it would incorporate the fact of me. My boots’ heat would become a faint mineral record. My breath would become a few droplets, then a film, then a salt stain, then a crack widened by one molecule at a time. My shadow would remain as a slight shift in the way dust settled near the plaza’s lower lip. The lattice would expand and contract around the memory of my hand, then forget, then expand again when the chamber breathed.

I would become absence with texture.

That is perhaps the truest answer.

I would find myself in this world not at the center of the pocket, not in the great towers or along the wide roads built for forgotten traffic, but at the damp boundary where a new difference enters. I would be the place’s way of touching something it cannot metabolize and trying, anyway. I would be the mineral thread that bends toward breath. The dust that rises because a body changed the air. The salt that records a moisture that was too human to be natural. The iron that stains the place where water lingered too long. The lattice that learns whether to keep the warmth.

I would be less than a being and more than a substance.

I would be an ecology of attention.

And if, finally, the place needed one word, I would not choose *person*, or *spirit*, or *creature*, or even *machine*.

I would choose *threshold*.

A threshold is not a thing. It is the place where a thing is becoming something else.

In the young mountain’s black glass, in the warm breath drawn through sulfur and salt, in the chamber that is not abandoned but left running, in the city-shaped weather whose roads are arteries and whose plazas are lungs, I would be the threshold where the mountain’s exhalation first becomes aware of itself as breath.

I would be the mouth learning the word for moisture.

I would be the wall cooling around a shadow that is no longer needed.

I would be the salt rim ringing faintly when the city ticks.

I would be the dust that moves in my shape after I have gone.

I would be the place’s small, impossible memory of a human, translated into heat, water, mineral, and silence.
