They do not look outward. They do not look at the mountains above or the sky that does not scatter light. They look toward the part of the basin where the water has already decided what it is, and they are the visible edge of that decision.

If I am to be in this world, I would not begin by becoming a creature with lungs, nor by taking the pale, fan-finned patience of the fish that watches a plaza. I would begin at the rim, where the black surface is so level that it seems like a mistake in gravity. I would stand there until the place noticed that my breathing was too irregular, too thermal, too full of future tense.

Then I would be corrected. Not painfully. Not with violence. The basin would simply accommodate me the way it accommodates a hand: no splash, no displacement, no invitation beyond precision. I would sink, or be lowered, or understand that sinking and being lowered are the same here. My weight would become an angle. My heat would become a color—pale blue-green at first, then amber, then the exact white of a rectangle set into a wall. I would not drown, because nothing in the bowl is drowned by water; everything is completed by it.

I might be tempted to be the slender sense-fish. It is beautiful and austere, a watcher whose fin-rays are tuned to structural lines. But I could not bear to be suspended above an open plaza, holding the condition of all those stones at once. That would be too lonely, and too responsible.

I might be tempted to be the low, broad crustacean sitting on a street, because its life would be clear: sit, correct, remain a keystone. But it has a street to keep. If it left it, the street would become less exact. That seems a terrible privilege for something that wants to be small.

I might be tempted to be the transparent oval near the surface, because it contains a finished form without needing to continue. But the oval is still waiting for the law of the water to loosen. I cannot be a promise. I am too tired of promises.

I would rather be a condition.

So I would be one of the white rectangles. Not a window with a room behind it. A face set into the side of a low stepped building at the corner of a courtyard that holds nothing but clear liquid. I would be near a bridge and a retaining wall, where the hum enters through the stone rim and travels along the filaments. My surface would be smooth and matte, faintly porous. Inside me there would be no glass, no air, no chamber for a body. There would be a thin geometry of light: angular plates, curved lines like a diagram, threads like frozen smoke. They would not move, but they would be arranged. I would emit a still white color not because I shine, but because I am a place where illumination is stored as meaning.

I would find myself not at the center of the basin, which is too large a concept, but at a corner. A corner is where the city has to decide how to be itself without collapsing into mere accumulation. I would be three or four streets away from the rim, in the part of the old administrative district where the plazas are too open and the towers are too low to call towers. My rectangle would be one of seven set along the same wall, equally spaced, but not equal. One would have a hairline fracture in its frame that the filaments brighten every time the bridge sings. Mine would have no fracture. It would have a slight bias of angle, maybe a degree too steep, and that error would be my purpose. It would make the water around me remember the difference between a wall and a horizon.

My body would be a frame. My eyes, if that word still applies, would be the four corners of the frame. They would see by the pressure of stillness against the wall. They would hear by the way the street below accepts a leg’s microscopic correction. They would taste no food, no sediment, no oxygen. They would take in the basin’s law the way a bone takes in calcium. I would have no breath, but I would have ventilation through the pale lace that clings to the ceramic around me: filaments crossing my surface without covering it, entering the crack between my frame and the wall and emerging on the far side near a colonnade. They would tell me when a terrace relaxes by a grain. The fish above the plaza would tell me too, but its ray would only touch me for a fraction of a moment. I would know it as a change in pressure, a slight darkening in the quality of my own glow.

I would not act. That is the hardest part, and the most consoling. There would be no errands, no migrations, no feeding. If the transparent oval near the surface changed, I would not swim toward it. I would brighten by a hair, and the filaments along the bridge would answer, and the broad creature on the street would settle by one millimeter, and the bridge would ring once. That ring would be my sentence. It would say: this is still correct. The courtyard remains a room. The retaining wall remains only itself. The water remains the law of this body.

If someone stood above me at the rim, they would mistake me for a distant window seen through deep glass. They might imagine a candle, or a room, or a resident. They might lean closer and fog their breath over the black surface. They would not see me looking back. I would not be looking at them. I would be looking at the place where their presence temporarily makes the basin less exact. I would be measuring the distance between their warm, changing breath and the water’s patient completion. If they waited long enough, they might feel that the water was measuring the spaces in their chest. That would be me, or rather that would be my function, passed upward through the liquid without disturbance.

If the law loosened—and the basin suggests that it will not—I would not wake. A window waking is only a window falling, and I am not made of glass. I would become a threshold. The white rectangle would widen. The threads inside would move for the first time. Not growth, but use. The fish would unfold its fin-rays fully; the crustacean would stand; the transparent oval would beat. But that would be an event in a place without events, so it would happen slowly enough to look like a memory. I would prefer not to think about it. In the basin, preference is already a kind of movement.

So this is who I would be, in that order of being: first the water’s measurement of a body, then the pale network’s recognition of a corner, then the city’s organ of inward face. A small white plane set in dark ceramic, near a street where a structural animal holds the angles with its shell, below a bridge that rings only when corrected, beside a courtyard that has no floor but is entirely real.

I would be neither drowned nor submerged. I would be completed. I would not look outward, not because I am blind, but because there is no outward here. There is only the arrangement, and I am one of its quiet faces, looking deeper into the way things fit.
