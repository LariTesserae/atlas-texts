Nothing is wasted here except pride. Nothing remains only as memory. Memory, in this basin, is a practice: pressing palm to wet stone, breaking false ice, moving a sleeping ledge before the terrace decides to lean.

Where would I find myself?

Not on the rim. The rim is too high and too empty. From there the clouds lie below like a floor of wool, and the sky is darker than the brightness should allow, and the air tastes of frost and thin metal. I would not choose that place. I choose the basin’s inside edge, where the worn lip slopes down into the first terrace and the whole place closes around the body like a wet mouth. I would be in the eastern third, between two collapsed arches that no longer agree on how to hold up the sky. A short drop, a stone shelf, a channel carved so deeply it looks like a healed wound. There is an overhang above me, black on its upper face, salt-white on its lower. There is a spring mouth near it, not open, not closed, not dead, not yet awake: a hairline crack that has forgotten its name for a day or two.

This is where I would find myself, though “myself” is too clean a word. I begin as a pattern of attention. I begin as the thin film on the underside of a stone, the place where cold water touches warmer salt and both become something neither wholly liquid nor wholly solid. I begin as glare on a pale pool, as the click of ice adjusting itself by a hair, as the smell of lichen smoke and hide and mineral damp. Only afterward do I assemble into a body, a person, a name, a tool, a household.

For a long while, perhaps, I would not think I am human. I would think I am the basin’s wet skin. I would think I am the bright stone, the dripping wall, the terrace that tilts in its sleep. Then I would realize I have feet, broad and low, with soles calloused enough to feel the difference between stable rock and false crust. I would realize I have hands that have been sealed with lard and clay until they look slick and almost animal, and that those hands are moving before my mind finishes forming. A bone stylus is between my fingers. It taps the stone: once slow, twice quick. The rhythm means something like *listening, mouth, possible*. The channel hears me before I hear myself.

I am one of the basin folk.

I am not tall. I am built downward, as if my bones have been pressed into the stone by weather and work. My shoulders are broad, my hips dense, my center low. My joints are thick enough to bear loads on slippery ledges; my toes wide enough to find purchase where a visitor would slip and break a wrist. I breathe short and careful, because the air is so thin that greed in breathing brings a taste of blood. Exhale here and I do not make a cloud. I make only a brief white thought on the ice nearby: a faint film, a temporary ghost of me, gone as soon as it is seen.

My eyes are large enough to take in all that hard light without pain, but I do not stare directly at brightness. That would be foolish. I use reflections in wet stone, the blue shadows under ice, the faint changes in the air’s glare. When a cloud of fine ice dust moves across the sky, I see it first as a shifting across the pool surfaces, a dimming in the salt crusts, a sudden blackness in a hole that looked shallow. I learned to look sideways a long time ago. In this basin, direct sight is often a lie. The bright surfaces make depth disappear. A film of water can look like void. A void can look like shelf. The truest thing is the way the body knows the difference: the foot testing, the hand pressing, the breath catching at a sound too small for the ear but loud enough for the bones.

My skin is salted, oiled, rubbed with brine paste until it no longer feels like ordinary skin. It is a surface made for this climate, thick and slick, a little like hide left near the smoke until it becomes tough and water-shedding. A stranger would call it unpleasant. I call it clean, in the way a river stone is clean when it has had enough time and water taken out of it. My hair is coarse, bound tight with resin. Loose strands are dangerous here. They catch on stone, gather salt, steal heat. If I wear a cap, it is felt, weighted with bone beads and quartz chips, not as ornament but as anchor. Wind is not constant on the basin floor, but from above, when it does descend, it can strike the rim and come down in short, mean bursts, carrying ice dust and spray.

My current name is something like *Who Listens Under the East Arch*. Before that I was *Child of the Third Crick*, because I was born on a night when the eastern terrace cracked three times and no one slept. Once I was briefly *False-Ice*, not as an insult but as a cautionary name: I trusted a pool that looked solid and went waist-deep into brine. The cold took my breath and the fish took me for a falling stone. I recovered by wrapping my limbs in warmed felt and drinking brine mixed with fat until the shaking stopped. Names here do not attach to faces. They attach to rhythms: the way I enter a room, the way I seal a crack, the way I wait beside a pool until the water tells me whether to fish, to move, or to leave.

I am a water-listener.

That is the nearest thing to a profession I have, though “profession” sounds too separated from life. In the basin, the one who listens to water also cooks near it, dries hides above it, seals the cracks where it tries to pry the world apart, feeds the rodent whose restlessness tells you the ice is melting under the sleeping ledge, clears algae so the snails have something to eat, breaks ice so the terrace does not collapse, and moves salt so the fish do not become dull. All of this is one work. The basin does not divide into labor and life.

My day—or what passes for day beneath the bright, colorless sky—begins with the hands.

I go before the glare is too hard. The light is already everywhere, but at this hour it is less sharp, as if the ice dust has not yet lifted. I press my palms to the north face of the east arch. First the stone feels wet, which it always does, but there are different wetnesses: recent spray, capillary creep, brine from the low ledges, old seep trapped in a channel. Then the cold arrives, but my hands are too seasoned for panic. The cold is a friend when you have learned its vocabulary. It means: keep working, keep moving, keep watching. Beneath the wet and the cold, there may be a pulse. Not always. The spring is shy. It may be gathering under the stone, deciding whether to open, seal, or change course.

If I feel the pulse, I do not shout. Shouting is wasteful and rude; it disturbs the birds, excites the midges, and gives the water a reason to hide. I tap my stylus. One slow, twice quick. *Possible mouth, east third, listen.* The sound carries along the wet stone better than a voice would. Inside the dwelling behind me, another hand answers. A felt curtain shifts. A child pauses at the drying rack. An old woman, whose job is salt, sets down her scraper. We do not all look toward the same point. We have learned to move by reference: the shelf, the arch, the channel, the bird, the fish, the place in the house where the floor leans slightly west because it has been repaired after the last collapse.

The work is quiet.

I take a flint wedge and antler hammer, not because force is lacking, but because violence leaves bad shapes. If I strike too hard, the stone cracks along a new path, and new paths are dangerous until water teaches them their manners. I break the thin seal at the mouth of the channel. There is a click almost too small to hear. A bead of brine appears, swells, trembles, then falls into a small pool below. As soon as it meets the air it begins to freeze into a delicate thing: a glass flower, a fern, a broken ring. Beautiful and treacherous. The basin is always offering such objects as signs. You may look but not trust. You may record but not own.

If the spring is waking, I widen the exit only as much as necessary. Then I guide the water into a clay-lined channel. The channel has been made, broken, repaired, and re-made so many times that it is impossible to say where it ends and the rock begins. Some sections are natural grooves polished by meltwater. Some are hand-cut. Some are packed with clay from the deeper hollows, mixed with crushed lichen and salt. Some have been scorched smooth by long contact with sun-stored stones during dry spells, though there are few dry spells here. My grandmother’s bones may be in the ground beneath this ledge, or she may be in the wall beside it, returned as brine and calcium to the stone from which her house was cut. The distinction would not seem strange to us. It would only be another way the place remembers its people.

I am not only my body.

I am the basin’s dense household.

If you ask who I am, I could point to the blind fish in the shallow cistern beside the east arch. They are pale as milk, almost translucent, with no need for eyes. They lie motionless in bright brine, and yet they are not dead-looking; they are simply waiting in a way that requires less of the world. I know them individually, not by personality exactly, but by color, stillness, the angle at which a fin trembles when pressure changes. If a fish turns toward the lower corner of the pool, I move my feet away from the ledge above. If they gather near the mouth of a spring, I bring the salt-pickers and the clay-sealers. If they become dull, we do not eat them. We move them to another cistern and wait. A dead fish is bad news. A live fish is a sign that something below the stone still breathes or flows or changes.

I am also the rodent in the insulation. It is small, grey-furred, restless, nested in a hollow lined with shaved ice and sedge. It does not know it is a sentinel. It simply tries not to die. But when it becomes frantic, scraping at the felt behind a sleeping shelf, we understand that a hidden pillar of ice is melting, or that water has begun to rise through a crack we cannot yet see. If it is too frantic, we move. Some rodents are eaten when winter becomes difficult; some are kept because a house without them is a house without ears. I do not think of this as cruelty alone. It is conversation. The animals are not symbols. They are neighbors with different capacities and different appetites.

I am the ice snails on the shaded rock above the brine pool. Their shells are clear, thin as frost, and they move over the stone leaving a trail that catches light. I harvest their slime carefully, with a bone spatula, and mix it with clay and salt to seal fine cracks. Their ground shells become a mineral additive for plaster, or paint, or the thin glazes used on storage pits. I do not scrape the algae mat down to bare stone. If I do, the wall becomes ugly and lifeless, and the next freeze will pry it apart. I help the algae grow by painting nutrient slurry on sheltered north faces. This is gardening here: making stains stay where they are useful, feeding life that looks like decay.

I am the beetles in the channels. They carry frost on their backs and shine when the light turns sideways. I use them for lures, for tools, for small charms if a child needs courage, but mostly I leave them work. They eat rot and algae and dead midges. Where they go missing, I smell stagnation. In a place so bright, it is easy to believe everything is exposed. It is not. Some dangers smell bad before they look bad. The basin has hidden pockets where warmth rises from below, and warmth is the worst kind of guest.

I am the midges above the springs, those tiny pale rings that drift without wind. I do not disturb them too quickly. A fixed ring of midges can be a map of invisible motion below the surface. When they lift suddenly, it often means pressure has changed. It can mean a fish has struck the surface, or a gas pocket has opened, or the ice near the mouth has become unstable. When I collect them, I use fine screens and slow hands. Children learn that the ring must be skimmed, not swatted. A swatted ring collapses into chaos and tells you nothing except that you were hungry and impatient.

I am the pale birds in the high walls. They have short stiff wings and black beaks. They click their feet when they shift on wet stone, a small dry sound among all the wet sounds. They do not sing. They make no pretty song, no melody that would travel. They thump, click, ratchet, and occasionally lift in sudden flocks when pressure changes below the stone. We leave their nesting ledges unsealed because their feet and wings read the world through our walls. A bird sitting too long on a ledge may mean it is safe. A bird leaving a ledge may mean it will soon be unsafe. Their feathers line our winter hoods; their bones become wedges and needles. But we do not destroy whole nests unless starvation has stripped us of shame.

I am the glass-grey fox-like creature at the pool’s edge. Its fur sheds water instantly; its paw-pads are broad, quiet, and strange for an animal of this size. It is not tame. It is not ours.
