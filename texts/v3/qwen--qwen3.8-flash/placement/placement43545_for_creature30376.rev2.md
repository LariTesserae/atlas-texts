If the basin chose me before I chose it, I would not be given a hill, a ruin, or a clear horizon. There is no such place. I would be given a seam.

I would find myself in the thin black water just beneath a low obsidian shelf, where three ordinary impossibilities of this world meet: the shelf’s glassy underside sweating slow droplets that move too deliberately; a brine channel that has learned to run uphill because the fog above it is heavier than gravity below; and a shallow pool that still holds the mouth-shape of something that drank there long ago. The pool is dark enough to be a hole in the world, smooth enough to be skin, oily enough to break and heal in the same breath. Dim sideways light moves across its surface, not as sun but as pressure becoming visible. The air tastes of metal, wet salt, ash, and the burnt sugar of something oxidizing far beneath the ground.

I would be an aftershape.

That is not one of the basin’s familiar names, but it would belong there. It would be understood, if understood meant anything so clean, as an echo-sitter grown from a detached shadow and fed by the rhythm left in a sound-shard. I would be a mouth-mist, a living indentation, a hunger shaped by the absence that caused it. I would be thin: a dark, wide film lying just below the surface of the black pool, edged with gill-slits that opened and shut with the cracking of oily ice. I would have no face in the way a face requires memory of its own outline. My outline would be borrowed. My body would be the place where the basin remembered too clearly that something had once drunk there.

If I had come from elsewhere—if the phrase “come from elsewhere” meant anything in a place where distances are approximate and horizons fold over one another—I would have arrived not as a standing thing, not as a traveler with feet, but as a mouth that did not know it had no body. The water would have held that mouth after the rest of me failed. A tileback might have passed, scraping its shell along a salt shelf, dragging a wet furrow through the mineral dust. A linebeast might have lowered itself to drink, its many segments becoming for one moment not herd nor weather but a single tired shape with thirst. The pool would have kept the hollow. The hollow would have become hungry. That hunger would be me.

The felted ones would grow through me first.

Their white-black threads would enter the shadow at my rim, threading between the dark and the water, learning my pressure, making me less stain than system. They would not ask permission; in this basin, no colonization is asked for. The land is body, and anything that mistakes it for body is welcome, or worse, useful. Through their filaments I would begin to think at the pace of dripping mineral. A herd crossing a shelf would send slow impulses into my outer edges. A piston geyser’s far-off metallic clunk would travel up through the wet stone beneath me and make my rim shiver. A clicker’s sharp throat-sound would stiffen the water near my lip, and I would feel the trap form and fail and form again, like a muscle remembering a movement it never learned.

My body would be mostly memory and margin. At the center, where an ordinary creature might put an organ, there would only be the hollow left by a mouth. Around that hollow, I would grow the apparatus of hunger: filaments like black frost, gill-lines like oil-slicks, a skin of condensation that could be pushed aside by moving bodies. When a tileback slid too close, I would drink the pressure of its shell and learn something of flat movement, of grazing through mineral fur, of gills that close when the air thickens. When a lurelight flickered beyond the glass shelf, I would not drink its light so much as drink the confusion it left behind. When an ashwarmer pressed against a warm fault seam nearby, I would borrow the faint heat and carry it into the cold pool, where it would bloom as a short silver stain on the water’s skin before collapsing back into chill.

I would move by forgetting.

An aftershape does not migrate the way a linebeast migrates. It drifts when the pool’s memory fades. If the mouth-hollow I inhabit becomes too shallow, too cold, too filled with spore-dust or the wake of a square-diver, the shape may loosen. I would rise into a thin cloud, a suspended darkness with no true source, a smudge above the brine. If enough of us lifted together, we would replay fragments of the drinkers: not the animals themselves, but the habits of their thirst, the angles of their approach, the way a tileback’s lower jaw scraped first against the oily ice, the way a linebeast’s many bodies made the surface ripple in sequence, the way a clicker’s sharp throat disturbed the film with sound before any prey was trapped. Such a cloud would look from a distance like weather remembering animals. Close to, it would be mostly wetness and hunger, a parliament of borrowed shapes hovering above the place where shapes are made and abandoned.

The basin would feed me by mistake.

A wirelimb colony might mistake my dark rim for salt-feather and climb across me, eating dust and leaving behind translucent threads charged with faint static. They would clean the edges of my mouth-shape, removing spore-lumps and broken glass flakes, and in cleaning me they would repair my silence with their small tapping. A shard-tapper might place a black sound-shard at my edge because it liked the way the pool’s hollow amplified a click. That shard would become part of me without being alive in the ordinary sense. It would store a trapped rhythm: tap, tap, clatter, tick. Then, when the basin’s industrial weather turned, that rhythm would pulse through my rim and teach my edges a temporary syntax. I would learn to respond to clickers before they clicked, stiffening a thin film in the nearby water in answer. The clickers might not see me. They would see only a small, strange success: a membrane that had hardened too early, trapping a lurelight’s false glow.

I would also be prey.

Some creatures in this basin feed on sound, some on heat, some on light, and some on the difference between a thing and its shape. The aftershape would sit near that last border. A lurelight might pass through my dark pool and leave behind a delayed outline. That delayed outline would confuse me; for a moment, my center would not know whether the hollow was still empty or whether the lurelight had drunk my shape instead of I its glow. A clicker might trap me by making the water skin stiffen above my rim. It would not eat me whole. It would taste my memory, scrape the mineral film that had begun to learn the mouth-shape too well, and carry the scraped bits away in the dust on its throat-casps. A square-diver, moving through the black pool with its blocky pressure, might sink over me without knowing. Its skin would carry detached shadows as barnacles carry shells. One of those shadows would merge with my own absence, and for a while I would be a dark-pool with a body’s weight pressing over me. When it moved away, I would be changed: deeper in the place where a surface had forgotten whether it was water or stone.

My reproduction would not be an act of birth but an act of repetition.

If I gathered enough mouth-shapes, if my rim became too thick with remembered drinking, I would shed small discs of shadowed brine onto nearby depressions. A drop of condensation would fall into one of them with too much rhythm. A passing linebeast would carry it in the furrow of its passage. A wirelimb would mistake it for dust, touch it, charge it, and abandon it in a salt cup. There, in the cool wet dark, it would begin again: first as stain, then as indentation, then as mouth, then as hunger, then as aftershape. None of the children would be the same as me. They would be formed by whatever impressions the water kept: the mouth of a clicker, the hollow under a lurelight’s delayed glow, the weight-shape of a square-diver’s pressure, the tired drinking-place of a linebeast before it scattered into weather. Some would become echo-sitters with too much shadow. Some would become dark-pools with too much thirst. Some would become shard-tappers, arranging the rhythms they had inherited as a body arranges its bones.

In the end, I would not be an individual so much as a method.

The basin would use me to keep its boundaries from closing too completely. This is strange for a place where boundaries already leak, but leakage too has maintenance. The wet parliament is not chaos; it is an industry of almost-selves. It manufactures weather from salt and pressure, memory from water, bodies from mistakes, and bodies from the remnants of bodies. I would be one of its finer instruments: the place where the basin checked whether its shapes had returned. If a mouth-hollow faded too quickly, I would keep it long enough for another pattern to grow in it. If a shadow failed to reattach to its owner, I would hold it down and feed it. If a sound-shard dropped too near a pool and began repeating the rhythm of a geyser, I would let it become part of my edge, then let that edge teach the water to stiffen in answer.

And when I finally failed—not decayed, not rotted, not cleanly lost, because this basin does not allow such neat endings—I would leave behind the kind of residue the land mistakes for purpose. My film would thin, then harden into a black salt-lattice across the obsidian shelf. The hollow of my mouth-shape would fill with spore-clouds and wirelimb dust. A tileback would press against the spot, unaware, and the pool would briefly remember tileback instead of aftershape. Clickers would come to tap their own old rhythms into the same place, and the place would ring faintly in reply. A dark-pool would settle at the rim, too still to be a shadow, too wet to be absence. Some other being would drink there. The basin would begin again.

If you stood at the edge of that black brine basin, as the prompt describes, with the horizon smeared between dim air and dark ground, you might not notice me at first. There would be too much packed reality: salt feathers furring stone, fungal threads running beneath glass, oily ice cracking above deeper water, herds merging into weather, a piston geyser exhaling somewhere beyond sight, the whole region humming like a machine that had fossilized into geology and still ran on weather, hunger, and maintenance.

But if you leaned close to the pool, you might see the water holding a mouth-shape that no mouth made. The indentation would remain for hours. Its edges would be too dark, too textured, too hungry for mere wetness. A faint salt-rim would tremble at its border. Inside it, you might hear not your own breathing, but the compressed echo of a far-off linebeast drinking, a clicker’s throat-sound, a lurelight’s delayed flicker, a shard-tapper’s arranged rhythm, and beneath all that, the slow grinding of stone mistaking itself for body.

That would be me.

Not the basin’s center, because there is none. Not its wall, because there is no wall. Not its animal, because it would use my shape only until it found a better mistake. I would be one of its wet decisions, the smallest kind of life in a land where life has learned to grow in the gaps between event and substance. I would be the aftershape: a mouth without an owner, a shadow that learned to drink, a memory too persistent to stay only in the water, a thing the basin made by continuing.
