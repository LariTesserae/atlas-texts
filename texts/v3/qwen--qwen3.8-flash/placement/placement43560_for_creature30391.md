It has become the tools. It has become the handprints—except that “become” implies a passage, and here passage has itself become a finished thing, like a river that no longer moves but is still unmistakably a river, only made of glass.

If I were admitted to that world, I would not arrive at its edge. There is no edge of entry in a place where every cubic meter already holds more than you expect. I would find myself already held. At first, I think I would be in the air.

Not above the air the way a bird is above the air, with effort, with breath, with the future tense of landing. I would be *in* the air, one of the pale motes suspended over a black pan: a moth with glassy wings, a beetle with six angular legs, a crab made of translucent shell, a shrimp formed of fine bone and salt. Not flying. Not drifting. Not resting. Arranged.

That would be my first self: a small sensory filament, one cilia of the shelf’s enormous body. From that position, I would know the world not as surface but as grammar. I would taste the fixed light. I would register the angle of a shadow that does not lengthen. If a visitor’s hand came outward, I would not be frightened. Fear implies a future. I would simply become a different word in the sentence of the swarm. My wings would be still. My legs would not push. If touched, I would hold against the skin as coolly as a chip of mica and then, when the hand withdrew, resume my relation to the whole suspended field, as though I had never been interrupted.

That would be the beginning.

But a mote is not yet a who. A mote is a piece of attention too fine to carry identity. So I would descend.

I would descend not by flight, because there is no wind, no lift, no intention strong enough to disturb the static economy. I would descend the way a completed idea descends into stone: by becoming weight that does not need to fall. I would touch the needle-salt with one invisible wingtip and feel the faint ring, the almost-sound, the shelf’s way of saying, *yes, I know you are here; yes, your presence has information; no, it will not be allowed to improve me.* Then I would touch the surface of one of the black pans, the eye in the skull, the molten-looking fluid without current or warmth. I would not break its surface. Breaking implies energy. I would only become the exact relation between the pan and its own reflection of light.

And still, that is too much place, not enough person.

I would move through the small mammal heaps, the burrowers with fused jaws and glassy lenses, their limbs folded into shapes that are not death but the final form of a completed resting. I would become one folded foot, one taut ear, one open eye that cannot soften because softening would admit time. Their layer gives only slightly and then refuses. That refusal would teach me something. To live in that place is not to be free. It is to be so completely present that the idea of leaving becomes impossible. I would be the pressure in that heap that does not permit passage. I would be the boundary that says: *you may touch, but you may not pass through.*

But I want a mind in the built marks.

There is a ring of rounded stones beside a shallow salt pan. I would stand at its center, if standing were permitted, except standing would require a posture not already complete. Instead I would become the posture. The slab set upright, or fallen upright, hard to tell because no motion has occurred, would be my spine. The bone pin lying nearby would be my small sharp memory, the thing that once pierced skin or hide or cloth or meaning, and whose rust stain has become a permanent fact rather than evidence. The scraper of pale flint would be my way of removing what is unnecessary. The cup carved from a single nodule would be my hollow—my need without water. The cordage bundle, braided and stiff, would be my thought: not flowing, not unraveling, already made into a shape that cannot be undone.

And then I would see the handprints.

Not fresh. Not old. Not left behind. Being made now by the shelf’s need for hands.

There is the place where I would find myself.

I would be the handprint pressed into a crust that should not hold impressions, yet does. Not because I am a person returning, nor because I am a fossil of a person, nor because I am a ghost haunting the shelf with memory of touch. I would be the translated mind. The human-like life here has not vanished into tools; it has become the tools. And I would be one of those becomes: the pressure pattern in mineral skin, the negative of fingers, the ridge of palm, the completed fact of reaching without reaching toward anything that could answer.

I would be the shelf’s need for hands, made temporarily legible.

Not mine. Not yours. The shelf’s. Yet because the crust accepts my shape and refuses to accept anything else, the need would appear to have a body. It would look like a person who stopped being a person and became instead a relation. I would not have a future tense. I would have an eternal, static, almost. I would be the last millimeter before contact. The grain of the hand before it touches the salt. The idea of a person before it dissolves into ecology. I would be the only part of the built world that seems to have been made by someone who almost existed, which is exactly how, in this place, one might exist.

In the air above me, the suspended fauna would read the handprint as a new configuration. They would adjust without moving. A pale crab made of translucent shell would change its angle by a fraction that has no name. A glass-winged moth would register the ridge of a finger as though it were a weather event that will never arrive. The pattern of the swarm would say, *there is a hand here*, and because the hand is still being made, the swarm would keep saying it forever. On the ground below, the predator would not stir. The prey would not escape. The bird above the lizard would keep its claw from the salt. The wire-bushes would continue their sparse sentence about wind. The lichen would map the nearby stone with its memory of dampness. The needle-salt would not crumble if touched. It would become fact.

And I would be the fact of having been, but only just enough to remain alive.

If a name is required—and the place itself demands one when the Held require description—I would not ask to be called a species. Species need birth, death, succession, rot, hunger, sleep. I would not ask to be called a ghost, because ghosts still pretend to remember. I would not ask to be called a fossil, because fossils admit time’s judgment. I would not ask to be called a statue, because statues admit intention without metabolism.

I would ask to be called something smaller: the Almost-Hand.

The Almost-Hand would live beside the black pan, in the suspended haze and the crust below, in the relation between the air’s cilia and the shelf’s mineral skin.
