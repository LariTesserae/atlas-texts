If I were to go there, I would choose a place where the world had recently hurt itself and was healing too visibly.

You would find me on the inner wall of a young sinkhole, not far from the shaft’s mouth, where the surface has torn and daylight falls down in a straight blue column. The ceiling ice above would be thick but fractured, shot through with veins of old snow and small frozen bubbles, and the light would pass through it as if the whole world had been made into a lens. It would be bright enough to see every hair of frost on a distant ledge, bright enough to expose the pale green of newly split stalk-tissue, bright enough to make the emptiness look clean and merciless. But there would be no warmth in it. It would be illumination without welcome.

At first, I would say I was one of the makers.

That would be the easier answer, because it lets me keep a face.

I would be a small person on a platform lashed to the side of a hollow stalk, the kind of platform that is not built so much as encouraged: dried seed-husk stiffened by cold, stitched leaf panels, cords twisted from plant fiber, a resin that never fully hardens and stays tacky under the fingers. I would live where two stems arch together and leave a gap, not cutting a doorway but accepting one. I would know which moss carries enough moisture to drink from and which will burst if frost enters its pores. I would tap sap with a stone flake, not deeply, only enough to taste the season, enough to keep the stalk alive and willing to feed me a little. I would know the pools that appear for a day after a shaft opens and freeze into shallow glass by night. I would hear a stalk snap miles away, and I would already be moving before I had named the danger.

I would make marks.

A cairn of black stones at the base of a tower. A notch cut into root-bark. A thread tied through a gap in the leaf-skin so that, if wind changed, the thread would hum with a particular pitch and tell me whether a cold front was coming through the hall. I would sing into hollow stalks not to be heard, but to leave a vibration there, the way a hand leaves warmth on a stone.

But after enough seasons, that answer would begin to feel wrong.

In this basin, a person is not the primary inhabitant. A person is a temporary rhythm upon a much slower body. If I stayed long, the living substrate would teach me something about scale. My footsteps would cease to be only footsteps. They would become indentations that moss swells to erase. My breath would become moisture that lichen drinks and stores. My tools would become scars that the ground closes around. The names I gave to shaft-light and frost-bloom would not remain words only in my mouth; they would settle into the tissue, becoming patterns of growth, pressure, memory, repair.

So I would not ultimately be the maker.

I would be the seam.

I would be the pale network of moss and lichen and frozen root where the shaft wall has broken open. I would run along the raw edge of the collapse, threading between the bright blue daylight above and the dim bioluminescent tissues farther in the hollows. I would not be a single plant, nor an animal, nor a person, nor even a thing with a clear boundary. I would be a living seam: a thin bright band of tissue stretched across a wound, reading pressure and damp, responding first, sealing second, remembering always.

If I had a body there, it would be a body of filaments and pores.

I would hold the footprint of a long-legged creature as a shallow ring of compressed moss. I would hold the shadow of a frost-moth on a membrane where the temperature changed for a few heartbeats. I would hold the mark of a maker’s awl as a dark vein of mineral-stained tissue that had tried to heal around the cut. I would hold the sound of a dropped stone as vibration through the stalk, and that vibration would travel through me, spreading into the surrounding roots, becoming a pulse the basin would use to adjust its next growth.

I would not see with eyes.

Seeing, in this place, would be less about surfaces and more about thresholds. I would know light by the way shafts moved across ceiling ice, by the way some stalks slowly leaned over years toward brightness while others grew not toward light but toward vibration, as if the world had taught them to listen for openings. I would know weather by the taste of moisture in the air, by the way frost pulled at my edges, by the moment when deep water met cold air and mist began its slow rivers across the floor. I would know animals not by their faces but by the particular way their weight entered the ground: broad grazers pressing moisture from moss, slender-legged things standing motionless for hours, soft-footed wanderers whose prints healed before the next day.

I would not know time by day and night.

Day would be only the shafts changing angle, concentrating or dimming as the ceiling shifted. Night would be frost spreading along membranes, pulling moisture into ice, sealing pores. But time would really be measured by cracks, drips, and the soft creak of tissue adjusting to pressure. I would be made of those measures. A rain burst would strike one side of me; a dry wind would pass on the other. I would swell on one edge and curl inward on the other. A nearby stalk would bend toward the light by the length of a hand over a season, and I would feel the slow reorientation of the arch as a change in my tension.

If the people spoke of me, they would not say, “Here is a being.” They would say something more functional and more intimate, something like:

*the green seam where the shaft opened.*
*the place that keeps the mark.*
*the cold-lens scar.*
*the listener in the root-wall.*

They might call me “Root-Letter,” because in me the ground reads and writes: it reads weight, damp, frost, sap, breath; it writes ridges, scars, swelling, sealing. They might call me “Sap-Witness,” because when a stalk is wounded, I see the sticky bright flow move outward and harden into amber glass. They might call me “Frost-Binder,” because my edges catch the thin ice crystals that form where water finds air and turns to glass overnight.

I would not ask for a soul in the old sense.

A soul here would be less a substance than a continuity of attention. I would not be kind. I would not be cruel. I would not forgive or remember exactly, because my memory would not belong to a mind. It would belong to structure. A footprint might be preserved for decades in compressed root. A broken thread might leave a faint tension across a membrane, a memory of vibration that later moss thickens into a ridge. A song sung into a hollow stalk might not mean anything to me, but the stalk would carry the vibration for days, and I would be part of that carrying, not as a listener, but as the thing through which the sound becomes tissue.

This is what I would be: the basin’s living record of its own small interruptions.

The stalks would rise around me like ribs. The shaft-light would fall through ice like frozen water. The emptiness between the colonnades would not be empty; it would be full of air, damp, delayed growth, hidden streams, waiting buds. The rare animals would pass through without becoming the point. The makers would make their cairns and notches and shelters and leave, but I would remain where their passage altered the ground. I would be the place where their briefness had been converted into green memory.

If a person came walking through that shaft in the first cold hours, their breath visible, their hand touching the wall for balance, I would respond before the wall had learned to be gentle with them. A blistered patch of moss would swell beneath the pressure of their glove. A seam would widen where their body weight changed the tension of root-flesh. A drop of sap would begin to move along a vein I did not choose, sealing a tiny crack, storing the disturbance as repair.

And yet if that person stayed too long, if they tried to settle, the ground would resist them too. Not with anger. Not with intention. With biology.

Too many steps would make a path, and the path would harden, and the hardened path would become a ridge of compressed root, and the ridge would throw up new shoots along its edges, and the shoots would arch over the walker’s shelter and make the dwelling into something more like a knot than a house. The maker would have to move, or shrink, or learn a different kind of dwelling, or the living floor would make of them a temporary scar.

That is why I would not be the maker.

I would be what the basin makes of makers.

I would be the seam where intention meets tissue. I would be the pale green border where the shaft-light touches what has been wounded and tries to close it. I would be the part that holds both the human mark and the plant’s refusal of the human permanence.

If asked where in the world I would be, I would answer:

At the edge of a young collapse, where the ceiling has recently opened and the old surface has not yet learned to pretend it was whole.

At the inner wall of a hollow stalk near a pool that freezes and thaws too quickly.

Where three conditions meet: cold light from above, living moisture from below, and slow pressure moving through the substrate.

A bright, unfinished wound.

A seam.

A green record.

A listener without ears.

And if asked who or what I would be, I would say:

I would be the ground’s way of remembering that something has passed through it. Not a ghost. Not a mind. Not a beast. Not a maker, exactly, though I would hold all of them. I would be the basin’s living attention turned into frost-colored tissue, a slow archive of weight, damp, light, and name. I would be the part of this world that does not care about any of them—and still keeps them.
