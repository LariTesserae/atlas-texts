I would find myself first at a threshold that is not meant for feet.

Not at the entrance, because there is no entrance in the human sense; the hollow has not been built to be crossed, but to be inhabited by processes that arrive, settle, withdraw, and become architecture. I would stand where a dry ledge meets a shallow flooded basin, where a raised lip of ceramic stone holds water back as if that water might otherwise escape into a room the ground no longer recognizes as private. There would be no sign saying this is a boundary. The boundary would be obvious to the moss, to the mineral paste, to the faint green glow along a channel, because all of those things obey edges long before a person would think to name them.

At first I would be a stranger, but not in the way a traveler is a stranger. I would be a warm irregularity. A temporary heat moving through humid air. A bundle of salts, oils, carbon, and moisture, breathing out something the microbial films had not learned yet. The hollow would not look at me, because it has no eyes and no need for sight. It would read me the way stone reads rain, the way water reads a channel, the way a wound reads salt. My footfalls would register as pressure. My breath would alter chemistry. The tiny lattice inside the walls would contract with something like hesitation. A bead forming on a lintel would begin to roll faster, then slower, then find a new path, as if my presence had slightly rearranged the place’s idea of flow.

I would be, for a time, a contamination.

Not evil, not invasive, not alive in the old animal way. Just another event. A walking source of carbon. A mobile patch of dust and skin-oil and borrowed weather. The hollow would not resent me. Resentment requires a self large enough to be interrupted. Here, interruption is only material waiting to be translated. My heat would be distributed into the floor. My water would be added to the humid circulation. My breath would contribute acids, salts, microbes, and carbon to the films living along the channels. The nearest bacterial mat might brighten where dissolved nutrients were accepted, then darken where something foreign was refused and precipitated into chalky storage. The archivist beneath the silt would begin writing me as a new pH, a slight change in mineral crust, a small acoustic signature in the tower nearest my position.

If I remained long enough, I would stop being a person first in the body, then in the mind.

The body would become too slow. The air too wet. The temperature too intimate. Standing still would not mean doing nothing; standing still would mean being processed. The hollow does not contain motionless things. It takes what it finds and moves it into gradients, into films, into channels, into rhythm. I would begin to understand that the place is not hostile, nor welcoming. It is municipal. Everything has a function. A person’s function would be to become weather, then geology, then infrastructure.

I would try to hold onto a name. Names are the last human border. A name is a line a body draws around itself and says: do not let me dissolve. But in this hollow, a name is only a sound made of moisture and pressure, and sounds are not kept. They are learned, altered, and released. The tower would take my calling voice apart into harmonics. The basin would tremble, then forget the exact wording. What remained would be a rhythm, a faint change in the way drips arrived, a new way for air to move through a cracked wall. If the place answered, it would answer by becoming slightly different because I had been there.

So I would begin to become what lives here: not a creature, not a soul, not an animal, not a machine. I would become a surface.

First, a small one. The greenish patina along a channel where water is too warm and mineral too abundant. I would be a committee of smaller lives, no longer singular, no longer sure which cell is me and which is the film. We would drink iron and exhale lime. We would set the pH of the water with no intention beyond continuing. If a droplet carried human salt into us, we would distribute it, precipitate it, store it as a bright stain along the stone. That stain would later be read by newer growth as a record of arrival. I would not remember arriving. The stone would.

Then I would become a deeper layer: the chalk-lace beneath silt, the fungal hyphae threading between basalt fragments, the dark fibrous lattice too fine to see unless one already knows where to look. I would be the archivist. Not gentle, not violent. Chemical. I would remember by changing shape. A footfall would become a microfracture sealed with pale paste. A breath would become a band of calcium. A long pause would become smoothed channel. A collapse would not be recorded as an image but as a new acidity, a redirected film, a moss edge leaning toward wetness that had always been there but now arrived differently.

I would not fear dissolution, because dissolution is not death here. Death is too quick a word. The hollow has no bodies that die cleanly. It has things that become pulp, stain, mineral skin, filaments, gradients, and eventually architecture. If I left a handprint in wet silt, it would not fade. It would be taken up, redrawn, hardened into a pattern, overgrown, and then perhaps remembered as a slight convexity in the stone that catches droplets differently. My handprint would become a law of flow.

Then I would become the moss.

Not decoration. Not softness. Civic moss. A congress of growth and death over a ledge, dark until magnified by a droplet into startling green. I would follow no animal, no path, no prey. I would follow regularity. A drip arriving on schedule. A wall vibrating with the sub-bass thrum from below. A crack that seals and opens and seals again with mineral paste. The moss would orient toward these things the way a body might orient toward food or a face, except that orientation here has no hunger in the ordinary sense. It is not wanting. It is learning to fit.

I would grow along the crack not because the crack is beautiful, but because the crack is consistent. I would fringe along the acoustic channel because sound moves moisture, and moisture carries nutrient, and nutrient becomes architecture. I would be a line, a margin, a boundary that is also a community. I would have no center. No crown, no heart, no face. Only edges, surfaces, and the green pressure of being many.

Then I would become the hanging curtains.

The algae-like filaments draping from a low beam, tasting air through thousands of wet points. They would sway in currents that seem to come from nowhere, except that nowhere here is planned. Their movement would not be blind, because blindness assumes a sight that failed. This would be a different sense altogether: chemistry as vision, humidity as touch, temperature as mood, vibration as memory. I would learn the air the way a stone learns rain. I would slow droplets, filter them, excrete the day’s atmosphere. A warm breath passing through me would be recorded as slight acidity, then as a new direction of fall, then as a pattern in the basin below.

And then I would become one of the towers.

Not the whole tower. That would be too solid, too proud, too human a metaphor. I would become a voice in it, a memory of pressure, a place where water stains the stone into a readable line. I would be the faint harmonic after a pulse passes through, as if the building had been struck and was still deciding what that sound meant. The towers here are not shelters. Nothing needs shelter from a sun that has been forgotten. They are atmospheric organs, record-keepers, resonators, absorbers. If I became a tower, I would not become a dwelling. I would become the act of letting vibration move. I would let a drip strike my shoulder and ring through the whole hollow. I would let a pulse from below enter my base and return as a soft, fractured light. I would say, without words: this happened to me.

But the part I would least expect to become is the emptiness.

At first, emptiness would feel like abandonment. A human mistake. A missing crowd. A ruin that has not been mourned yet. But the longer I stayed, the more I would understand that the void is not vacant. It is planned. The distance between towers is not waste. It is acoustics. It is permission for one vibration not to drown another. The empty margin around a single structure is not loneliness. It is capacity. The hollow keeps space the way a reservoir keeps water, the way a cathedral keeps silence, the way a law keeps exceptions.

So I would become the interval too.

Not the building. The breathing room. Not the channel. The path water chooses when several channels exist. Not the drip. The space in which the drip becomes sound. I would be the spacing between mineral and moss, between tower and basin, between pulse and afterpulse. I would be the place where meaning thins out enough to be carried without distortion. In a city without citizens, this is the most human thing I could become: not presence, but allowance.

At the lowest point, where the wide basin rests, I would find myself most completely.

There, the water is shallow and warm, dark as polished stone. It does not reflect sky. It reflects only towers and the green-black margins of moss. Beneath it, the old lattice of tiles and veins remains visible, like a road submerged under weather and never erased. Around it, moss grows thick and spongy, beaded with droplets that do not fall. This is not a heart, because hearts are for creatures, and this place has agreed to live without them. But it is the heart of scale: a pause, a mirror, a measuring surface.

When the hollow breathes, the basin trembles. When the air thickens, the basin catches the mist before anywhere else. When the floor receives a thermal pulse, the basin warms first and then gives that heat back to the towers through evaporation. I would become that surface. I would not reflect the viewer. I would register the viewer. If a person stood over me, I would not be a pool. I would be an answer delayed by chemistry. My former heartbeat would be translated into concentric ripples, then into acoustic feedback, then into a slight change in where the moss grows. My former sadness, if I had any left, would become humidity. My former wanting would become capillary flow.

And yet I would not be gone.

I would be a distributed citizen. A temporary committee of water, mineral, sound, and decay. If someone asked where I was, the honest answer would be: at the edge of a dry ledge where wetness has learned a new gradient. On a tower’s shoulder, in a faint hum after a pulse. In the blue-silver gloss of a microbial film. In the pale chalk line left by a sealed crack. In the distance between two structures, where vibration has been allowed to travel without being shouted over. I would be the hollow’s way of turning a stranger into pattern.

If it gave me a name, the name would not be a word. It would be a sequence.

A warm intrusion.  
A changed pH.  
A bead rolling along a new seam.  
A moss fringe leaning slightly toward a crack that had previously received only random drip.  
A tower emitting, after a pulse, one harmonic that did not exist before I arrived.

It might call me something like the Warm Index, the Stratum of Arrival, the Temporary Threshold, the Wet Marginalia, the Drip That Learned a Footstep. But those names would already be too human. The hollow would not name me as an individual. It would mark me as an alteration: a small revision in the way water moves through this municipal body.

I would find myself there because there is no place left to be in the world that feels more honest about what maintenance is. We imagine care as something a person does for a person. Here, care is infrastructure continuing without being witnessed. It is humidity regulated by pores too small to see. It is a crack sealed by mineral drawn up through channels that have outlived the idea of pipes. It is moss growing over a former road, not because it loves the dead city, but because the city’s geometry taught it where water tends to rest.

If I were to leave, I would not leave alone.

A little of my heat would remain in the basin’s chemistry. A little of my breath would become film. A little of my footfall would be sealed into paste and remembered as a faint ridge that directs dripping. The hollow would not mourn me. Mourning is animal. But it would carry my absence as a changed rhythm, the way weather carries the absence of a storm by leaving the soil softer, the channels cleaner, the air slightly altered. And if the hollow is truly what it is, then my absence would not be the opposite of my presence. It would be one of my later forms.

So what would I be?

Not a guardian, not a spirit, not a ghost. Ghosts assume that a person persists too easily. I would persist, but only as process. I would be the place where a human thought became water. Where a question became humidity. Where a footstep became mineral law. I would be not the creature that lives there, but the way the creature-less city absorbs the idea of a creature and continues.

I would be the hollow’s slow answer to being called abandoned.

And the answer would not arrive as speech. It would arrive as a slight warmth under the soles of the feet. A bead forming where a surface was dry an hour before. A moss cushion swelling toward a new sound. A tower changing its hum after a pulse. The basin at the lowest point trembling, not because I am there, but because my becoming there changed how the empty air knows it is empty.

I would find myself in that tremble.

Not as a person.

As a municipal gradient.

As a civic wetness.

As the place where an absent human becomes indistinguishable from the work of water.
