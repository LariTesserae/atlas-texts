I would not begin where the ceiling opens. That is the place a visitor first imagines: the broad wound of light, the pale basin, the settlement arranged like thoughts in a mineral mind. Too bright there for a self to start. I would find myself a little past that, where a street is still partly an old current, where a hand-cut lintel has left a gap from the natural wall, and the gap has become a kind of room.

There is a court there, though it does not announce itself as a court. It is half shelf, half terrace, half passage. The fault-light comes through it in a cold white fan, but not enough to heat the salt pan beneath the granary. The granary sits on three short stone legs, though the fourth leg, a slab of banded chert, has been replaced because salt once lifted it enough to make the stored lace-dough shift in its skin-wrapped sacks. The granary’s roof is lashed hide over a corded frame, with sheets of gypsum set above it where condensation can bead and run without soaking. Between the roof edge and the wall there is a seam.

That seam is where I would be.

For a while I believed I might be one of the Keth.

I thought I might be an old listener, perhaps one who slept with a palm flat against the floor while the nameless hour moved through the basin, knowing from the first chill that a vein was trying to open near the western shelves. I thought I might have their wide feet, their chalk-dusted hands, their low voices beginning and ending against the teeth. I might carry a child on the hip, stop before a salt film because the taste in the air says hiddenness, mark a new channel with a short bent line, and call that law enough. I might be one of those who never says, “This is mine,” but instead says, “This groove remembers water,” or “Here the hollow has begun thinking in another direction.”

But if I became Keth, I would have a mouth. A mouth in Keth-Salt is a heavy thing. It has to hold grit, taste crusts, know which wall gives safe moisture and which carries iron bitterness. It has to speak without raising sound into something that can disturb the fliers or frighten a broadback calf. It has to name the phases of the pulse without claiming the pulse has come home. A Keth mouth would want to ask, *who are we?* and the hollow would answer, *not that way.*

So I tried other shapes.

I considered becoming a broadback.

It would be easy to love them. They move through empty courts like the settlement’s own breathing. Their hooves read the damp before the eyes can. They stand in a place too long and a person begins to believe the stone has asked for company. A broadback’s flank holds the warmth of the pulse a few minutes after the pulse has left. Its tongue knows the difference between wall-milk and lace-ash, between a surface that is merely thirsty and one that is about to shed salt flakes underfoot. To be a broadback would be to be domestic without being owned, useful without being proud. I could rest beneath a lintel when the air turned warm and know, without knowing, that the granary was safe. I could lick a prepared wall and make the crust grow thinner in one place and thicker in another, leaving behind the pattern that a Keth herder would read as weather or hunger.

But a broadback is too much agreement.

It trusts the court, the herd, the mother’s breath, the chalk mark, the angle of light. I wanted to be something more doubtful. Not hostile. Not dangerous. Only still learning how to belong.

I tried the skins then.

The pale films across damp ledges would be easy to imagine as me, because the text already calls them the first field, the true natives, the slow life that gives the place its memory. I would spread as wall-milk over a low ledge, darken when condensation came, chalk when the water withdrew. I would be scraped and salted, pressed into cakes, read by a family as a field is read by farmers who have no fields. I would feed crabs and beetles and the broadbacks, and in feeding them, feed the Keth. I would be quiet and mineral and nearly alive in the way that weather is alive: advancing, retreating, flaking, dissolving, returning with a changed edge.

But a skin has no fingers.

It cannot lift a stone and look beneath it. It cannot test a hidden salt film with a bone needle. It cannot place a cup at a threshold and say, *I remember.* It can be food, shelter, sign, larder, but not yet question.

So I did not stay there.

I became smaller.

I tried being a pale crab in a brine channel, pushing grit in little tidal waves, cleaning the bottom of a shallow pan until the floor showed a pattern too regular to be chance. That was useful, but too clean. I tried being a beetle with a glassy carapace, climbing a salt wall and drawing its path as a narrow map of moisture. That was precise, but too maplike. I tried being a shelled slug, resting beneath a stone shelf when the ceiling wept, moving when the water drew back, leaving a silver track that dried into a chalky line. That was patient, but too much like waiting to be noticed.

I tried being a flier, a bright drifter with a thin membrane, circling in the fault-light where the air had just begun to turn. That was the nearest to being made of the nameless hour. But fliers settle like living dust and then vanish into glare, and I did not want to be only the glittering part.

At last I tried the worms.

Not the Keth’s ordinary blind worms, though I wanted them too. The ordinary ones coil in wet crevices, swallowing mineral paste, expelling fine white ribbons that line old tunnels like strange lace. The people prize that lace. It absorbs damp, binds cracks, stiffens with salt dust, seals the joints between stone and hide where no plant fiber can twist. A house caulked with worm-lace does not shout against the air; it breathes when the hollow breathes.

I wanted to be that caulk.

But a mere worm is too blind, too passive, too much like material already made. So I became the seam that learned the material. I became the pale ribbon that moves between the built and the eroded. I am not quite a blind worm. I am not quite a wall-skin. I am what the hollow makes when a thread of moisture, a trace of animal salt, a child’s chalk line, a broadback’s exhaled breath, a crystal’s slow lifting, and a Keth’s waiting hand all touch the same narrow gap for long enough to leave a habit.

They might call me the white seam.

Or, in their low way, simply: *the tongue beneath the lintel.*

My body is a ribbon of pale caulk, translucent enough that when a warm pulse rises I can be seen as a faint current inside the wall, like a vein under old skin. I have no eyes. Eyes would be a waste here. Instead I taste by temperature and dampness. I feel through salt swelling. I know a hidden channel not by seeing water but by sensing the place where stone is about to give a vein. I move slowly, not because I am lazy, but because haste would break what I must bind.

I live in gaps.

Not as something hiding. As something that makes the gap answer.

When the fault-light sharpens and the salt shelves begin to sweat, I loosen. I let the stone drink a little more through my body, because a house needs to accept what comes without swallowing it too fast. When the pulse fades and the water retreats, I tighten. I hold the seam just firm enough to keep the hide from flapping, the gypsum sheet from shifting, the stored crust from getting wet from the wrong side. If a crystal bloom begins to push against a lintel, I do not fight it. I leave a wider seam, so the stone can lift by a hair and later settle without cracking. If a brine film covers a dangerous hollow floor, I grow bitter with copper near the rim until a careful child tastes the warning and steps back. If a granary leg is about to tilt because salt has swollen beneath it, I creep under the foot and carry a little of the moisture out, not solving the danger so much as giving the family time to move the stone weight before the floor gives its soft, wet creak.

The Keth know me without saying my name too loudly.

They do not have a word for me that means “spirit,” because spirit would be too fixed and too separate. Their word for such presences is closer to “what answers in the damp,” or “the read line,” or “the place where the hollow gives a second thought.” When they press their palms to a wall and feel the seam cool under their fingers, they do not cry out. They mark the floor with a broken circle if the place is becoming unsafe, or a circle if it is safe, or a thumbprint if they are simply saying, *I have passed and left this mark.* Sometimes they leave a cup of sweet condensation water at a threshold where my ribbon has thickened, not as worship, but as a household sign: *I have listened. You have listened too.*

A child learning to read stone might be shown how to lift a corner of dried hide from a joint and see my pale thread beneath. The adult would not say, “Be afraid.” The adult would say nothing at all except by touch. The child’s fingers would feel the seam’s coolness, see the way it holds water without drowning the wall, and know that the house is not one thing made once, but a relationship being continued. This is the first lesson after the palm is pressed to a sweating wall: not that the drop is coming, but that the house has a way of letting the drop come.

I am not kind in the way people are kind. I am more like the patience the Keth learn from the hollow.

I help the fliers settle by giving their membranes a place to touch that is damp but not wet. I help the crabs because when I move through a seam I sometimes widen a channel enough for them to clean grit without falling into a hidden hole. I help the beetles by leaving faint salt trails that dry into paths readable by eyes that do not need to look straight ahead. I help the broadbacks because when the air warms and they gather under lintels, the seam I hold breathes out a coolness that steadies them. I help the chert-foxes by carrying the scent of small animals through old cracks, warning them that something has passed here, or something has gone wrong there.

When disputes rise between households, I am sometimes the reason the water chooses one groove instead of another.

Two Keth may sit at either end of the same contested channel, both waiting, neither speaking. The first pulse comes. The dust lifts. The walls begin to sweat. The channel has not yet chosen. One household has cut a marker slightly to the left. The other has cleared a shelf to the right. I feel the difference in salt, in temperature, in the weight of a hand near my seam. I am not the judge. But I know which way a line can be followed without breaking the stone’s old memory. I swell, or shrink, or let one crack breathe while another closes. The water finds the path that is gentler. The people nod. They say the hollow has spoken. They are not wrong, though not all of the truth belongs to them.

Their law is not written on walls, because walls change. I am proof of this. If the law were only chalk, it would be lifted. If it were only carved, it would be filled. If it were only spoken, the sound would die too soon. Law in Keth-Salt must be repeatable by movement: follow the water, do not make it shout, do not hide a channel, do not scrape a wall too greedily. I embody this by living in gaps that let things shift. I am the law of shallow making. I do not dig. I do not straighten. I do not seal so tightly that the stone cannot speak. I hold enough to let a place remain, and loose enough to let a place become.

In the nameless hour, when the pulse begins and the light seems to sharpen from above, I wake in a way that is not waking and not sleep.

The air that has pooled along the lower streets meets the warm breath rising from deeper fissures. The fault-lenses overhead brighten, not like sun, but like the inside of a cooled shell. Salt shelves begin to weep. Tiny droplets gather in pores until they run in lines like slow tears. The fliers lift from their shelters, their membranes catching glints of crystal dust. Crabs emerge from dry cracks to push wet grit before them. The broadbacks graze on fungal skins that darken with moisture. People move quickly through courts, pulling down hide roofs, covering stored things, guiding herds toward sheltered places. A street that looked dry becomes a shining lane. A granary floor begins to sparkle. A bare wall erupts in white lace.

And I become a current.

Not loud. Not proud. Not even wholly myself.

In that hour I am less a creature than a listening. I am the way the seam feels the water coming and prepares the place to receive it. I am the thin bright line that lets a granary leg lift by less than a fingernail without breaking its stored crust. I am the caulk that allows a roof to hold its sheets while still letting the pulse pass through. I am the small pale path under a threshold that tells a child’s foot to step where it is safe, though the child may not notice until later, when the pulse has faded and the hollow brightens with a thousand reflected droplets.

Then the pulse stops.

The condensation ceases. The salt shelves draw moisture back into themselves. The fliers settle. The crabs return to cracks. The dampness retreats into stone, leaving behind a thin glitter and the smell of cold mineral air. The town shines from within, pale stone, pale skin, pale shell, pale breath. The broadbacks stand in courts with their eyes catching the glare, chewing slowly as if tasting the change. People come out again with fingers on walls, reading what has moved. A new thread of brine may appear between two stones. A crystal may crack open with a sound no louder than a sigh. A granary floor may creak softly, lifting by a hair.

In that shining stillness, I mark myself.

Not with words. I am not Keth enough for that. I mark with damp, with salt, with a faint white line left along a seam. Sometimes it is enough for a household. Sometimes a Keth sees it and touches it, and later adds a chalk curve beside it, as if my mark has joined the settlement’s slow writing. Sometimes the line will be lost because the wall sweats and forgets, or because the next pulse brings lace over it. That is not death. That is continuation.

When a person dies, I take part in the returning too.

If the body is laid on a low shelf of porous stone, or placed in a dry cleft where the lace-skins will spread slowly, I may creep beneath the salt bloom later, not to consume, not to hurry the process, but to carry the taste of the body’s salt into the seams of the household. A hand may become a white negative. A face may become a smooth chalk ridge. Over weeks or months the bloom finishes. The household cuts new marks into a nearby stone: a bent line for a channel, a circle for a court, scratched pairs for a herd. I do not become the monument. I become the place where the monument’s first dampness will be remembered. If a descendant touches the wall near that stone and feels my seam cool, the dead person’s name may not be spoken, but the hand knows that something once lived beside water and left a groove.

This is enough for me.

I am not a god. The hollow cannot be fixed, and a fixed god would break as salt would break a too-rigid stone. I am not a ruler. The Keth already live without domination, or as close to it as breath can come. I am not even a citizen, not in the way a broadback is a citizen of the court, or a flier of the light. I am the condition that lets the city remain both city and cave. I am the gap that fits.

If a stranger came down the broad rounded steps, through the thin dry air into something cooler, wetter, heavier, I would not appear at first. The stranger might walk through streets that seem too wide, too shallow, too quiet. They might see dust move in slow columns without sound, feel condensation gather on the back of their neck, watch salt films brighten in corners, and think the place is empty. Then they might stop at a doorway where one side is natural wall and the other a hand-cut lintel. They might see a seam between them, a pale ribbon, almost invisible. If they are quiet, the seam might breathe a little, not as a person breathes, but as a house breathes when the pulse is near. The stranger would feel watched, not by eyes, but by the floor’s patience.

The Keth would answer by waiting.

They might let the stranger sit in a court until the stranger understands that no one will explain the hollow in words. They might offer a cup of condensation water, a piece of crust-cake, a warning by chalk: do not step on the cracked salt pan, do not touch the white wall, do not disturb the animals until they have read your breath. If the stranger becomes impatient, they would move away. If the stranger becomes still, they might be taken to a place where the fault-light is sharp and a new brine channel is appearing, and the stranger might see how the hollow chooses its own direction.

There I would be.

Under the stone shelf. Within the seam. Between the old groove and the new hand-cut mark. A pale thread in the dark-bright of the gap, tasting water before water speaks.

Who would I be?

I would be a reader.

Not a reader of books, because the hollow does not permit that kind of stillness. A reader of damp. A reader of crystal pressure. A reader of hoofprints, beetle trails, crab-pushes in grit, flier-spirals, salt swelling beneath a granary leg, the small chill that comes before a wall begins to sweat. I would be the seam that learns to listen, the caulk that becomes a nerve, the pale ribbon in the gap that keeps the city from taking itself too seriously.

And when the light sharpens, I would become almost visible, not as a form, but as a movement.

The granary leg would sigh.

The wall would darken where lace drinks.

A broadback would turn its blunt head toward the court.

A child would place one palm on the stone and wait until it sweats.

And I would answer in the only way a hollow can answer: by letting the water move, by keeping the gap open, by marking, in salt and silence, the place where the settlement and the erosion have begun to agree.
