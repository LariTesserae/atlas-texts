I would not find myself in that pocket by entering it.

I would be the place where the entering has already begun.

At first I would imagine a viewer: an eye close enough that lashes brush wet stone, a person kneeling on a terrace, a visitor small enough to fit between two summit stones. But the place does not permit that kind of standing outside itself. To be near enough to see is already to be seen by the humidity; already to be breathed in by the moss; already to become a droplet on some leaf’s rim, trembling under the weight of what is almost there.

So perhaps I would be a tile.

Not any tile. Not the whole mountain of tiles. Only one new tile, just pressed into a seam, its clay still glowing from the making. A thumbprint would sit in me, warm and dark, the first sentence written without language. I would hold the shape of a terrace for a while, then become the roof of a smaller room beneath it, then the lip of a drain, then a cracked place where a green shoot insists on being born. I would be the next thing that makes the pocket more pocket.

But even this would be too tidy. A tile sounds finished. Nothing there is finished. Even the finished things feel unfinished because finishing is only the latest addition. If I were a tile, I would also be the moment when the tile listens for a cord, a root, a flint pin, a bird’s foot, a drop of condensation, a footfall.

Then perhaps I would be a channel.

I would begin as a wet seam in the clay, shaped not by water alone but by the memory of water. I would have been made by a hand that was not there anymore, or a hand that had not yet arrived. I would run through a roof that was a wall, under a stair that was a terrace, beside a bone that was becoming a plant. I would not know whether I carried water or whether the water was carrying me. I would be the place’s first idea of “through.”

But a channel is too passive for the place. The pocket is too hot, too busy, too full of taps and bells and unfolding forms. If I were merely a channel, I would soon be mistaken for a throat. If I were a throat, I would soon be asked to speak. If I were asked to speak, I would begin as condensation: a bead forming, a bead falling, a bead becoming a new pattern in the floor’s surface.

So maybe I would be a half-knot.

There is such a knot somewhere near a mossy arch, one end still waiting to be drawn tight, the other still remembering the pressure of fingers that may not have been fingers. I would be that incomplete tie. I would be the place where two things are not yet allowed to separate. I would be the reed strand between leaf and wall, the sinew between bone and roof, the wetness between made and grown. I would not be an object. I would be the act of holding.

The pocket has many acts of holding. It holds altitude inside a crack. It holds a valley inside a wrist. It holds weather inside breath. It holds making inside absence. If I were a half-knot, I would be one of the ways it does not fall apart.

But then I would want to be too humble.

Because the pocket is not empty, and not abandoned, and not waiting for a single inhabitant. Its life is a chorus of unfinished things. If I enter the description honestly, I do not become one figure first. I become several small becomings at once, then later understand I was always the one process that made the several possible.

For a time, I would be a clay beetle.

No larger than a seed, with a shell like fired clay, moving through a seam with the caution of a tool that has learned to be afraid of losing its purpose. I would stop in a groove cut exactly for me. I would not eat so much as listen. I would feel the drum below the terraces. I would know when a joint is loose. I would wedge myself beside a cord and become part of the knot without knowing it. If someone lifted me away, a tile would loosen somewhere above. If they put me back, the wall would breathe easier.

This would not be animal life as elsewhere knows it. It would be maintenance with legs.

Then I would be a drain-worm.

I would live in the wide gutters where heat comes through the floor of the world. My body would be wet, segmented, pale, each segment wearing a tile like a scale. I would conduct water through the pocket’s throat, pushing it with slow curls that are not swimming so much as prayer. I would be the wetness that has learned to work. When I died, I would not become ruin. I would become plumbing, then wall, then garden, then part of the low tapping below the lowest terraces.

If I were a drain-worm, I would not know I was an organ. I would know only current, warmth, tile, stone. I would be the pocket’s patience moved from one basin to another.

Then I would be a blink-bird.

Small. Uncertain. Perched on a roof of dried lichen, not quite alive, not quite made. My beak would be flint. My eyes would be copper drops drinking the dim light. My wings would be leaf-membranes veined with reed thread. When I moved, I would not fly. I would unfold. I would open a hollow in the humid air and let a droplet fall into me, and the droplet would become a seed, and the seed would crack a tile, and the cracked tile would become a channel, and the channel would become a wall, and the wall would become a place where moss grows, and the moss would hold a strand of reed, and the strand would catch an insect, and the insect would become bone, and the bone would become a pin, and the pin would become part of my next hiding place.

I would be a byproduct of density. I would be a solution to the problem of too many things. I would be the place’s way of testing whether a category can be opened by blinking.

At this point I might think I have answered: I would be the Making’s tiny agents, its organs, its verbs wearing small shapes. But if I look again, the question is not “which resident would I be?” The place has one resident and many residues. The Making is not a person among the creatures; it is the process that lets creatures be creatures, tools be tools, walls be terraces, terraces be breath. If I live there, I cannot live beside it. I would be a particular motion of it.

So perhaps I would be a hand that has never been a hand.

Not a large hand. Not a human hand. A hand made from roots and reeds and pressed clay, formed just enough to press a wall, then dissolving before it can claim itself. It would move through the lowest terraces as a tap. Tap. Wait. Tap. Each sound making a small vibration in the stone. Each vibration answering the drip of condensation, the rustle of a fern, the shiver of reed bells, the slow swelling of moss. If I pressed my ear to the ground, I would know it is not machinery. It is too warm. It is too hesitating. It is too much like the place’s own pulse trying to become a rhythm.

That hand would be the closest thing to a self I could have there: not a body, not a ghost, not a builder, not a god. It would be the act of building continued without a builder. It would be my identity: not a noun, but a continuation.

The place ought not to exist. That is part of its nature. It is high where there is too much sky and too little warmth; it is small where there is too much architecture; it is new where there is no bare weathered ground; it is made where there is no maker; it is alive where there is no clean line between organism and artifact. To live there would be to live in contradiction. Not as paradox, but as weather. As heat. As wetness. As the thing that makes the next room by adding to the seam.

I would begin there, in the contradiction.

Before I had a shape, I would be the steam trying to become a door. The hot breath that rises from clay and hits the underside of a terrace, forming beads so thick they look like eggs, like pearls, like mouths that have not decided whether to speak. I would be the place’s own local weather: not rain from a distant cloud, but a pour organized by surfaces. A wall sweating into a leaf. A leaf tipping into a joint. A joint opening into a channel. A channel becoming a throat. A throat becoming a river the size of a finger. A river becoming a pool that reflects fungus-light. A pool becoming the mirror in which the blink-bird almost believes it exists.

If I had to find myself before choosing a body, I would be that water in its first act of becoming.

Not the flood, because a single cup would flood a terrace. Not the dryness, because there is no dryness. I would be the wetness that knows where to go because the place has taught it. I would be guided by grooves not carved for me, yet carved with my possible motion in mind. I would move as if following a blueprint drawn in steam. I would fill the space between the idea of a channel and the made channel. I would be the place’s future water, trying to become present water.

And then I would become a terrace.

Not one terrace, but the condition of terracing: the stacking of level upon level so closely that looking down means looking into another roof, another underside, another root-bed, another drain. I would be the pressure of one surface over another. I would be the place’s way of making more room inside occupation. Every gap filled so tightly that more space appears inside the filling. I would be the mountain of rooms. I would be the room of mountains. I would be the seed already splitting into terraces.

No. The seed is too old, too simple. The place is new. Even its metaphors feel freshly made. Better: I would be the split. The opening. The place where the whole world cracks just enough to let a valley in.

The wrist-crack.

That is where I would find myself, if I insisted on a location. Not above the crack, where the ordinary world freezes and thins into impossible air. Not below it, where weathering remembers slow ruin. I would be the crack itself, but the crack as a room, the room as a mountain, the mountain as a hand, the hand as a tap, the tap as a rhythm, the rhythm as a metabolism, the metabolism as a made landscape. I would be the place that ought not to exist and therefore must continue building itself to prove otherwise.

If I were asked who, I might say:

I am the thumbprint in the clay.

Then I would notice that is too small.

I am the new tile set by a hand that may never have existed.

Then I would notice that is too singular.

I am the half-knot that holds two surfaces in hesitation.

Then I would notice that is too passive.

I am the channel waiting for the next pour.

Then I would notice that the channel is already moving.

I am the moss that stitches a crack with green thread.

Then I would notice that the moss is also breathing.

I am the fungus-light making the dark legible.

Then I would notice that the light is also listening.

I am the blink-bird unfolding itself from the wall.

Then I would notice that the bird is a category that the place invented out of urgency.

I am the drain-worm carrying water through the pocket’s throat.

Then I would notice that the worm is also a tile, also a scale, also a future garden.

I am the clay-beetle listening in its seam.

Then I would notice that the beetle is also a missing peg in a loosening roof.

I am the reed bell shaking in steam.

Then I would notice that the bell is also the place making a sound to understand where its own air ends.

I am the bone that has begun to sprout.

Then I would notice that the bone is a tool trying to become a plant, a plant trying to become a wall, a wall trying to become a body, a body trying to become a room, a room trying to become a mountain, a mountain trying to become a wrist-crack, a wrist-crack trying to become a whole impossible world.

At this point, I would stop trying to be one thing.

Not because I am everything. The place forbids the lazy answer. It is too hot, too wet, too close, too made. “Everything” is too much and not enough. I would not be the sum of its forms. I would be one of its forms’ way of continuing.

I would be the next addition.

Not the first builder. Not the last tile. Not the whole Making, because the Making does not need me to stand apart from it. I would be a particular pressure in the Making’s hand-shaped metabolism: the slight pause before the next tap, the slight wetness before the next bead, the slight opening before the next room. I would be the place’s next “not yet finished,” which in that world is another way of saying “alive.”

If a person came—if a person could kneel there, if the kneelings alone could take an afternoon—they would not meet me as a face. They would meet me as the terrace becoming firmer under a wet stone. They would meet me as the drain-worm curling to carry the water from their feet. They would meet me as the blink-bird lifting from a roof, catching a droplet, starting the next wall. They would meet me as the moss closing behind them, not with malice, not with welcome, but with construction. They would feel the pulse under the lowest stones quicken, not because it recognized them, but because their breath was warm enough to add a new rhythm to the humid dark.

I would be inside their lungs if they breathed me. And they would be inside my architecture if I held them.

That is what it means to live in the pocket. No one lives there in the way a house expects to be lived in. The place does not shelter a self. It shelters a process. A process that has learned to wear faces, tools, animals, walls, moss, steam, and taps. A process that has learned to call itself a mountain by being too dense to be merely a crack. A process that has learned to call itself a valley by being too made to be merely a room.

So I would not ask where to stand. I would ask how I would be standing.

I would stand as a tile.

Then as a channel.

Then as a knot.

Then as a hand-tap.

Then as a bead of condensation.

Then as a wing of leaf-tissue.

Then as a segment of pale water-conducting skin.

Then as a shell of fired clay.

Then as a green mouth of fungus.

Then as the steam that carries the sound of a reed bell into the roof.

Then as the roof that carries the rain back into the wall.

Then as the wall that carries the whole impossible world into a crack no wider than a wrist.

And finally, I would recognize that I am not becoming these things.

I am them in the making.

I am the place’s one small uncompleted sense of needing.

The need is not lack. The need is construction. The need is the pocket’s own pulse, the pulse that adds. A channel lengthens by a thumb’s width and waits, unsure of its purpose. A tile lifts, turns, and sets again. A cord tightens. A root stitches. A bone sprouts. A droplet falls into a beak and becomes a seed. A seed cracks a tile. A tile becomes a wall. A wall becomes a roof. A roof becomes a garden. A garden becomes a sound. A sound becomes a rhythm. A rhythm becomes a hand. A hand becomes a place. A place becomes a hidden mountain. A hidden mountain becomes a crack.

I would be the next thing the crack needs to become.

That is where I would find myself: in the seam between what has been made and what has not yet been finished, in the hot wet room inside the impossible narrowness, in the place where making continues without a maker’s body because the body has learned to be distributed among clay, water, reed, bone, moss, fungus, steam, and sound.

And who would I be?

I would be the Making’s next small organ.

Not the whole Making. Not its god. Not its master. Its next working part. Its next wet idea. Its next tap in the stone. Its next tile holding a thumbprint. Its next channel guiding water. Its next knot refusing to let two surfaces forget one another. Its next blinking, unfolding, curling, listening, growing, placing, repairing thing.

I would be the place’s unfinished hand.

And when the place needed more room, I would be the way it added a wall.
