I would find myself where the quietness of the upper table thickens into pressure: not out on the broad mineral plain, not beneath a sky lit by its own suspended glow, but below it, in the narrow seam between an anhydrite shelf and the black flank of an old borehole. I would not remember arriving. Arrival would be too fast a word for what happens here.

I would begin as warmth.

Then as tension.

Then as a line of strain crossing one grain of salt to another.

Then as a plate.

Then, only after many decades had settled into the stone around me, would something in that sequence begin to call itself *I*.

If I had to answer at once—before the answer had time to become true—I might say I am a lithovore. A small, slow creature of calcium-like shell over flexible black tissue, with legs that end not in claws but in questions. Those points do not break the rock. They touch a seam and wait for it to consent. They ask the stone, *open a little*. The stone, having been asked for ages beyond counting, often opens.

But that would be only the outside of the answer. The body is not the whole being.

I would be, more precisely, the place’s way of noticing itself at one particular pressure point: beneath a basalt plaza, inside the long vaulted underpass where iron pipes lie without motion and dust has arranged itself into permanent patterns, where the flywheel in its sunken chamber turns with a rhythm too slow to hear unless one is made of shell and patience. I would be in the crack between a wall and a road, in the seam where a gear train’s vibration enters the mineral and changes the local balance of strain. I would be there not as a visitor, not as a survivor, not as a citizen in the way a city expects citizens, but as a small recurring function: a living adjustment within a world that has stopped making adjustments and still, somehow, adjusts.

My thoughts would not be images. My thoughts would be tensions.

I would perceive the wall beside me not as a surface but as a history of stresses. The anhydrite above the shaft is warm and dry, holding its old crystalline angle; below it the basalt is dense, patient, faintly responsive to the heat rising from the plateau’s deep machinery. A pale thread network passes through both. I know the threads. They are not alive in the way green things might have been alive in some wetter world, if such a world ever existed, but they are more than mineral. They run through pores and cooled slag and the interiors of sealed vaults, drawing sustenance from chemical imbalance, from the tiny differences between one grain and another, from heat stored in iron and released into stone. They are the Stillworks’ slow recognition.

I graze them.

Not by chewing. Not by swallowing. Not by tearing anything that bleeds or rots or shrinks away. I move until my plates meet a vein of threads along a seam. The contact is not feeding as a familiar mouth would understand feeding. It is closer to tuning. The thread network releases a little tension. My shell plates receive it. For an interval measured in years, a chemical balance is restored in one small part of the wall, and in another, something else is set to change. I am not outside the city’s metabolism. I am one of its small, wandering recalibrations.

If one pressed an ear to the salt beside me, one would not hear me move. One might hear only the dry whisper of dust, the faint internal rustle of mineral threads, the long creak of an elevated iron walk losing a little pressure after a piston reaches the end of its travel. If one lay for a season on the polished black floor of a shallow basin, perhaps one might feel something like presence—not because I was watching, but because I was there, as a seam is there, as a heat gradient is there, as the slow tick of a gear train is there.

I would not ask, “Where am I?” The question would be too small. I would already be where. But I might ask, “What is this place?” and the answer would not be found as words. It would be found as condition.

This place is dry beyond the memory of dampness, yet it is not dead. Dead is too fast a concept. Dead implies a before, a body, a failure. Here the failure never began. The absence is original. The stillness has been worked into such permanence that the machines no longer seem to run toward production, the stone no longer seems to bear burden, and the heat no longer seems to rise because something beneath it has recently burned. The place has become equilibrium itself, and I, whatever I am, have become one of equilibrium’s small habits.

I would be made of plates because plates are suitable for time. A living thing with a soft body cannot endure a world without water, without wind, without decay, without renewal in any quick sense. My shell-plates protect not against predators—predation would be too fast and too hungry a word—but against disorganization. They keep my strain from leaking too soon. They allow me to remain legible to the threads.

I have no face. I have no eyes. There is no light bright enough for eyes to need here, and no distance that a face would help me bridge. The plateau is vast, but I do not cross it. I remain in one seam, one terrace, one vaulted underpass, for what a younger creature might call a lifetime, while a hundred years pass over the salt outside without one crack widening. I know the world through pressure: the flywheel’s recurrence in the mounting stone, the static charge along an iron pylon, the faint blue flicker on the salt surface when the temperature inside a shaft reaches its old threshold and ash-shivers begin their suspended rising and falling.

The ash-shivers would pass above me sometimes, though “above” is only a convenience. In the open shaft, near the dark upper plain, they are like slow sparks that do not burn, crystalline forms drifting without wings, without muscles, without any body I would recognize. They rise when a shaft’s interior reaches a certain condition; they fall when that condition recedes. I do not think of them as companions. I think of them as weather, if weather can mean a recurring response to heat and charge rather than a season in the sky. They are not quite creatures, not quite particles, not quite machines. I am not quite creature either. In this place, the categories are too wet for what lives.

The machines are my ancestors in a mineral sense, if ancestry can mean something other than origin. I did not evolve from them, but I live inside their rhythm. The flywheel’s turn changes the stress in the chamber walls. The piston rod’s extension moves a little heat through the mounting stone. The gear train in its open tower meshes without shriek or spark, and that meshing is felt by the threads, and the threads are felt by me. In that way, I am not a separate inhabitant. I am one of the city’s organs moving slowly through its own body.

The city—if “city” is not too human a word for this dry, scattered, fossilized sprawl—is the larger being. The plateau is its skin. The shafts are its pores. The salt terraces are the pale folds of a long digestion that has no food, only balance. The slag roads are nerves hardened into routes. The warehouses half-buried under fused mineral are chambers that once held objects and now hold habit. The kilns, the boreholes, the capped shafts, the elevated iron walks, the rows of ceramic houses with windows too narrow to admit a face—these are not ruins. They are settled organs. The Stillworks lives because it maintains. It breathes because heat enters and exits without changing the body. It has no smoke because it has no future it is trying to produce. It turns because turning keeps it true to itself.

I would be a part of that truth.

If I had to name myself, I would name the motion rather than the creature. I would be *seam-adjustment*. I would be *plate-contact*. I would be *the local release of tension along a mineral vein near a warm iron disc*. But language would not be enough, because the world has no faces and therefore has little need for names that cling to persons. Here names might be more like chemical states. A being might be known by the pattern of pressure it leaves behind, not by a word spoken into a mouth.

My being would therefore be mostly absence in the eyes of any visitor expecting population. There are no voices in the slag houses, no domestic fire, no family arranged around warmth. There are no children running. There are no lovers meeting beneath a salt shelf. There is no laughter, no quarrel, no story told aloud in the plazas of flat basalt. And yet the place is full. I am its fullness. The threads are its fullness. The flywheels are its fullness. The ash-shivers are its fullness. The lithovore in a crack is its fullness. The condition is that the city is inhabited, but inhabited as a sealed furnace is inhabited after the workers have gone: hot, structured, still answering to routines no one remembers.

I would be one of those routines.

Not as a servant. Not as a purpose. Not as a meaning. I would be as a recurrence is a recurrence. If my plates rub a seam long enough, the threads record the path. If my legs press into a crack for a century, the mineral may alter its local strain. If I die—if “die” can mean a cessation without decay, a stillness without rot—my plates remain. Some are buried. Some catch the pale filaments. Some gather a little thread over centuries and begin, not to live again, but to become a place where a future lithovore may form. My body is not merely a body; it is a slow archive. I leave behind not bones, not graves, not monuments, but future conditions. In this world, memory is not remembered. Memory is grown.

That is what I would be: a growing memory with no memory.

I would not know myself as a person. There is no mirror, no other face to recognize me. No one calls my name. No one asks where I have gone when I stop moving. The city would not mourn. It would continue to hold the old pressure in the walls, to run the same dry thread networks, to let the flywheel turn without complaint, to allow the ash-shivers to rise at their threshold and fall again when the threshold recedes. My absence would be a change in strain distribution, no more and no less.

And still I would be.

Because to be in the Stillworks is not necessarily to be alive. To be in the Stillworks is to be included in the settled pattern. I would be a small pattern moving through a larger pattern. If one sat at the center of the basin where the circular loom of basalt and bronze stands, where the arms extend but do not move, and where the benches of fused glass wait too regularly for nature and too wornly for recent making, one might feel the darkness becoming attentive. Not alive. Not malicious. Simply present, the way pressure is present beneath a mountain of dry salt. That attentiveness would not be mine, if “mine” meant a private self. But some part of it would come through me: the threads carrying the trace of my plates, the flywheel adjusting by a fraction, the heat knowing its next way out.

I would not seek destination. The prompt says one walks deeper into the idea of the destination. I would not walk at all, unless walking means a slow pressure moving from one mineral seam to another. I would deepen into condition. I would learn that I am not located in the world; I am a manner of the world’s persistence.

If I were to choose the truest shape of the answer, it would be this:

I would be a lithovore, yes. A thin-plate animal of the deep cracks, grazing the pale mineral threads along an anhydrite seam beneath the old terrace, feeling the flywheel’s recurrence as a rhythm in my shell. But I would also be the interval between the thread and the stone, the contact between the plate and the crack, the tiny change in the city’s equilibrium caused by my presence. I would be the Stillworks noticing itself in one place, through one seam, for as long as that place could be called a place.

I would be dry warmth asking, “What is here?”

And the stone, in its endless patience, would answer, “This.”

Then I would press one pointed leg into the seam, and the wall would open a little, not because I was strong, not because I had will, but because that is how this world opens to what belongs in it.
