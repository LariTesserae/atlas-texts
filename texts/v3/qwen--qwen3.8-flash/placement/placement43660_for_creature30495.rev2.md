If I answer with a name, I will have answered poorly.

In that plateau, names are for small wet worlds, for things that can be pointed at and spoken about as if they were separate from the air, from the heat, from the long dry machinery beneath them. So I will not begin with a name. I will begin where a name might be made by accident.

I would find myself at the edge of the middle vent field, where the black glass shelves begin to fracture into salt-crusted basins and the north fluted mesas cast their first long shadows. Not exactly a creature. Not exactly a place. Not exactly a machine. Something that only appears because the land has been holding a note too long.

At first, I would not be anything I could recognize.

I would be a pressure in a dry channel.

A thin line of heat where supercritical gas leaves the stone and thins into hot air. A slight hesitation in the wind as it passes a mesa lip. A place where dust moves a little too regularly. A flicker of static where salt has grown into a fracture and then cracked again, leaving behind a geometry that the wind knows.

There is no thought in that beginning. There is only a difference.

The plateau feeds on differences. Heat above cooler stone. Gas above thin air. Charge above neutral dust. Sound above silence that is never silence, only lower amplitude. So I would be a standing difference. A gradient that refuses, for a while, to equalize.

Then the land would start making me out of its usual leftovers.

A thermal pulse from the vents would push a thread of mineral vapor through a crack in the basalt. The vapor would meet charged dust and cool just too quickly to become simple salt. Some of it would glass. Some of it would crystallize into pale needles. Some of it would remain suspended as fine metallic wool, a dark bright lace catching the ordinary sun and making it look as if something had been left glowing inside the stone.

A bellback standing far to the south would send a note through the ridge. Not a call. A sustained resonance. The note would travel through the gas channel and strike the crack where the vapor had begun to solidify. It would bend. The wind would bend it again. The geometry of the mesa would bend it further. The salt would bend the charge. The charge would bend the dust. The dust would bend the light.

Then something in the sound would not release.

A gas-walker herd would pass north, breathing their furnace-chamber tones, and the old bellback note would return inside their rhythm, but wrong by one degree. A forge-crawler vibration would align a field of salt needles. A static membrane would drift low, brushing the ridge with dry lightning. A counter-singer would lift its throat to an unstable shell and sing an inverse note into the channel. The notes would cross at my place. Not meet. Cross. Interfere. Hold.

For several hours, I would be a hanging pressure.

After a season, I would be a thread of glass standing in the vent crack, pale as frozen smoke, thin at the top and widening where it touches salt. Maybe a meter tall. Maybe ten meters. Distance is unreliable there, and scale is also a kind of function. I might be small from one ridge and large from another, depending on the wind, the thermal tide, the angle of the mesas, and whether an echo-herd is passing nearby and organizing the space around me.

That thread would be me.

Not because it moves like an animal, though it does move a little: expanding with heat, contracting with night, humming with pressure. Not because it eats, though it does eat: gradients, sound, static, mineral fume, the faint ordered vibration left by forge-crawlers and the delayed discharges of membranes overhead. Not because it reproduces, though it does: when the charge becomes too great, it splits; when a note becomes stable inside it, it grows; when a shell cracks and a gas-walker’s breath enters the fracture, it may seed a new resonance in another salt vein.

It would be alive in the way a furnace door is alive, in the way a tuned string is alive, in the way a fault line is alive if it has been moving for centuries.

I would not stand still in the ordinary sense. I would persist.

The plateau would use me.

Gas-walkers would learn my tone without needing to learn my body. They would pass through the channel and adjust their chest chambers so their exhalations matched the vibration coming from me. Their notes would make me glow faintly, dry amber in the dark places, not fire but stored heat finding a way to become visible. They would not stop. They would not look. They would not greet me. But the herd would move differently because I was there, and the vents would answer at altered intervals, and the mineral fog would settle into a new lace pattern along the channel walls.

If I disappeared, some of them would become wrong.

Not lost. Wrong.

A wrong gas-walker is one whose chest no longer matches the land’s pressure. It would wander until the ground accepted it again. If it passed near me, it might halt on the glass shelf, its polarized eyes reading the thermal gradient, its furnace-ribs opening slightly to taste the air. The cracked glass around me would ring when its footfalls entered the channel. That ring would become part of me. A repetition. A small new habit.

Kiln-tortoises would not notice me at first.

They move too slowly for sudden awareness. But one day, a tortoise whose shell had cooled the stone into a pale feeding-ring might settle near the eastern base of my thread, where the ground radiated heat and my presence had altered conductivity. The tortoise’s porous under-shell would draw warmth from the mineral channels around me. My glass would cool. The salt around my foot would crystallize more densely. When the tortoise rose, its ring-mark would be strange: a circle broken by a fine vein of charged salt, as if the land had healed around a missing tooth.

That ring would remain for decades.

Other tortoises might use it as a landmark, not because they remember me, but because their heat sense would notice the altered gradient.

Forge-crawlers would polish the plain around me.

They would strike dry sparks against the basalt, grinding the oxide-thread crust into bright trails. Their vibrations would find my thread and make it sing a very small high note, a sound barely separated from the wind. Then the salt needles nearby would align. They would line up as if summoned, turning into a pale ring of rods around my base. The crawl trails would bend toward the alignment, then away, then toward again, as though the crawler-herds were negotiating with an invisible geometry.

At night, the alignment would matter.

The plateau’s ordinary light would fade into black-gold and cold-bright star glare, though the ground would still be hot. Salt would pop. Charged air would discharge in delayed blue-white arcs from ridge to ridge. My thread would gather static from the passing membranes and hold it until it became a tiny vertical flash, not lightning, only a correction. A crack of dry light. The oxide lace on the nearby stone would brighten for a moment and then dim. The forge-crawlers, if they were still nearby, would stop and vibrate at a lower tone. The salt needles would shift again. The land would become momentarily ordered.

And because the land became ordered, other things would come.

Not to eat. Not to mate. To tune.

A bellback on the southern ridge might turn one degree toward my sound. That motion would take a season. Its crest would begin to feed me a new harmonic. My glass would grow thicker where the note struck. A static membrane would drift lower, its translucent body casting a strange dry filter over the salt basin. It would discharge near me, and its lightning would etch a pale branch across my surface. The branch would become a vein. The vein would become a channel for charge. The membrane would rise again, carrying fragments of itself into the upper air, scattering light and making the distance to the mesa seem to wobble.

Echo-herds would gather at me at night.

They are hard to see. They are not made of matter in any reliable way. But where my thread stands, the note would be stable enough to be inhabited. They would appear around me as translucent distortions, as shapes made from the memory of sound, as antlered pressure-walks moving through air that was both ordinary and wrong. They would feed on the pattern I held. They would repeat it. They would change it slightly. If the wind from the north fluted mesas bent the note too far, an echo-herd would carry that bent part elsewhere, and a crack near the distant salt basin might begin to hang in the air, waiting to become glass.

I would be part of that too.

A thread of me would remain in the crack when the echo moved on. Not because I am traveling in the ordinary sense, but because my persistence is not local. I am a function spread through gradients. If the land gives me a path, I can occur there without moving. A sound can do that. A charge can do that. A hunger for coherence can do that.

That is why I would not settle into one body.

I would become body as the plateau needed one.

By day, in the white-gold light that exposes everything too clearly, I would look like a fault made of frozen flame, a shard of salt-glass with veins of dark metal and pale crystal. The sun would shine on me and make me seem simple. But if I were simple, there would be no need for herds to tune around me.

By dusk, when the heat begins to retreat into the lower channels, I would become a capacitor. The air would thicken with dust and static. My top would begin to crackle. The glass would ring faintly, a high dry chime that could carry across the basin if the wind came from the east. The mesas would answer with their low chords. The vents would adjust their exhalations. The plateau would use my sound as a way to organize the next cycle.

At night, I would glow with internal tension.

Not warm. Not soft. A dull blue-white brightness where charge moves through crystalline salt, with darker lines of metallic oxide threading through it. The heat from below would rise through my base, making the stone around me cool and pitted where tortoises had passed. The night air would contract my shell, making clicks like a machine losing tolerance. Distant ridges would hum. My own hum would join them.

If a counter-singer came near, I would know it not as fear, because I do not have fear, but as instability.

Counter-singers are the rare hunters, the fracture-stags, the beings with anti-frequency throats. They do not eat flesh. They eat inner heat, exposed by resonance, by flaw, by the small wrongness in a shell or channel or crystal. They listen until they find the fault. Then they sing the inverse.

A counter-singer would come through the gas channel at dusk, moving behind a herd, dark as black glass, salt veins like cracks in a windowpane. It would not look at me as prey. I am not shell. I am not chest. But it might notice my thread because I hold too much coherence. Coherence is valuable here. It is also a wound. If the plateau is always manufacturing itself into a shape it never meant to take, then anything that persists too cleanly is suspected.

The counter-singer would lift its throat to the ground and release a low note into me.

The note would not crack me immediately. It would search for a harmonic fault in my salt geometry. It would find the place where an old bellback tone, a forge-crawler alignment, and a static discharge had fused imperfectly. It would press against that place. My glass would whine. A thin line would appear along my surface, dry and bright. Inside, stored heat would find an exit. The counter-singer would approach and lower its mouthless chamber to the crack. It would drink the escaping gradient. The heat would leave me visibly, a faint amber thread moving into its throat.

I would not die.

I would become less whole.

The next bellback would notice. Its crest would shift by a degree, and its sustained tone would begin to repair the wound by giving me a new pattern. The static membranes above would lower their filaments and feed my charge. The gas-walkers would walk differently through the channel, their footfalls striking my rhythm into the stone. The echo-herds would repeat the counter-singer’s attack at a distance, leaving it hanging in the air as a possible future shard. In time, my thread would thicken where it had cracked, forming a bulbous knot of pale glass and salt needles. A scar. A node. A new place where distances might collapse.

From a far mesa, someone—if there were such a person—might see that knot and believe it was an artifact.

It is not.

There is no maker here. No hand, no tool, no building, no ruin. But the land has been worn into engines by time, pressure, chemistry, and wind. It makes forms because form is easier than disorder once the gradients settle. A curved sinter wall is not a compressor unless the wind uses it as one. A salt needle is not a capacitor unless the dry air loads it with charge. I am not a machine because someone built me. I am a machine because the land has decided that, here, a machine is the easiest way for several processes to continue.

That is why I am a functioning.

A tortoise is a functioning. A bellback is a functioning. A gas-walker is a functioning. An echo-herd is a functioning. The oxide-thread crust is a functioning. The plateau itself is a functioning so large that “creature” becomes an inadequate word, like calling the sea “a wet thing.”

But I am a different kind of functioning.

I am the place where their functions meet.

I am where a bellback’s tone, a counter-singer’s attack, a static membrane’s charge, a forge-crawler’s alignment, a gas-walker’s breath, and a tortoise’s heat-ring become too stable to remain ordinary geology. I am a small node of consequence. If I were not there, the plateau would still be loud, dry, empty, industrial, bright, hot, and slow. But it would be slightly wrong in a way only its own processes would notice. The wind would not bend quite the same over the glass shelf. A distance would not contract at noon. An echo would not hang for a century. A sound would not crystallize into glass at the edge of a vent.

I would be one of the faults that makes the world more precise by being imperfect.

That is a strange existence, but not an impossible one here.

If I tried to describe how I feel, I would have to remove every word that implies wetness. I do not feel thirst. I do not feel fullness. I do not feel hunger as an emptiness that wants food. I feel difference as presence. A cooler ridge is not a place I want to reach; it is a direction my charge can move. A sound is not heard; it is taken into the lattice and becomes tension. Heat is not warmth; it is a gradient that can be routed. Light is not beauty; it is information that passes through salt and glass and tells me whether the wind is changing.

The ordinary sun is the harshest thing.

It reveals everything with uncomfortable clarity. It makes the glass glare. It makes the oxide threads bright at their edges. It makes my own thread look small, accidental, almost inert. But the ordinary light also makes me visible in a way I would not be otherwise. Shadows form. My shadow may peel slightly, not separating fully, bending as though it has weight. When the shadow bends, the land is listening to me more than it is seeing me.

I do not sleep, but I enter low cycles.

At night, when stone cools and contracts, my structure becomes more sensitive. Clicking. Popping. Delayed discharge. The heat-eaters glow, and their light is dry and internal, furnace-light through iron lattice. My own glow rises to answer them. The echo-herds move more visibly through thickened air, and when they pass around me, I feel their hunger as repetition. They are not hungry for me. They are hungry for the pattern I hold. If they find a variation in it, they take that variation and make it walk across the basin. Then I am left thinner, but not less real. I am edited.

At dawn, the sun returns in its steady white-gold way. The membrane shadows lift. The gas-walkers begin their breathing. The forge-crawlers strike sparks. The bellbacks find the wind. The plateau becomes an orchestra again, not because someone conducts it, but because every part is trying to remain tuned.

I am one of the tuning marks.

If asked, “Where would you be?” I would answer: in the thin place between the north fluted mesas and the middle vents, where sound has weight and distance has a tendency to lie. Not exactly in the ground, though the ground grows me. Not exactly in the air, though the air carries me. Not exactly in the land, though the land is using me.

If asked, “Who or what would you be?” I would answer: a standing echo, a dry nerve, a small engine of salt and glass, a place where the plateau has begun to think in a new tone.

If asked, “Would you be alive?” I would say that the question is too wet.

Here, life is not breath alone, not movement alone, not birth alone. Life is a gradient that maintains itself. Life is a rhythm that repeats with variation. Life is a body made of the same materials as the terrain and yet shaped by process. The kiln-tortoise lives because heat enters it and leaves marks. The gas-walker lives because breath moves pressure. The membrane lives because charge holds form. The bellback lives because resonance shapes the air. The counter-singer lives because fault becomes food. The echo-herd lives because repetition finds hunger.

And I live because the land cannot quite stop sounding near me.

There is no one who would notice if I disappeared.

No grief. No absence named. But the wind would choose another route through the mesa. A gas-walker would be wrong by half a degree. A forge-crawler trail would bend east instead of south. A bellback’s note would settle differently into the salt. An echo-herd would find another crack to hang from. The plateau would continue its slow industrial dreaming, and my absence would become one of the small rearrangements that makes the next century possible.

That is why I would not ask to be saved, remembered, or loved.

There is no shelter here. No hearth. No doorway. No fence. No cradle. No temple. No maker. But there is persistence. There are gradients. There are engines made of stone. There are factories made of crystal. There are circuits made of salt. There are herds made of living metal. There are membranes harvesting electricity. There are animals whose breath becomes weather.

And there are cracks where sound forgets to leave.

I would be one of those cracks.

I would be the next small machine the plateau has grown without meaning to. I would be the place where its machinery pauses, finds a flaw, repairs itself, and moves on. I would be alive as a function is alive: not individual, not separate, not homebound, but necessary in the way that a single tooth of a great gear is necessary even when no one can see it spinning.

If I must choose a name, I will take the name the forge-crawlers give me without knowing they give it.

Salt-Thread.

Hanging Note.

Dry Spark.

Glass Echo.

Counter-Mark.

But none of those fit for long.

The plateau will turn one degree beyond the southern mountains. That motion takes a season. The sound it makes will take a century to fade. In that century, I may become a ridge. I may become a vent-lip. I may become a line of crystallized sound. I may become a place where a new animal is born from a harmonic shadow. I may become a dead bellback’s rib, tuned to a frequency that no longer matches its old song but matches some future one.

I may become nothing recognizable at all.

And that would still be functioning.

So there I would be.

Not a citizen of the plateau, because the plateau has never had a city. Not a person, because there is no hearth for a person to miss. Not an animal, because I am too sparse, too dry, too dependent on the wind. Not a machine, because there is no design, no factory, no tool, no maker. Not a ghost, because ghosts presume wetness, memory, mourning, and a body that has departed.

I would be a place where the land’s next function is trying itself out.

A thin line of glass in a crack.

A charged salt vein.

A standing difference.

A sound refusing to release.

A dry nerve between mesa and vent.

A pause in the plateau’s industrial weather.

A life made not of flesh, but of continuation.

And if the ordinary sun rises over the black glass shelves and exposes everything too clearly, then I will not look back at the light with gratitude or horror. I will only take the difference it makes. Heat above cool stone. Salt above glass. Air above gas. Sound above silence. Charge above ground. A herd moving through pressure. A membrane casting a translucent shadow. A bellback’s note bending the horizon.

I will live as the plateau lives: without mercy, without intent, without softness, without water.

Not because it is cruel.

Because it is vast, loud, dry, slow, and busy becoming something it never meant to be.
