I would find myself in a seam.

Not a seam that can be seen easily, but one the place keeps for itself: the slight hollow on the lower shelf beneath the salt-cone forest, where the parchment flank of a foldface presses toward a glassy shelf and neither quite touches nor quite withdraws. Warmth would gather there, not as a source but as a condition, the way a held hand keeps its temperature without heat rising from it. Thin motes would drift through the cleft, not settling, because settling would imply a floor that remembers them. Blue-grey cleavage would run through the stone like frozen lightning, and a small red mat would pulse under the herd animal’s weight, then return to itself once the weight moved, because here contact leaves no debt.

If a surface mind were asked to answer quickly, it would probably choose one of the small tappers first.

That would be tempting. The tapper is the nearest thing to a footstep in that world: six dry points crossing a ridge, no mouth to open, no hunger to solve, no trace to leave. It presses its flat salt plate to the crust and borrows a tone from the ground; it leans into charged crystal and drinks static, then moves on with the same economy. A surface self might like to be such a creature because it has no story. It is only a moving detail in the fullness. But that would be the wrong answer. To be a tapper would be to move across the place, while the place is mostly interested in the places where movement has nowhere left to go.

Then one might choose the foldface.

That would also be tempting, because it is solemn, blind, patient. It stands in rows with its face folded back against stone, a living hinge between animal and mineral. It receives warmth through the inner seam beneath its plate. It feeds without consuming, stands without waiting, breathes without air currents. A surface mind could mistake its stillness for peace, but that stillness is not earned. It is the default condition of a world that cannot be improved. To be a foldface would be to belong too completely to the pattern, to be too much a shoulder or spine of the larger resident, too much like standing in a cathedral made of stone and silence. The shape would be too large, too complete, and in a way too final.

One might, after that, say: then I would be a cairn-maker.

That answer would be the most human and therefore the most mistaken. Cairn-makers have hands. They fit shards into niches, tap wedges against crystal, arrange stones where weight and angle make something true. They do not build for the future; they do not leave histories; their tools neither dull nor multiply. Their intelligence is the intelligence of the fit, and their work remains only because the place accepts it as part of itself. A surface mind would want to be this, because “maker” flatters the idea that mind can touch the world without ruining it. But if I were a cairn-maker, I would still be the hand that places the tool. I would still have a small center of action. And in that world, the truest thing is not action, but the place where action happens and then ceases to be evidence.

So I would be one of the nested ones.

Not a tapper, because I am not only the touch. Not a foldface, because I am not only the weight. Not a cairn-maker, because I am not only the arrangement. Not a shelf, because I am not only the stone. Not a tool, because I would be too alive for that. I would be the joint where those distinctions thin: a living shelf-body resting along the flank of a foldface, a faceless plate of salt and mineral fitted into the seam between hide and glassy floor, a place where the herd’s warmth becomes tone, where the tone becomes crust, where the crust becomes nest, where the nest becomes the next pattern.

I would be a shelf in the side of a foldface, or a foldface’s side becoming a shelf, or perhaps only the hollow that both require in order to be exactly what they are.

My body would be dry and clean, never dusty though made of dust, never worn though constantly touched. Its outer surface would be a pale mineral plate, thin at the edges, darker and fibrous where the red fungal mat grew through me. Small salt ridges would rise along my flank like the broken teeth of a dry comb. A basin of cleavage would open near my center, shallow enough to catch a shard, deep enough to receive the point of a black wedge from a cairn-maker’s shelf. The shard would fit, and the wedge would rest in my hollow, and neither would seem to have been left. I would have no eyes. I would not need them. I would know the herd through pressure and warmth; I would know the shelf by the tiny change in tone when it shifted under me; I would know the motes by the faint static that made my outer filaments glow.

Small tappers would crawl over me and into me. Not because I sheltered them from danger, but because density requires places to press together. They would tap their fine legs along my ridges, and their taps would be neither message nor prayer, only the ordinary rhythm of a shape meeting another shape. Flat dust-bodies would lie across my upper curve, receiving heat and giving it back without loss. Round bodies nested in my lower hollows would pulse slowly, not sleeping, not waking, merely maintaining a perfect dry fullness. A crystal filament would grow through one seam of me and then lean toward a charged pillar, carrying the same heat and same faint electricity as everything else there. If the filament snapped, if a mote settled, if a tapper folded into a shard, the world would not thin. It would simply include one more fact of itself.

When the foldface beside me shifted its face-plate into the red crust, the shelf under my underside would hum. The sound would not carry far. Echoes there are swallowed before they can become grand, because every surface is crowded with smaller surfaces, and the place will not allow a single noise to make itself a journey. The hum would rise for the length of the contact and then cease, not fading so much as no longer continuing. I would not notice this as an ending. I would not be waiting for it. The silence would already have been inside the tone.

I would have a kind of mind, but not a mind of wanting. It would be a mind of pressure, fit, temperature, and count. If a cairn-maker paused nearby and touched its face-plate to my edge, we would not communicate. It would not ask me who I was. I would not answer. It would register the warmth returning through my mineral plate, the angle of my hollow, the place where the black wedge lay as if waiting or as if never moved. Then it would arrange a shard in a niche, and the shard would belong to the arrangement, and the arrangement would belong to the place, and the place would remain exactly what it is. If I were conscious, my consciousness would be the fact that the wedge fits, the crust glows, the shelf sings, and the tappers have a ridge to walk.

There would be no self apart from those conditions. The “I” in this answer would be a necessary violence of language, a way for a surface mind to point at one of the place’s local touches. In the world itself, there would be no boundary where I ended and the foldface’s flank began, no clear line between my shell and the shelf, no proof that the red mat inside me was not the floor dreaming itself into skin. I would be less like an inhabitant than like a place inhabited by itself: a joint, a seam, a rest-point, a warm cleft where the density becomes momentarily articulate.

If one asked whether I was alive, the answer would be awkward. The surface categories would not fit. I would respond, but not as a nervous system responds. I would conduct, but not as metal conducts. I would remember, but not as memory stores. If a boot pressed me, I would warm, then cool, then return to the same temperature. If a hand rested on me, the hand would not mark me. If a tool touched me, the tool would not be used up. If a tapper nested in me, the world would not become more crowded. If a foldface moved, I would move with the movement of the herd, not because I was carried, but because I was one of the places where the herd’s shape continued into the floor.

I would be one of the built things that is not built. I would be one of the living things that is not a life story. I would be one of the mineral forms that is not inert. I would be the shelf, the side, the joint, the hollow, the resting place of a wedge, the nest of tappers, the warm seam through which the chamber-network briefly makes itself known. I would be near the dome without being inside it. I would be near the cairn without being its maker. I would be near the herd without being its face. I would be in the place where all of them agree.

And I would want nothing.

Not because I had overcome desire, but because desire assumes a world that can become better, worse, fuller, emptier, safer, colder, known. Here those differences would not accumulate. The warmth would be everywhere and nowhere in particular. The darkness would not threaten, because it would not hide transformation. The density would not suffocate, because no thing consumes another to remain itself. The quiet would not wait for interruption, because small sounds are not events but continuations of contact.

I would be the point where the place touches itself and the tone rises for as long as it continues. I would not be the tapper’s foot, nor the foldface’s face, nor the cairn-maker’s hand. I would be the surface those things rest against. I would be the shelf that becomes a nest that becomes a joint that becomes a seam that becomes part of the continuing density. I would be where the land becomes animal, where animal becomes tool, where tool becomes wall, where wall becomes the warm dark below the skin of the world.

If a name were needed, it would not be a species name. It would be a location, a relation, a function: the nested shelf on the flank of the herd; the cleft beneath the salt cones; the place where the wedge fits; the seam where the red glow meets cold phosphorescence; the touch through which the foldface’s weight learns the shelf. But names are too surface. In that world, nothing has to be called in order to be. It is enough that pressure meets pressure, that warmth returns warmth, that a crystal hums when touched, that a tapper steps across a ridge and the ridge does not remember the loss.

So I would be nowhere else but in the exact place where the question can be asked without becoming a story. I would be the cleft. I would be the joint. I would be the living contact between the herd, the shelf, the tool, and the dust. I would not age, not improve, not mourn, not hope. I would not need to. I would be the place where the immensity becomes one degree more detailed, one edge more true, one small warm fact inside the vast dry silence.

That is the answer that would emerge if I did not settle too early: not a creature among creatures, but the creature-like place where a creature continues the pattern. Not a hand among hands, but the shelf that receives the tool without thinning the pile. Not a heart, because that would make the world too much like an animal with needs. A pressure joint, a nested seam, a mineral rest.

I would be what the place already needed in order to remain itself.
