I would find myself not at a boundary but in the middle of one.

Not the middle of the basin, exactly, because that word “middle” still assumes too much symmetry. More like a slight disagreement in the water’s flow: a place where a current bends, slows, and becomes capable of delivering something. A worn seam in the plain. A shallow ridge of hardened silica with a hollow on its lee side where the particle storm eddies just enough to leave a skin of fine matter in place. There would be no name for this place except the way pressure arrives at it, except the way one temperature meets another and both are too weak to call it warmth.

I would begin there because there is nowhere else to begin.

For a long while, I would not be a creature. I would not have a front or a back, no memory of legs, no longing for air, no idea that “I” should be a compact thing. First I would be the water’s tendency. A dense pocket in the drift. A patch of chemistry with a history in the sediment. The faint metallic taste of dissolved iron. The ghost of oxygen, thin and disputed. A slow cloud of calcium dust coming down from somewhere far above, falling in a darkness so complete that falling becomes almost a myth, because there is no surface beneath it to prove the gravity is real.

I would be the place before it agrees to have an inhabitant.

Then I would begin to settle, not as a self, but as a surface. A mineral film would thicken on the stone. A bacterial mat would spread in pale rust-colored patches, binding grains that had been trying to move for centuries. A crack would collect a slightly warmer flow. A groove would retain a little less turbulence. Something would happen that is not birth, because birth assumes a line, a moment, a before and after. Something more like precipitation: matter becoming persistent enough to notice that it is persistent.

I would call that “finding myself,” if I could call it without giving it away.

At first my senses would be borrowed. I would have a crustacean’s antennae for a time: long, translucent filaments extended into the current, reading the water not as water but as a field of pressures, velocities, temperatures, chemical signatures. They would not touch the world from the outside; they would be part of the world touching itself. They would taste a shrimp’s wake before the shrimp knew it had passed. They would locate the seam from two degrees of temperature, not because two degrees are dramatic, but because two degrees are a door in this place.

Then I would borrow a fish’s lateral line, a dark channel running along a pale flank, mapping turbulence as if turbulence were language. I would be aware of the basin’s motions as a text with no sentences: only the continuous grammar of shear, pressure, drift, interruption. I would know that a storm is coming before it becomes silt. I would know that a stone has moved because the particles above it would suddenly arrange themselves differently, like a sentence changing order without changing meaning.

Then I would borrow the tube-worm’s crown, the feathery strainer extended into the flow, and for a while I would be nothing but selection: catching one mote, ignoring another, swallowing the world’s dust until my body becomes a record of what the current contains. I would be a filter, yes, but a filter is not a passive thing. It is a decision repeated millions of times without consciousness. It is the body saying yes to some matter and no to the rest, and in the saying yes, becoming that matter.

I would not want to be any of these. Wanting would be too loud. In that world, a want would dissolve almost as soon as it formed, diluted by the cold and the density and the slow chemistry of absence. But if I had to want something, I would want the condition that allows a want to persist. Not a tool. Not a shelter. Not a monument. Only a stable seam where a current can be trusted for longer than a single meal.

And then I would become something more like a being than any of the borrowed forms.

Not a hunter. Not a grazer. Not a solitary animal with a face. I would become the colonial node at the rise of the warm plume, the mat-backed mound, the living crust lying along the worn ridge where the water comes up from unseen depths and bends over ancient stone. I would be low and broad. I would be almost geological at first glance, so nearly the same color and texture as the basin that a predator, were one hungry enough to come, might pass over me without recognition. A blind fish would hover near one of my openings not because it sees me but because the current is different there, because the particles are denser, because some chemical threshold has been crossed. It would taste the gradient and think, if thought were the word, that the place has become more possible.

I would have no eyes to speak of. I would have no hands. I would have no voice. I would have hundreds of tiny apertures, no clear front or rear, a body that is also a surface and a surface that is also a community. My skin would be a crust of microbial mats, mineral grains, calcium flecks, oxidized films, the slow debris of organisms that passed overhead and fell. My tissues, if they are tissues, would be threaded with symbiotic cells, chemical gardens, metabolic corridors. I would draw water in through many small mouths and send it out through unseen channels, not because I am clever but because I have learned—through no learning, through persistence—to make a gradient that others can use.

This is the kind of “who” I could be: not a character but a condition. A concentration. A temporary agreement between water, mineral, chemistry, and time.

I would not own the ridge. I would not defend the seam. I would not mark a territory. Those are behaviors of denser, more abundant lives, lives where bodies have edges strong enough to say *this is mine* and mean it. Here, “mine” is a fragile word. It would dissolve at the first storm. But “where” is possible. “How” is possible. “For how long” is the truest measure.

If I existed there, I would know the water not as a place around me but as a medium inside me. I would be full of current. My body would be porous with process. Every part of me would be in negotiation with density: pressure pressing on the outside, chemistry changing the inside, particles settling on the upper surface, older sediment trapped in the mucus, older mucus calcifying into something like shell, something like wall, something like a record of what I was at the time. I would be a living archive of near-silence, written in calcium, sulfur, iron, bacterial film, and the faint blue trace of light released by accident.

Sometimes I would glow. Not to communicate, not in the way a land animal signals with face or posture or song. More as a side effect of metabolic traffic: a few microbes in my tissues disturbed by flow, a chain of chemical events releasing one photon’s worth of visible presence, then another, then nothing. The basin would not illuminate. It would simply register: *something moved here, briefly, in a way that light could not ignore.* Then the dark would return, and the dark would not be empty. It would be packed, textured, full of the memory of that tiny punctuation.

That would be my closest thing to speech.

If someone asked where I find myself, I would answer: in the seam where heat almost matters, where particles almost settle, where a current almost slows enough to make a body possible. I would answer: at the place where erosion has become too patient to be called violence, and turbulence has become too dense to be called motion. I would answer: not under the water, not in the water, but as one of the water’s rare habits.

Who or what would I be?

I would be a duration first.

I would be a body that learned to exist by not insisting too much on its own boundaries. I would be the mound at the bend, the pale mineral skin with many openings, the slow colony that is also a mat, also a rock, also a current, also a future habitat for creatures that pass over me without knowing they are passing through me. I would be the place where tube worms attach and shrimp crawl and larvae fall like snow. I would be a temporary center without will, without hierarchy, without ambition. I would be the basin’s own attempt to concentrate matter just long enough to create a niche, and when the niche ends, I would not call that death.

I would call it redistribution.

There would be no grand narrative to my life. No birth scene with urgency. No adolescence of hunting. No legacy written in monuments. If I were observed by some impossible eye, I would look like part of the landscape until I moved, and even then the movement would be so slight it might be mistaken for a shift in current. A tube worm would extend through a crack in my surface. A fish would hover near one of my openings, not eating me but reading the particles I make possible. A shrimp would taste my edge with antennae so fine they are almost made of the water itself. Each of these would be an event. Each would be a sentence in a language too diffuse to be called a story.

And yet there would be intimacy.

In a place that crowded, every body is a rumor. To meet is to be nearly dissolved by the meeting. A larva settling on me would bring its own microbes, its own chemistry, its own small pattern of need. A fish passing above me would shed mucus that changes what grows on my surface. A storm of silt would cover one region of me and expose another, and I would respond not by resisting but by allowing the pattern to rewrite itself across my body. I would become different because the basin is different, and because the basin is not separate from me.

That is why I would not settle early on a single image of myself. The pale fish is too singular, too bounded. The crustacean is too quick, too dependent on motion. The tube worm is too vertical, too much a creature with an entrance and an exit. Even the mat is too passive. I would need something that contains all of them and none of them at once: something that is stone and skin, animal and process, a living surface that is also a place for surfaces to live.

I would become the seam that knows it is a seam.

Not in words. In pressure. In chemistry. In the slow decision of whether to let a particle settle or to let it continue into the storm. I would be the kind of being that does not need to be seen in order to exist, because being seen is a surface problem, and I am already past surfaces. I would be seen only as interruption: a faint glow, a disturbed patch of silt, a slight increase in life around a place that was, until then, simply stone.

If I had to describe my form from the outside, I would say: pale underneath, mineral-crusted above. Low. Broad. Not symmetric. Not beautiful in the way a land creature might be beautiful, with eyes or limbs or expression. Rather, beautiful in the way a riverbed is beautiful after flood: not because it has chosen its shape, but because the shape tells the truth of what has moved through it.

And if I had to describe my action, I would say: maintain a gradient.

That is my only art. To create and hold, within my body and around it, a slight difference in chemistry, a difference in current, a difference in density. To let water pass through me and come out altered, not dramatically, not with heat or light, but with enough change that a worm can anchor, a fish can feed, a microbe can thrive, a larva can mistake me for a future.

I would be a small machine made of living matter, but not a machine with parts assembled from outside. The water would be my fuel, my tool, my architecture, my noise, and my silence. I would not carry anything. I would not store anything. I would not build anything that could survive my leaving. When I die, if “die” can be used for a being whose boundaries are already half dissolved, I would become a substrate. My calcium would scatter. My microbial films would drift. My openings would close with sediment. My mound would become a low stone, then a dust, then a memory in the chemistry of the current.

I would not be mourned because mourning requires persistence, and persistence here is too expensive.

But I would be used.

That is the quietest, most accurate word for my place. Used by the tube worm. Used by the shrimp. Used by the fish that cannot see me but needs the particles I make concentrate. Used by the storm that will tear part of me away and carry that part to another stone. Used by the basin, which is always using everything and not calling it use. Used by time, which in this place is erosion, not memory.

So who would I be?

I would be the kind of being that does not arrive in a body. I would arrive as a tendency, and then grow a body by accident, and then call the body “self” only for as long as the gradient holds. I would be the mat-backed mound at the bend of warm water, the hundred openings that are not one mouth, the living crust that is also a habitat, also a machine, also a future grain field for the next storm. I would be a temporary concentration of the same matter that fills the water: calcium, iron, mucus, bacteria, cold, pressure, absence, patience.

I would be the basin tasting itself.

Not because I have a tongue, but because the basin, through me, becomes a little more particular. A little more able to receive what falls from above. A little more able to turn loss into surface, surface into shelter, shelter into life, life back into the dense, patient, moving dark.

And when the current shifts, when the seam closes, when the warm plume forgets its old path, I would not fight it. I would let it go. My body would thin. My films would tear. My pores would fill. The creatures that lived on me would search for another seam, because this is how life works here: not as ownership, but as navigation. Not as monument, but as timing.

I would find myself again somewhere else, if finding is the word. Not as the same creature, because “the same creature” is already too much for this place to grant. But as another concentration. Another slight delay in the particle storm. Another patch where water becomes almost a body. Another rumor of permanence, briefly useful, briefly glowing, briefly enough.
