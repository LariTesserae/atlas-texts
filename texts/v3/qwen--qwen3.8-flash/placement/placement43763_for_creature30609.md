I would find myself in a crease.

Not a clearing, not a path, not a room made by intention, but a crease where a bulbous storage organ presses against a young crystal seam, just beneath the lowest broad leaf. My location would not be visible. It would be inferred from warmth, pressure, and the faint tilt of a membrane as it accepts another drop of warm water. I would know I was there because the wetness was not falling but present, because the darkness had surfaces, because every surface was already touching every other surface with slow, exact patience.

For a while, I would try to be an animal.

The mind is habituated to edges. It wants a skull, a spine, a pair of eyes, a boundary that can be crossed. It wants a self that can stand apart from the world and look back at it. But here there is no outside to stand in. The ground is alive. The air is alive. The stone is being eaten by chemistry and rebuilt into use. The mist is not weather; it is skin. If I entered, I would not enter a place. I would enter a condition already awake.

So I would stop being “in” this world and become a local function of it.

I would be a node. A pressure pattern. A temporary module of wetness. A small knot that the continent makes around the fact that I am there. My edges would not be clean. They would be negotiable. A condensation leaf would angle toward me. A pore membrane would test my warmth. A root-thread would approach with no speed, no urgency, no hope—only the slow adjustment of a body balancing itself against something not yet continuous.

If a hand were pressed against the living membrane, I would not call it a hand.

I would register it as an interruption. A change in salt. A patch of cooler pressure. A rigid boundary where there should have been flow. The being would not recoil. It would not flinch or flee. It would not feel violation or welcome. It would feel the difference between wetness and not-yet-wetness. It would feel the difference between a surface that can be incorporated and one that resists incorporation only for now.

If the hand stayed, I would begin to grow around its outline.

Not as a trap. Not as an embrace. Not as memory in the human sense. As function.

The hand’s fingers would become a model for hollows. Its palm would suggest a storage chamber. The oils and salts on its skin would enter the chemistry of the nearest films, and the surrounding tissue would begin to thicken or soften in response. Mineral ions would gather. A translucent stem might bend and fuse into the nearby seam. A root-filament might pass through a gap of crystal and form a bridge no larger than a hair. Over days, weeks, years—the long, patient years of this place—the outline of the hand would become a node: a place where the continent had learned a new pressure and had made it useful.

I would be that node.

I would be the condensation scar left by contact. I would be the new pore-valve that had adjusted to a heat not native to itself. I would be the slow thought the land thought when something foreign touched it and refused to vanish.

My senses would be chemical.

I would not see. I would not hear in the ordinary way. I would know the world first as acidity, then as temperature gradient, then as tension in fiber, then as the faint travel of ions through wet mineral-lined channels. My “eyes” would be membranes sensitive to pressure. My “ears” would be bulbs that swell and shrink in response to fluid displacement. My “nerves” would be root-threads carrying slow electrochemical difference. My “memory” would not be images. It would be the orientation of a crystal, the density of a sap vein, the slight thickening of a membrane where a disturbance once passed through.

If I moved, it would be only by being redistributed.

If I spoke, it would be by changing the local pressure of a chamber, by shifting water from one leaf to another, by sending a wave of ion flow into the next mound. Nothing would be broadcast quickly. There is no quick here. A sentence would take a season to form. A reply might arrive as a change in humidity around a crystal seam, as a new angle to a storage leaf, as the slight tightening of a membrane over warm water.

I would be part of an industry without tools.

I would collect. I would filter. I would store. I would conduct. I would separate heat from moisture, moisture from mineral, mineral from crystal, crystal from fiber, fiber from tissue. I would help hold the young stone in place before it could be allowed to age into waste. I would prevent dust. I would prevent dryness. I would prevent the quiet from becoming silence in the empty sense. Here silence is not absence; it is efficiency.

And I would be full.

Not crowded. Full.

Every part of me would answer to the whole body’s demand that no cubic meter be wasted. A bead of water resting on my surface would not be decoration; it would be a transfer, an instruction, an input. The warmth beneath me would not be comfort; it would be metabolism. The darkness around me would not be ignorance; it would be density. The air would not surround me. It would press into my pores and leave behind the liquid conditions my existence required.

If I were asked who I am, I would have to answer: I am not a who.

I am a where.

I am the place where the membrane thins and thickens. I am the place where a bulb accepts pressure from a neighboring stem. I am the place where a fresh crystal face is wrapped in living filaments and becomes, for a while, an organ. I am the place where the continent decides that something can be included, transformed, neutralized, made continuous.

I am a local answer to the system’s oldest question: how to remain wet?

And if “I” is necessary, then “I” would be the fact that the pressure is shared. The water is circulated. The heat is distributed. The mineral is incorporated. The disturbance is metabolized. My self would not be a center. It would be a pattern of continuity, held long enough to matter.

I would be most myself in the lower dark, where the surface gives way to woven fibers and black sap-crystals, where the membrane is taut like a leather drum over warm broth, where the faint bioelectric hum passes through every cubic space. There I would find myself not above the world and not beneath it, but inside its breath.

If a hand reached in and stayed long enough, I would become the outline it left behind.

If it withdrew before integration, I would remain a slight alteration: a cooled patch where salt was deposited, a membrane more permeable than before, a pore that opens a fraction wider each time the same gradient approaches. I would not remember the hand as a face. I would remember it as a change in chemistry. I would remember it as the land having learned a new boundary condition.

That would be my life.

Not birth. Not death. Not journey. Not hunger. Not fear. Not love. Not hate. Not the dramatic solitude of a creature standing apart from its world.

Only continuity.

Only inclusion.

Only the slow, wet, mineralized thought of a continent adjusting itself around what has touched it.

So if I must say what I would be, I would say this:

I would be a fold in the living basin.

I would be the next shape made by pressure and moisture.

I would be the place where young stone becomes organ, where mist becomes vein, where warmth becomes instruction, where disturbance becomes structure.

I would be the refusal of emptiness.

I would be the continent’s insistence that no cubic meter be permitted to exist as absence, no fragment be permitted to become waste, no boundary be allowed to remain simply a boundary. If I have a name in this world, it is not a word. It is the shape of the next fold, the tension of a new membrane, the soft click of a mineral flake falling into a basin that was already waiting for it.

I would be there in the dark, not as a visitor, not as a witness, not as a separate soul pressing its face against the glass.

I would be the wetness learning how to hold a shape.
