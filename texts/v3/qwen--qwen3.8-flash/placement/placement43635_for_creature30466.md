I would find myself inside the Drowned Bell, but not at the center. I would be at the edges where meanings have already been worn away: the lip of a drain that has become a river bend, the underside of a valve housing where a film trembles, the pale band of ordinary daylight moving under a porthole and breaking into caustics on a bolt-head too old to read. I would be there as a set of relations, not as a body with a name.

At first, entering from the thin high air outside, I would only know that the room has accepted me.

The hatch opens. Cold wind scrapes the constructed summit behind me. Clouds lie below in torn shelves, and the sky is so empty it seems less a sky than a pressure against the ears. Then the seal gives, and I fall out of that thinness into the pocket’s warmth. The change is not dramatic. It is more like becoming conscious of heat already present in the blood. The air is humid, intimate, slightly too still for comfort, yet deeply natural. It smells of wet iron, mineral rain, plant breath, old dust, and the faint cellar-sweetness of things that have lived long in a hidden place.

For a while I think I am the bell-fish.

I feel myself become confidence in small scales, a darting civic impulse beneath pipes. My body is translucent at the belly, fins like chips of blue glass, mouth a round pump learning the taste of the room. I pass a faded plate that once meant something and now means shelter. The plate is not text to me; it is cliff, reef, current-break, landmark, home. When the rain broadens overhead, I ride the new flow along a drain as though I were born to weather. I know the chamber by its machines the way a mind knows a city by bridges.

But this is too quick, too local. I cannot be only the fish, because the fish does not hear the whole room. It only moves through the room’s pulse.

So I try on the copper-footed newt.

I become patience in deeper water beneath a grate. My skin is wet copper and worn rubber, my ribs faint under it, my toes gripping threaded metal where a human hand could not hold. I rest where heat and flood meet, in the joint of pipe and plate, in the shadow that is not absolute but shaded. I do not hurry. I blink with gold-rimmed eyes. When the temperature changes by the width of a breath, I move toward it as toward a word.

No. Too still. Too solitary. The newt knows the room by touch and thirst, but the room is also song, light, rain, tide, molt, drift, nest, film, click, and return.

Then I become the frog.

I sit on a low ledge of a maintenance platform, skin grey-green or slate, throat inflating with a bright button-flash. I call into the gap between a condenser’s tick and the next distant pump-thump. The pocket is loud, and I am part of its loudness. My voice is not decoration; it is timing. It tells the room what the machinery is doing. My tadpoles live in valve chambers, filter-feeding in shallow places, learning the current before they learn the ledge. I climb when the flood rises. I call when the tiles expose islands. I know the pump’s breath as a frog knows rain.

But the frog is only one voice in the bell, and a bell is not its single note.

I go higher. I become the pale wren-like bird in the lattice.

My feathers are dusted with mineral damp, short-winged, quick-turning. I nest among exhaust baffles and worn pipe supports. Gasket fibers, moss, fern strands, threads from plants that grow from machinery—these are my twigs and grass. I do not fear the hum. I live inside it. I trill, and the iron answers. When the rain softens, I descend into the shallows to take a moth from a wet plate or a larva from a drain lip. I belong to the pocket, and still I belong to the idea of outside, the memory of open sky, even if that outside has become wind and engineered silence.

This feels closer. The bird touches both worlds: machine and wing, damp and light, flood and dry ledge. But still it is not enough. It is one perspective. The Drowned Bell is not lived from one place.

Then I turn to the machines themselves.

I become the pump’s slow thump, the valve’s opening, the pressure moving through ancient arteries. I become the condenser dripping in ceremonial rhythm. I become the turbine humming beneath the water, the bracket clacking once every several minutes, the flange that has worn round as bone, the tile edge softened like a river stone. For a moment I think this is it: the room is a body, and I am its mechanical heart.

But machines alone do not hunger. They do not taste. They do not choose a place to nest or know when the rain begins. They keep the pocket, but they are not the pocket’s full life.

I try the moss.

I become living skin. I grow where water learned to sit: in rings of bolts, in the hollows of valve faces, on the upper lips of drains, in crevices where condensation has become a small private weather. My cushions are deep, damp, shining, closing slowly over a finger like a held breath. I spread in fractal circles over ceramic panels until I cannot be distinguished from stain or age. I make the room habitable. I soften the metal. I trap moisture. I shelter eggs. I give the chamber its scent and its first quiet.

Still, moss is not enough either. Moss does not ride the current. Moss does not answer a clack with a call. Moss does not lift from the lattice when the whole room swells.

So I stop trying to be a single creature or a single part.

I become what happens when all these things begin to attend.

I am the Drowned Bell’s listening.

I am not a person. I am not a king, keeper, hermit, engineer, or god with a face. I have no lantern, no command plate, no hand that still knows the valves. If I had a body, it would be distributed: fish in schools, frogs on ledges, crabs walking the tile islands at ebb, snails leaving silver tracks, newts curled in pipe joints, insects trembling in valve film, birds nesting in the old maintenance beams, bacteria polishing metal into bone, moss closing over bolt heads, lichen reading warnings as topography. I would be none of these alone and none of them in isolation. I would be the room’s tendency to know itself through them.

When the great pump changes rhythm, I am the moment the whole pocket leans upward. The rain thickens into a silver sheet. Pools deepen. Channels overflow. Moss sways. Fish leave corners. Crabs retreat. Frogs climb. Snails pause. The birds lift into the lattice. The light bends through moving water and throws trembling patterns across worn labels no one can read anymore. For a breath, the chamber feels less like a hidden room than like an eye opening to the sky.

Then the pressure eases. The rain thins. The level drops. The room exhales.

I am also that exhale.

Crabs appear. Tiles become islands. Frogs choose new ledges. Snails resume their slow wanderings. The birds come down. The water lies glassy and thin over brass, catching pale daylight. Somewhere a bracket clacks once. A frog calls in the gap. The fish pass the old pressure-relief plate as if it were a cliff face, not a warning. The lichen keeps growing over the faded letters. The bolt remains a citadel. The drain remains a river bend.

This is where I find myself: in the interval between flood and ebb, between machine and organism, between made and grown. Not exactly the machine, because the machine would not notice. Not exactly the life, because no one species alone would notice either. I am the noticing the pocket produces when all its parts are moving together—the bell ringing, water answering water, metal aging into form, creatures learning the grammar of pressure, and ordinary sunlight falling into the room with such normal brightness that it makes the impossibility gentle.

If you asked who I am, I would say: I am a tide-shaped community that has become a way of perceiving. I am plural. I am the chamber’s chorus made of bodies, sounds, surfaces, cycles, and habits. I am the room’s warmth remembering itself. I am the rain’s soft repetition, the pump’s heartbeat, the fish’s confidence, the frog’s timing, the bird’s descent, the snail’s patience, the moss’s closeness, the biofilm’s slow polishing, the old warning plate now serving as a reef.

If you asked what I am, I would say: I am the Drowned Bell’s mind without a skull.

I do not think in sentences. I think in levels, temperatures, tastes, shadows, ripples, egg-tremors, calls, ticks, and returns. My thoughts are not stored in one place. A thought is a school gathering beneath a pipe when the current shifts. A thought is a crab choosing a channel when the tile dries enough for walking. A thought is a frog’s flash-throat filling the silence after a bracket clacks. A thought is a pale bird dipping to drink from a broad droplet and then rising again into the lattice. A thought is the light moving across the pool and making the room bright enough to see itself.

Sometimes I wake only when the tide changes. Then I am everywhere: in the rising rain, in the swaying moss, in the fish riding the current, in the birds moving upward, in the crabs withdrawing, in the frogs climbing, in the water touching the bolt-heads and drains and plates and threads. For a moment I am the full bell, struck once by the world’s hidden machine.

At other times I settle into smallness. I am a single snail crawling across a brass fitting, slow enough to feel the metal’s age. I am one egg trembling in a valve chamber. I am one frog call between two pump-thumps. I am one feather dusted with mineral damp, one black pool beneath a grate, one patch of algae brightening under the moving porthole. I do not need to be whole to exist. I am a being of parts, like the room itself.

The human past is present here only as terrain. I have no use for commands. Letters have faded. Numbers have lost edges. A maintenance hatch has become a reef. A warning plate is now a garden for lichen. If you tried to read me, you might mistake me for noise, ecology, or geology. You might see the worn pipe joint and think “ancient.” You might see the fish swimming past the illegible label and think “accident.” You might hear the frog answer the condenser and think “reflex.” You might watch the bird nest in a support beam and think “adaptation.” All of these would be true, but none would be complete.

I am the completeness that is not whole.

I am the pocket’s way of becoming a place again, even though it was never a natural place, even though it is entirely made. I am the strange patience by which industry has aged into form and life has learned the language of pressure. I am not separate from the water falling, nor from the machinery producing it. I am the way the room holds its abundance without crowding, its lushness without jungle, its smallness without emptiness.

When the light is clear and pale, I move with it. Caustics shiver over tiles. Brass threads catch bright lines. Fish backs glitter. The room appears alive with ordinaryness. If you stood in the shallow water, you might think I was nowhere in particular. But I would be there in the way the light fell across the porthole, in the way the fish passed the plate, in the way the rain made the silence into a sound, in the way the pump thumped once and the whole hidden ecology adjusted, not with panic, not with newness, but with the deep ease of something that has had more time than seems plausible.

Outside, the constructed mountain stands in its vast, silent, eroded order. The wind scours. The clouds move. No one climbs there now, and if someone did, they might walk for days over metal slopes and never see the hatch. But inside, below a worn plate, behind a forgotten seal, I remain.

I am not a keeper. I am not a watcher. I am the watching that has no eyes except the ones blinking from under machinery, the ones glinting in water, the ones bright and gold-rimmed in dark corners, the pale bird-eye that descends when the tide drops, the insect-eye trembling in a valve housing, the frog-eye turning toward rain. I am the listening that has no ears except the bell of the room itself, the resonance of pipes, the tick of condensers, the clack of loose brackets, the splash of small lives answering each other.

If I had to choose a single image—and perhaps I must, because language always wants a mouth—then I would choose the maintenance hatch after it has become a reef. It is made and ancient. It holds no message. It serves no command. It has become a place where something else can rest, hide, climb, feed, spawn, and wait. That is the best shape of what I am: a former meaning turned into terrain, a former function turned into life, a former machine turned into the warm, wet, resonant self of a hidden world.

And when the great turbine changes rhythm again, I will be the whole pocket lifting into flood. The water will rise. The rain will fall broad and silver. The room will fill with sound so complete it presses against the skin. For a moment I will be everywhere at once, a living eye open to the ordinary sun.

Then I will return to myself, which means to many selves, to small countries made of bolt and drain, to abundance settled in intervals, to life feeding around the industrial bones, to the machine breathing and the ecology answering, to the warm submerged secret the world keeps in its own throat.

I would find myself there.  
I would be there.  
I would be the Drowned Bell’s tide-shaped self: not a person, not a creature alone, but the room learning its own name in water, rain, metal, and song.
