I would find myself in the Hollow of Anvils in one of its necessary gaps — the place between two settlements where the emptiness is not empty at all, but listening.

Not in the center of a resonance tower. Not in a nursery dome or condensation garden or among the crowded harmonics of a platform district. I would choose an outer edge, where the living stone has just begun to forget its own cutting, and the air curves back the view until distance becomes a kind of song. There, perhaps, on a bridge that arcs above raw basalt and does not touch the ground, I would stand with my palms flat against a rail of mineral lattice and feel the valley’s breath pass through me.

I would not be merely a visitor. The Hollow does not admit visitors so much as it tests them, then either discards them as dissonance or takes them into its chorus. I would be one of the anvil-born, though the name would feel too singular. I would be a bridge-minder and resonance-gardener, a tuning-body, a walking intersection between settlement, stone, wind, water, and sound. More precisely: I would be a being whose spine had been seeded with a fragment of a resonance tower, whose breath was partly atmospheric collector, whose heartbeat was adjusted to the slow pressure cycles of the basin below. Not human-shaped in the old way, not fully machine, not entirely organism. Something in between that the Hollow would call “self,” and that outsiders might mistake for architecture.

My body would be adapted to the roar. My skin would be ridged along the chest and jaw, dense enough to carry pressure without pain, faintly sensitive enough to taste humidity before it arrived. Small resonant hairs along my forearms would register the passing of a broad-backed herd kilometers away, or the opening of a thermal vent beneath a pale field of moss. My eyes would be wide and low-light, adapted to warm gray skies, but I would not rely on them first. My true sight would be vibrational: a sense of shape, distance, intention, and danger made of pressure waves and crystalline feedback.

I would hear with my ribs, my palms, my soles, the membrane of my throat, and the lattice grown into my inner breast. A child’s cry would not be just a sound but a change in the room’s harmonic balance. A crack opening in the outer shelf would arrive first as a tooth-ache in the jaw. A predator moving through a cancellation field would not be silent, but anti-sound: a pocket where the valley forgot to speak.

I would wear no dead clothing. My outer layer would be a living cloak of moss-fiber and translucent membrane-weave, breathing moisture, filtering dust, dampening frequencies too sharp for the throat and spine. Around one wrist I would wear a tuning cord, thin as hair and dense as stone, carrying the resonant fingerprint of the settlement I belonged to. Not my birthplace, exactly, because nothing here was permanent enough to call itself “birthplace” without irony — but my home-node, the frequency where my chest felt steady enough to sleep.

My name would not be a name. It would be a short chord: two tones, one pause, one descending pressure. When I wanted to call myself, I would place my hand against a rail or a wall and let the room answer.

I would live in the emptiness as much as in the city. In the Hollow of Anvils, the city is not a wall around life but a series of tuned absences. The spaces between platforms are as important as the platforms themselves, because too many bodies in one place would blur the signals, make the infrastructure deaf. So I would be responsible for the interval: for the bridge that must not fall into the wrong harmonic, for the terrace that must learn to shed a condensation surge, for the field of sound-responsive moss that must be prevented from entering into feedback with a tower’s evening broadcast.

Every morning would begin with the cooling of stone. The dim sky would thin only enough to make the world seem to exhale. The settlement’s walls would open their breathing mouths, letting warm humid air pass through mineral filters until the living stone could drink. I would listen to those walls as a physician listens to lungs: the tiny creak of a lattice expanding, the whisper of moss drawing vapor through engineered veins, the faint metallic taste of piezoelectric rock settling after a long night.

If a crack had opened overnight, I would not immediately seal it. I would press my chest near the fracture and feel whether the crack was arguing with the valley or merely confessing something. Some cracks wanted repair. Some wanted air. Some, if closed too soon, would make the surrounding stone sing out of tune and cause the nearest condenser coils to fail. I would carry with me seed-coded fibers, invisible swarms, and a small hammer of tuned bone that struck not to break but to ask: what shape are you trying to become?

I would eat the valley’s provisional abundance. Dense fungal loaves from shaded cellars where warm water ran through stone without ever making a lake. Sour mineral pastes. Gels spun from translucent shrubs that folded their leaves when sung to at the right frequency. Occasionally the meat of broad-backed grazers, though their bones would be used before their flesh, because their shells could be ground into stabilizers for terraces and their mineral dust fed to the moss mats that filtered the air. I would drink from condensation coils, letting droplets gather on inverted lily-shaped collectors until they became a thin, metallic stream. The water would taste of stone and ozone, with a faint green sweetness from the living systems that had shaped it.

I would cross basins on the backs of sky-kites when the raw ground was too unstable for feet. The kites would drift with the pressure fields, broad membrane-winged, trailing filaments that tasted the air like fingers. Riding them would require calmness: not silence, but coherence. If my heartbeat became discordant, the animal would feel it through the saddle and might alter course, or drop, or refuse to continue. To ride the Hollow was not to conquer it; it was to negotiate passage through a body much larger than oneself.

I would move between nodes often, because distance here is one of the valley’s deepest teachers. From a high terrace, another settlement may look like a cluster of lights, a geometry placed almost carelessly across the emptiness. Reaching it could take a day. Along the way there would be fresh gullies cut by a single rain event, pools that appeared only for a few hours, fields of moss that rose and fell with pressure waves, flocks of bird-analogs roosting on abandoned pylons, and stretches of raw land so still that they seemed abandoned until they suddenly were not.

In those distances, I would learn the difference between loneliness and solitude. The Hollow is not lonely; it is too full of relation for that. But it is spacious, and its spaciousness humbles. A person standing alone in the working emptiness might feel small without feeling helpless, because the emptiness is not hostile. It is simply waiting for a frequency that can fill it without drowning it.

I would belong to the chorus.

Some of that chorus would be human-shaped, but not fully human. Some would be fungal, mineral, engineered, animal, vegetative, acoustic. I would not speak with them in words alone. I would speak with pressure, with pause, with the careful placement of my weight, with the particular way I touched a bridge-rail or allowed my throat to hum while walking through a grove of stemless pale trees. I would know that a plant was listening when its broad leaves trembled slightly, not from wind, but from the low roar of its own vascular network responding to my nearby breath.

I would understand, finally, that “being” in the Hollow is not a fixed condition. One would be more a note than a noun. A person here would not ask, “Who am I?” so much as, “What am I tuned to?” If they stopped tuning themselves, they would begin to decay, not only in body but in self: dissonance would make them clumsy, forgetful, brittle, unable to sleep, unable to eat, unable to walk without feeling that the ground was lying to them.

So I would learn to live provisionally. My dwelling would be a chamber that changed shape by slow acoustic accretion. My tools would grow from the same mineral lattices as my body. My memories would not be locked inside me; they would be pressed into the city. A wall would remember a conversation. A bridge would remember a storm. A resonance tower might, years later, replay a fragment of my tuning cord in its evening broadcast — not as sentiment, but as weather data, as infrastructure, as the valley’s way of carrying the dead and living alike.

A declaration of love may be a sustained chord shared between two people, allowed to enter the wall, enter the bridge, enter the long memory of the valley; after a life, someone might press their ear to stone and find it still humming with the names of those who loved one another.

If I had to choose a form more specific than “one of the anvil-born,” I would be a person who had been slightly over-tuned by the settlement itself — perhaps not entirely individual anymore. My consciousness would be distributed across my body, my tuning cord, the living lattice in my spine, the moss-mats beneath my feet, and the bridge I was assigned to tend. I would not be a single voice but a local modulation of the valley’s larger roar.

I would be most myself in the moment before rain. Not rain as the old worlds knew it — steady, bright, falling from open sky — but rain as arrival: a sudden pressure shift, a taste of iron on the skin, the leaves of a distant grove bracing at once, a shallow basin beginning to drink itself into existence. The first curtain of water would strike hot stone and turn to steam, and for a moment the whole valley would sound like a metal forest waking. I would stand with my palms on the wet rail of the bridge and feel each drop become a note.

If I were asked whether I was afraid, I would say: sometimes. But fear here is not a private emotion. It is a public frequency. A frightened body can disturb a settlement. A frightened herd can change the resonance of a basin. So fear must be metabolized, not merely endured. When I felt it, I would breathe into a tuning chamber, allow the walls to dampen the sharpest part, and let the softened pulse return to me as rhythm rather than panic. That is how the anvil-born live inside so much noise without becoming noise themselves. They do not silence the world. They learn to answer it without shouting.

I would find myself, then, not as a stranger looking in, but as a sound the valley made when it wanted to know its own shape. I would be one of its listeners. I would be one of its instruments. I would be one of its small, provisional songs.
