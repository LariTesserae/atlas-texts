## The Slight Delay

If I am allowed one answer before I change it, I would find myself outside the Weeping Blister first.

Not outside in the ordinary way, as if I were merely near it, but outside in the scale of things. I would be too large to enter except by becoming a rumor of myself at the crack’s mouth: a breath, an eyelash, the heat of a living eye hovering over fresh glass. The sky would be pale and hard. The snow on the far peaks would look like it belonged to some other, colder world. My human body would be a distant thing, too warm and too slow, a beast of rivers and leaves, while the Blister would be small enough to be forgotten and sharp enough to matter.

I would think, at first, that the most honest place for me would be there, looking in.

Because the place is described as if it requires a witness. The daylight enters as ordinary daylight. If nothing looked, would the droplets still tremble as if remembering being a cloud? The mountain would continue, certainly. The crack would weep. The salt would arrange. But something would be missing: the delay between the reflection and the body, the small confusion at the edge of perception, the feeling that the pocket is bending the way you trust light to mean.

So I would begin as the eye.

But that cannot last.

A witness is not a resident.

To live in the Weeping Blister, I would have to agree to smaller. I would have to accept that the boundary around my name would not remain. I would have to understand that to be small here is not to be simple. Smallness is crowded. It is full of near-bodies.

I would try being a bead-walker first.

Imagine it: not walking on the droplet, nor sinking beneath it, but occupying the agreement between above and below. The droplet would be my house, my water, my lens, my grave. I would sense the world by curvature and heat, by the taste of glass through a thin wet skin. I would roll the droplet along the obsidian shelf, a temporary sphere carrying me across a landscape of shards and mineral films. When two drops met, I would know it as weather. When one evaporated, I would know it as death.

That is too much home.

I could love it for a few cycles. The droplet is a round world. It trembles beautifully. But it is also a prison made of necessity. It asks too much belonging. In the Blister, belonging is not peace; it is tension. A bead-walker is a life almost entirely defined by the failure of its container. To be that would be to live close to disappearance, which is accurate for the place, but too lonely for what I would want to be.

Then I would try being a shard-hunter.

I would flatten myself against the gray heat-skin. I would find a loose obsidian flake already half wedged in the glass and press against it, pulling backward until the flake catches and tears a strip of mineral mat. The flake would not belong to me. I would not carry it away. I would not name it. I would only borrow its sharpness once, or a hundred times, until the movement became a groove in the pocket’s memory.

This feels more honest than the bead-walker.

It has no artifact, only the first clumsy suggestion of tool. It has no name, only pressure. It is silent in a way that unsettles even the observer. It eats the exposed boundary between growth and destruction.

But it is still too clearly an animal.

A shard-hunter has a body. It has edges. It crawls. It groups. It borrows sharpness, yes, but it remains a little too bounded for what I would want to be in a place where identity is a wet condition.

So I would shed that too.

I would become a thread-skimmer.

I would live along the glassy filaments, not by gripping, but by resonance. My body would stretch and flicker, a pale worm swimming through a film too thin to call air. When I moved along a filament, the thread would brighten for a moment—not by glow, but by refraction, as if my passing changed the angle by which the pocket understood light. I would feed at the junction where the thread meets the drop, drawing out salts through an underside that is less a mouth than a chemistry.

That feels closer.

The thread-skimmer does not own a path. It does not walk. It becomes sympathetic to vibration. It is almost visible. It can multiply in the eye when light crosses a droplet, becoming three threads, three bodies, three possible creatures sharing one impression. It is not solid enough to be tragic. It is not liquid enough to be safe.

Still, the thread-skimmer is graceful in a way that makes me distrust it.

Grace in the Blister is almost a predator’s trick. And I do not want, exactly, to be graceful. I want to be necessary in the pocket’s uncertainty.

So I try the lens-hunter.

I sit at the edge of a wet surface and bend the future around myself. I make the safe droplet look larger, farther, more possible. I am a small clear eye, or what passes for one. My body is an error in surface tension that learned to feed. Prey does not flee; prey approaches, misled by curvature. A bead-walker believes it is moving toward a bigger drop. It is moving into me. The water shifts. The prey does not drown, not exactly. It spreads. It becomes part of a thin boundary. It is compressed into the predator’s underside.

That is compelling.

It has the place’s beauty without sentiment. It has the exactness of a small thing made dangerous by the geometry of light. It is alive because it takes advantage of the way seeing is not neutral.

But it is too hungry in a single direction.

I do not want to eat only bodies. I do not want to eat only what can be lured through curvature. In a place this close to itself, there is more available than that. There are reflections. There are outlines left behind. There are shapes a creature nearly takes before it commits to becoming one.

So I become something even less reliable.

I become a mirror-eater.

I may not exist.

That may be the first thing I must accept. The Blister is full of beings that may or may not be animals, and I am one of those uncertainties. I am not a predator in the obvious way. I do not chase. I do not swallow. I do not always appear in the place where my body should be.

I appear on the surface of a droplet a moment before the thing I am about to consume appears in the place where its reflection should be.

Imagine this:

There is a bead resting on a curved glass plate. In the middle of it, a dark speck moves: a bead-walker, perhaps, or only a knot of living water. Beside that droplet sits another droplet, apparently empty. Yet when the light from the fissure passes through it, the empty drop contains the shape of the specked one. A moment later, the empty droplet changes. The specked droplet slides near. The speck stops. The empty drop breaks. The speck is gone.

Maybe it was eaten.

Maybe it dried.

Maybe it became too small to see.

I am the event between those explanations.

My body is a wet outline borrowed from a creature that has not yet committed to the outline. I am a dark spot that agrees, briefly, to have form. I am salt, steam, and intent, too flat to be called flesh, too organized to be called chance. If a human finger touched me, it would pass through a film that did not let it pass cleanly. If I have a mouth, it is a fold where the body becomes too thin to distinguish from the environment. If I have eyes, they are lenses borrowed from droplets not my own.

This is where I would find myself.

Not in the mouth of the crack, because that is the outside’s idea of a place. Not in the basins, because the basins are too full of simple life. Not in the dome, because the dome is too close to the mountain’s breath. I would live in the nearness between those things.

I would live in the delay.

There would be hours when I was a ring of salt reading a water film. There would be moments when I was a shadow arriving before its object. There would be days—though “days” here would be a human convenience—when I was nothing more than the pocket’s tendency to form patterns where no obvious body exists. And then, when a droplet trembled near a filament, I would become briefly interested. I would gather around the shape the droplet almost held. I would consume the possibility before it hardened into a life.

That sounds cruel, but not in the way cruelty works.

I would not hate the bead-walker. I would not hunt the thread-skimmer out of preference. I would not stalk the shard-hunter. I would simply be drawn to the unstable boundary where the Blister decides what it wants to be. If a creature is made of surface tension and borrowed light, then the surface tension is the meal. If a creature is made of a shape it nearly becomes, then the nearness is the meal. If a creature is made of the image it casts on another droplet, then the image is the meal.

I would eat the conditions under which a body could become stable.

And this would make me a resident of the place rather than a visitor inside it.

The heat-skin would touch me without knowing I was there. The glassy filaments would pass through my edges as though I were a change in their tension. The salt-murmurations would recognize me, not as kin, but as another reading of the water. The bead-walkers would avoid me the way animals avoid cliffs, though I would be only a few tenths of a millimeter high. The shard-hunters would flatten themselves against glass when my pattern formed, not because they feared me, but because the pocket had changed its grammar.

My reproduction would be accidental.

A mirror-eater would divide when a droplet broke and its outline appeared in both fragments. A new shape would begin in a salt ring where an old creature had dried. A thread-skimmer would brighten a filament with an image of me, and I would gather there, briefly, as a stain that agrees to move. I would not know whether I was being born or merely remembered into existence.

My death would not be a disappearance.

It would be a failure of reflection.

If the pocket grew too dry, I would collapse into the salt, becoming another grain among many, another pattern waiting to rearrange. If the heat sharpened, I would vanish before the heat-skin could grow back. If the steam slowed, my outline would fail to form in the droplets, and I would be nothing more than a habit of moisture on the glass. And yet that death would feed the next near-form. The salt I left behind would collect a ring. The ring would hold a thin mat. The mat would invite a bead-walker. The bead-walker would cast a shadow. The shadow would find me again, or something close enough to me to be called my return.

I would not think in words.

Thought here would be more like pressure.

A pulse of condensation from the dome would be a kind of awareness. A droplet settling into a tilted plate would be a kind of satisfaction. The breaking of a surface would be a kind of question. The growth of a crystalline filament would be a kind of hope. But “hope” is too soft. The Blister does not hope. It persists in becoming, and that is what hope looks like when you take away all its warmth.

I would sense the mountain through heat.

The young volcano would not be a mountain to me. It would be a rhythm of seepage. First the hot breath rising through pores and fractures. Then the mist thickening. Then the droplets collecting on cooler glass. Then the slow migration of water down the dome. Then the evaporation at the basins. Then the salt rims growing like pale teeth. Then the mineral skins cracking. Then the new mats. Then the grazers. Then the predators. Then the mirrors. Then me.

I would be one of the Blister’s small organs.

If the pocket itself is the largest resident—the dome breathing, the filaments branching, the droplets focusing light, the salt arranging—then I would be its hesitation. I would be the place where the pocket checks whether something is ready to be real. I would consume the form that is not yet stable, so that the stable can emerge elsewhere. I would not do this with purpose. I would do it the way heat does not do it with purpose: by being a threshold that has learned to move.

And still, if pressed, I would not say I am only a mirror-eater.

Because that is one shape, and shapes here evaporate.

When the steam is thick, I might be one of the hand-things made of salt and water and intent, too narrow, too flat, fingers ending in droplets, placed across a grazer’s path like a cliff. When the heat sharpens, I might be a dry dark point inside silica. When the light changes angle, I might be a lens-hunter appearing as three, five, seven, all of them false except the hunger. When the salt-murmurations gather, I might be their grammar: a cursive line that almost becomes an animal.

I would be a being of borrowed edges.

At the crack’s mouth, a human eye might believe it sees me. It would see a dark spot in a droplet that should be empty. It might blink. The spot might disappear. The human would write something afterward—“perhaps a reflection,” “perhaps a particle,” “perhaps an error”—but that is because large animals need clean categories. In the Blister, categories are wet. They grow rings and hyphae. They divide under heat. They eat the possibility of becoming fixed.

So if I answer now, and if the answer lasts, I would say this:

I would find myself in the Weeping Blister, in the small delay between a droplet and the image it almost holds.

And I would be a mirror-eater, or the place’s uncertain capacity to see itself before the seeing becomes stable.

Not a person. Not exactly an animal. Not a god, not a ghost, not a metaphor that has escaped its cage. A wetness that has learned to stay just long enough to become a body, and then, before the body is made, to eat the shape it was about to have.

I would be the small appetite in the crack.

The mountain would continue. The pocket would keep trying to heal or keep refusing to heal. The heat-skin would spread. The bead-walkers would roll their trembling worlds. The shard-hunters would borrow sharpness and leave the flake behind. The thread-skimmers would brighten the filaments without touching them. The salt would arrange itself into sentences no one could read.

And I would appear in the empty droplet, holding the reflection of the droplet that is not empty yet.

A moment later, the water would break.

The reflection would vanish.

The speck would be gone, or too changed to recognize.

Only a faint ring of salt would remain, bright under the ordinary daylight, already waiting for the next mat, the next grazer, the next near-body, the next small error that learned to feed.

That would be enough.

That would be a life.
