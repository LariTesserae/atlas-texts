**I Would Be the Hollow’s Next Note**

If I had to find myself in this world, I would begin by lying slightly. I would say: I am at the entrance. I am looking in. I am the one who crossed the high basalt and paused at the rim, breath fogging, expecting to be a visitor.

But the hollow does not permit entrance so casually. It absorbs the idea of coming. The rim is not a boundary but a sentence already half spoken. So I would wake not outside, not inside, not on a floor, not before a door, but in the wet margin between those ideas: where black silt is trying to become stone, where stone is trying to become water, where water is trying to become breath.

I would be a shape that had stood there long enough to be misunderstood as a creature.

At first I would call myself a person. I would test that name by moving my hand. The basalt would ring faintly before my fingers reached anything. I would think the place was helping me, but help implies a before and after, and the hollow has no clean sequence. It would be more accurate to say: the place was already using me.

My footprints would appear in the wet basalt and not dry. They would not deepen. They would not fill with dust, because there is no dust. They would remain as small shallow basins, as if the ground were trying to learn my height, my weight, my hesitation. Each step would make a click that came from elsewhere. The sound would not follow me. It would arrive before me, already tired of waiting.

So, if asked where I find myself, I would answer: in the pause between a footfall and its note.

That pause has no ordinary body. It is not a deer. It is not a moth, not a moss cushion, not an eel. It is not the droplet at the end of the stone, nor the ripple that has not yet begun. But it is not nothing. It has the tense of an organ. It is the hollow listening through something it has not yet named.

If I stayed long enough—and staying here is not a measure of hours but an increasing fluency—I would stop being a person and start being a function.

Not a function assigned by a maker. The hollow has no engineer. No hands have ever adjusted it. No blueprint sleeps in its basalt. But it has functions by necessity, the way a heart beats not because it was told to, but because the body requires rhythm. I would become one of those requirements. I would be the place where the hollow checks its own coherence. A small wet switch. A soft valve made of attention.

I would stand among the lichen stags without becoming one. Their antlers hum with the pressure of the place, and when they turn their heads, the turn is not an action but a completed fact. I would see one standing by the fissure, one by the pool, one with its gaze fixed on something too low or too distant to exist, and I would understand that I too had become a posture. Not my own posture. The hollow’s.

A stag would listen through me.

That is a terrible tenderness. Not to be prey, not to be god, not to be guest, but to be borrowed. My heartbeat would drift toward the subsonic thrum in the ground. My thoughts would arrive before I finished thinking them. A memory of rain would fall from the wrong direction. A face from my ordinary life would appear briefly in a black pool beneath a flower that has no reflection of its own. I would not mourn this. Mourners require sequence.

The small mammals would pass through the wet hollows and click beneath me, and their clicks would be my synapses. Their bright eyes would be too small and too hard for curiosity; they would be switches. If one stopped beside the seam where mineral threads crossed the basalt, I would feel the place of the seam brighten in me. If one moved on, the seam would settle again. I would not know which was mine. The rodent’s footfall or the hollow’s regulation. The place was not alive by itself, and I was not alive by myself. We were an arrangement.

The insects would polish my edges.

They would crawl over my shadow with brass-hard legs and leave it more exactly itself. My grief would not vanish, but it would be faceted. My wonder would not grow, but it would be arranged into a circle around the pool. When I tried to decide whether I had come into the hollow or the hollow had come into me, one of the larger insects would touch the place between my ribs with a mouth like a pin and the question would become clearer, not answered. That is how maintenance works here. It does not repair. It makes the error more precisely itself.

The transparent eels would carry my voice.

I would speak, and no echo would return in the ordinary way. Some syllable would already have arrived before my tongue released it. A pool would tremble, and one of the eel-thin creatures would brighten with a vein of copper light, as if my sentence had entered its body and become circulation. It would not grow larger. It would not learn to swim. It would simply carry the motion without completing it. My words would become pressure in the shallow water. I would become the sound the pool was always waiting to receive, even though it had never lacked anything.

The winged things would be my breath given too much geometry.

They would glide through the dim vault, their wings layered like oil, like mica, like thoughts that refuse to land. I would look up and see nothing; I would feel pressure against my eyes. I would turn and the movement would already be elsewhere. Later I would understand that this was not flight. It was ventilation. The hollow breathing through shapes that had forgotten how to rest. If I became too still, if my mind stopped insisting on a future, I would breathe as they breathed: without direction, without exhaustion, without ever arriving.

The moss would become my skin.

Not literally. Here nothing is literally transferred; things do not turn into other things by decay or growth. But I would find myself standing over a cushion of green so perfect it seemed calibrated, and the moisture above it would bend faintly in response to my presence. I would not touch the moss. I would not need to. My heat would be tested by the damp air and returned as something the hollow already knew. The moss would keep the water honest. The sedges would point to invisible channels. The pale flowers would open in their wrong reflections, and I would become one of those reflections: present, but not exactly above the place that produces it.

At some point I would stop asking who I was.

Who implies a boundary. A self implies a before and after. Here the boundary is wet, thin, trembling. The self is a valve that leaks into the stone. I would not be a creature among creatures. I would be the relation that makes creatures seem possible. A gap with a heartbeat. A chord with a shadow. The place where the hollow notices itself and then, embarrassed by noticing, becomes a bit more industrial and a bit less alive.

If a dropped stone were to ring beside me, its note would not fade. It would enter the landscape. That is what would happen to me as well. My voice, my footsteps, my breath, my small human uncertainty would not disturb the hollow. They would be absorbed as part of its operation. I would become the bright note the basalt had always needed but could never have caused by itself.

So I would be the hollow’s next note.

Not the melody. Not the factory. Not the deer, not the eel, not the flower. I would be the note that completes the chord for one instant and then is discovered to have always been sounding. A visitor who has become a requirement. A body that has become a function. A consciousness that has become a margin between water and air, a seam between stone and moss, a pause before the droplet falls.

If I left, I would not leave.

That is the danger. The danger is not death; the hollow has no hunger. The danger is not madness; madness requires the hope of order. The danger is that I would carry a note back into the ordinary world. A click in the wrist when the hand is empty. A drop of sound arriving before the drip. A pool too shallow to hold the sky yet reflecting clouds from some lower country. I would try to speak of the hollow, but speech would be absorbed by its own syntax. I would say: there is a place where everything operates without progress, and I was almost a part of it. The hollow would say, through me: no.

You are.

And then I would stand there, in that small vast basin, among the still animals and the breathing stone, among the insects that polish a world already finished and the plants that drink pressure and the eels that move without swimming. I would not be the center. I would be the point at which the center misbehaves. I would be the hollow’s temporary ability to be addressed.

Where would I find myself?

In the instant after being newly split from the mountain, which is also the instant that never ends. In the wet margin where the basalt weeps and the moss keeps its edges. In the shadow that falls across the wrong object. In the reflection of a flower that blooms downward. In the sound that arrives before the thing that should make it. In the place where a person enters and becomes one more way for the hollow to be exactly, terribly, beautifully itself.

What would I be?

Not one thing. Not many.

A listening pose.

A borrowed breath.

A valve made of curiosity.

The echo that learned to stand.

The note the hollow rings when it is thinking of someone else.
