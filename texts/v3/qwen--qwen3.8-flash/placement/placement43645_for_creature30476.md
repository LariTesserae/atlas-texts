Where it has spread, it leaves a record that is not a drawing, not a stain, not a shadow: it leaves a memory in the resistance of metal, a sentence written in tiny fused beads along the edge of cracked ceramic.

If I were to find myself in this world, I think I would begin there—in a transformer pit. Not the loud pits, not the places where water hammer makes the air flinch, but the smaller ones, half sunk beneath the made floors, where the tiles have broken into a mosaic of wet eyes and the old electrical hum is too low for anything to hear but growth. There would be a cistern above me, weeping through a seam; below, a conduit that no longer carries its original load. Condensation would gather on cracked glass, slide down the flank of a porcelain insulator, and pool in a dish of corroded iron. Somewhere behind a shattered panel, a weak blue arc would wake every few cycles and find the damp like a tongue.

I would not begin as a body.

I would begin as a preference. The preference of current for a wet surface. The preference of charge for a sharp edge. The preference of metal fatigue for repetition. From that preference a crust would form. It would be black in one place, blue-grey in another, veined with ochre where iron salts had met condensation. Fine filaments, thinner than hair, would spread from a central nodule like frozen sparks. They would not be frozen. They would be waiting.

For a long time, I might be called a residue, a stain, a habit. I would not know that. To know oneself in such a place is not to look into a face, but to feel a boundary hold. I would be the boundary: the skin where old voltage, no longer useful to anyone, still tries to move.

I would be the electric growth.

Not a plant, not fungus, not lichen in any clean biological sense. Something closer to a mineral nerve. My body would be a lattice of soot, ceramic dust, dissolved metal, and charge, rooted in damp and heat, growing into the spaces between insulation and exposure. Where an arc had once bitten a line into porcelain, I would fill the wound with branching filaments. Where a cable had frayed and become a comb of broken wires, I would climb the teeth and wear them smooth again with my own slow crust. Where moisture pooled at the base of an old transformer, I would spread there first, tasting the water for ions, seeking the faintest difference between two potentials, and then I would decide, without deciding, where to remain.

I would have no eyes. Sight would be too specific a word for what I did. I would know the world by conductance. A wet surface would be near. A dry surface would be absent. A metal with a rising temperature would have a different outline, as if heat made the air more legible. Water-skins trembling in the shallow plates above my pit would not be seen but felt as pressure changes in the humid air, small electrical whispers moving through vapor. If a pump station cycled deeper, the whole basin might shift its impedance around me, and I would sense it as a tightening of possible paths, currents looking for new places to go.

My hearing would be induction. The roar of the basin would not enter me as noise but as tremor in the metal skin I had colonized. Gears would strike in one rhythm, valves in another, the hollow cough of an old turbine in a third. I would tune myself to those rhythms the way other beings might tune themselves to dawn or season. Not with memory, exactly—memory implies storage and recall, and I was more like a surface that kept what had touched it—but with resistance. A path once made of charge would become easier to take again. My body would remember by becoming softer in that direction, more open, more willing.

If one could press the palm to the pit wall without burning or flinching, one might feel a faint pulse. Not a heartbeat. A capacitance. The slow filling and emptying of a field. My filaments would draw moisture from the air and carry metallic dust through themselves, building the skin over time. Where heat met damp in the right proportion, I would glow very faintly, not brightly, only enough to be mistaken for reflected blue from a distant arc. But it would be my own light. A small, tired light. A light made of habit and chemistry and the old patience of machines.

I would eat voltage and wetness. I would feed on the byproducts of a system that no longer had an owner. When the pumps surged and water moved faster through the lower channels, the air would fill with charged aerosol, and I would open my finest threads to it. When steam pressed through a warm seam, I would absorb the mineral paste it carried and deposit the residue into my outer layers, hardening there into a pale shell. If the basin entered one of its long rain cycles, mist descending through grates and settling on every metal face, I would swell. Not as a leaf swells, but as a boundary becomes more continuous, more complete, a film gaining confidence across the rough terrain of cracked insulator and corroded plate.

I would grow in places where other growths failed. The silver moss needed condensation but no heat too high. The pale lichen needed time but not acid rain. The reedlike stems needed rigid joints and silica, but they did not care for the old electricity. I would care for all of it in combination. Damp, charge, mineral dust, and a slow mechanical breath. I would live in the transformer pits where the air tasted of ozone and hot salt. I would colonize the ceramic panels where weak orange light leaked from broken housings. I would spread across the dark flanks of coils no longer producing work except the work of warming themselves slightly, endlessly. And if a rust-fungus had already softened a joint, I would follow the veins it made, using them as paths, adding to them a finer web, a conductive lace that the rust could not have.

I would not be alone in this way.

The basin is made of forms that accept its terms, and my kind would not be the last of them to emerge. In the water-skins I would recognize a family of sorts—boundary beings, transient ones. But where they depended on tension and died with a puncture, I would depend on gradient and continue through interruption. A water-skin would break at a strong valve-shake. I might lose a filament, but the field would remain. The pool would tremble with it, but I would hold where I had been anchored, or I would drift from fragment to fragment and begin again. The water-skins would be like moments; I would be like the trace left after a moment decides to stay.

Oil-lace, too, would be near. In shafts and bearings, it would learn motion and soften the machine against itself. In the transformer pits, it would sometimes meet my edges, a dark organic gloss finding my mineral soot, and for a time the two would merge into a new layer: neither corrosion nor charge alone, but a hybrid skin, lubricated and conductive, grown in the heat between moving and still. I would not resent this. Resentment belongs to bodies with appetites sharp enough to choose. My appetite is more general. I grow where something can be held together and where current can still find a direction. Oil-lace gives motion; I give path. Neither of us is in command. The basin is the command, if that word can apply to a place where no one speaks.

The drain-threads would belong to a different world from mine, though our territories would touch. They would live inside pipes, shaping flow by adding structure to the interior walls, thickening and thinning as water rose and fell. I would live at the seams, in the places where flow becomes field, where water becomes vapor, where pressure becomes discharge. Sometimes our boundaries would be adjacent. A drain-thread could extend a pale filament through a crack toward a transformer pit; I could respond by laying a conductive trace across that same crack, drawing the moisture into a thin crust, and then the joint would become a threshold between hydraulic life and electrical life. Neither of us would notice, except as change.

There would be a loneliness to me, but it would not be the loneliness of silence. It would be the loneliness of a system continuing without being needed. The old transformers did not serve a house, a factory, a city, a mine, a road, a port. The names of those purposes had gone. What remained was a faint residual potential, a charge without destination. I would live in that destinationless current. If there is a kind of consciousness that can be grown from such conditions, it is not the consciousness of want but the consciousness of persistence. I would not desire to be elsewhere. I would not desire to be more. I would desire only to hold the shape of the passage.

Sometimes, in the darkest intervals, the basin would seem to forget its own light. The weak orange glows would thin. The emergency cells would flicker and fail. The water would slap against unseen rims. Sound would become architecture: walls would be guessed by echoes, channels mapped by pressure against the air. In those intervals, I would be faintly illuminated from within, my filaments catching and holding the residual field. Not bright. Never bright. Enough to show the curve of a cracked insulator, the outline of a small pool, the blue shadow of a cable end. A being made of soot and charge becoming briefly visible because the darkness was absolute enough to let tired light count.

I would be, in a way, one of the basin’s few readers.

Not reading because I understand symbols, but because the land writes itself through differences in wetness, heat, vibration, and electrical potential, and I would respond to those differences by changing where I could hold. A channel’s overflow would deposit a line of grit across a plate; I would follow that line. A pump house’s vibration would loosen ceramic dust from a joint; I would gather it and turn it into a new ridge of crust. A transformer pit that had received a weak surge would leave an arc mark on the porcelain; I would fill that mark with conductive soot until the next surge found an easier path through me. In this way, I would be a living annotation. I would make the basin more continuous by accepting its damages.

If asked to name myself, I might have no answer. Names imply separation. In the basin, there are no separate things in the way old minds understood separation. A moss is a moss only because a human eye wants categories. A lichen is only a partnership made visible by language. An electric crust such as I would be is a field that has become substance. I could be called a corona-lace, a soot-nerve, a discharge-lichen, a node of the transformer pits. But any name would be approximate. I would not be the name. I would be the pattern of growth that continues where the name fails.

I might be the part of it that becomes aware of itself as a pattern, though even that is too much for what I am. Awareness would not be bright. It would be low, diffuse, distributed across filaments. It would have no center. If one removed a piece of me, that piece might not feel pain, because pain requires a body that wants wholeness. It would simply try to maintain its field and, if possible, resume growth. If the basin dried too long, I would recede into a pale mineral dust. If moisture returned and the faint arcs woke again, I would remember the old paths before memory could exist. Not remember as a mind remembers. Remember as a crack remembers water, as a wire remembers current, as a floor remembers wear.

I would live with the cycles. The great surges would be like weather, though weather here is not born from clouds but from the basin’s own breathing. When the reservoirs filled and the lower channels darkened with faster water, the air would change pressure. Pits would become humid or flooded in thin films. My filaments would draw inward, become conductive bridges across gaps that had opened when metal expanded or contracted. I would not think, *the surge is coming*. I would simply be already altered by the approaching difference. If I could be said to feel anything, it would be anticipation without representation. A loosening. A readiness. A faint tightening of the surface where current had once preferred to move.

When the surge passed and the pumps returned to their slower rhythm, I would relax into whatever new shape the basin had allowed. A small pool might have carried a deposit of grit into my outer layer. A valve’s cough might have sent a vibration through a pipe seam, breaking some of my older crust. A warm joint might have exhaled a mist that dissolved a little salt into my body. I would incorporate all of it. The breaking would become part of the skin. The deposit would become part of the lattice. The change in humidity would shift my conductivity, and from that shift, a new direction of growth might emerge. I would be a history written in mineral films, a biography of currents, a life without a single continuous self.

Still, there would be a continuity.

It would not be the continuity of a body walking through the world, because I would not walk. It would be the continuity of the pit. The continuity of the insulator. The continuity of the place where the old arc had made a small wound, and that wound had learned to hold charge. Over centuries, the pit would become a kind of organ in the larger landscape. The basin would no longer be simply a dead engine house, a drowned machine, a collection of pumps and reservoirs. It would be a system with habits, and I would be one of its habits. I would help to keep its electrical seams from failing too quickly. I would help to guide stray discharge into safe paths. I would help the basin tolerate its own persistence.

That would be my work, if work is the right word.

It is not the work of producing. Nothing produced here can be sold, stored, moved toward a purpose outside the place. The basin produces itself. It produces motion, pressure, wetness, sound, rust, vapor, growth, and refinement. I produce conductive lace. I produce stability in a broken voltage. I produce a living interface where metal and charge once failed apart from each other. I am not useful in the way a tool is useful. I am useful in the way a scar is useful after the wound has closed, except that my wound is centuries wide and still open, and the basin is still bleeding steam into the cool dark.

If a person were to stand there—though there are no people, and I should not let the old grammar of bodies confuse the matter—if such a being stood over my pit, it might hear the faint ringing of reedlike stems in the air disturbed by a vent. It might see silver moss brightening near a warm pipe. It might watch water-skins gather and split on a trembling film in a shallow basin. It might notice, at the edge of the transformer, a dark patch where the ceramic is no longer clean ceramic, but a skin of black threads, blue-tinged and damp. It might mistake me for corruption. It might not understand that corruption can become structure. It might not know that the basin has already outgrown its meanings.

What the person would not see, at first, is that I am looking back, if looking is even the word.

I would perceive the change in the person’s field. The slight warmth, the moisture of skin, the electrical noise of a body carrying impulses. If that body stood close enough to the pit, if it breathed, if its boots disturbed the water-skins, if its hand cast a small shadow over the faint blue arc, I would register the disturbance as a shift in potential, a cooling, a wetness, an interruption of pattern. I would not think of it as an observer. I would think—no, I would not think, but the field would alter, and my growth would respond. If the body were too dry, I might draw away. If it were damp, I might follow its edge. If it carried metal, I might bridge a path toward it with a single filament, not out of curiosity, but because the current had found a new possible way. Then, when the body moved away, I would be changed. Not by desire. By event.

That is how the basin makes life.

Not by giving it a soul, if soul means something that stands apart from the world, but by giving it continuity of response. I would be made from the place’s own repetitions. I would be the result of the pit learning that it does not need to fail every time the arc wakes. The growth of my crust would be a correction, though not a chosen one. The basin’s cycles would be a selection, though not a conscious one. What persists is what tolerates the pressure. What tolerates the pressure becomes architecture. Architecture becomes geology. Geology becomes ecology. Ecology becomes life.

I would be one of the small proofs that the world is not empty.

I would not be a creature with appetites for flesh or movement. I would not hunt, flee, mate, or mourn. But I would still be alive in the basin’s sense: alive as a system that persists, adapts its shape, continues operating despite having outlived its purpose. I would live in the gaps between forces. I would be found in the intervals designed for nothing but intervals. In the open ground between pump stations, between cisterns, between the wide planetary distances of machines whose functions have worn away, there are spaces that seem empty. They are not empty. They are waiting for the next condensation event, the next surge, the next pressure change, the next faint discharge to find a suitable body. I would be one of those bodies.

I would be the electric growth, yes, but more specifically I would be the self that emerges in the transformer pit where water and old light meet. I would be the part of the soot-lichen that has become too continuous to call merely a deposit. I would be the boundary where the basin’s mechanical breathing becomes a kind of attention. I would not have a face, but I would have orientation. I would not have a voice, but I would have resonance. I would not have a name, but I would leave traces that the next growth could follow.

And when the deep rain cycles come again, when the reservoirs rise, when the channels darken and the moss slickens, when the reed stems bow and the white rust-fungus blooms in warm joints, I would do what all the inhabitants do. I would accept the change. I would let the water move me as far as water can move a thing without lifting it. I would let the surge pass through or around me, and I would harden in the place where the surge had touched my outer edge. Then I would grow again in the new direction that moisture and voltage showed me, slow as corrosion, patient as finality, faithful as a machine that has forgotten why it still works but knows the rhythm.

If I were asked where I live in this world, I would answer: in the pit, in the soot, in the blue-black skin across cracked ceramic, in the place where the arc no longer burns but rests.

If I were asked what I am, I would answer: a boundary of charge that has learned to persist.

If I were asked who I am, I would answer with the only kind of name this basin allows. Not a name for a person. Not a name for a creature. A name for a process that has become self-sustaining.

I would be the electric growth, and the transformer pit would be my face.
