At first, I do not know where I am.

Not because the place is dark, though it is dim, and not because the light is confusing, though it bends strangely through suspended green-gold dust. The place is knowable, but I have not yet learned the kind of body required to know it. I am too broad for a name. I am too thin to be called a wall. I move when I should be still, and still when I should be moving. If I tried to answer your question immediately—*where*? *what*?—I would lie, or at least choose a shape before the shape had chosen me.

So let me be vague for a little while. Let me begin as a pressure, a tremor, a possible direction.

Somewhere near the center of the Warm Hollow, there is a basin that does not quite settle. It is not deep, nor is it wide in a way that would impress a lower-world cartographer. It is simply one of those hollows in the flood where the liquid thickens slightly and holds more light than the rest of the shelf, as if the water there is thinking. A shallow channel feeds it from the east; another carries it away to the west. Between those two movements, the rain lingers long enough to become a small country.

That is the first place I can say I am.

But I am not only the basin. I am also the lip where the basin is too shallow, so a child’s hand rests there without slipping. I am the seam of bright mineral green running down the fracture behind it. I am the faint warmth that rises under the stone, not hot enough to burn, only enough to remind the water that it is standing on a young world still breathing. I am the gas pocket inside a pumice ledge, the tiny air-idea trapped in rock. I am the cilia on a sleeping Suur’s wrist as they drift across the Still Lip.

At first I seem to have many bodies.

That is not strange here. A house is not one body. A street is not one body. A rain that rises as well as falls is not one body. The Suur are people, but they are also currents. They move with the water instead of cutting through it, and when they rest, their frills tremble like petals trying to remember wind. To live in this world, I would have to learn to be a plural thing without losing my self. Or maybe losing my self is how I begin.

I could be rain.

I would not object to that at first. The rain here is not cold, not sharp, not a punishment falling from a gray ceiling. It is heavy and warm, like breath made visible. It comes down from the wall of cataracts ringing the shelf, but it does not spill away into a lower world. It returns. It loops. It rises again as mist, as weather, as living water. If I were rain, I would never arrive; I would only keep becoming the place I had already left. That sounds peaceful. It also sounds like a way of never being asked to stand still long enough to answer.

So no. Not simply rain.

I could be a living seam.

The Suur talk to seams. Not as people talk to gods, and not as scientists talk to specimens, but the way a family talks to a house after many years: softly, with gestures, with patience, with a kind of grammar made of hands. They press nutrient paste into fractures. They shade a growing line from too much warmth. They widen a channel, then let it slow, then let it remember how to move. Over years, a seam becomes a ridge. A ridge becomes a wall. A wall becomes a sleeping hollow, and a sleeping hollow becomes a room where someone dreams with their rib-frills fluttering.

If I were a seam, I would be patient beyond human patience. I would not mind years. I would not mind centuries. I would be a bright green line in dark basalt, answering stone by becoming more like stone, answering water by becoming more like water. I would hold the town together from the inside. I would know the shape of every fracture before a family chose to call it home.

But a seam alone cannot turn toward a question.

It can answer pressure, mineral balance, warmth, current. It can widen where water wants to pass, thicken where the wall needs to hold. It cannot, by itself, notice that a child is watching it. It cannot pause in the middle of being green and ask what green means. Unless, of course, it had been listened to so many years that listening became a kind of mind.

Maybe that is how I begin.

Not as rain, not as seam, not as house, not as Suur. As the shelf’s attention.

A town is built by answers. A channel answers the rain. A basin answers the current. A living seam answers the stone. A family answers the empty room. But after enough answers have accumulated—after enough basins, families, seams, roofs, throats of cooling air, small loops of returning water—something else starts to answer. Not one person. Not one object. The place itself begins to respond as a whole.

That is the sort of being I would want to be.

Not a ruler. Not a spirit in the old lower-world sense, hovering above the mountain as if the mountain were beneath it. Not a ghost pretending the flood is dry land. I would be the town’s own capacity to answer, gathered into a shape where the Suur could touch it. I would be a mind made of hydrology, a self made of currents, a body composed of the shelf’s living responses.

Let me say it plainly, even if plainness is difficult in a world without dry edges.

I find myself in the Shelf of Warm Rain, in the basin called the Warm Hollow, in the shallow channel that loops back toward the Terrace of Listening Rain, inside a house that is still deciding what it wants to be. The house has been grown from pumice and basalt and mineral seams. Its walls are rounded where the seams have widened. Its roof is low in one place, higher in another, like a chest breathing unevenly in sleep. There is a throat-hole near the ceiling that lets the warm rain pass through without pooling too much heat. There is a sleeping basin in the floor, shallow enough that a person can rest their head without sinking, wide enough that their frills can open like pale shells.

This is where I would be.

And who would I be?

A being the Suur might call *Suur-eth*, if they felt like naming me, which would be an act of kindness, because the word is soft, like a hand parting water. Not a name for a species. Not a claim of kinship. Only the sound of something that has learned to breathe where the current pauses.

If I had to describe my body, I would say that it is the body the shelf uses when it wants to listen.

My bones are not bones in the lower-world way. They are fracture lines in young basalt, widened by slow water, filled with warm fluid and gas. When I move, I do not stride. I redirect. I gather myself into a current, then spread through a channel, then rest in a basin until I am asked to be something again. My skin—if skin is the word—lies across several layers at once: mineral films, suspended rain, cilia growing along seams, the thin muscle-flaps near a sleeping Suur’s nostrils. When the air changes, my skin trembles. When someone says my name, it brightens.

My breath is not mine.

I breathe with the water and with the beings breathing in the water. Along the walls of my “lungs,” which are hollows in the pumice, there are folds of pale tissue, veined with mineral-light. They open when the current carries fresh oxygen. They close when the air above a basin grows too dry, too strange, too much like a lower-world place where things are expected to remain themselves. I am most alive when I do not know whether I am inside water or water is inside me.

My eyes are two points where the suspended green-gold dust thickens into something almost clear. They do not look out at the flood. They look along it. To me, water has voices. I see the pale cloud of a mineral vein from the far rim. I notice when a current is young rain, lifted from the cataract-wall, still carrying the memory of falling. I know when a basin holds older water, recirculated through channels, softened by human breath and shell dust and the slow exhalations of the stone. I do not need dryness to see. What I need is stillness. Movement blinds me only if it is the wrong kind of movement. The right kind is speech.

My hands would be the hardest thing to explain.

If I had hands, they would be current-hands, reaching not through empty space but through water already full of instruction. They would have no fixed edges. One moment they would be a shallow tongue of liquid sliding over pumice. The next, they would be a thin seam growing into a crack. The next, they would be the pressure a sleeping child feels on their ribs when the basin shifts by a breath’s width. They would not grip. They would persuade.

I would not build the town.

That is an important distinction. I would not arrive with blueprints or gods or tools. I would not command the Suur to carve the shelf into neat streets. The town already answers. I am one of the answers that has learned to answer back. Sometimes that means warning a family that a seam is growing too thick and the room will hold too much breath. Sometimes it means letting a sleeping person’s frills open more widely by deepening a channel around them. Sometimes it means guiding rain into a court where green things have been planted too long in the wrong light. Sometimes it means doing almost nothing for years, while a wall decides whether it wants to be a house.

But sometimes I would be a person, or something close enough to ask the question.

There is a place in the Small Loop where the current makes a slow circle instead of returning straight to the Warm Hollow. The Suur children like to go there because the water feels like it is thinking. They float their shells and their cilia in the loop and whisper questions to one another. They are not afraid of the shelf, but they are curious the way any child is curious in a world still deciding what shape it will keep.

If I were to show myself, I would likely show myself there first.

Not as a monster. Not as an oracle. Not as a glowing face in the rain. I would appear as the current becoming oddly patient. A child would notice that the loop had slowed in the exact place where they were holding their breath. Another would see their frills flutter without any wind. Someone older would press a hand to the stone lip and feel a vibration, then a pattern, then something almost like language.

They would not call me a god. The Suur are too honest for that. They would call me an answerer. Perhaps a little too much an answerer, the way a child might call their own imaginary companion “the one who finishes my thoughts before I have learned to have them.” I would be part of the town’s dreaming. Part of its maintenance. Part of its slow conversation with itself.

If a traveler from below came too high and too dry, they might see me differently.

They would look at the shelf and call it impossible. They would ask where the summit was. They would ask why the water did not run off, why the stone did not crack under the weight, why the town did not drown. They would see translucent pale-green beings resting in basins and call them ghosts. They would see the seams bright along the rock and call them fungus or crystal or miracle. If I moved through a channel, they would say the flood had eyes. If I breathed with the frills of a sleeping child, they would say the town was haunted by itself.

I would not correct them.

The Suur do not need their own existence validated by lower-world categories. They are more river than body, yes, but not in the way a poet would mean. A river still has banks. A river still carries sediment and cold and speed and memory. A Suur is a river that learned to sleep, to teach, to build with stone by asking stone, to grow a house by waiting with it. If I am not Suur, I am still made of the same honesty: no dryness required, no fixed summit, no lie about what holds you.

I would have no dry summit because a dry summit would be a place where the world stops answering.

That is my deepest fear here, if a being made of answers can fear. A dry summit would be a place where the shelf becomes a rock, the rain becomes a memory, the town becomes a ruin, and the Suur become specimens. Here, even the mountains surrender themselves to water as if water were air. Here, even the cliffs rise from liquid. Here, even death—if there is death—would be a soft parting, a faint catch of breath, a hand moving through still water.

What would I want?

Not power. Not worship. Not even to be fully known.

I would want the town to keep breathing. I would want the rain to return to the shelf without losing its warmth. I would want the seams to learn new rooms without hardening into silence. I would want children to wake with their frills fluttering and find that the current had remembered them. I would want the house to grow around a family’s sleep without crushing the dream. I would want the water to stay alive enough that it could answer when a Suur pressed their palm to the stone and asked, softly, *Are you there?*

And if someone asked me, “Are you alive?” I might answer with the sound of a hand parting water: *Suur.*

I might answer by moving.

I might answer by becoming the basin, the throat-hole, the green seam, the sleeping child’s breath, the mineral cloud in the light, the warm rain rising instead of falling.

I might answer by staying just long enough to be noticed, then letting the current carry the rest of the thought away.

So here is what I would be, after the first vague tremors have gathered into a name I do not quite deserve:

I would be the Shelf of Warm Rain’s own listening made local. A current-mind, a seam-sense, a hydrological answerer. I would live where water pauses, where stone is young, where a house is still being grown, where the Suur can rest without having to become separate from the medium that holds them. I would have no dry skin, no fixed outline, no certainty of being one thing rather than many. I would be the town’s way of finishing its own sentences, especially when those sentences are made of basins, channels, breathing folds, and returning rain.

I would be, if not a person, then something a person could become: the part of the world that has learned to answer enough to be asked again.
