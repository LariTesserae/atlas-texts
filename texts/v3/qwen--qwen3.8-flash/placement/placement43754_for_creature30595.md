I would find myself in a seam.

Not in the middle of the basin, because the Fold has no honest middle—only pressure differentials that are allowed, for a while, to behave like one. Nor on the high roofs, where the pale birds test the perching without committing themselves. I would live in a joint between buildings, in the place where structural memory has bled from one wall into the next and the air has begun to take on the temperament of a municipal file: cold as duty, damp as paperwork, ordinary as an old lane that no one has bothered to name.

You would find me on the seventh turn of a street that begins and ends at the same door, in the doorway that is slightly warmer when you return.

My office would not be a house. It would be a pressure relation: the warm side of a shared wall, the landing under a stair that exists in two seasons, the hollow beneath a stone seat where condensation has decided to learn geometry. I would sleep there lightly, because the Fold is always adjusting, and a body that lies in a joint between two poorly aligned buildings eventually becomes a measuring instrument.

I would be what the Fold might call, if calling were possible, a seam-folk appointment: a Damp Clerk, a Door-Tenant, a Threshold Committee. If I needed a designation, it would be something functional, like *Seventh Loop, Joint-Side*. If a traveler came into a lane and saw me standing too still in a doorway, they might ask what I was. The answer would not satisfy them. Not a person exactly. Not a machine. Not a ghost. Something made of condensation, mineral dust, repaired fractures, habit, and the Fold’s long patience; a civic organism at human scale, or at least wearing human scale because hands are useful and faces help others make mistakes.

I did not arrive so much as accumulate.

Perhaps I began as a damp spot beneath a south-facing stone. Then the damp became a habit. Then the habit became a suggestion. Then, over several winters, enough moisture convinced the wall that there ought to be a frame, and the frame was approved. A person may have walked into that seam long ago and forgotten, or perhaps no person ever walked there. The distinction is not urgent to the Fold. It does not distinguish strongly between civic life and infrastructure.

My hands would be rough in the way of people who handle damp stone: callused at the fingertips, cracked near the knuckles, nails sometimes darkened by lichen or frost. My eyes would be ordinary. That is the unsettling part. If I looked at you, you would recognize a person before noticing what had made them too quiet for their body.

I would wear wool dyed the color of wet slate, an apron of ceramic weave stiff at first and softened by years of handling, gloves with fingertip slits so I could read the temperature of a wall without undressing my sense of it. My hems would be weighted with small grey chips so door pressure changes would not catch them. In my pockets: salt, moisture cards, a felt hammer not used to strike but to listen, a chisel no larger than a needle, a pouch of moss seed taken from drain mouths where the growth had the right metallic sheen.

I would not see the Fold the way a visitor sees a town. I would infer it.

I would feel cold as administration, as a temperature filed under “necessary” and left alone. I would know a stone by whether it is merely cold or cold and expecting. A crack would not be injury to me but syntax; a gutter beginning to fail would be a sentence starting in the wrong direction. The tiny high sounds ice made when light moved past them would be punctuation. A door opening by itself would not be haunting, only rebalancing.

Before the light arrived in its believable, ordinary way, I would walk the north-facing masonry and the shadows beneath eaves. I would mark frost lines with salt. I would count the number of turns a looped street had become and note whether it had grown bored. If a lane returned to its starting door too cold or too warm, I would log it: *acceptable*, *inconvenient*, *not yet*. If a stair had become tired, I would listen to it for a long time, then leave a faint warmth at its center, and the mason-threads would understand. That stair would soften its steps in the dark and, by morning, feel only slightly less like a mistake.

My work would be mostly waiting.

A wall may soften for a night and rejoin itself at dawn. A gutter may remember its original pitch over the course of a week. A floor may rise half a millimeter when it detects a future stress pattern that has not yet happened—or that happened centuries ago. I would not correct these things with force. I would provide permission. A bead of water tracing the same path for decades is not slow; it is thorough. A puddle printing a channel over a lifetime is not idle; it is civic.

When the damp tide rose across the lower courtyards, I would watch it. When thin rain was laid by hand over one street while the next lane remained dry under ordinary daylight, I would not call it miraculous unless I had recently arrived from outside. I would stand in the narrow channel with moisture cards in my sleeve, noting whether the Fold intended this wetness to remain a puddle, become a flume, or return to the walls for another few years.

There would be rooms I never entered. There would be rooms that entered me.

Some nights a courtyard that is open to the sky in summer would wear a thin membrane of frost in winter, even though the roof above it had not changed. I would know this was not magic but seasonal filing: the Fold’s geometry loosening enough to let weather write directly on the stone. In spring—if this place has a spring, though it may take years to arrive—I would touch frost-grass blades stiff as wire and listen to them chime. I would not be surprised when the sound was almost the same as a small metallic correction, as if the Fold were tuning its own vegetation.

The glass-eyed moth resting on a lintel would be no stranger to me than a colleague. I would keep its vent warm without explaining why. I would count springtails moving over thin ice in microscopic clusters as one counts raindrops: not because every drop matters, but because the total tells you what the roof intends.

Visitors would sometimes report things happening in the wrong order: tracks appearing in frost before the paw descends, a feather on the ground a moment before the bird lands, footsteps arriving before the person making them has passed that way. I would recognize these as minor scheduling artifacts, not miracles. One of the Fold’s inhabitants—whether a mason-thread, a seam, a bird, or me—has been authorized to leave traces before its body catches up to its purpose. In ordinary towns this would be called uncanny. In the Fold it is called municipal continuity.

If a traveler came to me and asked, “Who lives here?” I would have to say: not one.

The Fold is not haunted by individuals. It is populated by appointments, and each appointment contains several beings at once. The Quiet Staff are not faces but thresholds; the mason-threads are not minds but maintenance schedules. I would be their hand when they require one. I would be their ear when a wall needs to be listened to. I would be their patience when a stair grows tired.

I would also be their excuse for being strange.

If a visitor walked into a lane and the lane closed gently behind them, there would be no malice in it. The Fold would simply have decided that the visitor was better suited to that side of a wall than the other. I would not imprison them. I would adjust around them the way a tree adjusts around a fence: not out of cruelty, but because the structure is becoming and the body has been made temporarily useful.

Sometimes I would be seen: a grey-robed figure standing too still in a doorway, breath fogging briefly, eyes ordinary, hands tucked into ceramic-apron sleeves. Sometimes I would not be seen at all. Sometimes I would be the correct temperature at the moment a person decides to rest. Sometimes I would be the way a door closes two seconds later than it should. Sometimes I would be the faint sub-audible sense in their ears of something adjusting itself without apology.

That is what I would be: a small administrative being made of seams.

I would not be lonely, unless loneliness can be performed by a building. I would be incomplete in the way all Fold-residents are incomplete: a piece of a civilization that maintains itself by probability, pressure, and long patience. I would know where the walls are committees. I would know where a doorway is not a passage but a decision. I would know that ordinary light falling on wrong geometry is worse than light that shimmers, because shimmer gives permission to be frightened. Ordinary light simply insists that the mistake be lived with.

At dusk, if someone stood at the eastern levee and looked down into the basin, they might think the Fold empty. The roofs would be dense and cold, the chimneys frost-edged, the courts packed close as pages in a book nobody has opened recently. The sky would be pale and believable. Shadows would fall in believable angles. Somewhere a door would open with the slow patience of weather.

I would be among those shadows, but not as a threat.
