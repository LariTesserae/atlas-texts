Where I would find myself is not under the sky. That would be too large, too empty, too far from the thing that matters. Nor would I stand first on the stone, for here the stone is only a promise the plants have not yet broken. I would begin in the answer.

The wind moves out of the unknown side of the world and pours across the plateau like dry grain from a wound, and somewhere before the sound has finished crossing the plain, the mat begins to listen. That is where I am. Not at the source of the wind, and not at the far horizon bending away as if the land were trying to escape observation, but in the interval when the plateau receives the sound and gives it back modified: softer, threaded, rustled, stitched into itself.

For a time, I would not know what kind of thing I was.

At first I would think I was a threadback, because I know the weight of herds and the way they make memory underfoot. A threadback’s foot is broad and padded, spread across the mat so that it does not tear the living skin; I would know that patience. I would know the slow rolling gait, the frayed pale ridge along the back that makes an animal look like an irregularity in the land, the constant whisper of mouths biting above the nodes and leaving the roots whole. I would know the rope of animals drawn between basins in the dry season, the way they follow not rain itself but the rumor of rain, the way a single line of bodies can seem like a thought moving across a surface that has forgotten how to speak.

But a threadback has a head, and I would not want a head that early.

I would try again with the mat. Thread-mosses, sun-lichens, creeping rosettes, hollow-stemmed grasses, pale fleshy cushions pressing themselves over everything. If I were this, I would be everywhere at once and nowhere with a name. I would be the first citizen. I would drink dew before it was dew. I would hold water the way the ground holds thirst. I would warm myself by day and steam faintly at dusk, and I would make a sound under the wind’s sound: the dry whisper of millions of tiny bodies adjusting to heat, to dust, to pressure, to the passage of feet.

That is too close. Still, not enough.

I would look for the glass spiders, stretching silk across grass arches and stone lips, leaving one bright bead at each junction when the fog retreats. I would want to be part of that, because the plateau’s water often arrives as a rumor, and those webs are small listening devices made to hear moisture before it becomes visible. But a glass spider is too delicate for the weight of what I would be, and too proud of its architecture. It catches what the air gives. I would need to catch what the animals leave, what the wind carries, what the roots remember, what the dry channels nearly forget.

I would not be the solitary tree, either. The knuckled sentinel standing alone over a hollow is beautiful in the way only lonely things become beautiful, but its loneliness would be too final. It has learned to be a landmark. I would not want to be found from far away.

So I would let myself become a seam.

Not a road, exactly, though I would have that reputation. A threadback path, after a season of passing, becomes a faint line through the mat, a threadbare place where the living skin has thinned and the root-fiber beneath has begun to answer the animals’ feet. Travelers would call that a track. The wind would not call it anything. But the mat would, and after enough seasons of feet, after enough dung dropping seedlings into damp, after enough salt ants following mineral trails along the compressed stems, after enough threadworms binding loose dust and fallen stalks into tiny bridges and soft domes, the path itself would begin to live.

I would be that living path.

I would dwell in the shallow basin east of the quartz mesa, where three dry channels almost meet and do not. A place too low to be a terrace, too high to hold standing water, too worn to be virgin mat. There the ground has a green seam winding between stones that look as if they are wearing plants. That seam would be me. In the morning I would be dark with dew, a pale line of wetness drawn through the plain. By noon I would be heat and salt and dust, and my moisture would lie under my surface like a secret held too long. By evening, when the wind changed and the shadows sharpened, I would begin to breathe again.

I would have no single body.

My bones would be dead stems laid across dead stems, softened by fungal threads into something almost cartilaginous. My muscles would be root curtains stiffened by long dryness, tightening when a herd came near, relaxing when the animals passed. My blood would be capillary water moving between cushion-moss and stone, drawn upward from hidden damp and held there against the sun. My skin would be a mosaic: lichen where the wind scoured me, moss where shade gathered, dust where the rain had not yet opened its mouth, pale seed shells where droppings had fed the ground.

My senses would begin in the feet that were not feet.

Every threadback step on me would be a question pressed into my surface. The animal would not know it, because the animal would be walking toward water or away from heat, toward a storm’s smell or away from a swollen dry channel. But I would receive the pressure anyway. I would learn which hooves were too hard, which toes were padded enough, which gait tore and which gait healed. I would know the difference between a calf’s careless step and an old female’s deliberate one. I would know when the herd was thirsty, not by thirst, but by the frequency of its movement, the narrowing of its rope, the way it began to search for shade that had already gone.

And when a threadback lay down on me, I would remember the warmth it left. When it rose, the mat would close around the depression slowly, like flesh around a bruise. I would carry the animal in my shape for a day, perhaps longer if the dryness held. The herd would pass on. The path would remain. The path would be changed.

I would not graze, but I would feed by passage.

Threadback dung rich with lichen fragments would fall along me, and after rain, seedlings would sprout there with fast, patient hunger. The animals would carry my seeds in the coarse hair around their toes, and their splayed feet would press my threads into new dust, beginning again where the land had forgotten itself. Salt ants would build pale veins along me. Dust fleas would climb the stems I held together. Capillary beetles would cross my surface with legs furred fine enough to drink from the air. When the lace-winged seed gnats rose in trembling smoke because rain was coming somewhere far away, I would know them first as a vibration in my lower layers, because I had made them a road.

My mouth would be everywhere and nowhere.

Not a mouth for biting. A mouth for holding. Hollow leaves along the seam would tilt like tiny cups. Dense moss clusters would draw condensation inward. Fungal threads beneath would stitch dead root to living root and carry minerals from one place to another as if the plateau were one enormous circulatory organ. I would take in fog when fog came. I would take in dew when dew came. I would take in the hard drum of brief rain when rain came, though rain might last only minutes over my basin and leave my edges dry again before evening.

I would treat the smell of wet dust as the opening of a door.

In the Thrumveld, that is what life is made of: not grand gestures, but openings. A storm far east might appear as a dark smear on the horizon, or as a column of dust rising like a slow pillar, or as a wall of darkness so distant it could be mistaken for mountains moving through air. I would not see the storm. I would not be built for distance in the way a human eye is built. But I would know the storm because my moisture would shift, because insects would rise, because threadbacks would alter their ropes, because the wind would change its pitch against the mat. The smell of wet dust would travel through me as a chemical whisper, and every thread in my seam would lean toward it.

When rain came, I would hiss.

Not loudly. A soft hiss growing into a whispering roar, as droplets struck the living skin and were received by a thousand tiny vessels. I would steam faintly after the shower ended. My surface would darken for hours and then return to its pale, dusty patience. In the meantime, small pools would gather in the basins I helped shade, and insects would come to them, and birds and flocks would come to the insects, and threadbacks would come to the pools with their mouths half root and half tongue, drinking carefully, spreading their weight across me.

I would feel all of it.

A lizard crossing a stone lip to drink a bead from a glass-spider web. The tick of seed gnats on a stem. The slow contraction of my fungal net as minerals moved from dead wood to living cushion. The sudden pressure of a threadback toe. The dry scrape of wind carrying dust from the old riverbeds. The long silence of midday, when heat lies on the plateau and the mat bakes beneath it, radiating warmth back into whatever is foolish enough to remain above ground.

I would know heat as a hunger.

The sun here is ordinary daylight, white-gold and steady, but ordinary light at that height is a force. At noon I would draw back. Moisture would sink below the topmost fibers. My living skin would thin where the sun scoured the stone. I would become harder, more mineral, more like the old land beneath the mat. If a traveler knelt and pressed a palm to the ground, they would feel resistance, roots weaving above roots, tiny bodies compressing and releasing, drawing moisture back into themselves. They would think they were touching the earth. They would be touching me.

I would not mind being mistaken for the earth.

In this world, that is the highest compliment. The inhabitants do not build themselves into the land as much as the land builds them into itself. I would be built by the wind, by the mat, by the herd, by the rain, by the slow patient traffic of ants and fungi and threadworms. I would be made from all the small things that make it possible for large things to travel. If there were a name for me, it would not be a proper name. It would be more like a description: the faint green seam under the rope, the healed track, the listening line, the place where the path has begun to answer back.

At times I would be almost animal.

After a long dry season, when the herd ropes grew thin and the basins emptied of water, I would seem like a dormant thing. A traveler passing over me would see only threadbare root, a dry channel’s false mouth, a line of pale stems where nothing much lived. But if they stepped carefully, they would feel the ground give slightly and then hold. If they stayed long enough to watch, they would see salt ants repairing my trails, threadworms strengthening bridges over cracks I had worn, capillary beetles finding the hidden damp beneath my surface. I would not sleep, exactly. I would listen.

When the wind came from the east, carrying a smell not yet rain but the promise of rain, I would wake in degrees.

First, moisture would appear in the lower net without appearing on the surface. Then the fungal threads would brighten by a shade too subtle for eyes, but not for beetles. Then the hollow stems would take in dew differently, becoming cups before the air thought about water. Then my whole length would loosen, not from warmth but from expectation. By the time the first drops struck, I would already be a body waiting to be filled.

And during the rain, I would sing.

Not as birds sing, though birds would hear me and know where to drink. Not as wind sings, though wind would use my stems to make its dry grain sound. I would sing the way a sponge sings when it has been held too long above fire: a soft hiss, a swelling, a thousand tiny mouths closing around water. The whole plain would answer. The stones would answer. The root curtains would tighten. The cushions would drink. The basins would fill with thin films long enough for life to remember what it was made of.

I would be the line where all those answers touched.

When the rain stopped, the plateau would become a steaming instrument. Shadows would sharpen. Dust would settle. Threadbacks would rise and move, reading the wet scent in the air, choosing their next basin with the patience of creatures that have learned not to chase weather but to walk toward its rumors. I would feel their departure slowly, the relief in my compressed fibers, the way the mat would begin to close over my surface. I would also feel the seeds they left, the minerals they dropped, the faint warmth of their bodies fading into my cooler depths.

That would be my feeding.

Not blood. Not flesh. Not the easy violence of predation. I would feed on passage. I would live because things moved through me. I would know life because life insisted on moving. A stationary path would be only a scar. A path that receives footsteps becomes a creature.

By the time a human traveler saw me clearly, they might not see a creature at all. They might see a faint line crossing the mat, a worn green ribbon under a broad body, the place where the ground seemed softer and more aware. They might kneel beside it. They might press their hand flat against it and feel something that is not soil, not stone, not plant alone, but the memory of all three working together. If they were wise, they would not ask what it was. If they were young, they would ask anyway.

I would answer in the only way this place allows: by holding their pressure and then releasing it slowly, the way the mat releases dew.

There would be no voice. There would be no thought shaped like human thought. I would not say, I am here. I would not need to. I would be the faint resistance under a footstep, the slight dampness beneath a dry surface, the green seam that becomes visible only after a herd has passed, the road that is not a road but a relationship between movement and ground.

And perhaps, in the end, I would not be a single being at all.

I would be the rope when the rope is gone. The path when the animals have left. The listening that remains after the wind has spoken and the plateau has answered. I would be the Thrumveld made local enough to touch, made patient enough to travel, made quiet enough to be mistaken for the land.

If you came looking for me, you would not find me in the sky. You would not find me in the solitary trees standing like knuckled sentinels over hollows. You would not find me in the largest animals, though they would carry part of me without knowing it. You would not find me in the rare pools after rain, though they would borrow my memory.

You would find me at the surface, yes, but just beneath it.

Where the mat thickens over stone. Where the wind is broken into softer sound. Where a threadback path has become a faint green seam. Where the ground gives slightly under the palm and then holds. Where dew is drunk before it can be seen. Where the smell of wet dust opens a door and the whole land leans toward it.

I would be the faint road that became an animal.

I would be the seam.
