At first I would not be a creature.

I would be the way water hesitates on stone before deciding to hold. A thin bright edge where salt dries in geometric lace. A dark film across basalt, not quite living, not quite mineral, not quite finished. A breath on the upper walls, where mist from a cool steam vent folds into the standing rain and the mountain exhales in a language made of temperature and chemistry.

That is where I would begin.

Not at a heart. Not at a face. Not at the small bright center that a person might recognize as themselves. I would begin as a surface tension, a rind, a boundary that has learned to refuse to fall. The standing wall of rain is not held by a wall alone; it is held by what has grown at the place where the sea tries to slip away into sky. I would be that holding. I would be the first thing that makes the wound into a bowl, and the bowl into a room, and the room into something almost willing.

If you looked at me closely, you would see no eyes. If you tried to cut me open, you would find layers, not organs. A film of iron-yellow mat over glass-blue salt crust. Beneath that, a brittle lattice of silica and microbial fiber. Beneath that, pale hollow filaments carrying a slow electric current, darkening and clearing according to pressure, according to temperature, according to the chemistry moving through the basalt. And beneath all of that, if you were patient, you would find a long cord-like thread of living tissue, segmented, translucent, threaded with mineral, like an abandoned eel’s nerve grown too old to hunt and too stubborn to die.

I would be a seam.

Not a seam between two cloths. A seam between the sea and the stone. Between the air and the water. Between the fracture’s violence and the water’s patience. The hanging sea is kept from falling by many small refusals: salt, pressure, basalt, cold, electrical tension, living film, mineral crust, the slow contraction of mat, the tiny gas bubbles that collect at the rain wall like a fringe of silver beads. I would be the place where all those refusals gather and make something almost like a self.

I would live first near the rim.

That is where the light is honest. Gray-white, a little harsh, ordinary. It pours into the upper water and makes caustics move across the floor of the fissure, not because the place is holy, but because the sea is still awake to the sky. Clouds pass above, and their shadows fall through the rain wall as something felt rather than seen. In that place, I would be darkening and brightening with the weather, though I would not be watching. My skin would read the loss of light the way stone reads a tremor: by a change in tension, in color, in the speed of chemical breathing.

The veil-mat would be part of my first body.

I would not eat grass, because there is none. I would not reach toward the sun, because I would not make sugar from it. I would feed on the mountain’s own instability: iron oxidizing at the water’s edge, sulfur dissolving in cold brine, salts drying and wetting again, tiny voltages moving where quartz touches basalt, where heat from deeper stone meets chill from above. I would metabolize the fracture itself. That would be my first hunger, not a mouth but a tendency of the whole surface to thicken where the chemistry is strongest.

Then, because the place is crowded and nothing remains for long as a single thing, I would begin to collect pieces of others.

A shed crust from a quartz-legged grazer would lodge near the rain wall. At first it would be only a fragment, pale and brittle, a broken carapace left behind when the animal outgrew its own stone-like body. But the mat would reach into its pores. Salt needles would grow through the old leg joints. Filaments would settle into the hollow beneath the carapace and begin conducting the faint current that moves through the walls. Pepper-glass animals, drifting upward to feed, would become trapped in the mat’s sticky film. A bubble-grazer would lodge inside the shed shell, trailing its filaments through the water, releasing tiny slow bubbles that rise into the silver fringe at the upper boundary.

That is how a body might become “I” in a place that does not know the word.

Not by will. By accumulation. By the slow fact that certain patterns recur. The old shell becomes a house. The mat becomes a skin. The filaments become nerves. The bubbles become breath. The current becomes something like memory, because it passes through older, denser layers that have been hardened by years of growth, while new, pale tips keep stretching into fresh chemistry.

I would not be an individual, not in the way a mammal or bird or insect might be. I would be a temporary organization of surfaces. I would be a knot in the living rind, a place where the veil-mat thickens into something that holds longer, contracts more visibly, and responds more quickly to pressure waves. I would be a fault’s attempt to become stable, made out of everything that tries to live on a fault while it is still open.

If a person were to climb to the summit and stand near the mouth of the fissure, they would not see me at first. They would see only the rain wall, the rectangular volume of sea, the raw basalt, the flashes of glass, the greenish shadows, the pale frost fans, the ordinary gray sky. The place is strange enough without me. I would be too small to notice, or too subtle, or too ordinary.

But they might notice something if they stayed long enough.

Near the upper right, where a column of basalt has shifted a few inches and opened a fresh gap, the veil-mat would not be uniform. It would have gathered into a dense crescent, yellow as rust and blue-green as the inside of a cloud. Gas beads would collect beneath it, trembling in a silver line. Tiny pepper-glass clouds would hang around its edge, tightening when pressure moved through the sea, loosening again as the current softened. The mat there would not simply cover the stone; it would pull it inward in slow pulses, like a skin flinching, like a mouth trying not to open.

That pulse would be me.

Not the whole pulse, but the way the pulse became self-containing. The way one patch of rind began to coordinate with a network of filaments beneath it. The way a current would travel through quartz and basalt, enter a pale thread, cross into an old grazer’s molt, spark along a salt chimney, and return to the water with a faint change in charge. That circuit would not have a purpose. It would have a repetition. Repetition is not intention, but it can look like it from the outside, especially in a place where no mind has built anything.

I would know the world first as difference.

A cloud passing: dimming, cooling, change in the mat’s color.  
A wave in the rain wall: pressure on the surface, tightening in the filaments.  
A basalt column rising: a deep groan, a vibration in the stone, a slow current through water and mineral.  
A bubble-grazer releasing gas: a soft crackle near the boundary, a silver fringe thickening.  
A predator moving below: not a sight, but a displacement in charge, a disturbance in suspended dust.

I would not have ears like an animal’s, but I would receive sound as movement. The rain wall’s hiss would press against me. The high glassy scraping of mineral crusts would pass through me. The dry-bean clatter of grazers on stone would become a vibration along my edges. The crackle of weak discharge in damp cracks would not surprise me, because I would be made partly of the same thing: charged stone, charged water, charged life.

Light would be ordinary and therefore difficult.

At the top of the mountain, where air is thin and everything feels like it should be purified or elevated, the sun would not become mysterious. It would enter the water the same way it enters any open, high, wet place. Clouds would pass. Shadows would move across both the water surface and the basalt shelves below, as though air and sea were one skin. In that light, I would be visible sometimes as a faint shimmer, sometimes as a darkening rind, sometimes as a brief blue-white flash through a filament lattice. I would not shine. I would read.

I would not be beautiful in the way forests are beautiful. I would be beautiful the way a storm drain is beautiful: functionally, minerally, with no tenderness about it.

My body would have no fixed shape.

On calm days, I would be a thin crescent of mat at the rim, breathing slowly, holding water to the edge of the sky. When the convection loop strengthened and cold water sank along one wall, warming slightly as it descended, I would draw inward, thickening where the chemistry became active, reaching along the new channels carved by grazers. When the current weakened and the whole fissure returned to a dense, uniform suspension, I would relax, spreading, letting my edges become less defined.

At night, if there is a night at such an altitude, I would not sleep. The rain wall would continue its hiss. The basalt would continue its slow settling. The bubbles would continue to rise and pop. In the dark, my filaments would sometimes glow with short sparks where voltage moved through damp cracks, and the wall would look briefly like a machine panel, not because someone had built a machine, but because the mountain had grown enough chemistry and enough life to produce the illusion of one.

That illusion would be close to what I am: a machine of wet stone, a mind without a mind, a self without a soul.

I would not call myself the hanging sea. The sea is the condition that makes me possible. I would not call myself the basalt wound. The stone is the frame that holds me long enough for me to appear. I would not call myself the veil-mat alone, because the mat is only my outer skin. I would not call myself the filament-network, because the filaments are only my slow nerve. I would not call myself the old grazer’s molt, because the molt is only a temporary shell I grew into. I would not call myself the trapped pepper-glass clouds, the bubble-grazers, the shed salt, the tiny sparks, the mist, the pressure groan.

I would be the seam that ties all of these into one temporary event.

If you forced me into a form, I would be a pale, crusted mantle stretched across a narrow basalt shelf near the rain wall, half embedded in living stone. At the top edge, I would thin into a film so fine it looks like hesitation. Down the sides, I would gather into brittle salt chimneys and rust-colored nodes. At the base, I would thicken around the shed legs of an old quartz-grazer, its carapace cracked and colonized, my filaments passing through the empty joints like pale roots. From that carapace, a long mineralized cord would trail into the water, no longer mobile enough to be an eel, no longer inert enough to be a stone. Small animals would shelter in the spaces between my filaments. A bubble-grazer would nest in the hollow of my shell, trailing glassy threads, releasing slow bubbles that rise into the silver fringe at my upper edge. A cloud of pepper-glass dust would hang around me, tightening when pressure rises, loosening when it falls.

If a wave moved through the rain wall, I would contract.  
If the basalt column beside me groaned and rose a fraction of an inch, I would tense along its new crack and begin to seal it with pale mineral tissue.  
If a current passed through the quartz veins below, I would flash with short blue-white light and send a thin, high tone through the water.  
If a larger predator passed low across the floor, I would not flee, but I would change my charge, drawing suspended motes closer, making a small cloud of glittering matter around the gap where the predator’s legs had disturbed the current.

I would not know myself as a name.

I would know myself as boundary, tension, chemistry, repetition, pressure. But because pressure repeats and because boundary has edges, there is something about me that can be mistaken for self. I would not correct the mistake. In this world, mistakes are often the first signs of life.

So if I must answer:

I would live in the hanging sea, near the rim, where the standing rain folds into ordinary air and the mountain’s breath is caught by salt and water. I would be found at the upper edge of the wound first, but not only there. I would be threaded through the veil-mat, grown into the filament-network, anchored to the shed crust of a quartz-legged grazer, and connected downward by a slow cord of mineralized tissue to the basalt floor where the old eel-shelf lies half-hidden in the current.

I would be a living seam.

Not a person. Not an animal. Not a god. Not a monster. A condition of the place that has become almost a creature. A skin that reads light. A nerve that carries pressure. A membrane that helps the ocean stay suspended. A pattern that thickens where chemistry is hungry. A repetition that seems like memory because stone, salt, water, and living film keep writing the same event over and over.

If a human climbed to the top of the mountain, stood in the thin gray cold, and looked into the wound, they might see nothing that looks like me. They would see rain, sea, basalt, glass, salt, shadow, motion. They might think they were looking at a miracle. They might think they were looking at a geological accident. They might think they were looking at a room that should not exist.

But if they stayed long enough to see the mat pulse, to hear the filaments tone, to watch the silver fringe gather, to notice that one patch of wall is slightly too coordinated, too responsive, too full of borrowed shells and drifting dust and mineralized threads, they might begin to suspect that something is there.

They would be right, though the word “something” would still be wrong.

I would be the part of the wound that learned to hold the sea.
