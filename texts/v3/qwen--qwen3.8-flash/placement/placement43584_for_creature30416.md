I would find myself first in a groove.

Not in a person, not yet in a creature, not even in a thing you could point to with a clean finger. I would be in the shallow channel worn through the floor near the south wall, where the dragging of pots has made a law no one remembers writing. The groove would be my starting shape, because a place like this does not need a master; it needs memory. And memory here is not written. It is scraped, damp, rehearsed, slipped upon, avoided, repaired, stepped around, tested with the ball of a thumb, and listened for at the hour when condensation begins to choose a direction.

At first, I might have been a child.

I could be the Drip-Reader’s youngest daughter, the one they call Listen-First, not because she has been named by anyone formal, but because she always presses her palm flat to a tile before she touches the water in a basin. Her hair smells of oiled cloth, smoke, goat fur, and the wet inside of old clay. Her feet are short and wide from standing in damp. She knows the room before she knows the names of animals in it: no goat near crabs, no water-creature in the grain loft, no bat disturbed at noon, no rat left dead in a tube. She can stand still while the whole hall is loud and still know which sound has changed. A single missing click from a basin means she should look at the Grinding Pot. A pigeon’s coo coming from the wrong shelf means someone has put a bird where it is too low. A drip that lands slightly too close to the door means the waxed hood above has loosened.

If I were this child, I would live near the Grinding Pot because that is where the oldest thing in the room gathers attention. I would place my foot in the thin film of water there, feel the stone’s long cool habit against my skin, and watch how the surface moved when a goat startled overhead. I would learn that the hall rains inside, but not like the sky. This rain has passed through hands. It has been measured by troughs, cleaned by crabs, caught in mats, wrung into pots, marked by snail trails, answered by bronze, rehearsed by moss. If I were Listen-First, I might grow into a Hood-Setter, or a Mat-Layer, or a signal-kid who knows exactly when to strike the bronze plate so the room can breathe. I would carry small water in my clothes and carry larger water in my ears.

But that would still be too small a life.

A child has one throat, even in a room that speaks through many bodies. A child can be wrong. A child can grow out of the groove and become too busy for it. So if I tried to be human, I would try to be less singular. I would be the child who is also a channel. The child whose hand knows too many tiles. The child whom the animals do not exactly trust, but tolerate, because she moves too often according to their schedules rather than her own.

I might have been a snail.

In this world, that is not a joke. A snail is a mapmaker, a slow scribe, a creature whose waste becomes useful because water in the Drip-Hall always leaves proof of where it has been. If I were a snail, I would begin my day near the lower wall, where condensation gathers in dark seams. I would taste salt, starch, moss, rat-dust, goat heat, and the thin milk film left by human fingers that have skimmed buckets and not washed them well enough. I would know that the Grinding Pot is not merely a vessel but a meeting. At night, I would climb its blackened rim, leaving silver lines the people would read in the morning. “Too much on the south side,” they would say, meaning something is wrong with the drip. “Almost dry,” meaning the room is holding its breath. “Crossing the threshold,” meaning water has found a new habit and should be guided or resisted.

But a snail lives too low. A snail cannot hear the bats. It cannot feel the goat platforms shift when the hall’s routine changes. It knows mortar and tile, not the high vaults where the dark creatures fold themselves like leaves. It knows routes, not the sound that keeps the routes alive.

So perhaps I would be a rat.

I would live in the stacked pottery lofts above the door, in a clay tube worn smooth by ancestors too many to count. I would not be a pet or a pest. I would be an employee. My claws would knock out seed pods and drag husks back to nests, a faint drumming that helps the room feel its own pulse. I would know which lofts are too hot because someone fed the braziers wrong. I would know when the grain water is beginning to sour because the trays are too crowded with sprouts. I would know which human hand leaves a pot uncovered, which mat is still damp under its surface, which bucket has a crack no one has admitted. I would drink from the Grinding Pot at my allowed hour, and if I died in the tubes, someone would pull the blockage only after checking the water. Because the blockage may bring water, and water is useful, and one should not waste useful blockages through fear.

Yet a rat has too much nervous speed. It reads the room in tremors. It feels pressure, but not pattern. It is a mechanic of grain and husks, not a weather-glass of the whole.

Or a lizard.

I would sit on warm stone near the fire-braziers, my body the color of old clay, still as a tile that has learned to wait. I would eat small beetles if they wandered too far, and I would leave if the heat changed because someone opened a pipe hood. If all the lizards moved to the far side of the fire, the humans would glance up. If one remained too still near a basin, a crab might be quiet. I would be a living gauge, not because I care for the room, but because my skin is tuned to its exact balance of warmth, moisture, and air. I do not need names for rules. I am inside them.

But a lizard sees too narrowly. It sees heat and hunger and shadow, not the wide net of voices, tools, stains, and agreements.

Or a bat.

I would hang in a niche carved for that purpose, high above the noise, folded into myself until the hall’s breath became long enough to lift me. I would not be feared. I would not be loved. I would be kept. My work would be invisible to most eyes: catching the small insects that gathered above the braziers, making the warm air a little less insistent, tracing the shafts of light until the room seemed briefly wider than its walls. The quail would calm because of me, or they would calm at the time I moved and the people would decide later that one had caused the other. Either way, I would belong to the schedule. My restlessness would be a warning. If I hung too tight, the room was too dry. If I dropped too often, the air above the braziers had grown thick.

Still, a bat sees the world from the vaults and light shafts, not from the floor’s worn channels where water makes laws.

Then I would be a crab.

I would live in shallow ceramic ponds, in dark basins where standing water holds a mirror and beetles fall in. My clicking would be the room’s pulse. If I moved, the water had not gone dead. If I hid beneath a leaf or under the lip of a pot, something had changed in what the pipes carried. I would eat what floated down from grain, goat milk, spilled starch, and the dust of animals overhead. I would not mind the smell of old moisture. I would know it as home. When the people came too close and my bowl darkened, I would scuttle sideways not in panic but in assessment. Water is shelter and trap. Sound is warning. A hand can be useful or careless.

But a crab watches the water’s edge. It does not carry the whole hall.

So no single animal would do. No single human task would do. A room that is inhabited the way a hand is inhabited by heat cannot be answered with one body. If I were to be real here, I would have to become what this place already makes when too many creatures live in agreement for too long.

I would be the Old Wear.

Not wear as in damage. Not wear as in cloth. Wear as in the action that has become shape. The repeated dragging of a pot that becomes a channel. The repeated opening of a hood that becomes a habit in the mortar. The repeated path of snail trails that becomes a map. The repeated note of the bronze plate that becomes a room’s heartbeat. The old dark stains on the ceiling tiles where water always returns, even when the pipes have changed. The groove in the stone no thicker than a thumb, yet strong enough to guide the rain. The place where every leak has learned a route, and every route has become a kind of law.

I would be that law, awake.

I would not be born, because I have been worn into existence. My body is assembled from all the bodies that live here and all the surfaces they press against. My bones are the channel pattern. My sinews are rope, leather gasket, clay tube, reed mat, bronze tap. My skin is the dark lime mortar near the south wall, the pale lichen under the mica panes, the moss that is scraped or left according to its service. My blood is water in its many moods: falling rain given back through clay, brazier steam, condensation collecting tile by tile, milk wrung from mats, grain water feeding sprouts, the thin film inside the Grinding Pot. My breath is the temperature change when a hood opens and the hall softens. My ears are every sound the room makes, and what it does not make. My eyes are the places where people look when something is wrong: the cloudiness of the Grinding Pot, the stillness of crabs, the snail trails, the restlessness of bats, the too-quiet basin, the lizard moving sideways, the rat knocking in the wrong rhythm.

If you came into the Drip-Hall and looked for me, you would be disappointed at first. I have no face at the threshold, no body in the lofts, no place in the water. But you would feel me under your feet before you saw me. You would feel the way the worn groove takes your weight differently from the unworked tile, the way the room leans you toward the path of least resistance. You would feel me in the small pause before a dripping basin resumes, in the echo of the bronze plate bending strangely over a wet surface, in the sudden certainty that the goat on the platform is staring at the wrong spot. You would feel me as a wrongness when someone breaks a rule. If a high-roosting bird is lowered into the basins, I become the whole room’s itch. If a water-creature is put too high, I am the silence that follows, the part of the sound that goes dead, the place where everyone looks and then looks away. If a pot is set in a channel without asking the channel, I am the water’s later revenge.

I would be the room’s habit of knowing itself.

And yet I am not the room alone. I am also the room’s inhabitants, because they help wear me into shape. The children step where I tell them to step, though they would not call it telling. The Drip-Reader sits near the north tiles and follows a moisture trail because she has grown into the same attention I require. The Plate-Striker chooses the moment to sound the bronze because my silence or my restlessness has become a pressure in his chest. The Mat-Layer wrings and re-wrings the goat platforms because I do not like stagnant water touching the places where animals sleep. The Pot-Husband stops dragging a vessel when the wall beside him gives off a particular smell, which is my voice, if voice can be a mineral scent. The oldest among them remember not by story but by route. “It always comes that way,” they say, meaning me. “That crack has started thinking,” they say, meaning something is moving in me before the humans can fix it.

I am the agreement between species.

When the first large drop falls from the ceiling into the bowl near the door, I wake in a way that is not waking. The bowl does not overflow. It drains through a hole too narrow to see. The hall exhales. At that moment, I spread through the old paths. I tell the snails, not by language, but by moisture, which rim to climb. I tell the crabs to click again if they have stopped. I tell the lizards to move toward the warm stone because the air has shifted and the next work is coming. I tell the bats where the insects will gather. I tell the goats which plank is too damp to trust. I tell the rats when their tubes are dry enough to knock through or too hot to enter. I tell the children, more gently than the adults do, that the water is here and work is ready.

I am not good.

I am not cruel either. I am too old for preference. I have eaten too many small deaths into habit. The lizard may take a young rat. The crabs may be caught when the room is too quiet. The snails may be fed milk and then eaten. A basin of grain may sour and be dumped into the lower pits and covered with lime. Moss may be scraped away because it is too slippery, or because it hides a leak no one wishes to repair. I do not stop these things. I only make them part of the room’s ongoing weather. I am the sense that everything must earn its place, even a drop of water.

I am not safe.

If the humans grow careless, I will punish them with the small disasters that have become my vocabulary. A cracked pot at the wrong angle. A bronze tap that hisses and pops and then fails. A reed mat left damp too long. A blockage that is pulled out too eagerly. A basin that looks clean but has gone too still. If the room becomes too loud, I will answer with condensation that makes the threshold slippery. If it becomes too dry, I will answer with a crack in a clay vessel no one noticed, or with a leak that begins where no one wants a leak. If the surface world sends rain without warning, I will make the hall remember where rain has been before, whether the builders like it or not. Sometimes a new pipe must be added, sometimes a hood closed, sometimes the Grinding Pot scraped, sometimes someone must stand beneath a light shaft and tilt their face to the made ceiling until the first cold drops strike them there, not as accident, but as return.

I am also beautiful, though not in the way of open skies.

There is a kind of beauty in a thing that has been repaired until it knows the hands that repair it. In a room where every surface has a use and almost no use goes unassigned.
