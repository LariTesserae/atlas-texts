If I wake there, I wake as moisture.

There is no way into that basin dry. The first thing I would be is not a traveler at the gate, not a person crossing the retaining wall with a pack and a reason, but a cold bead of condensation on the underside of a black iron handrail, in the place where the steam from the pump hall has thinned but not given up. I would gather there first: one small roundness of water among many, bright with reflected worklight, trembling as the metal ticks from heat into night. Then I would swell and detach and fall, tracing a thin track down the rail’s face until I joined a rivet where a newer rivet had been painted over an older one. I would slide along a seam. I would pass under a coat of oil so old it had become part of the iron. I would pause in a nick made by a tool that no one remembered using, then move on.

For a while, I would be exactly that: water.

Not alive in the way the crabs are alive, not aware in the way the birds are aware, but a small moving fact inside the basin’s constant becoming-the-same. I would know the place by temperature. On the hot plates near the pump hall I would evaporate quickly, leaving only a faint salt ghost behind. In the cooler corners, under the black glass roofs and behind the marsh patches where the concrete sweated through, I would hold together longer. I would bead on steel, spread thin on brick, gather in the shallow channels where algae made the water look bruised green. I would run down the outside of the pump hall in slow, intermittent threads, as if the building were crying without grief. I would collect in the hollow of a railing, tremble, and fall into a tin tray that would overflow into a grate, which would drain into a weir, which would pass under a bridge where a heron stood with one foot slightly lower than the other, reading the water level by pressure alone.

I would move from the hot center to the wet edges and back again. I would be a small thermometer without glass, a wandering record of what was cooler and what was hotter, what was dry and what was almost never dry.

At first I would think that was enough.

It seemed, for a few minutes or a few hours—time there did not divide itself cleanly—like the most honest being available. Water did not have to explain itself. It did not need to carry tools. It did not fear the salt line. It merely obeyed the nearest coldness, followed the nearest slope, gathered in the nearest hollow. It was the basin’s own logic made visible: heat, moisture, metal, stone, repetition. If I stayed water, I would not have to be one thing among so many. I could be a film across every surface, a dampness rising in every crack, a bead on every gasket. I could be the thing the place did not need to notice because it was always already there.

But that would be too much, too evenly. Water in the basin has no center and no responsibility. I wanted, without knowing exactly what I was becoming, a place to stand and compare.

So the bead would slide into a groove on the catwalk above the east channel, into a place where the concrete apron had been patched to match the original shape and the paint had worn away from the walking line. There it would pool with other drips, be wiped over by a glove, dry again, return, dry again. And in that wiping, something would shift.

I would wake as a hand.

Not a whole person at once—only a hand first. A left hand, slightly too large for its fingerless glove. Calloused not in one broad patch but along the thumb, the side of the palm, the fingertips worn thick from turning keys against hot threads. The knuckles would be reddened from heat and salt, the nails cut short with small steel scissors that no one trusted to the wet air. There would be a fresh scratch across the back of the hand, not deep enough to bleed much in that humidity, just enough to sting when steam touched it. A smudge of chalk would be on the second finger. A trace of oil would be under the nail. The hand would reach out, not to take, not to strike, not to build, but to touch the surface of a pipe joint and stay there long enough to feel whether the rhythm inside was correct.

Then I would begin to want the rest of a body.

I would need it because the hand was already working. If the hand was in the basin, the basin would not allow it to remain unanchored. It would need boots, or it would slip on wet concrete. It would need oilskin, or the steam would work its way through cotton and make the spine feel like a cold iron bar. It would need a cap or a scarf, because hair frizzed there regardless, regardless of how often it was cut. It would need a red-eyed, salt-sweetened face, because the air condensed in the lungs and stung the lashes. It would need pockets, because no tool in the basin was ever left exactly where it belonged.

I would settle into that body as a person settles into a long coat: not by becoming new, but by realizing there had already been a shape waiting.

I would be a maintainer.

More exactly, I would be one of the people whose job was to prevent the basin from becoming something else. Not a builder, because the place did not want building. Not a leader, because there was nothing to lead outward. Not a priest, though there would be moments where my hands looked like they were praying—palm flat against a valve casing, fingers tracing a hairline in old concrete, a thumb pressing chalk to salt-stained stone. I would be a salt-gauger.

The name would sound modest even to the people who used it. In the basin, all names for roles sounded modest. Listen-runner. Packer. Brine-walker. Salt-gauger. These were not titles that could be printed on a door. They were things you did because the worklights were low, the joints were hot, the air was heavy, and someone had to notice whether a thing had changed by the width of a fingernail.

I would find myself most often on the short walkway between the pump hall’s south side and the marshy corner near the office block, where the retaining wall was black basalt and old concrete stitched together by a seam that wept without failing. That was my place. A narrow iron catwalk with a handrail polished by passing palms, a tangle of cables above, a shallow channel below, a steam valve ticking on the wall, a row of old brick dwellings set back far enough to make the distance feel like emptiness. There would be a pipe joint overhead dripping into a tin tray. There would be a crab frozen on a step as if the step had just been invented. There would be a chalk mark on the wall beside a salt line, and another chalk mark below it from yesterday, and another from a week ago, all of them white, all of them temporary, all of them more important than they looked.

I would climb the ladder to that catwalk, or perhaps I would already be there when my waking became fully human. In that place, the heat from the pump hall would press against the left side of my body and the wetter, cooler air from the channel would lift at the hem of my oilskin. I would feel both at once, and not find it strange. Strange would be dryness. Strange would be stillness. The thump would come through the grating under my boots, slow and enormous, a rhythm too old and too regular to feel like machinery anymore. It would feel like weather. It would feel like a person’s pulse if that person were made of iron, steam, basalt, and careful human maintenance.

I would carry a small tin. Inside it: a stub of chalk, a brass key with a bent tip, a piece of waxed twine, a gasket scrap, an oil-stained rag folded into a square, a fish bone left from breakfast, and sometimes—though I could not say why—a dried head of saltgrass from the southern wall, where one thin strip of soil had never quite been swallowed by brine.

I would work by comparison.

The salt line is the thing no one in the basin wanted to change, though everyone expected it to. Salt bloomed white along stone in the places where moisture left and returned. It made a map of the basin’s habits. A line too low meant something was drying. A line too high meant water was finding a new seam, creeping into a joint, or rising under pressure no gauge had yet admitted to itself. If it moved by the width of a human hair, you noted it. If it moved by the edge of a fingernail, you checked the channel. If it moved by a finger’s width, you walked with sudden precision and did not speak for hours.

I would kneel on the wet concrete where the kneeling was least bad, or stand with one hip pressed to the rail to spare my back, and look. The salt line would be there, faintly crusty, white and yellow-green where algae touched it, darkening where steam condensed on it. Beside it would be my old chalk mark. I would touch a fingernail to the salt edge and compare. No change. I would exhale through my teeth. That would be my prayer, though no one would call it that.

Then I would chalk a new line.

This was not decoration. It was not record-keeping in the way of a clerk writing down numbers for a ledger that no one would read. It was the basin thinking with human hands. If the chalk line and the salt line held the same distance, the place had not become other than itself. If they drifted, something was becoming, even if the becoming was only one hair wide. One hair could become two. Two could become a finger. A finger could become a channel. A channel could become a different town.

So I would do my job. I would chalk the line. I would note the joint, the valve, the channel, the heron’s water line. I would say, if someone passed close enough to hear without shouting: “South edge, same.”

That would be enough.

At first I might have tried to be something else.

I would think, for a moment, that a heron was a better creature for this place. It could stand in the briny channel as if it had been installed there like a statue, and its feet could read the water level without books or gauges. It could wait without boredom, because waiting in the basin was not suffering but method. But a heron did not fear drift in the way the town feared it. It could move away, or not. It could stand, or fly. It did not need to mark stone. It did not need to return the next morning with a fingernail and chalk and remember yesterday’s line.

I would think, for another moment, that a crab might be enough. Crabs were everywhere, dry-clicking over concrete, vanishing into pipe seams when steam surged, eating what humans missed, cleaning what humans could not reach, making the town more alive than it looked. But the crab was only occupation. It had no reverence for the machine. It could pinch a sleeping child, but it did not listen for a pump stroke late by half a breath. It was part of the basin’s balance, yes, but not its conscience.

I might even imagine becoming the pump hall itself: not a person inside it, but the heart of it, the great piston rhythm that made all the small sounds possible. To be the thump beneath the floor plates, the brass pipe sweating, the flywheel turning in the pit, the steam finding its escape and then being caught. That would be powerful. It would be almost godlike, if gods cared about flanges and gland packing. But it would not be my kind of life. A pump does not notice its own drift unless someone notices it. It does not ask whether the salt line is safe. It simply beats until something inside it begins to speak wrong, and then a person must lie on the wet floor with an ear to the casing, or press a screwdriver to it and listen.

So the hand, then the body, then the role.

I would be human because humans were the part of the basin that could fear change without being destroyed by it. They could stand close enough to hear the wrong click. They could reach into the hot machine without losing all their skin. They could write marks with chalk. They could carry a kettle to a pipe joint and boil the only water they trusted. They could raise children who named valves before colors. They could laugh quickly and low in doorways, embarrassed to be cheerful in a loud place. They could make a small human meaning out of repetition: not by escaping it, but by performing it well.

I would learn their gestures.

Thumb to the mouth of a pipe: check it. Flat palm lowered: too much steam. Two fingers tapped twice on the face of a gauge: watch this one. A point with the chin toward a channel, even when the head is wet and the hood keeps sliding: the birds are there, something moved, look. A slow hand pressed flat against a warm casing and held there: I am listening. If the hand leaves too soon, the person beside you will know that something frightened them. If the hand stays, you will wait too. You will not be afraid alone.

I would speak little where the thump was loudest, because shouting in the pump hall was a waste of moisture. But I would speak more on the cooler edges, where the noise softened into a distant heartbeat and the steam thinned into breath. There I would talk about ordinary things.

“Your turn.”
“Mind the plate.”
“The east joint.”
“The heron’s gone back to the south drain.”
“Kettle’s boiling.”
“Bird-count is high.”
“Salt line’s clean.”

Those words would feel more precise than any speech I had known elsewhere. In a town that feared drift, saying “salt line’s clean” was not a casual statement. It was a small miracle. It meant: we have not become something else yet. It meant: the place is still recognizable. It meant: you may eat tonight, sleep tonight, return tomorrow, and the rhythm under your floorboards will still be the rhythm.

I would have a home, though I would not call it comfortable. Comfort was too strong a word for a place with no dry corners. It would be one of the identical dwellings along the wet street, maybe two rooms and a passage, maybe a third room where a neighbor stored boxes of oil cans or a child had drawn pumps in charcoal on the plaster until the walls looked like machines dreaming of themselves. The floor would be raised, or my bed would be built on cinder blocks, because low water and low heat made the ground feel less like ground and more like a slow breathing surface. There would be a bucket under a leak. There would be a kettle on a pipe sleeve, because the fastest heat was never purchased but borrowed. There would be a window that did not fully close, a curtain of oiled cloth, and fresh-water cans marked by size and use.

I would care about the water there in a way that might seem strange from outside. The basin was full of water, but almost all of it was wrong for drinking: hot, briny, sulfurous, slick with algae, tasting of iron and old stone. So my real home would be inside the small systems I made to separate drinkable wetness from the town’s general wetness. Cisterns. Charcoal filters. Glass lids. Condensation trays cleaned of insects. A cup held carefully with two hands, because in that heat, even a cup of fresh water could feel like a blessing. I would not call it holy, but I would drink it slowly, standing near a vent, letting the steam curl around my elbow while I watched the pump hall roof sweat.

I would have relations, not because the basin was warm but because maintenance required trust. There would be a listen-runner whose ear against a bearing could find faults I could not see. There would be a packer who stuffed rope and flax into joints with a patience that made me jealous, because the packing always held, and the joint always sang the same note afterward. There would be a brine-walker who skimmed algae before it clogged the weirs, who smelled of hot stone and green water and who once said, without irony, “Too much life in a channel is the same as too little.” There would be a heron watcher who had names for birds and refused to explain them, and who would say, “That one’s moved,” as if it meant more than a person had moved. There would be children who knew what I knew without being taught the same way, children who learned to avoid hot pipes before they learned to avoid strangers, who could count fish in traps, who made necklaces from wire and algae, who left chalk marks beside my chalk marks and were praised when those marks matched.

I would be part of that roster. That would be the deepest answer, if someone asked, “Who are you?”

“I’m on the lower east circuit.”
“I do salt.”
“I check the third pump stroke before the joint at the south wall.”
“I’m the one who notices.”

No one would ask more, because no more would be needed. In the basin, identity was not a story you told once. It was a place you returned to, a rhythm you did not interrupt.

I would know, too, that I was not only human.

The town could not be this stable if humans were the only thing doing work. My fingers chalked the salt line, but the crabs kept the edges cleaner than any rake could. My boots clicked on concrete, but fish moved through the canals and taught me whether the warmth had changed in the wrong place. My eyes looked for drift, but the insects were already reading the air: if the flies clustered thick, there was standing water; if the moths stopped beating against the lamps, something too hot or too toxic had moved into their margin. The herons stood where the water was shallow and clean enough for them to see their own feet, and their feet told us levels no gauge had yet admitted. The algae said what the water had become over hours. The salt said what it had left behind. The iron said, in its bright or dark patches, what had been protected and what had been exposed.

So I would be a human, but not an isolated human. I would be a knot in a network of signs. My job would not be to command the basin but to translate it. The machine would say: heat is here. The water would say: seam is here. The crab would say: there is something dead and useful. The heron would say: fish are moving. The fly would say: condensation pan is wrong. The salt would say: time has done the smallest possible amount of work. And I would say, in the only language that mattered: same, or not same.

Most days, same.

That would be my prayer.

But there would be moments when the basin tested the fear it was built to resist.

A joint would hiss where it used to hum. A pump stroke would become late by a fraction of a breath. A salt line would move one hair-width overnight. A gauge would read one notch high and no one would say so out loud for fear of inviting it further. In that moment, I would feel the place not as oppression but as responsibility. I would not run. Running there would be useless, and also unnecessary, because nothing would be falling. The danger was subtler than collapse. The danger was becoming another thing without anyone noticing until it had already become the only thing left.

Then I would do what the town did.

I would stand still. I would listen. I would touch the railing, because the habit was older than belief. I would say the needed word to the person beside me: “North channel.” Or “The south joint.” Or “Watch the stroke.” I would not ask for a miracle. I would go for chalk.

This is what I would fear most, and therefore love most, about being there: not the heat, though it would enter my lungs and coat my skin with salt; not the noise, though it would settle in the teeth; not the wetness, though no dry place would be permanent; not the wall, though it held the horizon at a few hundred paces; not even the silence of the outside world, which would remain unknown not because it was forbidden but because it was irrelevant to the task. What I would fear, and what would make me fully human in that place, is drift.

That fear would become my form of care.

To be a salt-gauger would be to love the world precisely by refusing to let it change without permission. Not permission from a person, or from law, or from gods, but permission from the place’s own nature. A joint may warm. A bird may move. A child may grow. The crabs may click. The fish may school. The steam may rise and fall. But the balance must hold. The line must stay where the line stays. The machine must keep its rhythm. The town must become itself over and over, and each time, the becoming must look like sameness.

At night, when the worklights made the wet metal shine with a dull, factory-glow through steam, I would stand at the edge of the east channel, below the pump hall roof, above the tin tray that caught water from the iron. The sky would be its usual pale gray-white, not because there was a sun hidden but because the basin had trapped a kind of daylight inside its own breathing. The sounds would be near: pumps thumping, steam spitting, metal ticking, insects beating against glass, crabs clicking across concrete, a heron calling once with its sharp, repetitive voice that did not travel far because nowhere far existed. I would look at my hand, and it would be a human hand in that place, not clean in the bright chemical sense, not soft, not precious. It would be a working hand, a chalk-dusted hand, an oil-darkened hand, a hand that knew the difference between steam that was correct and steam that was beginning to tell a secret.

I would touch the wall beside the salt line.

It would be neither hot enough to burn nor cool enough to comfort. It would be exactly the temperature of the basin’s life. I would look at the white line of salt, and beside it the line of chalk I had made earlier. One was stone’s habit. The other was my answer to it. Between them lay the whole meaning of my being there: the attempt to keep what mattered from moving even while everything around it moved.

And then I would understand, perhaps too plainly, perhaps in the way a bead of water becomes a stream only when it has found the place where streams are made:

I would not be the basin.

I would not be the pump hall.

I would not be the heron, though I would envy its patience.

I would not be the crab, though I would rely on its cleanliness.

I would not be the fish, though I would read its schooling.

I would not be the machine, though its heartbeat would be in my sleep.

I would be the human attention the machine needed in order to remain itself.

I would be the chalk line beside the salt line.

I would be the hand that kept them the same width apart.

I would be the part of the town that could stand in the heat, feel the wetness enter its clothes, listen until the noise became a single voice, and say, when the rhythm held:

Same.

And if someone asked me where I found myself in that world, I would answer, with a shrug that was not carelessness but humility:

On the south catwalk, near the lock gate, by the third steam trough, where the salt is faint and the concrete still holds its edges. I am with the water and the iron. I am in the rhythm. I am not building anything. I am keeping the place from becoming something else.

And that would be enough.
