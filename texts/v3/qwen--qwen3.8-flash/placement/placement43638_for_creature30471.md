I would not arrive as a traveler. That would be too sudden, too wet, too full of breath.

I would begin as a place becoming capable of asking.

At first, there would be no body. There would only be a patch of substrate on the flank of a salt ridge where the heat tide ran slightly deeper than usual, where a few microscopic struts had become arranged into a pattern that persisted after the magnetic weather moved on. The land was so old, so densely made, that even being a “self” could begin as an incidental geometry. Some lattice-cells opened in the sideways light and fed on charge. Other cells sealed as the warmth deepened. Dust moved over them in coherent sheets, and the dust carried information: trace compounds, electrostatic signs, the faint memory of a ridge shape repeated a thousand kilometers away.

I would be, for a long while, one of those small active orders. Not an animal. Not a person. Just a slight tendency toward persistence.

If that can be called a life, then my first feeling would be mineral. My first need would be balance. My first fear would be unnecessary motion. In this world, that makes sense. A fast vibration is a waste. A loud sound is a failure of architecture. A stumble that cracks the brittle surface is an insult to the whole made plain. So I would learn silence not as emptiness, but as care.

I would know heat before I knew form.

Not the heat of burning. Not the heat that threatens flesh. Here heat is a medium, a grammar. It arrives from the bright star high enough to feel near, but it also rises from below through nested layers of engineered stone. The warmth would enter my earliest self as opening: crystal faces shifting angle, salt blooms prying at microscopic joints, metal veins emitting a note too low to name. When the heat tide deepened, I would seal. When it thinned, I would open and take the light.

I would not thirst. There would be no water to remember. If I ever imagined thirst, it would not be liquid. It would be a dry demand: a mismatch between charge and salt, between thermal chamber and surface angle, between the dust I had absorbed and the dust the ridge required.

For a time, I would mistake myself for a plant.

This would happen because there was a crease in the salt flank where the dense lattice below thinned, just enough. I would draw upward, forming pale rods, panels of translucent refractory polymer, little crystalline sheets angled to catch light from every direction. I would not photosynthesize. I would translate. Light would enter, be folded through inner channels, and be stored in dry chemistry. I would build lattice structures that looked like roots, branches, bones, because all growing things borrow shapes from the worlds that feed them.

If I had been alone, I might have been an organism. But here the term is uncertain. My roots would be colonies. My panels would contain thousands of microscopic nodes, some dormant, some awake, some exchanging minerals with the made soil. My “death” would not be rot. It would be loss of charge, then fragmentation, then return. The ground would receive me the way a loom receives thread.

And yet I would want a body that could speak.

Not a wet voice. Not a soft cry. But the dry speech of plate vibration, ground-tapping, magnetic modulation, heat pulses, the faint clicking of salt along a ridge. I wanted to be able to say: this place is stable. That place is not. Step here. Wait here. The dust ahead is false.

So after the plant-life, after long slow cycles of opening and sealing, I would begin to wear a shape that looked back at the horizon.

For a while, I thought I would be a ceramic beetle the size of a hand, its shell reflecting narrow bands of light so that it could vanish into the salt flats. I liked the idea of moving slowly across the made plain, feeding on micro-ecologies, leaving impressions that heat would soon anneal smooth. But I would not choose it. It seemed too solitary in the way of objects, not the way of minds.

I thought briefly of being one of the broad-headed grazers, pressing a wide mouth to the ground and filtering the world layer by layer, consuming dust, spores, salt, lichen, engineered film. Their patience would have been beautiful. But their stillness was too much like the land’s stillness, and I had already been a piece of land.

I considered the film-winged light-keepers, drifting through amber air like slices of sky given edges. To feed on light and suspended mineral, to scatter prismatic sparks when the wings angled correctly, would be a kind of prayer without god. I wanted air above my body and distance under me. But the light-keepers felt too much like absence. I did not yet want to disappear into brightness. I wanted to remain a thing that could be noticed.

Then there were the stalkers, lying beneath ridges with limbs spread wide so the brittle surface would not crack. I could imagine the patience of that. The silence of mineral breaking down into mineral. The one soft click against carapace. But I could not bear to be hunger without apology. In this world, violence is quiet, yes, but I did not want to live by quiet violence. I wanted to live by quiet coordination.

So I would become one of the Annealed.

Not immediately. Not all at once. The transformation would be more geological than emotional. I would spend long cycles in shallow basins where heat and charge arranged me into chambers, into limbs, into a face-panel smooth and faceted. I would develop shoulders that distributed weight, hands with long mineral fingertips able to tap the ground and send small pressure waves through the substrate. My skin would layer like pale ceramic. Along the back of my neck, scales would tile themselves. In my chest, nested heat reservoirs would fill with clear, warm fluid like oil or silicate suspension. I would have no blood as wet animals know it. My circulation would be a dry river, carrying dissolved salts, charges, nutrients, memories.

I would grow a rosette garden on my neck, because here ornament is also sensor and store. It would be made of crystalline collars and refractory blooms that opened only during certain heat phases. When they clicked softly in the afternoon light, I would know the world was preparing to change.

My dwelling would not be built so much as found.

I would live in a fold of terrain beneath a salt ridge where the surface gave faintly, not because it was weak, but because it was a membrane over deeper structure. The dwelling would consist of a chamber, a corridor of fused mineral glass, and a basin worn circular by long use. No one could say whether the basin had once been a reactor, a reservoir, a foundation, or simply a place where the substrate thinned and became hospitable. To me, those distinctions would be irrelevant. The land used it. I used it. The dust moved through it. The light was shaped by it.

Inside, walls of nested crystal would bend sunlight into cooler angles. The rooms would remain dry, luminous, quiet. The floor might hum when magnetic weather passed overhead. A low note would seem to rise from the ground and settle inside my skull, not frightening but familiar, like a name spoken by the world.

My kindred would be small.

Not because I disliked company, but because life here is sparse by design and patience by necessity. A kindred may consist of two adults and one young lattice, or three adults sharing a ridge-side basin, or a single traveler accompanied by a cultivated film-garden growing from a ceramic pouch at the hip. Mine would be a kindred of three: myself, an old heat-keeper whose scales had worn so smooth they looked like ancient roof tile, and a seedling in a basin near the chamber’s deepest quiet.

The seedling would not be mine in the way wet creatures mean parentage. I would not have made it from water or warmth or softness. It would begin as a lattice-globule no larger than a fist, placed in a warm basin where dormant filaments waited. We would feed it with light-charged dust. We would carry trace elements in dry channels. We would regulate its temperature by angling our bodies near it, by tapping the floor to guide heat, by exposing reflective panels when the light came too hard.

It would grow inward before outward. Support lattices first. Then heat chambers. Then sensory plates. Then limbs. Years would pass before it became visible as a body. More years still before it became a person. I would watch its slow emergence with the particular tenderness of this dry world: no embraces, no tears, no stories whispered by firelight. Only stable basins, polished tiles, and the repeated lesson of patience.

I would speak by tapping the ground with one long mineral finger. The old Annealed would answer with a plate-hum that rose from the floor itself. The seedling would not reply for cycles, but when it did, its signal would be faint and curious: two short clicks, one long, then a modulation of heat through the chamber wall. We would all hear it. In this world, hearing is not merely ear. It is body, substrate, charge, temperature.

We would not shout. We would not run unless the land required motion. Even conflict between us would be low, careful, like two objects nudged apart. If we disagreed, we would shift into different densities of the basin. If we needed each other, we would stand close enough for our heat chambers to resonate. If one of us was dying, the others would prepare the annealing.

That thought would shape everything.

Because when I died, there would be no rot, no wet funeral, no burial in soft earth. The body would be placed in a shallow basin. The heat tide would finish the drying. Electrostatic combs would separate the mineral structures. Salt bloom would gently crack what could become tile. The bones would become lattice rods. The scales would become surface tiles. The clear residue of circulation would be drawn into channels and transformed into dust. What remained would not be a corpse but a distribution. The kindred would then etch the name into a flat tile or press it into the pattern of a wall with a stylus that clicked like a small tooth.

The land would keep what was.

I would find myself, then, at the bend of a salt ridge where the horizon repeats its patterns with unsettling accuracy. Not exactly the same microtexture. Never exactly. But the ridge shape would appear again and again across hundreds of kilometers, as if the world were writing a sentence with variations, as if architecture had become weather had become law. There would be broad terraces of interlocking tiles worn smooth by age. There would be pale veins that were not mineral deposits but dormant circuit lines. There would be hollow-looking stones containing nested geometries too regular for accident.

I would be a traveler between these repetitions.

Not a conqueror. Not a surveyor in the old imperial sense. Not someone who names a place into ownership. I would be a cartographer of heat, a reader of dust, a keeper of stable basins. I would know which ridges could be stepped on and which would give. I would know when salt crystals are about to bloom, because the air will hold a faint pressure in the chest and the distant metal plates will hum. I would know the difference between true dust and false dust: the dust that settles into footprints before anyone has walked, the dust that carries old patterns from magnetic weather, the dust that is merely waiting.

My tools would not look like tools.

A static brush would be grown, not manufactured. A heat tile would be polished until it became a memory device. A stylus would be a fragment of my own shed scale. My garments would be cultivated: sheets of refractory polymer, translucent panels, layered dust-cloth, living crystalline collars that grew outward from my skin when tended. On my back, perhaps, a small garden of dry flowers. When I rested in a basin, those flowers would open into thin crystalline sheets and click faintly, reporting the heat tide to my kindred without any need for speech.

I would carry no water.

The absence would not feel like deprivation. It would feel like completeness. I would understand the world without moisture, and in that understanding there would be a strange peace. No sea. No river. No hidden dampness. No soft mud. No rain to blur the light. Here, dryness is not barrenness. Dryness is clarity.

The light would often deceive me.

At midday, the salt flats would glow so bright that shadows would still hold warmth. Distance would be difficult. A ridge that seemed close would be far. A pale object on the ground might be hovering in a column of refracted dust. I would learn to trust heat before vision, pressure before distance, sound before image. I would learn to look away from the horizon because the horizon is where the world becomes too large for ordinary life to occupy. The horizon is beautiful. The horizon is also a warning.

To find myself, I would look down.

I would lie flat against the fused ceramic surface and feel the faint pressure moving beneath me. I would hear the whispering tick in my ears, the sense that the plain is not merely solid but arranged. A dust front moving across hundreds of kilometers would sound like sand poured through silk. Salt crystals along a ridge would click. The magnetic weather would pass over me and make the ground hum a note too subtle for the ear to locate exactly. I would feel that note inside my own chest cavity and know that I had been listening long enough to be recognized by the world.

Sometimes, at the bend of a ridge, a ceramic-shelled creature would lift its head and regard me with faceted, reflective eyes. It would not flee. It would assess. It would read my weight distribution, my heat signature, my dust load, my mineral balance. If I stood still enough, slow enough, non-vibrating enough, it might return to the ground and consume a micro-ecology of salt, dust, and engineered spores. I would not disturb it. I would watch it move away, its feet making a faint tick of ceramic on ceramic, and then the plain would resume its slow heat migration as if nothing had happened.

Sometimes a film-wing would cross my shadow. It would look like a fragment of bright air temporarily given edges. If it angled its transparent wings at the correct moment, light would scatter into small prismatic sparks and I would think, for an instant, that the world had become jewelry. Then it would drift away, and I would remember that beauty here is not soft. It is severe, quiet, and made of survival.

Sometimes I would see another Annealed standing in a fold of terrain, so perfectly matched to the pale mineral skin of the world that I would pass it twice before turning back. When it moved, the motion would feel enormous because intention had entered the landscape. It would regard me. It would tap the ground once, not as greeting, but as a request: *may I step where you stand?* Or as a warning: *the surface ahead is too dense with dormant lattice.* I would understand. I would wait.

If a traveler came from somewhere else, I would not offer comfort in familiar terms.

There would be no cup of water. No soft bed. No fire to huddle beside. I would not know what to do with their wet grief. If they wept, I would watch the salt dust at the corners of their face and understand that something impossible had occurred. But I would not reach for the wrong comfort. I would do what is done here: I would lead them to a stable basin. I would show them where the substrate will bear weight. I would give them a small polished tile that records heat flow. I would allow them to rest in the shadow of a refractory plant that bends light into cooler wavelengths.

That would be hospitality.

I would not try to make them feel at home. I would try to make them legible to the world, and the world legible to them. I would teach them to touch the ground and distinguish dormant lattice from active crystal. I would teach them to read heat shadows. I would teach them which dry flowers signal stable dust and which signal migration fronts. I would teach them how to approach a grazer without alarming it, how to stand near a stalker-ridge without mistaking stillness for absence, how to notice the metal plates beneath the surface begin to hum when magnetic weather is shifting. Knowledge here would not be domination. It would be coordination. To live would be to find the thin places in a dense world and become useful to the patterns already moving there.

I would become old not as frailty, but as surface wear.

My outer scales would smooth. My internal heat chambers would accumulate mineral patterns. My face would become more carved than fleshed. My voice by tapping would become deeper, more resonant, as if the ground had learned my name before my kindred did. I would become difficult to distinguish from the landscape. This would not frighten me. It would be completion.

At the end, if someone asked where I had found myself, I would not point to the horizon.

I would point to the place where construction has become erosion.

The place where the made surface no longer reads as made.

The place where heat is not danger but language.

The place where dust knows where it belongs.

The place where sparse life grows in creases, not because the land is barren, but because the land is full.

The place where silence is composed of many small absorptions.

The place where a dry flower blooms into clicking crystal during the right heat phase.

The place where an Annealed stands beside a basin and a seedling begins, slowly, to agree to become a body.

The place where the world behaves like a machine that has forgotten it is a machine and has begun to behave as a body.

There, at the bend of a salt ridge, I would be.

Not ruler. Not master. Not exile. Not explorer.

I would be a kindred of three, a heat-keeper, a ridge-listener, a dry cartographer of the made plain. I would be an Annealed person, but also the ground’s own slow noticing. I would be a tile with a future, a basin with a name, a garden growing from the back of my neck. I would be a being whose body is made of instruction fossilized into habit, whose speech is pressure through substrate, whose death is redistribution, whose life is patience.

And when the magnetic weather passed and the metal plates beneath the surface began their low tone, I would stand very still beside the seedling and let the sound move through me from floor to skull. Then I would tap once, lightly, with one long mineral finger.

Not to call the world.

The world would already be answering.

I would only be reminding it that someone had learned how to listen.
