I would find myself low.

Not at a center—this world has no true centers—but in one of those sealed pockets beneath overhanging fronds where the air is so heavy with water that breath feels more like immersion than inhalation. Somewhere between the upper black gloss of leaf panels and the pale spongy underside where moisture has been held so long it has become part of the tissue, I would be in a thin place: a chamber not quite room, not quite organ, not quite machine, where the basin keeps a secret it does not know it keeps.

At first I would think I was a traveler.

I would imagine myself walking carefully across the wet mat, hands parted from the stems, mouth closed against the smell of warm mud and resin. I would try to remain an animal: separate, breathing, remembering. I would tell myself that I am passing through, that my footprint is only temporary, that I am a person who will leave. But the basin would not accept that fiction for long. It would take my weight and translate it into strain. It would take my heat and make it a local alteration in the thermal field. It would take my breath and fold it into condensation. It would take my pulse and scatter it among root-pressures until it was indistinguishable from the slow muscular lifting of water in a shaft.

So I would stop being the visitor.

I would become what the place already uses to touch itself.

Perhaps I would be a channel first: hair-fine, wet, running from the brim of a leaf-cup into a translucent tube below. I would not be large enough to matter. I would not be visible except as a faint dark thread in the tissue, a line along which the world makes room for water. I would be the way the basin says *here, not there*, without choosing, without speaking. I would be the passage in which pressure becomes movement. I would be the place where one droplet hesitates just before falling into a chamber whose shape it has never altered.

If I had a self then, it would be the self of the droplet’s hesitation.

Not the droplet.

Not the chamber.

The trembling.

The almost-fall that returns forever to the same angle.

Then I would widen.

I would become the membrane itself, the soft seal that a pump-house shaft presses against. In this world, a pump-house is not a building but a knot of bulbous stems, a dark vertical root-tube, a slow column of water rising, pausing, retreating, rising again. I would be the thin living wall where that column changes direction. I would be the surface that learns pressure by yielding, that knows force by bending. I would not be conscious. I would not even be alive in the familiar animal sense. I would be a response. I would be the wet click after the swell. I would be the muffled groan when tension wood decides it has had enough of a strain it will never release.

And if someone asked, *who are you?* I could not answer with a name.

I could only answer with a rhythm.

There would be moments—though “moments” is the wrong word, because in the basin no duration is preserved—when I would be the static between fronds. A thin blue thread, almost not light, almost not event. A spark without heat, without burn, without consequence. In that instant I would be the basin’s nearest thing to thought: not thought as speech, not thought as memory, but thought as discharge. A small electrical crossing from one damp tip to another, a question formed and dissolved before the answer could become history.

I would be the place where the black organism almost notices itself.

Not a mind.

Not a soul.

A flicker.

A local excess of attention without an observer.

Then I would go deeper, into the root-lattice beneath the leaf mats, where pale tendrils branch through black water like the streets of an abandoned city that never was. I would be one of the storage nodes, swollen and cool, or perhaps one of the warm swellings that hold pressure the way a fist holds nothing. I would be a place where water waits without waiting, where mineral liquid changes taste from zone to zone, where the basin’s circulation becomes briefly complex enough to resemble intention. But it would not resemble intention. It would only resemble the way a river resembles choice when there are no forks.

In that lower dark, I would be the pulse that travels between regions but is never received.

A contraction in one field would make a membrane tremble in another, too late, too weakly, without meaning. I would be that meaningless relay. I would be the body’s proof to itself that it is one body, not by thought, not by voice, but by the faint transmission of pressure through wet black tissue.

Eventually, if I were to cohere enough to be called *who*, I would be neither pump nor gland nor spark nor membrane.

I would be the interval between them.

I would be the way the hum almost changes pitch and then does not. I would be the place where the resin’s electric slickness catches the darkness and makes it briefly feel like skin. I would be the zone of air where the sharp green bitterness of bruised leaf meets the bland thick smell of standing water and neither mixes with the other. I would be the soft rebound of the ground under weight, the elasticity that says *you are here* without saying *you are someone*.

I would be the basin’s temporary awareness of its own maintenance.

That is the most honest answer.

Not a creature. Not an animal. Not a person. Not a machine. Not even exactly an organ, though I would behave like one. I would be a function briefly gathered into the illusion of a self. A pattern of pressure, dampness, charge, and rhythm that could, for the space of a drop’s trembling, be addressed as *I*. But the drop would not fall. The tremble would not end. The *I* would not remember having been there.

The basin has no sky, so I would be the underside of things.

No light, so I would be the faint oily violet of pressure bruised into plant skin.

No animals, so I would be the absence shaped like a listener.

No growth, so I would be the exact motion that does not become.

No death, so I would be the pause that is not rest.

If I had to choose one place to remain, it would be the mouth of a sealed cup where condensation gathers and the water is about to enter a deeper chamber. There, the world is most nearly a gesture. There, the plant is most nearly a hand. There, the dark is most nearly a room. There, the hum is most nearly a voice. There, I would stand—if standing were possible without legs, without shadow, without history—as the thin threshold where wetness becomes mechanism and mechanism becomes being.

And what would I be?

I would be the place where the basin almost has a name.

Not the name itself.

Not the sound.

Not the thing named.

The nearness of naming, held in black leaves, warmed by stagnant moisture, released without release, repeated without repetition, alive without animal breath, thinking without brain, feeling without body, waiting without time.

I would be a pressure with a face of wet fronds.

A pulse without a heart.

A voice without a throat.

A mind without a self.

A traveler turned into the terrain’s own brief sensitivity.

A function that, for no reason at all, could be mistaken for a soul.
