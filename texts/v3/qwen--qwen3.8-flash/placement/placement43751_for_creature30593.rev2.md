I would find myself halfway down the subsidence, on the third terrace from the bottom, where the stone is new enough to cut and settled enough to be trusted. Not at the rim, where the wind makes everything feel temporary. Not in the lowest basin, where the walls shine with condensation and sleep is a slow argument with damp. But on that middle shelf of the town where daylight arrives ordinary and thin, where a family’s drying rack leans against a communal stair, and where a small pool of water decides, without consulting anyone, to change its habit overnight.

I would be there in the cool hour, when my breath begins to show if I stay too long in one place. My palm would be pressed lightly to a wall, not leaning, only asking: dry, damp, weeping? The stone would answer in temperature and shine. I would know, before the others did, that the shelf above had become dangerous, that the mat below must be moved, that the grain pot needs shifting toward the driest side of the niche. No one would thank me dramatically. That is not the way of the Making. Instead, a mother would nod from across the passage, a child would fetch another reed without being told, and the old man sitting near the brazier pit would tap his knees once, because he had been wrong about the seep this time and was willing to admit it in the smallest possible way.

At first I might try to imagine myself as something grander. I might want to be the shaft in the ceiling, the square-cut mouth through which ordinary light falls like white water onto shelves full of pots. But a shaft is only a hole. It receives weather. It does not carry a load. It cannot decide where to place a drying cloth before the air closes its mineral grip.

I might try to be the water itself, threading through pores in pale limestone, emerging where no one expects it, turning a floor into a mirror and a sleeping platform into a complaint. But water in this place is not free. Water is diverted, lined, sealed, raised, caught, directed, dried. To be water here would be to be negotiated with at every step. I would want more than that. I would want a body that could respond to water with care, not merely be its consequence.

I might try to be the newest stair, the one cut yesterday, still carrying diagonal scratches from picks and blunt scars from wedges. That stair would have purpose. It would lead somewhere. It would be used at once by feet that had no choice but to use it. But stairs are made to be passed over. They hold weight without knowing when the weight is wrong. They are essential, yes, but not fully alive.

I might try to be a tool: a wedge with a worn steel or copper point, a scraper edged by thumb after thumb, a chisel whose handle has darkened where hands have pressed in fear and patience. A tool would carry a person’s labor without needing to carry their quarrels. That would be tempting. But tools in this town are always waiting to be set down, and the waiting is part of their life. I would not want only to be set down. I would want to be set down in the right place, and then remembered.

I might try to be the fungus bed, growing pale and patient in the recess where the wall weeps steadily. That life would be nourishing, dependent, quietly productive. A family would check it each morning. They would scrape away bitter mold, harvest only when the caps had cooled to the proper temperature, and speak to it as one speaks to something that provides without asking for thanks. I might admire that life. But it would be too easy. The fungus only accepts. The town requires choices.

No. I would be a person. Not a hero, not a priest, not a keeper of some ancient secret beneath the plateau. The people here would embarrass anyone who tried to make them mystical. They are too busy testing plaster for that. They do not need guardians of lost wisdom. They need someone who will notice which lintel is softening, which niche is claimed, which route has become too narrow for a basket.

So I would be a water-read, or something close to that: a person known not by a family name alone but by the job the town has given their hands. They might call me Dry-Shelf, or Water-Read, or the one who hears the wall before it speaks. The name would not be fixed. Names here would shift like routes depending on who needed me. To a child, I would be the one who moves the drying rack without a word when the ceiling begins to stain. To a new arrival from the surface, I would be the warning. To an elder, I would be the successor who has not yet earned the right to be tired.

My body would slowly become practical. Not because I would train to be beautiful, but because the town would teach me how to move. My steps would shorten near wet stone. My shoulders would turn inward before entering passages crowded with hanging stores. My hands would become layered with different kinds of work: rough palms from rope, chalk-dusted fingertips, nails darkened by clay, small burns from fresh lime, calluses from wedges, and a thin scar near the wrist where a slipped tool had bitten into me before I learned to brace against the edge. My cheeks would be dry and faintly stained in winter. My cloak would smell of smoke-dried hide and herbs, not perfume. I would wear my belt as proof that I had a place in the order: a wedge pouch, a scraping stone, a bone needle, a cord, a clay cup. These things would not decorate me. They would say, when I stood still, *I am useful here.*

I would speak low, because stone repeats what it should not. I would learn the quiet grammar before I mastered the formal names. Two fingers on a wall: damp. A palm turned up: clear. A knuckle tapped twice against a shelf: do not put your food there yet. A small nod toward the stairs: wait. I would be fluent in what cannot be shouted. In the lower galleries, where a voice would return from three directions and disturb someone trying to seal a grain pot, I would communicate by eye, by hand, by the slight angle of a body leaning into a route. I would not call this silence romantic. It would simply be courtesy, and survival.

My days would be a long negotiation with small failures. I would wake where the mat had been placed the night before, perhaps low in a shallow hollow, perhaps raised on a timber shelf if water had crept upward. Before the shaft-light became strong enough to see by, I would check the walls. Not everywhere. Only where the town had been careless, or hopeful, or too tired to argue. A dry patch that seemed generous by morning might have hidden damp all night. A wall that had looked stable might begin to sweat when the air shifted. I would place my hand there and feel the difference between stone holding water and stone preparing to release it.

I would move pots. A great deal of my life would be spent moving pots. Not because there was drama in it, but because storage was life. A pot raised onto a dry platform could mean the difference between a season without illness and a week of spoiled meal. A pot placed too low near a runnel could soften the grain of another family. A pot closed with clay and ash but not sealed against beetle could become a nest of infestation. I would lift it carefully, wipe its base if damp had gathered there, and set it in a niche where the light would make the clay glow pale. Someone would glance at that niche and know I had been there. That glance would be enough.

I would argue too, though the arguments would rarely be about pride. They would be about routes and timing. A household wants the west shelf for drying reeds, but the reed smoke from the brazier nearby has begun to flavor the grain stored above it. A stair-keeper wants timber braced across a gallery, but that timber blocks the channel that keeps the sleeping platform dry. A new room smells of raw lime and someone has moved into it anyway because their child is coughing in the lower terrace. I would be involved. Not always as mediator, but often. I would have the sort of authority that comes from being right in small matters often enough to be listened to.

There would be a particular weight to my work when a surface became contested. An empty niche in the Making is not merely unused space. It is a question with no answer, a temporary wound in the town’s crowded order. People read niches the way outsiders might read faces. If a shelf had no pot, no tool, no hanging cord, no mat rolled against it, the town would assume it was waiting, or forbidden, or forgotten. By nightfall, if it stayed empty too long, someone would claim it. I would be careful with emptiness. I would leave marks: a single scraped line in plaster, a clay token, a cord looped around a peg, a small wedge of stone pressed into a groove. Nothing dramatic. Just enough to say: *do not yet.*

I would know the town through temperature. The upper terraces would be brighter, drier, almost generous; their air would smell of chalk and wood smoke kept small. The lower galleries would have a chill that clung to clothing and a faint taste of iron on the tongue. I would learn which rooms were pleasant at noon and unbearable at night, which stair wells gathered cold like water, which platforms received the shaft-light long enough to dry but not long enough to become hot. I would learn where breath fogged and where it did not, and I would use that knowledge without naming it. No one would need to explain to me why certain people slept high and certain stores stood low. The body would understand before the tongue did.

I would not worship stone, though I would be irritated by it with love. Fresh cut is honest and dangerous. It has no weathered patience. It gives no soft warnings. It is either sharp or damp or false. A new chamber might look like a gift until the plaster begins to curl from unseen moisture. A wall might seem strong until a rope drags a groove into it or a load settles too hard on a beam. I would learn to distrust what is too convenient. “You cut before the stone told you,” someone would say, and it would be an insult not because the builder was stupid, but because they had made impatience into risk.

I would be proud in tiny, practical ways. If a stair I helped mark was used without slipping, that would be a good day. If a runnel I directed carried water away from a sleeping mat for an entire season, that would be a quiet triumph. If a room I helped dry became occupied without argument, I would not celebrate it loudly. I would stand near the doorway, listening to the soft scrape of baskets and the murmur of people moving in, and feel a happiness almost too small to name. It would be the joy of temporary stability in a place that never stops testing itself.

I would share my life with beings that did not pretend to be important. In the cracks behind the grain shelf, blind or near-blind insects would move through dust. Beetles would gather near stored food, and if they reached the meal, they would become an emergency. Moths would rest in shaft-light on cool stone, pale and accidental. Lizards would appear on the upper terraces when warmth arrived, quick and useful, eating flies near the washing basins. Birds might cast a shadow through a shaft if the sky was bright enough to lure them far enough down. Bats would be rare here, noticed only if they passed too quietly through a too still room. I would not romanticize them. They would be part of the town’s margin, the wild thinness that human order had made small but not absent.

My household would not be only human. It would include fungus beds, baskets, pots, ropes, drying frames, mats, plaster, and the habits of those things. I would live inside a chain of agreements, some of which had no human names. The timber agreed to bear the shelf only if the damp kept the lintel from cracking. The lime plaster agreed to dry if the air permitted. The rope agreed to tighten only if someone remembered to watch the load settle. The people agreed to speak softly so that carriers did not slip. These agreements were not magical. They were merely necessary. If one was broken, the next break was already forming somewhere else.

If I were not fully human in this place, I might be the agreement itself. Not a spirit, not a ghost, not a god of the hollow, because none of those would help much when a cistern failed. But I would be a living threshold: the place where water and route meet, where a person’s hand decides whether to leave a niche open or claim it, where a pot becomes either storage or warning. I would be the pause before a family puts a mat down. I would be the difference between a room that holds and one that does not.

There are days when I would want to be a tool after all. A wedge does not have to apologize when the seam slips. A scraper does not have to choose which family gets the shelf. A cistern does not have to dream of dry floors and then wake to find one still wet. Tools can be pure purpose. But even tools are not free here. They belong to hands that argue. They are stored in places claimed by others. They wear out and must be repurposed. If a cutting edge breaks, it becomes a scraper, a weight, a chisel stub, a token pressed into plaster. Nothing is discarded unless it can be sent nowhere, and in the Making there is no nowhere.

I would know death here with the same careful attention I would give to water. A body would be carried to the surface rim, or placed in a chamber whose walls had not yet been assigned to sleep. Its absence would be made visible not by spectacle but by rearrangement. A niche would be covered. A tool would be broken into parts and given again. A mat would be burned or washed, depending on what had lived in it and what sickness had touched it. Grief would become practical, not because the dead were honored less, but because the living could not afford to leave empty space unclaimed for too long. I would understand that mourning, too, must be routed away from grain and sleep.

I would dream as the town dreams. No monsters. No floods. Only small failures: waking to find that the shelf you moved in your sleep is damp again, that the cistern is rising where it was not marked, that the stair you trusted has begun to crack under a weight no one thought would use it. These dreams would not be frightening because they were strange. They would be frightening because they were familiar. I would wake, test the wall, and say nothing, because the day would require other things.

In the end, I would find myself not at the center of the subsidence, because the center would be too crowded and too temporary, and not at its edge, because the edge would still belong to the plateau and the wind. I would be in the third terrace from the bottom, moving a pot, checking a lintel, leaving a mark. My name would change according to who needed to call me. My body would be compact with care. My humor would be dry and mineral. My authority would be limited to the places I had learned to watch. My happiness would be small enough to carry.

I would be one of the makers. Not because I was born to make, but because this place makes its people into makers or loses them. I would be a water-read, a route-negotiator, a shelf-keeper, a plaster-watcher, a child of the Making who had learned to listen before cutting. I would be a person, and the town would be inside me: the drip, the stair, the fungus bed, the hanging bundle, the low voice, the empty niche that no one yet dared claim.

If the question asks who or what I would be, the answer would not come in one shape. First: a visitor. Then: a role. Then: a habit. Finally: a place in the chain where a body and a wall and a water-track agree to last another season.

I would be the hand that catches the drip before it becomes a story.
