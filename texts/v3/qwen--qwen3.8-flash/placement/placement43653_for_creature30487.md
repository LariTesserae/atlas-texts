I would find myself where the floor forgets it is stone.

Not at an edge. Not in a safe alcove with a back wall behind me. I would be in a place where the ground is too alive to be ground, too wet to be dry, too warm to be inert: a broad uneven shelf beneath a ceiling of white mineral veins, the air heavy enough to carry the hiss of ten thousand legs, the light soft and ordinary as an afternoon that has never seen an afternoon. Somewhere between the dust that breeds things and the dust that swallows them. Somewhere between the hut no one expects to last and the column not yet decided whether it will stand or fall.

I would not first be a person.

At first, if a “me” could be extracted from that place, it would be nothing more than a condition of contact. Breath on stone. Mineral taste. Water finding a shallow groove in a hollow shell. The faint iron-and-yeast smell of a film spreading across a ledge where, a moment before, there was no film at all. I would be the part of the Underfield where the crustacean wave hesitates, where a blind bird tests the hardness of a wall with its beak, where a snail tongue reads the future in a track of condensation. Not a self. A meeting.

Then I would have a body.

Squat. Strong. Built for pressure rather than speed. Broad feet with thick pads and curved claws, able to lock onto a ledge while the stone around them sweats and shifts. Short legs, longer arms, hands wide and clumsy in the way a person might imagine clumsiness, though there is nothing clumsy in them when they know what they are doing. Four long shell-tipped fingers, a short opposing thumb, nails grown into hard plates that can pry from porous crust, crack stony backs, scrape a mineral ridge smooth with the patience of someone shaping a spoon from a hillside.

My skin would be pale but chalked, damp at the seams, shedding flakes over the shoulders and hips that I would rub off with one hand and maybe chew with the other. Not from hunger alone. Not from habit. Because the world is made of minerals and the body has learned to listen to what it is made of. A young piece of plate would taste like home. A bad piece would taste like a wall that has begun to die.

My face would be broad, flattened, not because it had ever needed to look far, but because there is no far here. No horizon. No distance to organize the eyes. I would see mostly shine: the wet gleam on a shell, the thin blue pulse inside an amber spiral, the white bloom of newly forming dust, the silver flash of eel-things flowing through a trickle not deep enough to swim in. I would know whether a chamber beyond a ridge was full of water by the way the air changed before the water touched me.

And I would listen.

This would be my main work.

Not thinking, not planning in any long, clean line from past to future. Listening. The Underfield does not permit long, clean lines. It permits only short, sharp rhythms, the kind that can be checked by hand. A pebble tapped against stone. A bill knocking a ledge. A shell falling. A column beginning to crack with a high dry note, or an old shelf failing with a duller, almost bone-deep sound. A chamber packed with birds thunders differently from one packed with crawling bodies. Wet sand gives way in a soft rush; dry powder slides with a whisper like salt being poured through cloth.

I would be one of the builders, though that word would make too much of me.

Builders are rare here. If we were many, the caves would have become something else: villages, roads, fields, ruins. But there are no fields because there are no plants, no roads because every surface is a market of bodies that does not permit a road, no ruins because nothing is ever finished long enough to decay gracefully. I would belong to a band of three, maybe five. Enough to lift a slab. Enough to watch a ledge while another sleeps. Enough to remember where the water came from yesterday.

My name would not be a name. It would be a rhythm adopted from the place: *tap-tap-drip-left*, or *shell-crack-on-soft-wall*, or *bird-beak-at-lintel*, depending on which sound my body made when I moved through the wet chamber where I was first found useful. I would answer to it because the world answers to it. If I stepped wrong, if I disturbed the film on a wall, if I caused a bead of water to fall, the sound would change, and so I would know who I was by the noise my being made in the place around me.

I would live near a half-finished hut.

Not a grand dwelling. Not a symbol. A low dome of split dark glass and dried shell, weighted with pebbles, plastered with a paste of mineral mud and burned bone. It would already be leaning because the ground beneath one corner had begun to soften. Or it might be nothing more than a fence of pointed stakes around a place where the air was warm and the floor was not yet alive with a crawling wave. I would raise it the way a coral raises itself: with available bits, with patience, with no certainty that the shape will survive the night.

Inside it, or rather beneath its edge, I would keep a shell scraper worn smooth by use. A flint blade with a battered edge. A bone awl. A handful of fire-hardened stakes whose points were dark with work. Tools that did not dream of permanence. Tools that expected to be left behind. I would not mourn their loss. A tool abandoned is only becoming part of the Underfield’s memory.

I would eat from the floor.

Crustaceans scraped from ledges in broad waves. Chalky mats tasted for safety, then made into paste with rendered water. A snail’s body if the shell was not too bitter. A bird’s fallen egg if found before another bill claimed it. A slow animal’s shed scale if someone could pry it free. Sometimes mineral flakes gnawed from my own arms when the body required it, or from a wall that had become a kind of food by accident. Nothing wasted. Nothing too clean. The line between eating and touching would be thin, as all lines here are thin.

I would know the difference between loneliness and absence.

Absence would be everywhere. There would be no green. No wood. No root pressing into dust. No vine trying to find light that was not light, only mineral sky. But the absence would not feel like grief. It would feel like weather. The Underfield is not empty because it has no plants. It is full because it has no plants to stand between the surfaces. Everything must make contact directly: skin to stone, shell to shell, leg to leg, breath to mineral film, bird-foot to ledge, body to body until the boundary between “floor” and “life” becomes uncertain.

I would be uncertain, too.

Some mornings—if mornings can be named by a change in the rhythm of a seep—I would feel not like a creature but like an organ of the cave. My foot would be a place where water pools. My shed flake would become a roof for burrowers. My footprint would hold a eel-thing curled in waiting. My shoulder would serve as a perch for a blind bird that knocks its bill against the ledge beside my ear until my thoughts become stone and sound.

I would not mind.

Minding is for places with horizons.

At night—or what passes for night, the slight drying of the air, the birds quieting their bills, the water shifting from drip to slow creep—I would lie with my back to a wall that might begin sweating at any moment. The stone would breathe around me. The floor would ripple with small bodies. Somewhere beyond the nearest ridge, a young column would crack white and fall, and the dust of it would become the dust of something else, which would become the dust from which something new emerged, which would become the floor that something else walked over, which would become the place where I had built a wall and now had to leave.

That is where I would find myself: not safe, not settled, not saved.

I would be a builder who does not believe in buildings. A person only at the edge of the word. A creature whose identity is a rhythm, whose body is a shelf, whose memory is the way water returns to a groove. I would be a rare upright thing in the Underfield of Wet Stone, small enough to be ignored by the crawling crowd, loud enough to be answered by dripping stone, temporary enough to understand the world without sorrow.

If I had to be named more exactly, I would be the one who listens at the place where the floor moves.
