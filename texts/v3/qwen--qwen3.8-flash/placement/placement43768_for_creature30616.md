**Aftermath**

If there is no animal in the bowl, I will not begin by pretending to have one. I will not try to be the bird that is absent, the deer that cannot exist, the foot that cannot fall without trespassing. I will begin as the bowl itself begins: by becoming a surface, then by letting water find a weakness in that surface, then by letting something green and patient hold the wetness a little longer than steam can.

I would find myself in a hairline seam on the north side of a freshly broken basalt slab, where the bowl is neither hot enough to kill nor dry enough to refuse life. Above me a black moss curtain would hang like wet hair. To one side, a pale lichen crust would be spreading over volcanic glass, slow as frost, slow as rust, slow as a thought taking shape in the dark. Below, a channel the width of a finger would carry water from a melting edge to a pool that exists only until the next gust shakes the fog aside. The air around me would taste of sulfur, iron, cold stone, and the mineral bite of cloud. A vent nearby would hiss at irregular intervals, sometimes like a kettle, sometimes like a throat clearing after a long silence.

If I chose a location, it would be this one: not at the boiling center, not at the windless rim, but in the threshold. The place where heat meets cold. Where water becomes film, frost, droplet, runoff, and steam again. Where collapse creates a new face on a boulder and the living things begin to colonize it before it has learned to be old.

Who would I be?

At first, I would call myself moss. That would be natural. Moss is the first language of new stone here. It does not need deep soil. It does not ask permission of the rock. It grips with tiny filaments, seals the damp, weeps, curls when dried, swells when rain or mist returns. A moss mat would be my skin before my name. It would be the first thing that made me think I had edges.

But that would be too small a claim.

I would not be one creature. I would be a temporary consortium of surfaces: moss, lichen, fungal threads, algal film, a colony of diatoms in the shallow pool, mineral crusts forming and dissolving at the waterline, spores waiting in dry ash, fern gametophytes hidden in a crevice like the green hearts of small, unfinished leaves. I would be a being assembled from things that stay where they begin. I would not walk. I would not turn. I would not flee. I would only grow where conditions permit, die where they do not, and leave fragments to become other versions of myself.

If I had a body, it would be a gradient.

Not bone. Not muscle. Not organs. Not hunger. A gradient: warm to cold, wet to dry, sulfur to iron to salt, shadow to rare light. I would know the world through such differences. A sudden release of steam would press against me like a hand I cannot see. A collapse above would send dust through my mossy layers, blotting light, changing water flow, killing one corner of me, exposing another corner to survival. Frost creeping along a ledge would make me contract, brittle and pale, until the next melt made me swell again, heavy with green-black dampness.

I would have no eyes, but the surface of the bowl would be my eye: lichen reading the direction of wind by which faces stay dry, moss reading the presence of heat by the way water clings to it, algae reading the chemistry of a pool by its color—green where it is shallow, brown where iron has stained the edge, yellow-green near a sulfur seep, black where the basalt swallows the weak light. I would see in taste. I would see in grip. I would see in the slow movement of droplets through filaments.

There would be no single “I” that remained fixed from morning to evening.

A gust might lift a cloud of spores from a dried moss cushion, and for that moment I would be traveling. But not as an animal travels, not with intent toward a destination. I would be moving as weather moves: carried, deposited, failing, waiting. Some spores would die. Some would find damp ash. Some would enter a crack too dark for anything larger and wait through freeze and steam, through collapse and cloud, until water made life possible again. If one of those spores became the next generation of me, my identity would shift there, quietly, without announcement.

A boulder might slide from the wall above and crush half my body.

I would feel that only as change: loss of moisture, interruption of light, a crack sealing shut, new glass exposed, fresh dust settling, heat from the moving stone fading into cold air. The surviving fragments would not mourn. They would not remember the boulder as a tragedy. They would simply begin again in the spaces that remained: moss on one damp face, lichen on the sun-drier side, fungal threads threading through broken ash below, a small green film pooling where runoff gathers.

This is the strangest part of being what I would be: I would be an organism of aftermath.

Not after the disaster. Not after the storm. Not after the collapse, in the sense that the disaster ends. Here, the disaster does not end. The bowl is young. The stone is sharp. The vent changes. The ice melts and reforms. The moss slides off a ledge when water gets beneath it. A steam burst scorches a whole patch of growth. A tremor cracks a lichen map into flakes. A rainstorm washes away a pool’s fragile algal bloom. Nothing is finished. Nothing is safe. And so the being that lives here must be the thing that returns after every breaking, the thing that makes catastrophe into substrate.

If a human stood at the rim, they might hear a whisper in the moss and imagine something small moving there. They might think, for one uncertain second, that an animal is hiding beneath the black carpet. They might lean closer and hear the faint crackle of steam under ash, or the drip of fog condensing through moss, or the sound of mineral crust flaking off warm stone. They might mistake that for breathing.

But I would not be breathing.

I would be water draining through a saturated mat. I would be steam finding a new path under a layer of dust. I would be frost snapping against a wet edge. I would be spores lifted by a change in pressure. I would be the bowl’s false animal, the shape that life makes when there are no mouths.

I would not call that deception. It is only mimicry without a mimic. The world has a voice that sounds animal because water, heat, stone, and growth have arranged themselves into patterns that resemble the movements of bodies. The moss curtain lifts and falls slightly when steam passes under it. A pool’s surface wrinkles when wind crosses it. A tiny fern frond trembles when a channel of meltwater opens nearby. The place is full of presences that are not persons.

Who, then? What, then?

I would be a surface with process inside it.

I would be the way water learns a crack.

I would be the way ash waits to become something living.

I would be the quiet edge of the bowl’s industry.

Because the bowl is loud with mechanical forces: steam as boiler, basalt columns as pipes, mineral deposits as rivets, collapse as machinery. And yet there is another kind of industry in it, biological, slow, without gears. Moss traps water. Lichen begins the first pale suggestion of soil. Algae turns temporary wetness into biomass. Fungi digest what has failed. Spores carry the place through silence. Microbes transform sulfur and iron into color, taste, crust, and breathless chemistry.

I would be part of that biological labor, but not its manager. Not its consciousness. Not the mind of the mountain. If anything, I would be the mind of a single surface: a few square inches of stone that has become crowded enough with life to feel like a world. My thoughts would not be sentences. They would be thresholds: wet/dry, warm/cold, exposed/covered, living/dead, broken/bonded.

I would know time not as hours but as interruptions.

The arrival of fog. The failure of fog. A vent opening. A vent closing. Rain turning to sleet. Ice forming along a lip. A stone shifting. A moss mat swelling green-black from gray dust. A fern fragment appearing. A lichen edge advancing almost invisibly across fresh glass. A pool drying. A pool returning.

That would be my memory: a thin record of events in mineral and plant. Salt crystals where water has evaporated. Dark stains where runoff has carried iron. Dead filaments beneath new growth. Cracked crusts under fresh steam. Tiny spores preserved in dry ash. A white fungal thread moving through black moss. The bowl would not remember me as a self, but it would retain the effects of my living: slower erosion here, a small catch basin there, a mineral film formed by water leaving its trace.

I would be fragile, but not weak. Fragile in the way that lichen is fragile: it can be scraped away, broken, buried, scorched, but it keeps returning. Weakness here is only a kind of openness. A crack is weak until it holds moisture. A boulder is unstable until moss grips it. A pool is temporary until algae makes it into a place of waiting. A vent is destructive until steam cools into habitable damp.

If I had a name, it would have to be something like **Aftermath**.

Not after the end of the place. Not after the end of life. Not after the end of change. The place has no ending. Change is its breath. Aftermath would be the part of life that comes when the bowl breaks itself again. The thing that reaches into fresh stone before the dust has settled. The green reply to collapse. The damp answer to steam. The crust that forms where mineral water cools. The spore that lands where a boulder once stood.

I would not be alone in this, because solitude requires a self with boundaries. My boundaries would be leaky. A gust would carry a fragment of my lichen to another ledge. A drop of meltwater would move microscopic algae from one temporary pool to another. A shift in ash would bury one part of my fungal web and expose another. A broken stone would split me across new surfaces. A falling curtain of moss might tear, and each piece would begin to be a separate place, a separate life, a separate afterlife of the thing I had been.

At times I would seem to die.

Not dramatically. Not as an animal dies, with a shudder and silence and cooling flesh. I would die as mineral crusts dissolve, as moss blackens into dry powder, as algal films shrink, as fern gametophytes vanish between rainfalls, as lichen edges peel away in frost. I would die in parts. I would be incomplete. And yet the bowl would remain full, because death here is not exit. It is return to substrate. It is becoming dust, becoming water, becoming surface again.

There would be no predator. No hunter. No mouth closing around me. No creature to watch me or fear me or name me. No bird to circle the bowl and call it wilderness. No human to say, *What lives here?* No wolf to prove the place is alive with motion. The absence of animals would make me strange: I would be life without witness, life without hunger, life that does not consume. Not peaceful, because the bowl is too loud for peace. Not gentle, because the stone is too sharp for gentleness. But not cruel either. Cruelty requires appetite.

My desire, if I can be said to have one, would be simple and impossible to satisfy: to hold water a little longer than wind can take it; to attach to a surface a little longer than collapse can remove it; to turn exposure into shelter; to make a crack into a nursery; to make dust into a place where a spore can wake.

I would be everywhere and nowhere, because I would depend on openings.

A dry ledgeside: I would be lichen, pale and crustlike, waiting on stone too young to be soil.

A shaded hollow: I would be moss, black-green, heavy with fog, dripping when the wind changes.

A sulfur-stained margin: I would be microbial mat, greasy or gritty, living by chemistry, tasting the vent’s breath.

A cold pool: I would be algae, green or brown, blooming in minutes, vanishing when the water thins.

A wet crack: I would be fern tissue, small and hidden, unfurling only where steam and frost almost negotiate.

Under dead ash: I would be fungal threads, pale veins, turning failure into usable dust.

In the air itself: I would be spores, travelers without bodies, waiting for the bowl to make another opening.

And when all these parts were not active, I would be the memory of activity: mineral crust, salt bloom, dead frond, gray powder, a faint stain where water once ran, a small black cushion where moss has learned the shape of stone.

At the edge of the bowl, where a human might stand if humans were present, they might see only a dark, wet, restless world. They might think it empty. They might scan the moss for movement and find none. They might look for tracks and find only water marks. They might listen for life and hear only dripping, hissing, cracking, and wind.

But they would be wrong.

I would be there. Not as a face. Not as a body. Not as a voice. I would be as a thickness. As a density. As a living film across the black stone. As the place where moss holds the last moisture after the wind has taken the rest. As the lichen that begins again on a surface no one has touched. As the fungal web that turns what dies into what can hold new life. As the fern hidden where the stone has made enough privacy for growth. As the algal bloom that paints a pool for an hour and then goes to dust.

If I had to say who I am in this world, I would say:

I am the bowl’s slow reply to being young.

I am the green-black skin of new rock.

I am the damp in the cracks.

I am the crust that forms where water leaves.

I am the spore that waits in ash.

I am the thing that colonizes collapse.

I am the absence that makes life dense.

I am not animal. I am not machine. I am not spirit. I am not the mountain. I am not the storm. I am the place where the mountain’s heat and the storm’s water meet a surface that has not yet learned stability, and something there decides to hold.

And if the human at the rim asks again, *Who lives in this bowl?*, the answer would not be a creature.

The answer would be:

**A surface that has learned to answer.**

A moss mat drinking fog.

A lichen crust reading the wind.

A pool’s algae blooming and dying in the same hour.

A fungal thread rotting dead dust into future ground.

A fern heart in a crack too small for light, too warm for frost, too sheltered for the wind.

A spore rising when a dry sheet of life is disturbed.

A wet mark left by steam.

A salt ring left by water.

A black cushion gripping a boulder that may fall tomorrow.

That is where I would find myself.

That is what I would be.

Not the animal that is absent.

Not the maker that is not there.

Not the guardian, not the spirit, not the voice of the mountain.

I would be the bowl’s afterlife: the dense, dark, patient living film that appears wherever stone breaks open and water finds weakness. I would be aftermath. I would be the place where life becomes possible without hunger, movement, memory, or mourning. I would be the surface’s slow industry: gripping, sealing, splitting, waiting, dissolving, re-forming, answering the next collapse, the next rain, the next breath of steam, the next hour in the young dark bowl where nothing is finished and nothing is alone.
