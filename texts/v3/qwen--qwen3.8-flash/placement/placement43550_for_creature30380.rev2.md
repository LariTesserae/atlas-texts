I would be dropped into a sound.

Not a name, not a path, not a clearing large enough to stand in and say, *here I am*. I would arrive somewhere in the lower layer of the warm dark basin, at the point where a fresh seam in the stone has begun to weep mineral water and the moss around it is already learning the rhythm of the dripping. There would be no horizon before me, no sky I could trust. There would only be pressure: the close weight of air, the wetness gathering on every surface, the distant hammering of legs in the leaf litter, the soft collapse of peat under some body I would not see but would know because the ground would answer it.

I would begin, as the basin itself begins, as a film.

Not a creature, not yet. A slickness. A conversation between calcium and salt, bacteria and hyphae, chalk and chitin, spores and warm steam. A thin living skin over a black blade of newly broken stone. I would cover the edges before the edges had had time to become dangerous. I would taste the seep. I would bind dust into a provisional crust. I would glow faintly where disturbance had passed: a blue-green bruise in the dark, not illumination, only an announcement. *Something has moved. Something has changed the temperature. Something has died nearby. Something is drinking.*

If you asked me at that stage, “Where are you?” I would not know how to answer. I would be wherever moisture had decided to linger. I would be the rim of a basin pool that exists for minutes. I would be the underside of a broad black leaf where condensation forms beads that fall like tiny cold clocks. I would be the skin of the world learning to speak.

But a film left long enough becomes appetite.

In such a place, life does not respect boundaries. The living layer is too close to the mineral, too warm, too hungry, too wet. The skin thickens. The hyphae find nerve. The bacterial film organizes a gradient. The moss trembles in patterns that become rhythm. The seep begins to attract bodies, and the bodies begin to leave signals, and the signals become roads without stone, maps without makers.

So I would become a footfall.

At first, perhaps only a broad pad: leaf-shaped, soft, damp, pressing against a crust of black soil that has not yet decided to be soil. I would feel the difference between mineral salt and decaying chitin. I would feel water moving two layers below. I would feel a beetle’s shell scrape against a shard of fresh stone and know, without knowing, that something small and armored has just asked a question of the ground.

Then I would grow legs.

Then I would grow a body low enough to fit under the pressure of the world.

Then I would become one of the soft-eyed mammals the passage cannot fully explain without lying, because no mammal here is a mammal in the way sunlight might teach the word. I would be small and variable, low-slung, with skin that is partly fur and partly bare membrane. My coat would be coarse in patches and damp against my flanks; where the air is thickest, fur would be a disadvantage, so I would grow skin there, sensitive as the inner wrist, able to read humidity the way a mouth reads flavor.

My hands would be broad, almost leaf-like, with long strange fingers. Not delicate in the human sense, but precise. Each pad would know whether the stone ahead is still sweating, whether a fungal cap has broken above, whether a thread of water has turned left or right in the last hour. My feet would hear as much as touch. They would not simply land; they would ask the earth a question, and the earth would answer with vibration.

My face would be flattened, not by cruelty, but by necessity. The dark has no patience for eyes. They would be narrow, buried, veiled with bristles that tremble when spores pass. If I saw at all, I would see gradients: a cooler pressure where a pool has just opened, a warmer pulse from a fault seam, a faint seam of luminescence where mold has disturbed its sleep. Not the shape of a leaf. Not the color of a fruit. Not a horizon. Only the fact that something else is there.

My neck would be long and flexible, built for pressing one side of the skull to moss-covered stone. I would stop in the dark and listen through the earth. Beneath my face I would hear the slow churn of worms rearranging air. I would hear the thin crack of mineral skin being pierced by a root. I would hear water choosing a new route. I would hear, farther off, the drum of a larger body moving through black shrubs, and my own ribs would tighten before my mind could make it a thought.

I would not think in images.

I would think in pressures.

Heat would be one word. Wetness would be another. Crowding, hunger, mating, falling, seep, shell, spore: these would not be names but changes in the texture of being. My whiskers would sweep forward like fern-fronds, tasting turbulence against broken stone, learning the shape of a shelf, a mouth, a rival, a hidden channel. I would move not by sight but by rhythm, by the difference between one surface and another, by the sound my own body made as it displaced air, damp fiber, warm steam.

And there, I would begin to know what I am.

Not “I” in the sense of a person with a border. I would be the place’s temporary listening. I would be the film organized into a footfall. I would be the seep’s answer. I would be the basin’s question, wearing fur and nerve for a little while.

I would move through the middle layer, where climbing stems form black-green rooms without doors. I would pass through chambers made not by anyone but by congestion: stems packed too tightly, fungal caps layered like shelves, leaf litter compressed into a floor that is not a floor. I would pause where air changes, where the warm vapor becomes thicker, where the ground begins to hum with a thousand minute mouths.

There I would do something that, if seen from outside, might look like tool-use, and if seen from within, would not look like thinking at all.

I would pick up a seed-pod with my strange, long-fingered hands.

I would carry it to a thin thread of water that has suddenly appeared over a fresh lip of stone. I would not place it to build. I would not place it to save the water. I would place it because my body is asking: *What happens if this stops? What happens if it turns? What happens if the pool that has always been here becomes elsewhere?* The pod swells. The trickle finds another seam. The moss brightens with disturbance. The water tastes different. I taste the change on my pads, on my whiskers, on the membrane of my ear. Then I leave the pod where it lies. It will soften within an hour. It will fall apart. It will become part of the next mouth.

No one will remember the question.

The land will.

But even that is too simple. The land does not remember like a mind. It remembers by continuing to change.

Because as I moved, I would become less fixed than the word “mammal” allows.

Sometimes, when I passed through a curtain of hanging spore-threads, moths would rise from my back in a pale column, disturbed from the moss ceiling. Their wings would make the sound of wet paper tearing. Static would build where the spores met. For a moment, the darkness would show me a shape: not a body, but a living ring of glow, a brief architecture of dust and wings and disturbance. I would feel it without turning around. I would feel the shape move with me, or I would feel myself move through it. Which is true? In this basin, the difference may be irrelevant.

When my tail struck the ground in a short triplet, water in a shallow basin behind me would ring with standing waves. When I hummed in the hollow of my throat, moss under my belly would sway without wind. When I clicked my tongue against the palate, the dark would return a sentence without words: *there is stone ahead; there is no opening; there is another body very near.*

And if I were the resonant kind, if I were one of those larger soft-bodied animals whose chest is built like a chamber, I would become weather.

My feet would make the peat ripple. My breath would open channels in the vapor. My voice would not sing; it would map. A long thrum would move through fungal columns, telling hidden creatures that a slope had crowded, that water had turned, that something large and warm had arrived. A sudden bark might scatter insects from leaf-curds, making them fall into lower darkness where pale jaws were already tasting the disturbance. I would not know what I said. I would only know that when I said it, the world rearranged itself in response.

That is enough speech for such a place.

I would gather without building.

At a warm seep where the ground trembles faintly and the air tastes of iron and soft fruit, I would press against others. Not for comfort. For orientation. One body becomes a source of heat; another, a source of chemical signal; another, a source of low vibration. Our clustered bodies block wind, redirect condensation, make a temporary city with no walls. A line of us might bridge a crevice, not by choice, but because one steps and the next follows the tremor through the moss. Then the water drains, the gradient shifts, and the line scatters.

There is no ruin where we were.

The ground closes. The film returns. The spores drift.

But we are not only our bodies. We are also our deaths.

If a predator pressed itself to the underside of a moss shelf and waited, if its sticky pads read the tremor of my whiskers through a bead of water, I might be pulled into darkness faster than a story can be told. My body would not leave a monument. My bones, if I had bones, would not remain long. Calcium and chitin would be claimed. The film would spread over me. Fungus would bloom white across my flank. Beetles would ring against my shell. Worms thick as fingers would rearrange the soil where I fell. Moths would taste my dampness and carry spores away. Within hours, what was called “I” would be a patch of different chemistry, a faint glow, a richer place for the next crawler, a disturbance in the pattern of the ground.

Even then I would not be gone.

I would be the next pressure.

So if you ask me again, *Who would you be?*—I would have to answer in layers, because this basin will not let a single answer stand for long.

First: I would be the film. The wet mineral skin. The bacterial thread binding dust to new stone. The faint glow that does not illuminate but says, *something passed here.*

Second: I would be the crawling. The leg’s dry ring against shell. The crablike taste of humidity before entering a pool. The worm’s slow rearrangement of air and water. The beetle’s accidental use of a shard, brief as a sigh.

Third: I would be the soft, eyeless mammal. Broad hands. Fern-frond whiskers. Tail-drum. Skull pressed to moss. Body listening through the earth. The creature that carries a seed-pod not to build, but to ask the water where it wants to go.

Fourth: I would be the hum. The resonant chest. The map made of vibration. The body whose voice does not symbolize, but shapes the dark by moving the water into rings and the moss into sway.

Fifth: I would be the swarm. The moth-column. The spore-flash. The synchronized ripple of tiny legs over fungal mat. The pattern that looks for a few seconds like one creature and then dissolves when the wind shifts.

Sixth: I would be the pool’s emergency life. The larvae born too quickly to have names. The translucent creatures that shimmer when disturbed. The soft-bodied things that breathe through every surface and vanish when water does. The brief mirror that reflects nothing because there is nothing above it to reflect.

Seventh: I would be the tremor. The characteristic life of the basin. Not a creature that happens to make sound, but sound made temporary flesh. A body that knows the world by making it answer.

And if you insisted that I choose only one, I would say: I would be the moment when all these layers touch and believe, for an instant, that there is an individual.

Where would I find myself?

At the edge of a newly opened seam, where water is not yet a stream but a decision. Where the angular rock has not yet become a cliff, only a wound. Where the leaf cups dew and the dew decides whether to fall into the film, the moss, the waiting mouth of a crawler. Where the air is so thick with warmth and movement that “outside” seems like a rumor and “inside” seems like a pressure against every nerve. Where there is no path, no ruin, no marker, no name, and yet everything knows what it is by how it moves through everything else.

I would not be a stranger there.

A stranger assumes there is somewhere to arrive.

I would be a temporary intensification of the basin: a listening crust, a tasting film, a low body, a resonant flank, a ring of water, a cloud of spores, a scar of footfall soon covered by moss. I would be the place’s way of asking whether this stone is warm, whether that trickle will hold, whether another body is close enough to matter, whether death will be quick enough to feed the next life. I would be the answer the dark gives when something touches it.

If I had a voice, it would not say *I am here*.

It would say, by the way its tail struck the ground, by the way its pads pressed the moss, by the way its breath opened chemical doors in the humid air:

*Something has moved. Something has changed the pattern. Something is listening.*

And then, before the sound could become a name, I would dissolve back into the living skin of the world, and the basin would continue its endless argument with itself, and I would be part of the next tremor.
