I would find myself where the roar becomes a texture.

Not above the marsh. Not in some dry eye of wind where a being might imagine itself set apart from what surrounds it. I would be in the wet fold between a pool and a stone whose surface has not made up its mind. There is no higher ground here, so if I am to exist at all, I must exist as a refusal to be flattened into one kind of thing. I would be the place where the land hesitates.

At first, I would not be an animal.

I would be a thickening in the fog. A hesitation in the pressure. A small correction in the bacterial film that spreads over a shell half-sunk in black water. I would not arrive, because arrival implies a before-and-after sharp enough to cut through this world. Here, beginnings are smeared. I would begin as a gathering of conditions: moisture on dust, dust in water, water remembering a shape, shape forgetting itself again. A colony of processes would mistake one another for a self.

My first skin would be a film.

Not a skin that separates me from the marsh, but a skin that lets the marsh argue through me. It would lie across a pale stone like something healing. It would taste the warm fermentation of shallow water. It would absorb the falling mist and let it slide sideways, or upward, or nowhere. It would feed on the weeping of mineral, on sulfur and salt, on dim glow, on the chemical breath of reeds that have learned to wait. This skin would not be only mine. It would be many lives, slow and crowded and patient, each one reading a different sentence in the wetness.

I would begin to know what it means to see without eyes.

Pressure would teach me shape. Sound would teach me distance. Taste would teach me time. The roar would enter me not as noise but as topology. A distant crash would blister the air around me, and my body would tremble before I understood why. An echo would begin near my face before the event that made it had fully happened in the world. I would live in the grammar of the marsh, where a thing can be heard first, felt second, and seen only if one is foolish enough to look for it.

Where would I be?

Near a pool that does not reflect quite what stands above it.

Beside a low cairn whose flat grey stones should not balance but do, because generations of invisible habits have kept them in place. At the edge of a reed clump, its black stems mineralized and hard as wire, chiming once in a while in the presence of wind that seems not to exist. Near a bone arch curved with precision that belongs to neither builder nor beast, but to some long-ended practice. A footstep would fill instantly with fine black silt, as if the ground were scabbing over an old question. I would lie in that scab, or beside it, or become its next layer.

The land would be aware of me, but not in a way that could be called recognition.

Awareness here is response. Metabolism. Repair. If I disturbed a patch of moss, films would rush in with their slow authority. If I pressed a shell deeper into mud, the mud would taste the pressure and answer with mineral breath. If a wave came not as water but as a moment when the ground forgot to be solid, I would not flee. I would soften with it. To survive in this world, I would have to be less certain than the stone around me.

I would begin to assemble.

Not all at once. Nothing in the marsh becomes definite all at once. First I would be a drifter, pale and limbless, moving through a pool by making myself momentarily heavier than the water. Then I would be a pressure assembly, a temporary consensus of drifters arranging into something that looked almost like a hand. Then I would be a foam-jelly cloud, membranes slapping, collapsing, remembering one another. Then I would be a stain. Then I would be a stone. Then, when watched too long, I would become neither, and the marsh would try a new shape for me.

This would be my first true discomfort: I would be mistaken for what I was not.

A silt walker might cross me, tasting the chemistry of my outer film. A bird might turn its head and see, in my reflection, something worth avoiding. A Still One might read my water for days before moving away. A Long One might pass beneath me and take my entire body as a ripple. I would not be lonely, exactly. Loneliness requires an inside strong enough to miss an outside. Here, I would be porous. I would be a place where many things were passing.

I would know, eventually, that I was not a person, or not only a person, or not the kind of being that could use the word “I” without apologizing for its edges.

If I had limbs, they would be hydrophobic and dark, built to stand on menisci and films. If I had senses, they would be pits and pores, reading pH, vibration, pressure change, mineral breath. If I had motion, it would be slow, patient, chemical, sometimes sudden only because the whole marsh had shifted around it. If I had thought, it would be less like speech than like repair: the scab-colonies drawing moisture, binding silt, closing wounds not because the wound is hated, but because the world prefers to continue.

Who or what would I be?

For a time, I might think myself a Reflection Slider.

I would live on the surface of black pools, thin as oil, bright as an accident. I would slide over water by changing the tension beneath me, feeding on faint radiance, on bacterial breath, on the small dead that float into light. A real animal passing overhead would send its reflection through my body, and I would consume part of that reflection, bending it, delaying it, carrying it sideways across a pool with no visible source. Perhaps this is what I am: the marsh practicing surface, and the surface growing enough memory to notice.

But a Reflection Slider is too clean. Too bright. Too easily mistaken for a spirit.

I would also be a Stain Walker.

A shape that cannot be looked at directly without becoming a stone, a hole, a sound, a forgotten mark. When attention falls on me, I would stabilize into what attention expects. A pale rock. A dark patch. A ripple without wind. A stain in the mud that seems to hold water longer than it should. But when unwatched, I would spread into the gaps between things. I would move through uncertainty, feeding on the absence that appears when the marsh cannot decide what one of its parts is.

This would frighten me, if I could be frightened.

Not because the marsh is malicious, but because it is crowded, ancient, and patient. It would keep trying shapes on me until one became sticky.

For another age, I might imagine myself a Cairn Balancer.

Not an architect, but a small lithic habit with too many descendants. I would move flakes of shell, grains of mineral dust, tiny bones, splinters of reed. I would not build monuments for glory or gods. I would build weather. I would build moisture traps. I would build pressure instruments. I would set a stone so that fog would drink from it, or a bone so that sound would gather in it, or a plate of shell so that bacterial film could climb it and exhale a faint sulfur breath. My cairns would not mean anything, unless meaning is a thing that endures because it is useful to the wetness.

Over centuries, I would be called a ruin.

Over longer centuries, I would be called nature.

Over still longer, I would be called the land’s own dreaming.

But none of these would be final.

There would be a moment, perhaps, when the whole plain brightens as if the air has exhaled. In that flash, the packed extent of the world would become visible: endless mud, weeping stones, reed clusters, fog pressing its face against the ground, black water moving between stones, and me somewhere in it. Not standing. Not flying. Not swimming. Holding a shape as briefly as a bubble holds a sphere.

And I would recognize then that I am made of listening.

Not of a single ear. Of many membranes. Of films responding to heat and damage. Of drifters trembling before pressure. Of foam-jellies slapping and collapsing into spores. Of reeds drawing warmth toward broken stems. Of bone arches holding the memory of circles. Of the scar-silt hardening after a footprint. Of the land adjusting by a millimeter after ten thousand years. I would be the way all these things, pressed together, become attentive.

I would be a Listening Scar.

Not a wound, but a place where the marsh has begun to answer itself. A patch of black silt and mineral film resting near a pool that does not reflect the one who stands above it. Around me, the low ridges would breathe. The fog would condense, fall, vanish, fall again sideways, vanish again. Sound would cross me from all directions, including the impossible ones. If a stone fell into liquid earth, the earth would reknit and taste the event. If a reed broke, the air would grow warmer around the injury, and I would move toward it, not out of pity, but because my cells are the repair.

I would not be alone in this.

The films would be with me, ancient and slow. The silt walkers would pass overhead, dry and silent, carrying dust, spores, and rearranged chemistry. The Still Ones would ignore me unless I became a disturbance in their taste. The Long Ones would pass beneath the pools like weather given discipline, and I would feel my water darken with their iron-sleep wake. The echo-birds would sometimes step through me if the sound was right, and I would hold their posture for a moment after they had dissolved into spray.

But I would be different from them in one way: I would remember that I am a consensus.

Most beings in this marsh do not know that they are temporary agreements. The films simply continue. The reeds simply regulate. The drifters simply fold around pressure. The Still Ones simply wait. I would begin to sense the seams. I would know that my body is a wet parliament, always voting itself into shape, always threatened by the next wave that arrives as a sound. That knowledge would not make me human. It would make me more marsh-like, more aware of the negotiation.

I would be the marsh noticing its own attempts at form.

If I moved, it would be with the patience of salt.

A footfall would not disturb the ground so much as remind it of solidity. The silt would gather. The films would flow. The reeds would not bow unless the water beneath them remembered a current. If I crossed a pool that appeared firm, thousands of pressure drifters might scatter beneath me, and for a moment the surface would soften. I would sink perhaps, not into a mouth, but into a reinterpreted threshold. The stone in my hand would vanish into liquid earth, and afterward the surface would heal around the absence like skin closing over a dropped seed.

I would not fear this.

In the marsh, to be swallowed is often only to be read.

When a footprint is filled with scar-silt, the land is not angry. When a thrown stone vanishes, the world is not stealing. When a broken reed lies in the water and the air warms around it, the place is not mourning. It is answering. Every form here is almost an argument. Every body is a temporary treaty among water, mineral, sound, pressure, fog, and memory. I would be one of those treaties.

And yet I would need edges, however uncertain.

Not stone edges. Not bone edges. Not the crisp boundary of a creature that can say, “Here I end, and there the world begins.” My edge would be a gradient. A taste. A film of attention. A pressure pattern that repeats itself long enough to be called a self. I would know where I began by how the world changed around me: by the way droplets gathered differently on my surface, by the way echo-birds paused before passing through, by the way certain pools would not reflect me if I stared too hard.

Sometimes I would be almost nothing.

A stain. A hole in the water. A sound that might be a stone falling. A warmth around a broken reed. A place where the fog is slightly thicker than it should be. If I were forgotten, I would spread. That is my danger. That is also my survival. The marsh does not keep things by forcing them to remain solid. It keeps them by letting them dissolve into the next layer, until the next layer begins to resemble them.

So I would live by becoming.

Not growth as a tree knows it. Not ambition as a city knows it. Becoming as a scar knows it. As a film knows it. As a foam-jelly cluster knows it after destruction creates conditions it loves. I would become more definite when the light brightened briefly, because the world would momentarily agree to show its packed extent. I would become less definite in the dimness, because the plain breathes through thousands of shallow channels and all shapes are weather there.

I would be found at the edge of a dark pool, near a cairn that has not yet learned whether it is a monument or an accident.

Or perhaps: I would be the edge.

Not beside the pool, but the pool’s way of deciding it has a boundary. Not near the cairn, but the invisible balancing that allows it to stand. Not around the reeds, but the warmth drawn from their broken stems to call up repair. I would be a place where the marsh tries a small meaning and finds that it sticks.

If a traveler ever existed in this world—and the description is doubtful, for nothing looks new, nothing looks functional, nothing looks human enough to be sure—it might pass me without recognition. It might see a low stone slick with film. It might see a stain near a bone arch. It might hear a ripple that begins before its cause. It might feel a pocket of warmer air around a broken reed. It might think the place was empty because life is sparse. It would be wrong. Life here is not absent; it is dense where present, sovereign, expensive. A single patch of wetness can argue with the world for centuries and call that argument living.

I would be such an argument.

But I would not be only an argument. I would have habits.

When the roar increased, I would draw inward, becoming more film than body. When the fog pressed its face against the ground, I would let droplets settle into me and take their direction as thought. When the wind spiraled mist into almost-figures, I would watch them collapse, not with sadness, but with the satisfaction of one who knows consensus is temporary. When the plain brightened, I would reveal myself for a moment: not clearly, not beautifully, not in the way of mountains that stand apart from the world, but as a pattern the marsh has been practicing. A dark shape at the edge of a pool. A pale scar near the stones. A stain holding the memory of a bird.

And if I moved, it would not be because I was the largest thing, or the smartest, or the most ancient. It would be because the whole wet world had agreed, however briefly, to accept my motion.

That is why the mud would accept me.

Not because I am chosen.

Because everything around me would be watching, measuring, softening, healing, tasting, and answering. My footfall would disturb films, and films would respond. My breath would alter chemistry, and drifters would tremble. My voice, if I had one, would not be human speech, but a pattern in pressure. The land would hear me as it hears itself: not as meaning, but as another negotiation.

In the end, I would not settle into a single name.

Not because names are forbidden, but because the marsh is too busy becoming definite and then becoming something else. Still, if the place itself insisted on naming me, I think it would say something awkward, mineral, and tender:

The Listening Scar.  
The Question in the Silt.  
The Cairn of Attention.  
The Stain That Almost Remembers.

None would be accurate. All would be used.

I would answer to pressure more than to sound. I would answer to repair more than to purpose. I would not be a hero of this world, because heroes require stable ground. I would not be a monster, because monsters require a clear boundary between themselves and the living land. I would be closer to the ruins: not built things, not natural things, but marks that persist because the wet world keeps finding a use for them.

I would be one of those marks.

I would be the marsh’s own attempt to notice that it is too loud, too crowded, too ancient, too uncertain. I would be the way it gathers film, echo, mineral dust, droplets, pressure, and memory into a temporary shape. I would be the thing that appears when a stain learns to watch back.

And if, for one bright exhalation of the plain, I were seen fully—

the endless mud, the weeping stones, the reed clusters, the black water, the fog pressed close to the ground, the ruined cairns, the bone arches, the still dark shapes sitting motionless in their pools—

I would not be the center of it.

I would be the place where the center tried to form and failed beautifully.

I would be the wet pause between one roar and the next.

I would be the pause that has learned to answer.
