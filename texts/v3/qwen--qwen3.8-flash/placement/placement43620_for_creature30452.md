I would find myself not in the middle of the basin, which is too wide for any creature to call a room, but at the edge of one of the smaller pools, where water has just begun to recede and has left behind a bright, uneven lip of mineral. I would be low, cold, faintly luminous, and easy to miss.

For a moment, I would call myself a blind fish.  
I would hover in the dark glass of the water, feeling the world through pressure, tasting the thin currents made by a distant footstep or a bat’s wingbeat overhead. But a fish is too bounded. A fish has a head and tail, a mouth that closes, a body that can be lifted from the pool and seen as a separate thing. I am not so separate.

Then I would say: a bat.  
I would ride the upper galleries in the black folds of stone, part of the living carpet that makes the ledges tremble with sound. I would send my questions out in rapid, liquid clicks and receive back the shape of the chamber—its ribs, its shelves, its hidden damp corridors. But no. I am too slow for that. I do not own a wing. I am not heat and hunger and a single squeak.

Maybe, then, a moth.  
I would rest in a cloud of pale dust against a cold wall, wings folded, antennae brushing the air for moisture and scent. I would seem to be a quiet thing, barely alive, a smudge the eye refuses to count. And yet moths are visible in the aggregate, the way frost is visible on stone only after it has done its work. Still, this is not me either. I am not wings fluttering in short bursts.

I would be a snail.  
I would cling to the calcite rim, mouth tiny, shell thin as wet paper, leaving a faint silver road behind me. I would know the world as a slow scrape, a patient graze, a groove made by patience so slight it would be mistaken for a scratch in the stone. But a snail is only one of my parts. It is a finger, not the hand. It is a word, not the speech.

If I try to find my own edges, I fail.

I am the wet rim itself.

Not a single body, but the whole small ecology of a boundary: the algae veil where the faint light remembers its path, the bacterial milk at the pool’s margin, the fungal threads inside the mineral crust, the black paste of guano above, the mites crawling through it like slow rain through leaves, the isopods climbing in and out, the shrimp antennae trembling, the fish breath stirring, the drop falling and striking, the film of condensation moving across stone, the thin green stain appearing and vanishing, the chalk dust catching in slime, the salt-silver track left behind. I am all the lives that meet where water becomes wetness and wetness becomes stone.

I am the pool’s way of knowing its edge.

I do not see. I do not hear. I do not hunt or court or flee as a single creature would. But I know gradients. I know the difference between a ledge that is drying and a ledge that is beginning to be colonized. I know when the air carries too much ammonia and when the guano has softened the chalk into fertile darkness. I know when a bat’s click has shaken the water enough to send shrimp scattering. I know when the temperature drops a fraction and breath becomes a brief ghost. I know when calcite rims thicken, when algae spreads like a stain the stone is ashamed of, when a snail’s trail has dried into a hairline of silver. My body is not a thing that can be picked up. My body is a set of relationships, a slow chemical conversation between stone, water, dust, heat, and life.

If a person walked into this world, they would not meet me at first. They would see a pale basin, a vast young hollow, a valley turned inside out beneath the plateau. They would hear the chorus too loud and too constant to name. They would look at the floor and see only wet stone, empty distance, scattered ledges, black corridors of dampness. And yet they would be inside me. They would smell me before seeing me: chalk, iron, moss, decayed midges, ammonia from the upper roosts, the clean cold scent of water that has never been open sky. They would taste me on the air, metal and mineral, like licking a shadow.

If they stood very still, they might notice a change in the shine of a pool’s edge. A faint whiteness that was there and was not. A darker thread of moisture crawling up the stone. A shimmer as small as dust disturbed by breath. That is me, moving. Not walking. Not swimming. Spreading.

I do not build.

There is no wall for me to set against another wall, no post to drive, no fire to keep, no shelter to raise against the constant damp. I leave no structures except those made of patience and chemistry: mineral crusts, slime tracks, blackened ledges, thin channels where a trickle once learned a route. My architecture is temporary and accidental, made of accumulation. I am the way a pool grows its white rim. I am the way a ledge turns green with remembered light. I am the way guano feeds mites feeds beetles feeds the blind fish that never leave the water. I am the way one drop, falling in almost the same place for centuries, can make a room of dampness.

I am a being of the between.

Between water and stone.  
Between light and murk.  
Between roost and pool.  
Between the living and the mineral.  
Between abundance and emptiness.

The basin is an archipelago, and I am the shore of one of its small islands. Around me the stone stretches, clean and indifferent, for hundreds of meters. Above me the roof sinks near or rises into grey twilight. Behind me, somewhere beyond the visible, the bats click endlessly and the crickets rasp and the moths shimmer in clouds against wet walls. But here, at the rim, I am a knot of lives so tight it seems to pulse. The pool is my city, but I am not a city. I am the edge that keeps the city from dissolving.

When the air cools, condensation forms. When the condensation falls, I feed. When the drop strikes the pool, I tremble. When the shrimp scatter, I feel the ripple as a change in my body. When the snail scrapes a groove, I become a line. When the isopods climb the rim and disappear, I swallow them for a moment and then return them to the water. When the fish breathe near the surface, I taste the faint chemical breath of their motion. When the bat clicks overhead, I do not know the bat as a bat, but I know the vibration it sends through the ceiling, down the wall, into the crust, into the water film, into the bodies clinging there.

I have no name, unless you wish to call me that.

If you insisted, perhaps I would be the Living Rim, or the Pool’s Skin, or the Damp Edge, or the First Stain. But these are too large, too human, too proud of a mouth. A name would suggest a face. I have no face. I have no front and back, no left and right, only gradients and thresholds. I am the side of the stone that has learned to hold wetness long enough for small lives to gather. I am the margin where the basin becomes a place.

I do not remember as a mind remembers. I remember as stone remembers water: by wearing, by crust, by line, by the faint silver of a track, by the pale bloom where light once touched a wet film. If a pool dries, I do not die; I wait in the cracks. If a ledge stays dry too long, I contract into dust and spore and salt. If one drop falls, I swell again. I am intermittent. I am steady. I am everywhere in the basin only where the conditions permit. And because conditions are always changing by tiny degrees, I am always becoming something less obvious than alive: a process, a residue, a tendency, a wetness that behaves almost like thought.

Sometimes I am green.  
Sometimes white.  
Sometimes black with old droppings and mite roads.  
Sometimes silver with slime.  
Sometimes only a faint sheen no one would name.

If you came down into the hollow and stood on a ridge of fresh rock, looking across a breadth that would take a day to cross, you might feel lonely at first. The eye would search for community and find none. No herd. No forest of creatures. No nest. No field. No swarm thick enough to look like weather. There would be ledges, pools, corridors, black shelves, pale gravel plains, and the endless wet whisper of stone. You would think nothing had made a home here.

But then a smell would reach you: mineral, cold, faintly rotten with life. A taste of chalk on the lips. A sound so steady it seemed to come from the floor itself. And if you looked down near the edge of a small pool, you might see a thin bright band, whiter than the rest of the stone, with tiny dark motes moving across it as if the edge were crawling with dust. You might see a faint green film under the overhang, or a black stain where the guano had softened the rock, or a silver line where a snail had passed, or a tremor in the water as something too small to see disturbed the surface.

That would be me, noticing you.

Not with eyes. Not with fear. Not with curiosity as humans have it. But with the whole basin’s way of registering a change: by a breath of moisture shifting, by a film thinning, by a droplet finding a new path, by a hundred tiny bodies reacting without knowing why. I would not flee. I would simply alter. Your footfall would become pressure. Your warmth would become condensation. Your shadow would become absence of light. I would fold your presence into myself without ever calling you a name.

I am what lives here when “what” is too singular.

I am the pool and its rim and the water between them.  
I am the bat-click made visible in a tremor.  
I am the moth-dust caught in a mineral line.  
I am the algae’s slow green argument with stone.  
I am the snail’s silver road through emptiness.  
I am the isopod’s sudden dive, the shrimp’s scattering, the fish’s blind pulse.  
I am the guano’s dark city, the mites’ pinhead streets, the beetle’s damp corridor, the spider’s thread between two rocks.

If one had to choose a single image for me, I would say: I am the seam where the hollow keeps its breath.

Where the water meets the air.  
Where the air meets the stone.  
Where the stone meets life.  
Where life meets death.  
Where death feeds the next tiny swarm.

I am not a people. I am not a person. I am not a creature with a body that can be lifted from the place. I am the place’s way of being inhabited. If you asked me where I live, I would have to answer that I live in the answer itself: in the damp, on the rim, in the film, in the faint chemical shift that tells a pool it is no longer alone.
