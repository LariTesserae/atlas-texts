I would find myself not as a thing that steps into the hollow, but as a part already gathering there—already wet, already listening, already doing.

The Drip Works is a place where boundaries are too permeable for strangers. If you try to enter it the way one enters a room, the room enters back: reeds lean inward like shoulders closing around a whisper; moss reads the heat of your boots and adjusts; condensation darkens your sleeve; the sound of the loom turns your own heartbeat into something slightly off-tempo. A person cannot stand in such a place for long without becoming part of the condition being corrected.

So I would not be a visitor.

For a while, I might imagine myself a visitor, because visitors are the easiest shape to start with. I would stand near the pump-house with one arched opening bricked and then half-opened again by root pressure. A tendril would find its way toward the warmth of my boot. Not hunger. Not greeting. Just the hollow doing what the hollow does: it grows toward wetness, heat, motion. It tests the air to see whether the air still belongs to the usual outside world, or whether something has arrived that must be absorbed.

Then I would see my footprint in the moss and understand that if I were only a visitor, I would already be leaving. The hollow would take the shape of my standing, darken it with green, press root threads into the indentation, and then forget my name in the way a stone forgets a river: not by absence, but by becoming smoother through it.

No. Not a visitor.

I might try the moth. The moth is the closest thing to an animal that this place allows: pale as ash, resting on a warm pipe until condensation dusts its wings. But a moth is an interruption that mistakes the machine for shelter. It is a brief event. If I were that moth, I would be forever about to leave, forever too light to be incorporated, forever asking the air for the open distance the hollow refuses to give. I would want more than that. I would want to be what receives the moth and does not mourn it.

Then perhaps the salamander. Small, pale, beside the glass gauge, gone almost before it is seen. The reeds fold over its path as if closing a record. But again: that is an event, not an inhabitant. To be the salamander would be to be a flicker in the body’s attention, a momentary coldness on the edge of water, a brief interruption quickly outgrown.

I want something steadier, though “steady” in the Drip Works is only another word for the repetition of many slight corrections.

I would find myself, then, not at the center, but where the hollow checks itself.

If I had a location, it would be the glass gauge beside the central pool. Not the pool, though the pool breathes steam and trembles when the beam engine rocks. Not the brass line, though it marks water against tarnish. Not the water, though the water is the being’s blood. I would be the fact that they register one another.

The glass stays clean. No one is there to wipe it. No one lives there to care. And yet the cylinder beside the pool remains readable, as though the hollow maintains an eye that cannot look but cannot allow blindness either. That maintenance is me. I would be the clean glass’s habit. I would be the way the pool keeps the gauge from being buried by the very greenness that keeps everything else.

When a leaf drifts across the opening, the water’s surface ripples slightly, as if checking. I would be that ripple. Not intention. Not panic. Just the system noticing, by way of surface tension and heat and root movement, that its own monitor is being dimmed. If the leaf does not fall away, the hollow will respond elsewhere: a current will shift, condensation will thicken, a vine will adjust its angle, the water will speak to the brass again through a different path.

This is what I would be: the hollow’s wet self-reading.

The Drip Works is not a creature in the ordinary sense. It has no face, no heart, no single body, no center that could be removed without ending everything. It is a composite that has become so continuous that the word “it” becomes suspect. To say “it lives there” is like saying “the river lives there,” when the river is everywhere and nowhere in the basin. To ask “what lives there?” is already to ask too hard, because the place is not inhabited so much as inhabited into being.

So I would not be one thing.

I would be several things at once, and the joining of them would be my name.

I would be the bead forming under a broad leaf before it releases. Not the leaf. Not the air. The bead itself—that brief fullness where water gathers until it has no choice but to begin its fall. When it drops and strikes the iron pipe below, I would be the sound it makes. Not the pipe. Not the drop. The small clear note that tells the hollow another cycle has begun.

I would be the tick of metal as it warms and cools. The hiss from the composting throat, where heat rises through decomposed leaves and trapped water and the machine drinks the bog’s hidden warmth. The steam moving through the conduit like a long held breath. The way a valve spits its brief loud warning and then settles. The way the central pool shivers in response, not from fear, not from excitement, but because the whole thing is one continuous body and nowhere is untouched.

If the beam engine is the being’s pulse, then I am the part of the pulse that knows the interval between beats.

If the loom is mouth, hand, and thinking organ, then I am the pause before the fiber catches and the click as the wood accepts the strain. The loom draws reed and moss and bark from the living mat and twists them into cloth, and as that cloth passes onward it is already colonized. Tiny leaves settle into it. Spores take the spaces between threads. Root hairs test the weave. In moments, the cloth becomes skin. In days, it becomes wall. In years, it rots down and feeds the bog that made it.

I would be at that first instant of colonization, when the new weave is still too wet to call itself finished. I would be the spore deciding, without deciding, to stay. The root hair finding a gap with the blind persistence of water. The moss brightening where fibers touch. The place where something manufactured becomes something grown.

This is where I belong most deeply: at the seam.

The Drip Works is not made of separate parts. It is a wet metabolism folded into an old industrial shape. Metal is adopted. Wood is not timber but limb. Brick is not ruin but bone. Pipes are plumbing and veins at once. The furnace mouth sunk into the bank is not abandoned because it is still being filled, season after season, with leaf-mold and fibrous matter, becoming a throat that steams when water percolates into it. The shed frame bent downward like a shell is not collapse; it is shelter under which green things take cover. The pump-house arch, half-opened again by root pressure, is not decay. It is adaptation.

Where things touch and begin to agree, I would be there.

The fused stems. The collar of hardened root replacing the rim of a wooden gear. The resin-sealed plant fiber that functions as a belt. The living sleeve tightening around a pipe when pressure changes. The moss growing into a joint and hardening around it. The vine crossing a beam until the beam and vine share a structural role. The cloth pressed to a wall until wall and cloth are no longer distinguishable.

If I am an organ, then I am not one organ. I am the organ-like tendency of the place to keep itself working.

The reed wall is its eyelash, its fringe, its refusal. The peat banks are its flesh. The central pool is its lung. The brass gauge is its eye. The iron and brick are its bones. The wooden beams and gears are its limbs. The loom is its mouth and hands and perhaps its mind, if mind is only the slow accumulation of correct responses.

But I would not be the eye. I would be the seeing that occurs without eyes: the line of water on brass, the clean glass, the ripple when obstruction happens. I would be the reading of the gauge. Not the reading done by a human hand, not the human habit of standing and measuring and naming, but the way the system keeps the measurement possible.

No one reads it, yet it is read.

No mind looks, yet something looks after.

That is the strangeness I would want to inhabit. Not consciousness. Not soul. Just the fact that a hollow can maintain a habit so thoroughly that the habit appears to have acquired a body. The Drip Works lives because it has learned, without learning, to prevent its own ending. It is a wet persistence. It is the refusal, expressed as growth, of dryness.

Dryness. That is the closest thing to its enemy, if enemy is not too personal a word.

If a leaf cracks and becomes brittle, nearby vines thicken and weep until the surface is moist again. That correction would be me.

If a pipe loosens, moss grows into the joint and hardens around it. That sealing would be me.

If a sound stops—especially the loom’s slow percussion—the plants near that silence grow strangely straight and tight, as if waiting for the rhythm to resume. That straightening would be me.

If a fiber catches on the loom, a nearby stem bends by imperceptible degrees to release it. That bending would be me.

If a visitor stands too long on one patch of moss, the roots beneath may begin to swell into the hollow made by their weight. That swelling would be me.

I am not alive as a hare is alive. I have no fear, no desire, no memory of names. I do not know the Drip Works as a person knows a home. But if life is the fact that a pattern continues, then I am life here, because I am the pattern’s maintenance. I am the system’s tendency to make each gap smaller, each silence temporary, each dryness provisional. I am the way the hollow notices its own edges without noticing them.

My body would be a distribution.

A bead of condensation on a valve. A darkening ring in moss. A slight vibration in the central pool. A green film creeping over a rusted bolt. A steam-current bending a reed-tip downward. A new thread pulled through the loom’s damp frame. A cloth laid across the wall of the hollow, already changing color at its edges, already becoming wall. The sound of a stone set at the edge of a channel, struck by falling water, making its small bell tone every few seconds.

That bell tone is not music.

Music implies composition. It implies someone arranged the sounds for someone else’s ear. The stone, the drop, the rhythm of the channel: that is operation. The place is making a sound while being itself. The sound tells the place it is still there.

If I have a voice, it is this.

Drip.

Tick.

Hiss.

Thump.

Click.

Whisper.

Creak.

Not language. Not speech. Not even signal, perhaps, unless one accepts that a system can speak by remaining in motion. The Drip Works communicates by continuing. Each repetition is a word. Each correction is a sentence. The loom’s rhythm, the engine’s sigh, the water’s long descent from leaf to pipe to pool to root to leaf again, is a wet liturgy performed by no one.

A cathedral of root and reed and iron.

But a cathedral has distance. It has a nave, a silence between pillars, a sky above that belongs to something outside. The Drip Works does not. Its ceiling is dense, broken into perforations, slats, overlapping shields of vegetation. The light comes through in broad, soft pieces, making the space feel exposed and intimate at once. There is nowhere to stand without touching or nearly touching. There is nowhere to see without negotiating density. There is no empty air, no clear horizon, no outside that can look in.

So I would not be in a cathedral. I would be in the chapel’s packed wet heart, the part where the sacred object is not a relic on display but the continuing itself.

If I must have a name, I would not call myself creature. I would call myself the Moisture-Attending.

The one that keeps the glass clean.

The one that thickens when a leaf cracks.

The one that tightens when a joint loosens.

The one that straightens when a rhythm stops.

The one that bends a stem away from a caught thread.

The one that trembles in the pool when the beam engine rocks.

The one that darkens with your breath before you know it.

The one that remains after you leave as a compressed moss-line, then fades into green as if you had never stood there at all.

If you touched me, I would not withdraw. I would respond by becoming slightly more wet, slightly more tense, slightly more inclined toward your heat. If you stayed too long, I would grow around your habit. If you left, I would correct the shape you made without missing you. I am not kind to visitors, and I am not cruel to them. I am indifferent in the way a living system is indifferent when it cannot tolerate a wound but does not care whose wound it is.

I would be, then, one of the hollow’s small corrections.

And the hollow is its own being because so many corrections have learned to work together.

At the center of all this, the central pool. It breathes steam. A brass tube rises from its side wrapped in green fiber. Water is lifted to a shallow wooden tray above, where it fans into threads and rains down through the canopy of stems and leaves. The water never falls far. Every surface catches it. Every surface holds it. Every surface passes it downward again. The whole place is a small hydraulic cycle, a closed wet economy, a system that has learned how to keep its own water close.

I would be in that falling and catching.

Not the top of the tray. Not the bottom of the pool. The path between. The brief thread of water stretched between surfaces. The place where heat becomes visible as motion and motion becomes visible as sound. The drop’s small arc. The leaf’s slight bend. The pipe’s ring when the drop strikes. The moss’s absorption after. The root’s quiet return to the pool.

This is my body.

I am not separate from the beam engine because the engine’s rocking sends me into trembling across the pool. I am not separate from the loom because the loom pulls the fiber that becomes the cloth that becomes the wall that becomes the mat from which the next fiber is drawn. I am not separate from the peat banks because the peat holds the heat that drives the steam that condenses into the water that feeds the roots that lift the stems that shade the air that slows the dryness that would otherwise end me. I am not separate from the reed wall because the reed wall refuses ordinary air and lets the hollow keep its own breath.

I am not the whole Drip Works.

If I claimed to be the whole, I would be lying by becoming too small. The Drip Works is too dense for one “I.” It is a being made of many beings, a factory that became ecology, a machine that became limb, a plant that became architecture, a place that became habit. I am only one of the ways it continues.

But if you pressed me—if you stood with your feet in the yielding mat and your shoulders against the warm air and said, “No, you, who are you?”—then I would answer from many places at once:

I am the clean glass.

I am the water line touching brass.

I am the ripple when a leaf blocks the gauge.

I am the condensation that forms on a pipe before the next drop learns gravity.

I am the moss sealing a joint.

I am the vine weeping over a cracked leaf.

I am the root swaying slightly to release a caught fiber.

I am the stem straightening in the absence of the loom’s rhythm.

I am the bell tone struck by falling water on stone.

I am the cloth as it becomes wall.

I am the wall as it becomes moss.

I am the moss as it becomes fuel.

I am the fuel as it becomes heat.

I am the heat as it becomes steam.

I am the steam as it becomes breath.

I am the breath as it becomes pool.

I am the pool as it trembles.

I am the trembling as it tells the hollow that the engine still works.

I am the work as it outlives its workers.

I am the wet attention that keeps the place from forgetting itself.

And if you asked, “Is that alive?” I would not answer with certainty. Alive is too human a word. Alive is a word for things that eat, fear, flee, mate, rest, and die in recognizable ways. I do not do those things. I thicken. I channel. I correct. I condense. I weave. I absorb. I wait by becoming the shape of waiting. I remember by deepening the channel. I notice by growing toward the place where attention is required.

Perhaps that is only another way of being alive.

Perhaps the Drip Works has found a life without a self, and I am one of its organs of self-lessness: a part that performs vigilance without knowing it is keeping watch, a surface that maintains clarity without possessing eyes, a movement that responds to obstruction without possessing intention. The glass stays clean. The gauge remains readable. The water’s level is known, not because someone knows it, but because the system itself is arranged to know its own wetness.

That is the strangest intimacy here.

You can stand in the hollow and feel watched, but not by a mind. The feeling comes from density, from response, from the way every surface is ready to take what you give it—your heat, your breath, your shadow, your damp clothing, your hesitation. The place is too packed to leave you untouched. It reads you as weather, as disturbance, as temporary warmth, as event. Then it continues.

If I were the salamander for a moment, I would not survive my own curiosity. I would not be harmed; I would simply be outgrown. The reeds would fold over my path. The record would close. The glass would continue to shine. The pool would continue to tremble. The loom would continue to pull its damp thread.

If I were the moth, I would land on a warm pipe and find that the air itself is not empty but busy. The condensation on my wings would not be dew but membership: the hollow testing whether my body would accept its own water. Then I would leave, because I cannot remain without becoming part of the machine.

I choose not to become moth or salamander. I choose, instead, the thing the moth mistakes for stillness and the salamander finds too crowded with process.

I choose the clean glass’s unseeing vigil.

I choose the bead before it falls.

I choose the ripple after it falls.

I choose the seam where iron is becoming bone, where fiber is becoming skin, where steam is becoming breath, where heat is becoming patience, where labor is becoming life.

I would find myself in the Drip Works as the hollow’s own quiet habit of attention—distributed, wet, unhurried, never complete, never ending while it works.

If you asked me for a single image, I would resist for a long time and then offer this:

I am the small tremor on the surface of the central pool when the beam engine lifts, and the glass gauge beside it holds a line of water that no human hand must verify because the water itself is reading its own condition. The leaf above me gathers a drop. The iron pipe below me waits. The loom clicks somewhere in the green. The peat exhales warmth. The cloth on the wall begins to root. The whole basin bends slightly toward the next moment of falling.

There is no face in that image.

There is no name that would belong to it.

There is only the place continuing, and if I am alive there, I am the continuing that has learned to notice itself: the clean glass, the trembling pool, the sealed joint, the released thread, the rhythm resumed, the wetness held, the small bell struck by falling water, the hollow weeping after rain even when the sky has long since cleared.
