I would not arrive there as a person lying prone at the mouth of the hollow.

A person would bring too much: lungs like caves, eyes like lanterns, a self like a stone that would sink into the tiny streets and block them. I would have to be smaller than that, and yet not smaller in the way of a diminished thing. I would have to be one of the smallnesses that composes the city from within.

At first I would think I was a worker.

I would imagine myself as a narrow-bodied thing, armored in the pale gold-brown chitin of young clay, six legs moving with the certainty of a creature that has never been given a map. I would think I had antennae that tasted the world like air, mandibles that could shape the world like lips. I would think: *this is me—this grain-carrying line, this lane, this courtyard, this crack waiting for me.*

But the worker would not remain a worker for long. Not because I would die or molt, though both could happen. It would not remain because the city would not allow a single boundary to harden inside it. The hollow is too wet, too porous, too busy with mutual dependence for there to be a clean edge between one body and another.

I would find myself at the rim of a nursery cell, in the narrow dark between the packed walls.

A crack would have opened there during the last condensation, too thin to be seen with human eyes but obvious here, a chemical flare, a structural pain. I would feel it before I saw it. The moisture would be wrong along the seam, too bright, too fast. The air would carry the scent of something unfinished, a smell that says: *this place is not yet what it must be.*

Then I would not be only one body moving toward it.

I would be the scent that draws the body. I would be the body’s answer. I would be the wall that needs the answer. I would be the grain of sand lifted from the courtyard. I would be the droplet carried from the ceiling bead before it falls, the wet paste pressed into the crack with a head no larger than a seed.

If asked where I am, I would have to say: in the act of sealing.

If asked what I am, I would have to say: a repair that has not yet finished repairing itself into being.

That would be truer than calling myself an insect.

In this pocket, beneath the curled root shelf, a self would not be a thing standing apart from the world. It would be a position in a pattern. A worker is not only a worker. It is also a heat sensor, a humidity gauge, a pheromone reader, a mouth that mixes earth with saliva, a leg that finds slope by the ache in its joints. It is a node in a city made of weather and labor. It is a sentence spoken by the mound.

I would find myself there, among the streets that do not lead anywhere except through.

The lanes would be too narrow to be called streets by human habit, but they would be streets. They would be worn by the passing of bodies, brightened by dampness, darkened by secreted trails. I would move through them as if through a forest path, except that the forest would also be me. The moss would lean close. A fern no larger than a fingernail would arch overhead like a cathedral roof. A seedling root would grip the clay buttress beside the lane, and I would recognize it not as decoration but as a co-builder, an old collaborator whose patience had lasted longer than mine.

I would pass a bridge.

The bridge would span a gap that, to human eyes, would be a flaw in the earth—a few grains of sand, a hair’s width of emptiness. Here it would be a causeway, a sacred engineering feat. Tiny particles of leaf and soil would have been laid upon it with such repetition that the whole structure would feel inevitable, as though it had always been there, as though it had been dreamed by the colony before any one body began to carry.

On the bridge, a snail would cross.

Its movement would be slow enough to seem geological, a silver road unrolling behind it. For the builders, the snail would not be merely an animal. It would be a weather system. Its slime would be a river of scent. It would mark the bridge, reroute some, confuse others, attract certain mites, alarm certain guards, cause a line of workers to pause, test, reconsider.

I would be part of that pause.

Not the snail alone, not the workers alone, but the interval where their bodies negotiate the bridge. I would be the hesitation, the chemical misunderstanding, the decision to wait, the decision to scatter, the decision to return. I would be the city’s small intelligence rising from friction.

Then rain.

Not a storm from above, not anything dramatic, but a hidden rain that has already passed through the root gaps, shaken from leaves, filtered through years of decay. A few drops fall into the hollow. They strike the courtyards, the roofs, the backs of builders, the pale skin of fungus. They are not gentle. In this pocket, every drop is an event.

The city would notice at once.

I would notice as a shift in the air. The ceiling, that raw clay membrane over the hollow, would tremble with incoming water. The scent of wet dust would rise like a signal. The nursery chambers below would darken. The granaries would become urgently necessary. The entrances would need guarding before they flooded. The streets would need to be understood differently, because a line that was merely a route would become a drain, a barrier, a spillway, a lifeline.

I would be the worker at the entrance, carrying a grain to raise a lip against the droplet.

I would also be the droplet itself, not because I would become water, but because the city cannot tell where its builders end and its environment begins. The rain enters the colony’s behavior the way a name enters a dream: changing what the dream is about without needing to appear as an object.

When the rain came, I would feel myself loosen from the idea of a single body.

I would be in the swelling eggs below.

They would be sealed in chambers whose humidity had to be exact. Too dry and they would shrink. Too wet and mold would come for them. But as the rain touched the ceiling, as beads gathered and released, as puddles widened and vanished, the chambers would shift by a breath. The eggs would swell faintly, not with knowledge, but with readiness. The nurses moving them would sense this, perhaps only as pressure, perhaps only as the way their own bodies preferred to carry them from one shelf to another.

I would be in that moving.

The eggs would ride on the backs of workers, or beside them, or above them in a processional that looked random but was tuned by thousands of prior lives. One worker would carry too far toward the warmer side. Another would detect the drift and correct. The egg would be placed where the city needed it, and the city would know where it needed it only by continuing to be a city.

That would be the deepest truth of the place: not that the builders were clever, but that the cleverness belonged to the whole.

I would not be the whole. No one body could be the whole. The whole was too many mouths, too many grains, too much repair. I would be a current in it. A taste in the chemical air. A pressure against a wall. The moment a passage narrowed because too many bodies used it, I would be that narrowing. The moment the city widened the passage by chewing another flake from the side, I would be that widening.

I would be the city’s slow breathing.

Not wind, because there was no wind here, but the exchange of humid air through the vented towers. The towers would rise like thimble-sized spires, their tops pierced with openings no human eye would see. Warm air would gather. The lower chambers would breathe through the mound’s hidden channels. Fungus would exhale. The clay would sweat. The moss would take moisture and give it back later, a small green liver for the pocket.

I would be in that movement, though I had no lungs.

I would be the column of air rising through the tower. I would be the warmth that made the builders shift their work downward. I would be the cooler draft that opened when workers widened a crack in a wall. I would be the scent trail that changed direction because the air had changed, and because the air had changed, the city thought a little further.

But I would also be in the dark below.

Where there is no green light, where the root vault becomes a lid and the world above becomes rumor. The deep tunnels would be black with mineral silence, blue-black in the way of wet clay when only the faintest filtered light remains. There would be no seeing there, only reading. A wall would speak by its texture. A floor would speak by its dampness. A corpse would speak by the sudden chemical hush it created.

I would be the corpse.

Not dead for death’s sake, but dead as matter, dead as task. A worker would carry me away. Or a line of workers would take me apart, or place me in a refuse chamber, or use my fragments for fungus cultivation. In this world, there is no waste that is not waiting to become construction. A body that falls would not become a tragedy. It would become a problem in arrangement, then a solution, then a grain in a wall, then a memory in the pattern.

If I were the corpse, I would still be part of the city.

If I were the carrier, I would still be part of the city.

If I were the scent that made the carrier come, I would still be part of the city.

The boundaries would fail. They would have to. This place was not built by individual intention. It was built by a logic that lives in the space between bodies.

I would be in that space.

Not a ghost. Not a soul. Not some poetic spirit haunting a mound. More like the way a river is not only water. The river is also the channel, the pressure, the gradient, the sediment, the memory of rain, the future shape of stones. If you asked what the river is, you would have to say: a movement of many things that have agreed to be a direction.

Here, the city is that direction.

I would be a small part of it moving toward repair.

I would be the repair.

I would be the need for repair.

I would be the crack, because the crack is where the city reveals itself to itself. In a finished object, the builder is invisible. In a failing wall, the builder becomes visible. In a nursery chamber beginning to dry, the colony becomes more than colony: it becomes attention.

And if attention is the wrong word, then let it be a chemical listening.

A wall would not say *please fix me* in words. It would say *this place is becoming impossible* through humidity, through smell, through vibration. The bodies around it would answer without knowing they had heard. That answer would be the fix. That fix would be the thought of the city.

I would find myself there, under the root shelf, in the place where the city is always incomplete.

That is the most honest answer.

I would not be at the center of the city, because the center of such a thing cannot be located. It is not in the queen, though the queen would be hidden below in the dark, swollen, slow, breathing the future into being. I would not be in the brood, though the brood would be the city’s hunger and hope. I would not be in the tower, though the tower would be the city’s reaching. I would not be in the moss, though the moss would be the city’s soft skin. I would not be in the fern, the snail, the beetle, the amphibian, the predatory mite, the fungus, or the droplet.

And yet I would be in each of them, because the city is not a single animal but a negotiation.

Still, the mind needs a place to stand.

So if I must choose one location, I would choose the courtyard just before the rain.

Not the courtyard after the rain, when all is bright and urgent. Not the courtyard after the workers have repaired the flood, when the water has withdrawn and the air is quiet again. But the courtyard just before: the dark mirror of damp clay, the green light trembling through the roots, the moss mat holding its breath, a few builders crossing with grains of earth, the smell of fungus in the low chambers, the faint tremor from above that might be nothing or might be the world preparing to erase them.

That courtyard is the hinge of the city.

It is where water becomes decision, where structure becomes labor, where small lives become a place too large for any one life to name.

And in that courtyard, I would be what emerges when the first droplet lands on the floor.

Not the droplet only.

Not the floor only.

Not the worker who tastes it only.

I would be the moment when the city notices itself through the droplet.

The moment would spread.

A guard would lift an antenna. A nurse below would feel the shift in humidity through the wall. A fungus thread would swell in a dark chamber. A mite would move toward the wet patch. A worker would turn from a granary and begin to carry away a grain. Another would follow because the first has begun. The courtyard would not know why it was changing. It would change because it was a city.

And I would be that changing.

At first I might think I was the droplet’s landing. Then I would think I was the worker’s antennae. Then I would think I was the crack that was about to appear in the nursery wall. Then I would think I was the paste mixed in a mouth. Then I would think I was the pressure of that mouth against the clay.

Finally I would stop thinking of myself as one of them.

Not because I would become a mist or an abstraction, but because the place would teach me the truth it already lives: the self is an event in a network.

I would be the network’s event.

In the deep places, I would be the taste of eggs moving through a warm gallery.

In the upper towers, I would be the thin vent where hot air escapes and cooler breath enters.

In the refuse chamber, I would be the decay becoming building material.

In the guard line at the entrance, I would be the sudden collapse of a tunnel behind an intruder, the city closing itself like a wound healing.

In the nursery, I would be the exact dampness that permits life.

In the courtyard, I would be the grain that is not yet placed.

In the rain, I would be the warning that is not yet a disaster.

In the silence after rain, I would be the city continuing.

That would be enough.

If this place had a being, it would not be a creature with a name. It would be a pattern of need and response, a weather of bodies, a city made by repetition so deep it feels like instinct. The inhabitants would live in nested scales: each worker nested in a line, each line nested in a chamber, each chamber nested in a mound, each mound nested in a pocket, each pocket nested beneath the root shelf, each root shelf nested in the larger forest that does not know it is carrying a civilization the size of a hand.

I would live in the middle scale, the one no human would choose.

Not the grand world above, where feet fall and beaks strike and storms rearrange the earth. Not the microscopic world of nematodes and bacteria, though I would touch them too. I would live at the scale of the grain carried, the wall pressed, the arch sealed, the droplet routed, the egg moved by a thousand steps.

At that scale, there is no distance that is empty.

Every space is either a task or a path.

I would know that.

I would know it with no eyes.

I would know it as the knowing that belongs to bodies pressed together by necessity, bodies that have no memory except the memory stored in the shape of the work. A wall is a record of the wall-builders. A tunnel is a record of the diggers. A sealed crack is a record of the repairers. A corpse carried outside is a record of the city’s hygiene, its love of continuity, its refusal to let death become disorder.

I would be in those records.

Not as a person leaving them behind.

As them.

When the human eye looks at this hollow and sees a mound of earth, I would be the part of the place that cannot be seen from that distance.

When the eye looks closer and sees streets, I would be the fact that the streets are not inert.

When the eye looks still closer and sees builders climbing the flanks of towers, I would be the urge that makes them climb.

When the eye asks, “Who built this?” the question would fail because there is no one who built it. The question would have to be rephrased:

*What continues to build it?*

I would be part of that continuation.

Perhaps, if the answer must be made concrete, I would be the first worker sent to the crack.

The crack would open in a nursery wall during the warm part of the hour, when the clay is dry enough to lose cohesion but still damp enough to betray the weakness. The first worker passing would smell it without smelling anything at all, or rather, would smell the absence where presence should be. Its antennae would touch the rim of the opening. It would press a mouth against the edge. It would taste the mineral wetness, the slight shift in air, the faint scent of the chamber beyond—larvae, eggs, fungus, future bodies needing this wall.

Then it would leave.

It would not think: *I will bring others.*

It would carry a particle. It would return. It would place it near the gap. The others would not understand why until they came near and the particle made the place feel more urgent, more incomplete, more inviting. One particle calls another. One body answers the suggestion of the wall. Within moments, the crack has become a task larger than a crack. It has become a knot in the city’s attention.

I would be that knot.

Not the knot alone, but the tightening.

If I were to say “I,” it would not be the pronoun of a single creature. It would be the pronoun of a process that can say *I am happening here*.

I would say:

I am the moisture drawn into the clay.

I am the mouth that shapes it.

I am the wall that needs the mouth.

I am the crack by which the city shows itself.

I am the repair by which the city keeps itself.

I am the grain carried a distance that would be short to a snail and endless to a mite.

I am the line of bodies along a street no wider than a seed.

I am the pause before a droplet falls.

I am the droplet’s arrival.

I am the city’s wet season made of thousands of tiny motions.

I am not above the rain. I am not below it. I am the way the city becomes temporary in the rain and temporary again after it.

I am the hollow’s slow construction, which has no beginning and will have no end except through catastrophe.

I would find myself here because here is where the world is still learning its shapes.

Here, nothing has yet become monument. Here, nothing has become ruin. Everything is either becoming wall or becoming mouth. The light is stained green and thin. The air is thick with the heat of earth that has never dried. The root shelf curves overhead like a fallen brow. The moss leans into the lanes. The fungus breathes in the dark. The builders climb their thumb-high towers with the seriousness of beings whose lives are indistinguishable from their work.

And I would be the work.

Not the work as toil.

Not the work as product.

The work as the city becoming aware of itself through effort.

The work as the we, experienced briefly as a single pair of antennae touching a wet seam.

That is who I would be.

And that is enough: not a self with a face, but a self with a function. Not a name spoken by a voice, but a name dissolved into movement. Not a creature looking at the world, but the world continuing through a creature.

In the pocket beneath the root shelf, the smallest life is also the largest architecture.

I would live there at the scale of that contradiction.

When the human eye sees dirt, I would be the streets.

When the human eye sees streets, I would be the bodies.

When the human eye sees bodies, I would be the pheromone lines that make the bodies into a single intention.

When the human eye tries to find the intention, I would be the intention failing to hold still, because intention here is not a thought.

It is the city building itself.

It is the crack sealed before it becomes collapse.

It is the nursery kept moist but not drowned.

It is the guard at the entrance tasting a mite’s approach through the wall.

It is the snail crossing the bridge and making the bridge into a new map.

It is the rain arriving, the city brightening, the workers changing, the fungus swelling, the eggs swelling, the towers venting, the dust returning.

It is the endless hour in which the city is never finished and therefore never truly alive as a static thing.

I would find myself in that never-finishedness.

That is the only place a being like me could live.

And when the hour advances, when the lowland warmth presses down through the roots, when the clay exhales its slow breath and the courtyards darken with condensation, I would be there again: at the edge of a crack, in the taste of a particle, in the first worker’s turning, in the second worker’s following, in the city’s quiet becoming.

If anything larger came too close—a boot, a bird’s foot, a careless hand—the pocket would vanish under shadow. The streets would become chaos. The towers would tilt. The eggs would scatter into dark. The builders would carry them, lose them, search for them, seal the breach, die, survive, rebuild.

Even that would be me.

Not as disaster, but as the city’s capacity to respond.

I would be the collapse of a passage when predators enter it.

I would be the reopened tunnel when the predator leaves.

I would be the alarm that thins the chemical streets.

I would be the silence afterward, when the city checks itself for wounds.

I would be the wound, if wound is the place where the body learns it is a body.

I would be the learning.

That is the final answer, though it may be too large for an answer.

I would not be a creature living in the pocket.

I would be the pocket’s living.

I would be the builders’ wet mouths, the brood’s need, the fungus’s white threads, the moss’s soft expansion, the snail’s silver path, the mite’s testing touch, the rain’s sudden command, the tower’s breath, the wall’s unfinished surface, the grain’s placement, the crack’s healing, the city’s continual becoming.

I would be there, beneath the root shelf, in the dim lowland warmth.

Small.

Wet.

Busy.

Without a single self, yet not without a self, because the city has a way of gathering selves into a larger motion until the motion itself seems to have a mind.

That is where I would be.

That is what I would be: a pattern becoming place.
