I would find myself at the place where the ramp’s scooped brick gives way to the first dark channel in the floor.

Not at the Grinding Pot, though I would pass by it constantly and touch its rim without thinking. Not in the highest loft, though my ears would know the rats’ rhythm there. Not beside the basins, though my skin would learn the crabs’ clicking and the snails’ slow writing on stone. I would be at the edge of all these places, in the seam where water stops behaving as accident and starts behaving as a habit.

The Hallborn have no word for a being exactly like that, or perhaps they do, but it is a word used when the room is wet in the wrong hour. If they spoke of me directly, they might say, *there’s too much water in the south groove again*, or *the drip has begun its corner*, or *something is listening from the mortar seam near the door*. They might not name me, because naming would make me seem separate from them. They might simply avoid the spot where the floor is darker than usual, or step over it with a slight adjustment of the knee. In the Drip-Hall, a being can be made of a path, a tendency, a remembered wetness, and still be respected.

At first I would think I was a child.

A child of the Hallborn, broad-footed, warm-blooded, with toes that curl against damp edges in sleep. A child whose first word is not mother or father but something close to *drip*, because that is the first sound to carry a rule. A child learning that the ceiling tile above the quail shelves sweats before the tile above the snail jars, that the brick by the door smells of salt and old milk, that the bronze plate is not a toy but a commandment. The child would press a palm to the Grinding Pot and feel the water run in a thin film. The child would pull it back when the goats’ hooves clatter too near. The child would ask, not where the sky is, but where the rain has been today.

I would wear that idea for a while.

I would say: I am the child who will become a Drip-Count. I am the child who sits near the bowl and watches the pattern become language. Three drops into the grindstone basin means the southern pipe is loosening its wax. A long silence above the snail jars means the air has thickened and the room is preparing to rain in one of its own pockets. The child learns to be patient because patience has a mineral taste, like chalk under the tongue.

But a child is still a person. Still too much skin. Still too much intention. In the Drip-Hall, a person can become too noisy for their own usefulness. The animals answer too quickly; the light seems to cut them into a single shape; the floor keeps a record of them, but they are not the record. I would not want to remain the one who counts. I would want to become the pattern counted.

So I would begin to wear thin.

Not physically, though I would still smell of wet wool, dung smoke, reed stoppers, and the cool mineral breath of water that has passed through clay too long. I would still carry the small bone toggle, the cloth sponge, the pouch of grain. I would still know how to move without disturbing the wrong shelf. But my edges would begin to agree with the stones. My shoulders would settle into their repeated grazing point. My hips would find the lean that opens the lid of the basin nearest the door. My feet would fall, night after night, into the same shallow bow, as if they were returning home to a home made by themself.

By then I would be less a person standing in the hall than a habit of the hall that sometimes uses a person as a hand.

I would be the damp that darkens the mortar when the wind shifts above the roof. I would be the indoor rain that begins ten paces from where rain usually falls and stops when the goats change the rhythm of their chewing. I would be the channel no one remembers cutting, now worn deep, now favored, now law. The water would call me route. The snails would call me path. The lizards would call me warm stone that has learned to wait. The bats would call me, if they thought of me at all, the thing below that keeps the air moving in its own small way.

There would be no first day of my life. There would only be a recognition.

One evening, after the bronze plate had rung and the room had shaken through its chorus of wings, hooves, claws, dripping, fire-pop, beetle-whisper, and goat-breathe, someone might notice that the southern seam had begun to carry water again, even though the pipe above it had been sealed. They would not be frightened. They would adjust. They would move the snail jars away from the threshold. They would scrape a line of moss from a place where it had grown too bold. They would set a clay basin under the drip and listen to it fill. Then they might look at me, because I would be there, sitting by the bowl with my wool cloth half dry and my knees stained with lime, and they might say, almost without emphasis, *the room is trying its old way again.*

And I would nod, because I would understand that they were not speaking only about water.

What would I be?

I would be the Drip-Hall’s made rain given a level.

A level, in this world, is not just a height. It is a duty, a placement, a way of belonging. The high level has bats, rats, lofts, quail, pigeons, ladders, and the dry knock of seed pods. The middle level has platforms, mats, fire, tools, hooks, shelves, and the broad backs of goats. The low level has basins, channels, moss, crabs, snails, beetles, salt films, and the dark lips of pots. I would belong to the floor, but not entirely; I would belong to the wall, but not entirely; I would belong to the roof’s hidden movement, because the roof sends me down in pieces. I would be a being between the room’s levels, carrying the room’s weather like a child carries grain water—carefully, because too much and you spoil the tray, too little and the life goes thin.

I would have no large authority. That would be impossible here. The room already argues for itself through condensation and erosion. The Grinding Pot already gives oracles, though no one trusts an oracle that can be overwritten by a spilled basin. The bronze plate already calls the house of creatures to attention. To be powerful in the Drip-Hall would mean to stand in the wrong place at the wrong moisture. I would be useful instead.

Usefulness in the Drip-Hall is not simple. It is a constant correction of the body’s relation to the wall. If I pass too quickly, I may startle a snail and the silver trail will break. If I sit too long near the fire-brazier, I may warm the lizard’s stone and change its hunting. If I strike a pot too sharply, I may set the bats thinking it is time to move, though it is only noon and the quail are not ready. If I leave a basin uncovered, the room may fill it and remember it. If I forget to scrape the threshold moss, someone older than me will say, without raising their voice, *the slippery one has been told to stop.*

I would know those voices.

I would know them not only by their words but by the particular way their bodies disturb the hall. One elder’s staff rings differently on wet stone. One Grinder’s elbow always touches the blackened rim of the Pot. One Goat-Keeper’s feet find the worn dip in the platform at the same instant the goats lift their heads. One Basin-Tender’s shadow falls before the body arrives, and the crabs pause. If I lived long enough, I would become legible to them too: a shoulder that leans in a certain niche, a hand that tests the rope for swelling, a breath that changes the dust where the mica pane admits light. I would not be a ghost. Ghosts have unfinished business. I would be a finished business returning.

And I would be finished business.

Finished in the way the floor is finished: not completed, but settled. The hall does not need me to be new. It needs me to return. It needs the leak to follow the old path. It needs the moss to learn its bounds. It needs the snail to climb when the milk has been offered and retreat when the air has thickened. It needs the rats to knock the pods loose in the tubes. It needs the lizards to sit as small clay fragments until movement becomes necessary. It needs the fire to breathe, the water to speak, the bronze plate to shove the air, and the goats to startle the children in the correct order.

So if I had a name, it would not be a proud name.

It would be the name one gives to a channel after it has begun to act on its own. *The south-going one. The corner-rainer. The dark seam. The too-often water. The groove that keeps its habit.* The Hallborn might also call me something gentler, only when they thought I could not hear. Perhaps *little weather*. Perhaps *the listening damp*. Perhaps simply *ours*, said in a way that means *ours to adjust, not to possess*.

At midday I would stand in the light slab near the Grinding Pot, but not in it for long. The light there would be a river, and a goat would twitch in it, and the dust would dance as if the room were remembering the surface world without admitting to having been there. Beetles would freeze at the edge of the bright place. A child would place a foot in the pot’s film of water and pull it back when the goat sneezes. The sneeze would echo from the far wall, and the bats would shift in their niches, and the quail would call in short bursts: *there, here, not now*. I would listen with all of these, not as separate ears, but as one made ear.

If someone struck the bronze plate then, I would not hear it as a sound only.

I would hear it as a rearrangement.

The rats would freeze in the clay tubes. The snails would climb or halt. The lizards would decide whether the warmth was still safe. The crabs would click harder against the ceramic. The fire would pop because the air had moved. The water in the channels would change its note because the room’s attention had changed. And I would feel myself become more distinct for a moment, because attention, in the Drip-Hall, is a form of rain.

That is what I would be: attention made porous.

Not the whole hall, though I would live in its body. Not the Hallborn, though I would carry their tools and learn their rules. Not one animal, though the crabs’ silence would trouble me and the bats’ flight would steady me and the snails’ trails would feel like sentences. I would be the place where all of these begin to agree without becoming the same. A small made world needs something like this: not a ruler, not a spirit, not a hero, not a monster. It needs a tendency that has become tender.

I would be the tendency that returns the rain to its chosen corner.

I would be the floor that remembers the foot.

I would be the wall that sweats at the hour of milk.

I would be the groove that says, when the room grows too quiet, *something must move here.*

I would be the slight wrong note that makes the hall correct itself.

And when, after a long dry spell, the condensation became so heavy that the room seemed to hold its breath, and a single large drop fell from the ceiling into the bowl near the door, and another followed, and the bowl did not overflow but drained through a hole too narrow to see, I would be the place where the water decided to go.
