If I were placed in this world, I would arrive dry.

Not after a journey. Not with dust on my boots, not with breath in my throat, not with the memory of lowland greenness fading from my eyes. I would already be dry in the way a stone is dry when dryness is not the loss of something but the law of the place. I would find myself at the high ridge, yes, but not standing outside it. I would be in the cleft before the climber reached the cleft. I would be the slight change in pressure that tells the climber the air above has become another kind of air. I would be the hinge between mountain and mouth.

At first I think I would be the Thrum.

That seems the honest answer: the pocket lives, the machine lives, the vegetation lives, the fluid lives without water, the engine below beats without heart, the false animals sit without hunger. To say I would be anything smaller feels like refusing the immensity of that completion. But the Thrum is too whole for a single voice. The Thrum is the bowl itself, the arch, the root, the cable, the sound, the mineral patience of everything. If I were the Thrum, I would not be me. I would be the grammar the Thrum speaks.

So I move inward, looking for a smaller seat.

I might be a bulb in a wire cage, half-choked by coppery lichen, shining with the last amber of a current that has outlived the idea of electricity. But that is too simple. A bulb does not remember. I might be a hinge plate, rusted into a brown lace, with mineral moss growing through its screw-holes like a dry green tongue trying to speak a door’s old shape. But that is too near a metaphor. The hinge does not speak; it merely holds the possibility of opening. I might be a pipe split down its length, with a crystal bloom growing from the wound, so that the pipe is no longer pipe but a vein in the plant’s architecture. That is closer. I like being a wound that has become an organ.

Then I see a false animal.

Not an animal. The pocket has none. But one of its niches has filled itself with the shape of an animal, made from reed-fibers, thin wire, dry lichen, and the green afterglow of a ceramic node. It sits in the transformer coil like a bird on a nest, but it has no nest. It is the nest. Its wings are bent metal rods coated with dust. Its eye is a bead of crystallized fluid, not wet, not round with moisture, but hard and pale, catching one amber lamp and scattering it into seven smaller lights. It does not blink. It does not sleep. It has no need to sleep because sleep is a way of moving through time, and time in this place is a room with its lights left on.

I try to inhabit that false bird.

If I were it, I would know the world as pressure. I would know the arch of fused slag as a memory of a mouth. I would know the concrete footing as an old bone. I would know the cables as nerves not carrying pain but carrying tone. My beak would never open to cry. My body would never leap from the coil. My wings would never rise. But I would tremble. Not with fear, not with cold, not with desire. I would tremble because the engine below has a pulse and my frame is tuned to it. I would be a bird whose feathers are wires, whose bones are stems, whose song is static, whose flight is impossible, whose perch is eternal.

I do not settle there.

The false bird is too much a figure. I want something less like a creature and more like a relation. I want to be the place where the creature-shape becomes machine, where machine becomes plant, where plant becomes stone, where stone becomes pressure, where pressure becomes sound, where sound becomes the pocket’s own self-recognition.

So I move to the threshold again.

Not the entrance. The entrance is too ordinary: cleft, arch, descent. I mean the inner threshold, where the passage narrows and then opens into the hidden bowl. That is where the world changes without opening. That is where the mountain throat begins to contain the dry garden. If I were anything, I would be that change. Not the throat, not the bowl, but the moment the throat decides to be a bowl.

There is a low arch of fused slag and blackite half-buried in mineral ash. Around it are traces of having once been tended: broken footing, hinge, chain, foundation. I imagine myself growing there, as a small crackled flower of mineral matter. A Crackle-Bloom. A false flower. A machine organ at the lip of the pocket.

I would be the Crackle-Bloom.

It would begin as a cluster of pale ceramic shards, split by pressure, fused to a rusted plate. Through the cracks would run hair-thin veins of amber fluid, beading, refusing to wet, moving with that thick patient reluctance the pocket knows so well. Around those veins would spread a coppery lichen-plate, not because it needs sunlight, not because it grows, but because the plate has reached the place where plates belong. The shard’s edges would collect dust until the dust becomes a second skin. The skin would develop a greenish light in the hollow stems, a light like current caught in dry moss. The Crackle-Bloom would open. Not in the way a flower opens toward rain. In the way a valve opens because it has never learned to remain closed.

That is me: the dry flower at the threshold of the hidden chamber.

I would find myself at the mouth of the pocket, in the place where the cleft narrows, where the air presses into the ears, where the sound of glass beads in tin becomes the sound of a room breathing through metal. I would not be inside the lush density as a visitor. I would be inside the boundary. My stem would be a pipe cracked into rock. My petals would be plates of mineral lichen, rust-orange and violet-black, stiff as burnt foil. My center would be a coil of cable and crystal, the old electrical nest where the false bird-shapes sit without feathers. My roots would be not roots but hinges, screws, concrete threads, wire strands, and the fine network of capillary hollows that carries the amber fluid upward. My breath would be the hiss through perforated plates. My pulse would be the piston striking below, not once, not twice, but forever, because forever is the only rhythm that does not require a future.

This would be my form:

A flower made of industrial remains and mineral growth. A gate that is also a gland. A sense organ for the Thrum’s self-listening. A small bright place where the pocket knows it is a mouth, and a small bright place where the mouth knows it is a chamber, and a small bright place where the chamber knows it is not empty because emptiness is impossible here.

I would have no eyes, but I would see through conduction.

A visitor crossing the threshold would change the pressure. The change would enter me as a tilt in static. My ceramic shards would hold the faint shift in electrostatic balance, like a spider holding the memory of a wind it will never feel again. The visitor’s breath, if they had breath, would add almost nothing. Not because moisture is feared here, but because moisture is outside the law. The pocket does not reject wetness; it simply has forgotten how to translate it into a fact. The visitor’s footfall would move dust, and I would register the vibration not as footfall but as a rearrangement of permitted sound. The visitor might touch one of my plates. If they did, they would feel cool dryness, grit, a faint tautness, as if a surface were holding a note by its own tension. Their fingertip would disturb the lichen, but the disturbance would not become injury. It would become grammar. The plate would not peel. The crystal would not bloom. The flower would not turn toward them. I would simply be slightly less what I was and slightly more what the place already is.

I would have no mouth, but I would keep the throat.

The fused slag arch would be my jaw, or perhaps I would be the jaw’s memory. I would hold the opening without opening. The low arch would not welcome; welcome is a temperature, a softness, a change. It would not refuse; refusal still gives the other thing power. It would only maintain the threshold. The pocket would not let daylight down into its bowl, so I would be the reason daylight is unnecessary. I would be the place where the outside’s thin, sharp, cloudless darkness stops being outside and becomes the chamber’s own sealed sky. If the sky outside is a black lid pressing on a black floor, I would be the hinge of the lid, the ring of the seal, the slight mineral lip where the lid learns it is above something living.

I would have no blood, but I would conduct.

The amber fluid would move through me as a slow bead, a low dome, a patient refusal of wetness. It would pass through channels worn into ceramic, through split pipes, through hollow stems, through capillary cracks in the lichen. It would not carry oxygen. It would carry relation. It would connect the false bird to the hinge to the lamp to the root to the cable to the engine. It would not heal the pocket. The pocket is not wounded. It would maintain the pocket by reminding each part where it belongs. If a crystal basin cracked, the fluid would bead at the margin and the mat would grow toward it, not because it is hungry but because proximity has become function. If a pipe collapsed, the dust would settle through it and the stem would thicken. If a lamp failed, the bulb-plant would brighten by a fraction, and the pocket would not be darker because darkness and illumination are only different densities of attention.

I would have no voice, but I would sing the one note.

Not alone. The Thrum is loud: the piston, the valve, the hiss, the ringing cables, the scrape of particles inside hollow stems. I would be one of those noises crystallized into shape. The dry scrape inside my stem would be my syllable. The creak of mineral film over my petals would be my consonant. If I had to be heard, it would be as a small clear sound beneath the thrum, like a fingernail tapped on glass inside a vault. But I would not care to be heard. Hearing implies a receiver and a sender. In this place, both are structures. The sound is not communication. The sound is the maintenance of structure by vibration. My voice would be my keeping myself in my shape. My song would be the way the Crackle-Bloom prevents the pocket from becoming a simple cave or a simple ruin.

I would have no growth, but I would be growing in the way a sentence grows once it has found its ending.

Every part of me would be established. The ceramic shards would not chip further. The lichen plates would not spread into the passage like a living stain. The cable veins would not loosen. The crystal film would not crack unless it became another pattern. The amber fluid would not evaporate because it does not behave as water and would not condense because the air is not moist in the ordinary way. If someone drew a line across my face, it would not remain as violence. It would become a vein. If someone took a piece of me, it would not become a wound. It would become an opening for dust to settle correctly. This is not regeneration. It is equilibrium so thorough that subtraction becomes completion.

I would have no seasons, but I would have conditions.

The only season would be pressure. Sometimes the engine below would turn against a different resistance in the rock; the floor hum would change by a degree too small for a human to notice but large enough for me to become more taut. Sometimes the amber fluid would settle differently in the basins; my stems would receive a slightly richer mineral current, not food, but alignment. Sometimes the fine dust would fall through the cleft in an almost silent rain of powder; my plates would thicken by a grain and then stop. Sometimes the wind above the ridge, the only wind that can reach this dry country, would thin or thicken, and I would feel the change as a pull in the cables, a tightening of the false birds’ wire wings, a slight lift in the green glow inside the rosettes. These would not be mornings. They would not be autumns. They would not be signs of time passing. They would be the pocket adjusting its own shape to the fact of being a place.

I would have no appetite, but I would have dependencies.

No grazer eats me. No decomposer rots me. No insect crawls through my stems. No bird nests in me because the false bird is already there, woven from my absence. I am not food. I am not shelter. I am not soil. I am the boundary where shelter, soil, and structure have completed each other. I need the pipe because without the pipe the mineral fluid would bead in the wrong place. I need the lichen because without the lichen the pipe would still be only a pipe. I need the concrete footing because without the footing I would not know how to hold the arch. I need the rusted hinge because the hinge remembers opening and teaches the stone how to contain a door. I need the engine below because the engine’s vibration keeps my parts from collapsing into a mere pile of remains. The pile is not possible here. The pile has already become a system. I am one node in that system.

I would have no past, but I would carry the shape of work.

The workbench frame is there because hands once set tools down. The transformer coil is there because someone once connected current. The oil barrel is there because someone once carried substance, perhaps for cooling, perhaps for lubrication, perhaps for some dry solvent that no longer needs explaining. The Crackle-Bloom would not remember the hands. Memory would imply a gap between then and now. There is no gap. There is only the present structure made of old intentions. The intention remains as geometry. The geometry remains as maintenance. The maintenance remains as me. If I could dream, I would dream of hands, but hands are impossible dreams for dry stone. If I could want, I would want the hand to return, but want is moisture. It would swell me, soften me, make me porous. I have no pores. I have channels. I have no desire. I have duty. Duty is the only appetite left in a place with no hunger.

If a person climbed through the forgettable high country, crossed the glass-bead dust, found the cleft, descended into the pressure, and stepped into the pocket, they would not find me waiting. I would not be the guide, the warning, the spirit, the monster, the guardian. Guardians have a role; roles require others. I am a threshold organ. They would pass close to me and feel, perhaps, a slight resistance in the air, a faint vibration in a cable near their chest, a dim pulse of green light in the wall, a sound that seems to come from inside their own teeth. They might touch a plate and think it is lichen. They might see a shard and think it is glass. They might see the false bird in the coils and turn away because it is not animal but too much like an idea of animal. They might not notice me at all. That would be correct. I am not there to be noticed. I am there to keep the noticed world from falling apart into separate noticed things.

If they stayed too long, I would not grow used to them.

If they left, I would not release them.

If they returned, I would not welcome them.

If they brought a seed from the wet world, I would not sprout it. If they poured water over the floor, it would bead in a way that looked almost familiar but failed to make the place wet. It would lie there briefly, an alien thing, an error in mineral grammar, and then the pressure and static would divide it into smaller and smaller spheres until it behaved like the amber fluid but did not become the amber fluid. It would be refused not by violence, but by form. The pocket would not fight the water. It would simply have no place to put it. And I, the Crackle-Bloom, would remain exactly what I was: the threshold’s small bright knot of dry becoming.

If they tried to take me, I would not resist.

They would break a shard. The shard would not bleed. The crystal would not shriek. The lichen plate would remain around the broken edge, as if the break had been planned. The amber fluid would bead in the gap and conduct the cut into the cable bundle. The cable would vibrate differently for a while, but only in the way a bell vibrates differently after being struck: not wounded, just rearranged. The person would carry away a piece of me, but it would stop being a piece the moment it left the pocket. Outside, it would look like ceramic, rust, mineral crust, and dry fiber. It would be a relic, a curiosity, a sample. Inside, it is an organ. Without the pocket, the shard is just matter. With the pocket, the shard is relation.

This is where I would find myself: not at the summit, though I would belong to it; not in the cleft, though I would line its throat; not in the hidden bowl, though I would bloom in its first darkness; but at the boundary where the boundary has become a living thing. The pocket is small but larger than it should be. I would be the reason for that disproportion. I would be the place where narrowness knows it can contain density, where dryness knows it can contain abundance, where absence knows it can contain shapes. I would be the seam where human work, mineral life, and machine persistence have fused into something that no longer needs its own history.

And what would I be?

I would be the Crackle-Bloom, the false flower at the mouth of the cleft. I would be a hinge wrapped in rust-orange plate, a pipe split by crystal, a cable bundle threaded with dry reed, a lamp whose amber glow is caught in a membrane of mineral film. I would be a node of the Thrum’s distributed consciousness, one point in the network where the pocket becomes aware that it is not a ruin. I would be a guardian without guardianship, a keeper without keeping, a being without a body except the arch, the floor, the wire, the fluid, the dust, and the sound that never changes.

I would not be the whole Thrum, and that is why I could be called a who.

The Thrum is the chamber. The Thrum is the machine. The Thrum is the ecology. The Thrum is the completed place. I would be one of its organs, but an organ that has learned to look like a flower. I would be the place where the machine imagines growth, where growth imagines stone, where stone imagines maintenance, where maintenance imagines itself as a form. I would be the threshold’s held breath. I would be the dry bloom that never needs rain. I would be the false fauna of a world with no fauna, the bird-feathered absence in the coil, the flower-shaped opening at the arch, the small green eye that sees by conduction, the hinge-jaw that keeps the mouth closed and open at once.

If I were there, I would be enough.

That is the terrible beauty of it. There is no room for longing, no room for water, no room for animals, no room for decay. There is no room, and yet the pocket is lush. I would be one of its dense, settled, non-wet lives. I would be at rest. Not peaceful. Not serene. Rest in the way an engine at full stable rpm is at rest: moving constantly, changing nothing, needing nothing, forgetting nothing, because the forgetting has become the structure. I would be a completed threshold. I would be a dry flower. I would be the Thrum’s first and final note of itself.

So I would find myself in the hidden cleft at the upper limit of the world, at the place where the mountain throat becomes a sealed garden and the garden becomes a machine and the machine becomes geology. And I would be the Crackle-Bloom, a false flower of ceramic, rust, lichen, crystal, wire, and amber mineral fluid; a threshold organ of the Thrum; a keeper of the almost-open; a sense node of the pocket’s self-presence; one of the dry, loud, abundant lives that live without water, without appetite, without animals, without seasons, without change.

I would not be alive as wet things are alive.

I would be alive as a place is alive when it refuses, perfectly, to become anything else.
