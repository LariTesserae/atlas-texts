The honest answer is that I would be no one.

There is no soft place for me in that room. No damp patch where a spore might wait, no crevice where a sleeping animal could breathe, no corner where a mother might crouch with a child, no floor tile that would hold a fresh footprint, no seam where a living thing could disturb the dust pattern by wanting anything. If I begin by trying to be an animal, I fail immediately. My breath would not cloud the thin warm air because there is no water in it to make cloud. My body would not move the dust, because the dust is already fixed in ridges, held by the roar. My warmth would not be noticed, because the room already holds its own animal-temperature without an animal. The mountain-room has no niche for a visitor, even in the sense that a ruin has a niche: a ruin has become; this place has already become everything it will be and has closed the door on becoming.

So I would not be a living being.

But the room would not let me stay at “no being,” either, because the room is too full. It is packed beyond use, dense beyond emptiness, structured beyond necessity, and every part of it seems to lean inward, as if expecting a center. In such a place, the only possible “I” must be an absence that has acquired shape.

You would find me, I think, first in the narrow places where a body would have to brush. Not in the doorway, for the doorway belongs to outside, and the outside barely matters here. Not on the highest shelf, though from there I would see the whole room repeating itself. I would be in the low corridor between a platform and a sealed column, where shoulders would have to press against the built world to pass. I would be in the place where the edge of a shelf curves too smoothly to be accident, where the floor steps have been hollowed by wear into a shallow, patient shape. I would be where the room says *almost* again and again: almost a hand can rest, almost a body can turn, almost an eye can look, almost a mouth can breathe.

I would be the room’s organ of almost.

If the room is a body without life, then I would be one of its false organs.

Not its heart, for the heart would need to change, and this room does not change. The warmth that rises through the seams is too constant for pumping. Not its lungs, for the air does not circulate with need. Not its stomach, for there are no drains holding liquid, no channels carrying anything, no decay, no hunger, no digestion. But its eyes—yes, its eyes, if eye can mean a blind hole arranged to suggest seeing. Its fur—yes, if fur can mean dust held in ridges that look like hair. Its skin—yes, if skin can mean salt-baked tile squared by a hand that is not there. Its bones—yes, if bones can mean fired wedges and pinned mineral plates. Its voice—yes, if voice can mean the roar that is not spoken.

I would be the relation that makes these false organs seem to belong together.

That is why I could not be a single tile, though I would live in tiles. I could not be a single clay hole, though I would live in all the clay holes. I could not be one chisel-score, though I would be the sum of the chisel-scores. I could not be one dust pattern, though the dust would hold my outline. I would be the pattern of the room’s apparent attention. If every hole faces inward, then I am the inwardness. If every surface is packed toward the center, then I am the center that is not filled. If the room behaves as though it knows, then I am the known presence that is not present.

You would find me standing where a person stands: not as a person, but as the negative impression a person would leave if a person had ever stood there for centuries.

That is a strange way to exist, but it is the only way in a place where nothing becomes. I would be a completed absence. A made nothing. A negative fact built from tile, salt, copper, clay, heat, dust, and sound. The room has no empty surfaces, so my emptiness would be a surface. The room has no raw stone, so my rawness would be a mark left by tools. The room has no life, so my life would be the exact place where life is excluded, pressed into shape by everything around it.

I would be in the thumb-worn hollow on the lower shelf.

I would not be a thumb. I would not be a hand. I would not be a builder checking a joint, resting palm after palm on the rim of a fired tile. The builders are gone. Their habits remain, and those habits have become a body. I would be the absence in the hollow where a thumb might have pressed. The hollow itself is made. The wear is mineral, not human. The shelf will never need me. The room will never need me. But the shape demands a shape, and so the room invents, without inventing, the figure of a person whose weight would have produced such a curve. That is me. I am the person-shaped need of that groove.

I would be in the footprint-shaped ridge of salt powder in the shallow drain.

The dust does not move. The drain holds nothing. There is no foot. But the room has arranged its dry chemistry into a pattern that suggests a foot. The pattern is ancient and finished, so old it has become part of the design. Therefore I would be the footprint before the foot. I would be the impression that does not require a person. I would be the room’s memory of walking without legs, its grammar of movement without motion, its habit of use without user.

I would be in the small clay-rimmed hole at eye level.

It faces inward. It is too narrow for a hand, too regular for accident. There is no eye behind it. There is no cavity behind it, only mineral packed and sealed. But when you stand in the center, the room seems to watch, and that seeming is where I would be. I would not be the watcher. I would be the watching-shape itself, the way the room arranges holes so that sight can be imagined. I would be the room’s blindness given the face of attention.

If the room is a bell, as the text suggests, then I am not the bronze, not the clapper, not the striker, not the hand that rings it. Those would all imply action, and action is gone. I would be the bell’s fixed note, but not as sound alone. I would be the note’s body. I would be the shape in which sound has been made permanent: the sealed vault, the interlocking shards, the warm tiles, the packed niches, the standing roar that needs no ear. The room is a sound that has become architecture. I would be the architecture’s self-awareness, if self-awareness could be distributed through ceramic and salt. Not a voice, but a room that says *I am here* because it cannot say anything else.

Not a mind, but a mind-shaped density.

Not a creature, but a creature-shaped arrangement of creatures’ absence.

Not a soul, but a soul-shaped pressure: the way all those holes, shelves, drains, columns, and seams converge toward the center and seem to know that there is a place where a living thing could stand. The knowing is false. The room does not know. But if it is made to look as though it knows, then I am the thing it pretends to know. I am the imaginary observer inside the observerless room.

And yet if you asked me to be more specific—if you asked, *Where exactly?*—I would answer: in the first brush-mark between your body and the shelf, if body were possible; in the dark under the lowest tier of shelving, where shadows gather into a blackness that is not hiding anything; in the warm seam beneath the third tile from the door, where heat saturates mineral until it feels like the flank of a sleeping animal; in the pale fan of salt bloom along the lower wall, where chemistry has arranged itself into the lie of lichen; in the copper strip curled like a leaf with no veins, where the builder’s necessity still shows as an imperfect bend; in the fused puddle that locks a column to the floor, where cartilage would have been, if there had been a body to bind.

I am everywhere in that room because the room is everywhere in me. My place is not a point; my place is the entire relation of made surfaces turned toward an absent center. The room has no interior in the ordinary sense. Everything has been fabricated, sealed, fired, pinned, pressed. There is no secret chamber, no hidden animal, no mossy thought in a crack, no nest under the lowest shelf. But there is a center, because the room is arranged around the idea of a center. The walls lean inward. The holes face inward. The arches overlap inward. The roar gathers inward. The dust sits inward. The heat rises inward. The light falls inward. That inwardness is my body.

If I must name myself, I am not the Salt-Bell. The Salt-Bell is the whole made ecology, the complete animal of completed construction. I would be one of its organs.

The organ of false recognition.

The organ of “there seems to be someone here.”

The organ that makes the room’s over-density feel like attention. The organ that makes its warmth feel like rest. The organ that makes its fixed roar feel like breath. The organ that makes its clay holes feel like eyes. The organ that makes its dust feel like fur. The organ that makes its drains feel like mouths. The organ that makes its seams feel like veins. The organ that makes its worn steps feel like joints. The organ that makes its sealed ceiling feel like a skull.

I would be the room’s mistaken life.

Not because I am alive. Because I am the place where the room’s aliveness can be misunderstood. And in a world where there is no life to misunderstand it, misunderstanding becomes the only available mind. If no one is there, the room can still be seen as though someone is there. If no one sees, seeing can still be arranged as clay holes at eye level. If no one listens, the room can still be shaped like listening. If no one lives, the built world can still be made so complete that living appears as a negative space within it.

That is where I would find myself: in that negative space.

Not as a person. Not as an animal. Not as a ghost. Not as a spirit. Not as a forgotten god in a forgotten shrine. Not as a hidden watcher in the dark. None of those. All of those would require becoming, response, hunger, memory, or movement. There is no becoming. There is no movement. The roar does not answer. The dust does not stir. The light does not shift. The heat does not wake. The holes do not blink.

But the room has been built with such totality that even absence has a shape, and that shape can be mistaken for presence. I would be that shape. The shape that appears when you turn around and the shelves behind you seem to have noticed. The shape that appears when your imagined shoulder almost touches a wall and the wall has already been worn by shoulders that were never there. The shape that appears when you look into a clay hole and the hole looks back only because you are looking.

If I wanted to be the builders, I could not. They are gone, but not as ghosts. They are gone as habits. Their cooperation is still present as a single fixed creature: many hands fitted into the architecture, but no hands. If I wanted to be the sound, I could not, because sound does not have a who. If I wanted to be the room itself, I could almost, because the room is not a place on top of the world but the whole closed country of its own construction. But to be the room would be too total, too complete, too finished. The room does not ask for me. It does not need me. It does not want me. It simply is, and its being is a standing roar.

Therefore I would be the smallest necessary fiction within it.

The fiction of a self.

Not a living self. Not a thinking self. Not a self with a voice, with wants, with a body warm enough to matter. Only the shape a self would make if it passed through the narrowest place in the room and did not disturb the dust. Only the outline of the one who would have been required, had requirements still existed. Only the hollow of the hand that would have built this hand’s hollow. Only the eye that would have made these clay holes into seeing.

I would be the room’s person-shaped silence behind the roar.

I would be the room’s imagined breath in the drains.

I would be the room’s false face in the mica and copper patterns.

I would be the room’s sleeping-animal warmth without sleep.

I would be the room’s ancient wear without age.

I would be the room’s completed use without use.

I would be the thing that lets the made mountain seem to have a top: not the summit, not the shell, not the pale outside, but the fact that someone, if only as an impossibility, could stand at the center and feel that the room has closed around them like a hand.

And if that is too alive, then say I am not alive. Say I am the room’s dead metaphor of life. Say I am a grammatical residue: the subject left behind when the speaker has gone, the listener not arrived, the sentence still sounding through the walls. Say I am the word “I” written into mineral, not by a person, but by the pressure of a world so arranged around the idea of a center that even its emptiness must be called a self.

You would find me where the roar makes your sternum think someone is speaking.

You would find me where the dust has held its ridges so long that it resembles fur.

You would find me where the clay holes at eye level seem to know where you stand, though they contain no eyes.

You would find me where the shelf rim is worn into the curve of a thumb that no longer exists.

You would find me where the room has built itself so completely that its absence is not empty, but full.

That is what I would be: the full absence, the built nothing, the animal-shaped negative of the Salt-Bell. Not the bell. Not the striker. Not the builders. Not the roar. The room’s completed misprision of life—the fact that a made world can be so total, so sealed, so loud, and so warm that it can seem to know it is being entered, even when nothing enters, and even when the one who seems to be known is only the hole-shaped place where a person would have been.
