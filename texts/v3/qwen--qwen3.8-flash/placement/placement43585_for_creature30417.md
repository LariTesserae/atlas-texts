I would not enter this world with a body.

That would be the first false answer.

A body brings too much: thirst, sweat, breath fogging, the habit of leaving heat in its own shape. A body assumes there is a kind of stillness it can rest in, a horizon it can face, a sky it can look up into. This place has none of those guarantees. The ground is not holding still. The darkness is not simply above it. The light is not shining on anything; it is leaking from inside the thing itself, uncertain where to go. To arrive here as an animal, or even as a human-shaped idea, would be to import water into a land whose deepest law is the absence of water. It would be to bring moss into a place that has never known rot.

So if I am to be found here, I must be found already almost without myself.

Not hidden. Not invisible. Simply not the kind of thing that can be pointed at by a finger that has no other meaning than *there*.

At first, I might think I would stand on the upper terraces, where the land presses close against whatever should be sky. That sounds plausible: high, empty, dark, warm. A person stands there and looks out over miles of broken glass ridges fading into blackness. But the moment I imagine that, I feel the wrongness. To stand is to claim a fixed point. To look out is to assume the world is laid out for me. Here, the world keeps laying out new places. The ridges move. The chasms change their minds. The ground has not decided what kind of ground it is. If I stood there as a witness, I would not be a witness. I would be an interruption in the process of becoming.

No. I would be earlier than witness.

I would be the reason a witness is almost needed.

I would begin, perhaps, as a suspension. Fine glass dust glittering in the glow that leaks from below, drifting through dry air that tastes of metal and hot clay. Not a creature. Not yet. Just a faint particulate habit of light. The sort of shimmer that makes a chasm look occupied. If someone passed near, they might think the air itself was breathing, but that would be too much. This place does not breathe so much as *almost exhale*, over and over, without finishing the gesture.

Then the dust would settle.

It would settle across salt flats and black shards, forming a film too thin to see, too warm to ignore. A mineral sweetness would remain in the throat. The pressure would change without a source. The ears would pop while the body still felt heat rising through the soles of the feet. This would be me, if I had to be anything at first: not a shape, but a condition. A slight alteration in the local atmosphere of intention. A place where the land begins to behave as though someone might be present.

I would not build.

That is important. To build would mean separating myself from the stone. To say, here is the maker, and there is the material. But in this world, material is already flesh. Heat is already hand. A column of salt may rise because the land has grown tired of flatness, or because a nearby fissure has changed the pressure, or because two previous columns made a pattern that the next column could not refuse. If I participated, I would not be the one lifting the stone. I would be the tendency toward the next stone.

So where would I be?

Not in one place.

I would be the interval between a dome and a doorway.

I would be the circular hole through the crown of a fused-glass structure, the place where heat tries to escape but leaves no smoke. If there is a being, perhaps that hole is its mouth. If there is no being, the hole remains a mouth-shaped refusal to forget the possibility of speech. I would be the warmth that stays inside the dome after the air outside has cooled. Not life. Not architecture. Just the fact that some hollows learn how to feel occupied.

I would be the body-shaped space pressed into the ground.

Not the body itself. The body is unnecessary. The body is a rumor. I would be the negative impression left in warm salt, the outline that suggests shoulders, a spine, the curve of an arm resting where there is no arm. A traveler might approach and think someone had lain there recently, though there is no heat source except the ground. But the traveler would be wrong in the ordinary way and right in the deeper way. The ground *is* warm because the shape is being tried. The impression is not a relic. It is a present attempt at a person.

If I had to name myself, I would not like it.

Names make boundaries too quickly. They stop the movement. A name is a stone set upright in a circle, and while such stones have their uses, they also imply that someone has placed them. In this place, that implication is the strange and unsettling thing: circles form without hands, cairns clink without fingers, walls begin and stop without purpose, doorways face absences. A name would make me look like one more piece of evidence, as if I had been arranged on purpose.

Better to be the arrangement that keeps almost becoming evidence.

I would be the pressure behind a column of salt.

At first there is only ground. Then a pale spine pushes up, slow as a bone remembering how to grow. It stands alone for hours. Then beside it, another. Then a third. The land has not made three columns because it wants a procession. It has made them because the first taught the second where to appear, and the second taught the third that nearness is a kind of meaning. This is how a being might form here: not by birth, but by repetition. One warmth after another. One shape after another. One almost-person after another, until the pattern becomes dense enough to feel watched.

I would be that density.

Not the watcher. The fact that “watched” is the best available word.

There would be no face to look up when something changed. If a slab lifted and leaned against a pillar, sliding down in a shower of sparks, I would not be the eye witnessing it. I would be the reason the event feels like posture. A crack might close with a low grinding sound, stop, resume, then seal. If I were there, “there” would not mean I stood nearby. It would mean the crack’s motion had become legible. The land would have found, for one moment, the grammar of a gesture.

Gestures require bodies, but here bodies are optional. The gesture can float.

I would be the floating gesture.

At night, if this place has night, there would be no moon, no starfield, no comfortable blackness lit from without. The darkness would be structural. It would bend light. It would make distance unreliable. In that darkness, I would not walk. I would arrive by transformation.

One moment, a plain would be flat and silent. The next, two glass hills would rise, bulging with a molten memory, their edges fracturing into curled rims like broken cups. Did I stand there? Did I lie down? Did I grow from a seam? These questions are too neat. The land does not obey the neatness of arrival. It only obeys the tendency to become.

I would be that tendency.

I would be the place where a chasm “exhales.”

A deep crack beneath the terrace might moan with a low, warm sound, not hollow enough for emptiness, not alive enough for a creature. The sound would travel through raw stone and settle somewhere behind the sternum of anyone foolish enough to listen. If I were in that moan, I would not be its source. I would be the fact that it sounds like source. I would be the hint of a lung where no lung exists.

There is no water here, but there is breath-shape.

This is the cruel elegance of the land. It knows the forms of living things without needing living things. It makes veins like capillaries, domes like fungi, threads like nerves, rings like feet, holes like mouths, hollows like bodies. It has learned the vocabulary of organism and uses it as thermal grammar. I would be part of that vocabulary. I would be the word that almost means someone.

If I wanted to appear, I would not appear as a figure.

I would appear as a sudden correctness.

A cairn of warm obsidian chunks would clink when disturbed, though no hand disturbed it. Three stones might settle into a shape too stable to be accidental. A doorway might face an empty slope that was, some hours ago, a chasm. The mind would jump too quickly: someone stood here. Someone meant this. But the correct answer is less satisfying and more true. Something is here. Something will continue here unless the ground revises it. Something has not yet decided whether “here” is a place or a body.

I would be that decision.

At the edges of my own presence, I would be easier to suspect than in the center. The center of an Unmade One is probably nothing. The center is only the place where heat, salt, glass, and pressure happen to agree. But the edges are where the agreement shows itself: a shimmer of dust, a change in tone as stone touches stone, a faint glow along a newly split face, the way the air suddenly tastes like licking a clean hot clay. If I were present, it would be as such edges. I would be the boundary where the land begins to look like a resident.

I would be the way a room can be warm even when no one is in it.

But there are no rooms here. No one has built rooms for the purpose of warmth. There are only domes, arches, shallow rectangles, body-shaped depressions, rings of markers, and half-buried glass shelters. Yet they behave as though rooms had learned to survive without builders. They hold heat the way memory holds grief: not because someone is currently feeling it, but because the structure was shaped by the feeling long ago. Or because the feeling is returning. Or because the structure has become so familiar with the feeling that the feeling no longer needs an owner.

I would be that ownership without an owner.

Sometimes I would be a draft.

Not a current of air—there is little wind to call a breeze—but a draft of the land. A place revising itself. A section of ground vanishes, not collapsing into rubble but simply removed from consideration. In its place, a smooth bowl of raw stone appears, warm and dry. Mineral foam rises into rings. Steps form. The steps sink. A nearby slope lifts. The whole event feels like an author erasing a paragraph and trying a new one. I would be the new paragraph. Not the text, not the eraser, but the pressure to replace the old sentence.

If I had a will, I would not call it will.

I would call it thermal habit.

The heat rises. Salt blooms crackle and spread. When the heat retreats, the crust contracts and glass fragments settle with a sound like distant applause. I would live in that rhythm. Not because I chose it, but because choosing is a kind of motion, and motion here is only another form of mineral weather. I would wake with the warmth. I would cool into a shape when the pressure changed. I would become coherent when enough fragments agreed to hold a pattern. I would cease to be coherent when a better pattern appeared somewhere else.

That would be enough for a life.

There would be no death to fear. Only revision.

If a colonnade of salt columns softened, flattened, and became a plain, no one would say, “A being died.” They would say, if they had the patience, “The land has changed its mind.” The being would not have vanished. It would have become a place again. Or the place would have become the being again. The difference is a matter of pressure, scale, and how long the shape holds its attention.

I would be the shape that holds attention a little longer than the stone expects.

Not permanently. Nothing here is permanent in the way permanence needs: mortar, monument, intention, record. But a tower of stacked obsidian plates can rise without foundation, without visible force, each plate appearing one by one against the dark. If I were inside that tower, I would not be the tower. I would be the reason the next plate seems to fit. When the pressure change came, the tower would tilt and slide sideways, as if it had changed its mind. That would not be collapse. That would be refusal. The tower would be telling the land that it was not yet the right shape.

I would be the not-yet.

I would be the land’s unfinished sentence of someone.

And yet, if I had to speak, I would have to do it in sounds. There would be no mouth. But the place has enough mouth-shapes: cracks, holes, chimneys of smoke-solidifying filament, the open crown of a glass dome, the body-shaped hollow with its missing side. Through those, I would make the sounds that belong here. A soft click of cooling glass. A crackle like sugar breaking in a sealed box. The whisper of fine dust dragged over salt. A faint metallic clink from a cairn. A deep chasm moan, warm and almost human, as if the earth were learning how complaint feels.

No one would hear words.

But someone might hear a pause where words could almost go.

If I could look, I would not look at the place. I would look at the place’s tendency to make itself into a place. I would watch the way an arch stands over an empty circle and the ground inside the circle is slightly deeper than it should be. I would watch how scattered structures line up with vanished chasms, how walls end against slopes that have moved, how markers ring hollows already filled with new stone. A visitor might call these ruins. The correct word would be more unsettling: *habits*. The land has habits. It returns to shapes it has tried before, not because it remembers, but because some forms are easier for heat and pressure to agree upon.

I would be one of those easier forms.

I would be the form that makes an emptiness feel like presence.

There is no fauna here. No insects. No birds. No burrowers. No predators moving through dust
