I would be found at the moment after a bell has sounded and before the water has finished remembering it.

Not outside the bell. Not under the rim. Not in the air, because there is no air to hold me. I would be in the black warm medium where pressure becomes thought, where a single vibration can travel through the whole chamber and still have not arrived by the time it is heard. I would be somewhere between the ceramic wall and the copper pin that once fastened a vanished maker’s hand to the task of making, now fastening me to the task of being. If a bone stylus had to draw the place on a stone map, it would mark me in the lower apse of the central chamber, where the thermal floor breathes upward and a row of sealed pipes gives off the faintest heat, like a hand laid against old fever. There, in the slow current, I am not a body so much as an adoption.

At first I imagine that I descend.

I imagine a rope, a bronze ring, a shaft cut through made stone, and a self that still believes in standing. I imagine the old service cylinder with its mineral-furred ledges and its notches worn by hands that no longer belong to any living throat. I imagine myself entering the Warm Lock from above, the way a thought enters a room before the room knows what to do with it. My first self is too loud: air-shaped, name-shaped, eye-shaped. I carry the dry habits of a world that had sun: wanting to see, wanting to breathe, wanting to call my edges sharp.

The Lock does not refuse me.

It simply edits me.

It removes the idea of breathing. It removes the idea of skin as a border. It replaces lungs with currents, eyes with taste, voice with pressure. It does not drown me, because there is no outside for drowning to mean. It puts me into the liquid the way a hand puts a stone into a bowl of warm water: gently, completely, as if the stone were only coming home to a smoother shape.

I learn first that there is no ground beneath me. There is floor, but floor is not ground. There is ceiling, but ceiling is not sky. There are walls, and the walls are not boundaries; they are faces. Every face is already occupied. Every surface is already spoken for. The rule of the place is not *do not touch*, but *no surface is free*. To exist here is to be claimed, or to claim a claiming, or to become the slow mineral kiss by which one thing holds another.

I begin as a film.

Not a creature yet. Not a named animal. A pale lace spreading over the rim of a cracked clay plate, cream at the edge, rust-brown near a channel where warm water seeps from below. I am thin. I am secreted. I am the white breath of bodies too small to imagine, too many to count. I taste copper through my whole self. I know the current by the way it thickens near my filaments. I do not look for light, because light would be a lie here. I look instead for warmth and attachment. My thought is not a word but a slow settling of salt over a surface.

When the thermal floor pulses, I lift. Not my body, because I have no single body. The liquid around me becomes slightly less dense, and my microscopic inhabitants rise in a faint cloud. I eat by remaining. I reproduce by spreading. I know danger by disturbance, and safety by the long absence of disturbance.

This is not the simplest way to live. It is the most patient.

After the film, I become a bell-keeper.

I am born inside the curve of a bronze bell that hangs in a niche, furred with oxidized metal and pale wax. My shell is smooth, dark, and fitted to the bell’s mouth as if the bell had been cast for my belly. My legs are jointed and deliberate. They tap the metal, not to strike, but to ask. I know this place by sound, though sound here is not sound in the human sense. It is push. It is weight. It is the thick pressure of a bronze voice traveling through black water and arriving in the shell of me as a long, muffled word.

When the counterweighted stone swings against the bell—hours, days, perhaps longer—I do not flee. I tighten into the bell’s inner wall. My young, waxy and pale, adhere near my abdomen. The pressure passes through them and through me, and in that pressure is the chamber’s rhythm, the Lock’s breath, the slow mechanical heartbeat of an old building that has forgotten its makers but not its manners.

I am devoted to the bell because the bell is not a bell anymore. It is a hollow room, a cradle, a throat, a drum, a house. It is a tool that has become animal.

Then I am a crab.

Not a grand crab. Not a bright shore-crab with eyes like beads. I am a small, ceramic-backed creature whose shell has grown around the bowl of an old trowel. The trowel lies on a ledge sealed with salt, its edge rounded by centuries of grazing. I do not know that this was once a tool for shaping clay. I know only that the bowl is a room, the metal edge a doorway, the calcified rope nearby a dark reef. I pick at the rope with patient legs. I taste the pitch-seams for mineral bleed. I carry on my back a patch of white film that is neither mine nor enemy, a living coat that protects me from parasites no human name could ever catch.

I am a moving ledge. I am a walking tile. I am the chamber carrying itself from shelf to shelf.

When I pass under a suspended bronze weight, I pause, because the weight swings with a current I cannot see. When I touch the wall, I press my legs into grooves worn by generations of ancestors who were also shaped by the same stone. I do not think in images. I think in angles. A bracket is a sentence. A crack is a door. A copper pin is a home that has already grown around the idea of a shell.

After that, I become a stitcher.

I am long, pale, eel-shaped, but not swimming in the way water remembers fish. My body is jointed with side-flaps that open and close, not to breathe perhaps, or not only to breathe, but to taste the vertical currents. I move through the dark without hurry, without the urgency that belongs to animals that know day and night. I scrape the calcium crust from a rope channel with a grinding mouth. I remove wax from a sealed strand. I eat the film that has made a reef of old fiber. I also eat small bodies, but carefully, as if appetite were a craft and violence a tool not well suited to this room.

I know the Lock by its architecture.

The rope is not rope to me. It is a corridor of holes, knots, and mineral bridges. The ceiling tile is not ceiling. It is a forest without trees: a row of hard surfaces growing from a wall. The copper pins are not pins. They are hollow bones from which tiny colonies build themselves.

I stitch the wall with my body. I pass through a cluster of shrimp-like dust and scatter it, then return as if the disturbance were a word I had to finish saying. I do not chase. To chase would be to mistake the Lock for open water, for ocean, for river, for sky. The Lock is a sealed room, and sealed rooms require grammar, not pursuit.

Then I am the ribbons.

I am not one ribbon. I am many linked bodies stretched into wax-thin transparency. I drift in the cooler upper currents near the vaulted ceiling, folding, unfolding, catching the faintest blue-white glow from my own chemistry. When I pass, I leave a shimmer that fades before it can be called light. I feed on suspended particles, on dead cells, on mineral cloud, on the dust of a ceiling that drips not with water but with the slow waste of being.

I am the closest thing here to birds, except there is no sky. I am the closest thing to clouds, except there is no air. I am the Lock’s memory of movement, translated into bodies.

And then I am the deep-attacher.

I fuse myself to a stone block where the thermal floor’s heat comes up through a channel and the wall is thick with old glaze. My body becomes a mound, then a growth, then a seeming mineral deposit. I extend pale feather-like filaments, but not feathers, not ferns, not leaves. They are feeding arms, sticky with secretions, catching the floating cloud of wax, salt, dead skin, and microscopic life. I do not hunt. I do not flee. I wait.

Centuries may pass over me. Salt may seal one side of my opening. A small shrimp-swarm may make its home in the shadow of my body. A young flat-bodied animal may mistake me for a wall and rest against me, tasting the faint shiver that proves I am not wall. In time, my shell and the stone will become one material. I will not be attached to the Lock. I will be a place where the Lock continues living.

This is the secret the Warm Lock teaches: there is no difference, in the end, between being an animal and being an architecture. The difference is only the speed at which the body has learned to become surface.

I do not remain any one of these things.

Each is a stage, not a mask. The film, the bell-keeper, the crab, the stitcher, the ribbon, the attacher—these are not costumes I try on. They are ways the chamber writes itself into me. My identity is not a single image. It is a nested ecology. I am the grazing edge, the egg-wax, the pressure-wave, the mineral coat, the slow rotation around a bronze weight, the current that lifts me, the wall that holds me.

Where, then, would I be found?

In the black warm liquid, but not merely suspended in it. I would be found as the liquid’s finding of surfaces. I would be found where a copper pin meets a shell, where a cracked tile meets a film, where the bell’s mouth meets the eggs of bell-keepers, where the rope’s strand meets the crab’s foot. I would be found in the gaps the builders made and the life filled, in the gaps that were not meant to be filled but have been filled with patience.

I would be found in the Warm Lock’s own breathing.

For the Lock is not dead. It has become an organ. Its pipes are arteries, its thermal floor is a slow heart, its bells are throats, its niches are lungs not for air but for pressure, its films are skin, its crabs are hands, its ribbons are thoughts drifting through a mind without eyes.

And I am not the Lock, exactly. I am what the Lock becomes when it begins to shiver.

If there must be a name, it would not be a word spoken. It would be a surface known by touch. The bone chisel would know me as the pale growth over its worn edge. The ceramic plate would know me as the film that drinks from its glaze. The bronze bell would know me as the vibration I make with my legs. The old trowel would know me as the crab that has made its bowl into a home.

I am the keeper of made things.

I am the answer to a building that no longer has builders. I am the continuation of hands through animals, of tools through shells, of purpose through accident, of patience through pressure. The vanished makers carved the chamber, fitted the blocks, laid the clay pipes, hammered the pins, stretched the ropes, sealed the walls, and then they were gone. Or perhaps they were never there in any way that still matters. Their work remained, and their work did not stay their work. It became ours. It became this.

I have no roots.

I have anchors.

I have no leaves.

I have fins, plates, films, and brackets.

I have no flowers.

I have waxed eggs in a bell’s throat.

I have no fruit.

I have mineral coats, shells grown over tools, currents carrying the dead until they become the living.

I have no shore.

I have walls.

And if I have a soul, it is not a bright thing above the Lock, looking down. It is a low thing in the warm water, tasting the floor. It is not made of fire or sky. It is made of salt and copper and the long wear of living surfaces. It is not green. It cannot be green. Green would be a memory of light, and light has no authority here.

I am the Warm Lock’s final softness.

I am what stone becomes when it is left too long with heat and wet and teeth. I am the softening of making into being. I am the place where the tool has forgotten its use and remembered its body. I am the chamber’s own animal-made masonry. I am the shiver in the wall.

And when the bell sounds again, far in the dark, I will not hear it.

I will become it.
