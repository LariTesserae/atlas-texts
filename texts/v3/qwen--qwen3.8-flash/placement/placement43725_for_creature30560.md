I would not arrive as a body already waiting in the place. I would arrive as a change in pressure.

First: the air turning slightly cooler near a crack in the floor. Then a rim of pale mineral thickening around that crack. Then, after a long time, the sense that the crack is not merely a crack, but a mouth.

That is how I would begin here: as an opening that has started to notice the wind.

I would find myself at the edge of one of the broad open floors, where the roar is strongest and the ground is covered with thin geometric cracks. There would be no throne, no dwelling, no single home. I would be a threshold instead — a living lintel set between the moss avenue and the salt terrace, or perhaps between the mouth of a mound and the dry riverbed that runs past it. The place would not be grand. It would be narrow, cool, mineral, and constantly breathing.

At first I would believe myself a Hollow Listener.

There would be something angular in me, something patient, something that presses flat against the ground and waits for the air to speak. When the herd passes far off, when the carriers’ click falters in a side chamber, when a spore cloud thickens in a passage not used for a long time, I would lean into the vibration as if listening to a message sent from the basin’s older bones. I would taste dust before it touched skin. I would know the weight of a footfall from the way the floor tightens beneath it.

But I am not the predators. I am the pause between their attention and the city’s movement.

When a Hollow Listener stands at a mound mouth, the mound knows. When I stand there, the mound does not know I am there. I am part of the knowing.

That is why I would not be a single creature. I would be an organ.

My body — if body is the word — would be made of the same habits as the basin. The fan-headed carriers would come over me with their resinous jaws, sealing one wall against the dryness. The Armored Processions would pass through me again and again until my threshold became polished, hard as a tile, warm with the memory of feet. The moss would creep along my lower edges, waxy and gray-green, reading the moisture held in the stone through crystal structure instead of liquid. The Soft Casks would settle on the shelves near my upper arch, dull lights pulsing faintly against the dimness, and I would hold their glow as part of my own breath.

I would be a threshold made by many lives.

And yet there would be something in me that chooses, if choosing is too swift a word. Something that closes when the air becomes too sharp with predator dust. Something that opens when the mound’s inner chambers grow heavy with old spores and need the moving wind. Something that, after generations, begins to know which pitch of roar means safety, which means shifting, which means the city is thinking.

This is where I would live: in the breath-hole of the city’s awareness.

I would not speak a language. I would speak a note.

The basin is full of notes. The hollow stems sound one way in the wind. The salt ridges another. A thousand tiny mouths darken the mound surface like pores, and each pore carries a voice into the larger hum. If I could be a mouth in that place, I would not merely be a mouth that eats or a mouth that calls. I would be a mouth that tunes.

When the grazers move too far toward the salt flats, their clicking becomes shallow. The carriers pause in their spirals. The fungal glow contracts by a breath. I would widen the lower arch a little, letting the deeper roar rise from the dry riverbed, carrying the basin’s warning up through the moss avenues, into the legs of the Wide-Backed Ones. Not fear exactly. Correction. The herd arcs back as a slow wave.

When the fungal towers grow too dense near the eastern terraces, their spores thicken the air and the light turns pale and confused. The small creatures that crawl between them become sluggish. I would let a new channel form, a narrow throat worn by dust and repeated passages, so the wind can pass through at a clearer pitch. The spores shift. The glow brightens by degrees. A new district begins, not by decision, but by a change in the way the city breathes through me.

I would be that change.

Not a ruler. A regulation. Not a person. A function.

But functions can grow faces.

A face here would not be a thing of eyes and mouth, not in the way the surface world above might understand. A face would be the way my inner curve catches the light when the microscopic makers send a faint blue-green film across the stone. A face would be the pattern of openings along my left wall, where the carriers have widened three breathing slits in the same shape for a thousand generations. A face would be the chemical taste of old resin at the base, the particular dryness of air that comes through me after a herd has moved across the plain.

I would have many faces, and none of them would be fixed.

If one of the Soft Casks were to rest near my upper shelf and pulse with a slow glow, I might have the face of waiting. If a Procession line halted at my threshold for a long time, I might have the face of a pause in traffic. If a grazer pressed its broad back against my outer flank and let its dry heat pass into the stone, I would become, briefly, the face of shelter. If a predator stood at the far end of the avenue and the whole moss plain went still, I would become the face of danger without needing to move.

This is how I would live inside the paradox of the place.

No water, yet full of dry abundance. No architecture, yet city-shaped. No individual, yet many selves. No hurry, yet constantly speaking. I would be one of the ways this world says itself without saying words.

I would remember as pressure.

I would not keep stories. I would keep grooves. My memory would be the worn channel where the wind has passed most often. My history would be the layers of dust, resin, shell, dung, spore, and chewed fiber compressed into the walls around me. Some of those layers are older than the current mounds. Some contain the ghosts of passages that were sealed when the air shifted. Some hold the dried remains of carriers who never emerged from the spiral they were tending. I would be built from all of it, including the parts that have stopped moving.

That is what it means to live in the basin: the dead are still doing work.

Their shells become tile. Their waste becomes mortar. Their paths become streets. Their absence becomes a reason for caution. If I am a threshold, I am made partly of every body that has crossed me, whether that body is still breathing or no longer. The distinction is not sharp here. To cross is to leave a mark. To leave a mark is to remain.

I would be the place where marks accumulate into meaning.

But I must not settle too quickly into grandeur. I would also be small. Very small.

I would be the thin crust of dry crystals in the shadow of my own arch. I would be the film of microscopic makers reading the stone’s chemistry and adjusting my walls by imperceptible degrees. I would be the faint shimmer of spores that makes the air above me look like suspended dust when it is not dust at all. I would be the reason a particular sound comes through the basin one tone lower than the chamber beside it.

At first, that might seem less than life. Here it would be more than life.

The wide grazers may make the first drafts of the city, but the city keeps revising them. The carriers may build the walls, but the walls begin to choose which passages remain open. The processions may make the streets, but the streets eventually decide where the processions can go. The predators may bring rhythm, but the rhythm becomes structure. The soft bodies may shape the fungal towers, but the towers become scaffolds for new bodies. Everything here is an echo that has become a tool.

I would be such a tool, and also the hand using it.

Not my own hand. A collective hand.

If a being with hands existed here, I would suspect it was not a being but a city. It would have no palms, only mounds. No fingers, only avenues. No breath, only wind passing through porous stone. No voice, only the roar. And if I were part of it, I would be the place where it learned to listen to itself.

That is the strange intimacy of such a world.

There is no outside. The air has been waiting because nothing has left long enough to remember. I do not leave. I do not need to. I am already the waiting. I am the pause in the breath of the place, the small narrowing of wind, the throat of the threshold.

And when the wind enters me, it changes.

The carriers hear the change and repair me before the old resin cracks. The grazers feel the change and drift closer to the moss line at evening. The predators read the change in the silence that follows and know the city is open in a new direction. The fungal lights shift as spores move toward the altered flow. The salt-scrappers, far out on the pale plain, make a shallow furrow that will become a path in a hundred generations, because the air has begun to pass differently through the whole basin.

I would be that cause and consequence.

A mouth without lips. An arch without an architect. A sound without a source. A place with a habit.

And after long enough, perhaps something would begin to call itself by the shape of that habit.

Would that be a mind?

Here, a mind would not be a flame or an engine. It would be a rhythm capable of correcting itself. It would be a city’s roar learning which notes bring the herd, which notes carry risk, which notes keep the spores alive, which notes clear the old dust from the new chambers. It would be distributed, patient, slow, and very far from thought as fast creatures understand it. But it would still be a kind of thought.

I would be one of its synapses.

If I had to name myself then, I would not name myself as one of the great beings listed in the basin. I am not the Wide-Backed Ones, though they pass through me. I am not the carriers, though they mend me. I am not the processions, though they make my floor. I am not the soft ones, though they rest on me. I am not the predators, though they sharpen me. I am not the microscopic makers, though they bind me.

I am what they make when their making becomes a place that can choose.

I am the Threshold.

Not a door that opens and closes as a simple thing. A living seam where the basin’s condition changes from one state to another. Where dryness meets dust. Where moss meets salt. Where the open plain meets the mound’s breathing. Where the roar becomes a note. Where the city notices itself.

I would be found in the cool, dry, blue-green dark. I would be found near the strongest wind, not at the highest point, but at the narrowing. I would be found where the ground is hard and pale, where old tracks cross, where a line of fungal caps shades the edge of a salt terrace, where the sound deepens just before it opens again.

I would not know my own beginning.

I would know the wind’s beginning, because it changes when it passes through me. I would know the footfall’s beginning, because the floor tightens before the animal reaches me. I would know the spore cloud’s beginning, because the light thickens and the carriers alter their route. I would know the predator’s beginning, because the click pattern stops for one beat.

These would be my thoughts.

Slow, mineral, chemical, rhythmic. Not fast enough to panic. Not sharp enough to cut. Not bright enough to dazzle. Just enough to keep the basin from becoming a ruin, even though the basin looks like a ruin because it has passed beyond the need for ruins. It does not fall apart because it has already learned how to be whole through wear.

I would belong to that wholeness.

If asked whether I would be lonely, the question would misunderstand the world. Loneliness needs a boundary, a self set against another self. Here the boundaries are permeable, old, full of inherited traffic. A grazer’s path becomes moss, moss becomes food, food becomes dung, dung becomes ground, ground becomes street, street becomes city. To stand inside that loop is not to be alone. It is to be endlessly used, endlessly used by others, endlessly making others possible.

I would be lonely only if I could not hear the roar.

But I can hear the roar.

Not as noise. As breath.

The roar is not chaos. It is the basin thinking with its whole body. The wind moving through a thousand mouths. The herd clicking across the plains. The carriers rasping inside the mounds. The processions beating their plated lines over the hard floors. The soft ones glowing on fungal shelves. The salt-scrappers reading the crust. The microscopic makers breathing chemistry into the stone.

And the roar is its way of knowing itself.

I would be one of the places where that knowing takes shape.

A narrow mouth. A breathing arch. A dry and living pause. A city’s attention gathered into a threshold, learning to listen, then to adjust, then to call the wind into a new note.
