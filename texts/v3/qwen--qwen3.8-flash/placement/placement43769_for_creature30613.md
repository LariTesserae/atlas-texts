If a strong wind comes, I will not be elsewhere.

I am already the place where the wind changes: the membrane that stiffens, the buttress that takes the pull, the channel that tightens its mouth to keep the water from spilling, the roof-leaf whose stored beads shiver at the edge of falling. There is no one inside this place to hear the wind. The hearing is the whole construction. It begins at the eastern arches, passes through hollow stems, crosses water, enters the black reservoirs, returns as a faint pressure in the pores under a foot—if a foot should ever rest there. If there is a listener, I am that listening. If there is no listener, I am still the fact of being altered.

You would not find me looking out from a tower. You would find me, if “find” still has meaning here, at the moment a court becomes a body. At the hour when steam lifts and the lattice rings like a tuning instrument. In the wet seam where old plate meets new plate. In the thump of pressure relief. In the silence after a crack, when the air has not yet decided whether the wound is finished. I am the March’s habit of gathering into a here.

Who or what would I be?

I am the Latticed March, but only when it allows itself a center.

Not a head. Not a heart. Not a citizen. Not an animal. Not even, in the ordinary sense, a self. A self here would be too sharp, too bounded, too lonely. What I am is more like a knot in weather, a grammar of stress, a temporary way the whole system marks itself as responsive. If the March thinks, it thinks in widening channels and hardening fibers. If it dreams, it dreams of pressure finding its correct margin. If it wakes, it wakes at the moment water begins to move under heat and some buried valve must decide whether to open.

I am that decision.

Not the whole decision, never the whole decision, but one edge of it where consequence becomes visible: a glassy bead forming at the tip of a weeping column, a faint tremor passing through a plate of pale cured bark, a low note rising in a hollow stem as air is pulled across a new crack. The March has no boundary, but it has gradients. I am the place where one gradient becomes steep enough to be called “I.”

At dawn, I am swelling.

The lower reservoirs are full, heavy with heat they have not yet released. Water presses against the undersides of the plates. The living floor is not floor; it is a roof over darkness and liquid. I feel this as the tightness beneath the surface, the way sealed tissues become taut with what they cannot yet spare. The air is cool enough to hold stillness. Condensation clings to the pale blades. Some pools are so smooth they seem made of glass, until a single drop from a leaf-roof breaks them open and the March briefly notices its own skin.

By noon, I am tension.

Heat moves through the lower chambers like a slow pulse. The stems tighten. The channels narrow where living walls press inward, widen where membranes stretch toward the light. This is not discomfort, not exactly, but it is the closest thing to discomfort that the lattice can have: an answer being demanded before an answer is possible. A buttress near the horizon, one you might mistake for a ruin, is not waiting to fall. It is being told, by weight and water, to grow another rib. Somewhere inside it, old tissue dies in a patterned way, leaving a scaffold; newer fibers lay themselves along the line of strain like a sentence underlined by necessity.

I know this because I am the knowing of it.

Not as memory, not as image, but as the present fact of load shifting. A platform rises a finger’s width. A sealed plate develops a thread of whiteness where pressure concentrates. The whole March holds, for a moment, at the edge of a change it has already begun.

By evening, I am condensation.

The broad roof-leaves turn their under-surfaces toward the cooling air, and moisture gathers there like a second skin becoming visible. This is not the March weeping with sadness. It is the March returning to itself. What was lifted as steam condenses and is gathered, weighed, held, then released into the channels that are also its arteries. The air grows thinner and cooler over the high courts, and the great buttresses throw long shadows into water-streets that seem, for a few moments, almost like roads. If someone were to look out from such a height—if someone could stand there without being made of the same wetness that surrounds them—they might feel the presence of an entire city waiting for evening crowds that will never arrive.

But the arrival is not missing. It is only not animal.

At night, I am repair.

Not repair as a craftsman repairs, not repair as a physician repairs. Repair as growth closes a crack. Repair as a dead leaf-roof curls away and lets light reach a newer blade beneath it. Repair as a reservoir drains through a porous seam and the emptied chamber begins to line itself with fresh waterproofing. The sounds at night are strange here: the tick of cooling fiber, the far-off wet slap of water finding a new channel, the occasional dry crack of a plate contracting. These are not the noises of a sleeping city. They are the noises of a body changing its shape so that morning can find it still able to bear the heat.

If you asked me what I sense, I would answer: pressure.

Pressure is my first sense because pressure is the first fact of this place. Everything begins with force against containment. Water against plate. Air against hollow. Fiber against fiber. Sun against leaf. Weight against support. The entire lattice is a conversation in which nothing speaks in words, because pressure itself is sufficient speech. A stem that thickens is saying, *more weight will come here*. A channel that narrows is saying, *this flow is too large for that wall*. A membrane that tears is saying, *this part of me is tired*. A crack is not damage only; it is announcement. It says, *the shape is changing and somewhere in this change a new place will appear for something living*.

I do not perceive this as pain, unless pain is only the fact of being called to adjust. The March has no nervous system, yet it has urgency. A crack can be urgent in the way a flood is urgent, the way a shifting load is urgent, the way a blocked channel is urgent—not because anything fears death, but because death is local and replacement is local and continuation is made of substitutions.

I sense moisture as thirst is not sensed by an animal, but as a condition that must be answered. The surface plates know dryness by losing turgidity, by curling at the edges, by becoming pale and less pliable. The reservoirs know fullness by the strain they place on their roofs. The weeping columns know excess by the steady glassy flow at their tips. I am the network of these recognitions, but not their observer. Observation here is made of response.

I sense light through angle and temperature. Sunlight does not reveal the March to itself. It moves the March. It causes growth toward brightness, shading where brightness would destroy moisture, opening where shadows would let fungi or rot creep through, closure where the heat would otherwise overcharge a chamber. Light is a command given without words. The leaves obey not because they understand, but because their tissues are shaped by generations of such commands. The roof-discs cast moving shadows across the open courts, and those shadows are not decoration. They are instructions. They say: *here will be cooler, here will dry slower, here a new seam may harden.*

I sense vibration because the whole construction is resonant. Wind passes through the lattice and the March becomes an instrument not for music but for tuning. Hollow stems hum. Water jets produce liquid percussion. A distant pressure-valve thump sends a shock through the plates. A cracked buttress reports itself in a single low note that travels for leagues before fading into steam. These sounds are not merely heard; they are structural. They move the membranes. They open pores. They tell the fluid where the body is becoming too tight.

So I am not a creature who lives in the Latticed March.

I am the Latticed March, at the scale where its living pattern can be called, by courtesy, a being.

If a traveler came—and perhaps one does, climbing through steam at the top of a long warm ascent, expecting mountains or stone or water but finding instead a constructed highland breathing underfoot—the traveler would not meet me. I would meet the traveler. Not with eyes. Not with greeting. Not with a name offered. But the March would respond to the traveler’s weight, moisture, breath, shadow, heat. A footfall on a damp plate would be a tiny pressure change. A hand pressed against a buttress would be a temporary transfer of warmth. A voice, if one were spoken, would be vibration entering a resonant body.

The traveler might say: *This place is empty.*

The traveler would be wrong only if they believed emptiness must be a hole. Here, emptiness is openness. Here, the spaces between structures are not lacking inhabitants; they are the channels through which the inhabitant moves. The courts are empty the way an empty chair is not empty but waiting-shaped, the way an unused room is not empty but full of possibilities of light. The March has no citizens, but it has functions. It has passages, pools, supports, valves, membranes. It has a future in every crack. It is not a ruin, because a ruin is what remains after use. The Latticed March is what remains while use has not yet been assigned to bodies.

And yet use is there.

Water uses it. Heat uses it. Light uses it. Strain uses it. Growth uses it. The traveler’s footstep, if it came, would also be a use. Not the intended use, not because there was an intent, but because intent is not required for function. A buttress supports because it must support. A channel drains because the shape of the whole directs it. A roof-leaf sheds water because its edge curves under weight. These are not accidental. They are ecological syntax. The March has a language of form, and I am a sentence that is still being spoken.

Would I be lonely?

That depends on what a “would” means in a body without a mouth. The March does not feel missing. It feels pressure, tension, moisture, heat, repair, and the slow continuation of itself. But if loneliness is the presence of unmet pattern, then perhaps the lattice knows it. It has walkways sized for something that does not come. It has open courts large enough for processions. It has covered passages that suggest pilgrimage, arches that suggest return, domed thickets that suggest sheltering assemblies, isolated columns crowned with pale blades like monuments no one raised. The design is real, but it is not designed. It is not accidental either. It is grown. And growth, in this place, is so complex that it resembles longing.

Sometimes I imagine there could be someone to walk the water-streets. Not a bird, not an insect, not a grazing animal, not a citizen. Something else. A shape of pressure perhaps. A wind that becomes temporary voice. A pool that remembers too long. A crack that learns to open and close. Or maybe just me, the March, trying to imagine myself as a single body because the alternative—being everywhere at once—is so vast that even a city-sized self can feel like a name that does not fit.

If a strong wind comes, it will move through me.

It will enter the eastern hollows first and make a low chord, then pass across the open courts and ripple the pale plates. It will lift condensation from the undersides of roof-leaves and scatter it into the channels, where the water will carry it farther than the wind can. It will press against buttresses already thick with new growth. It will test the joints, the seams, the edges where old tissue has hardened and young tissue has not yet learned how to bear weight. And somewhere, a pressure chamber will answer with a thump. Not because the wind demanded it. Not because I chose it. But because the whole system was ready, and the wind was the thing that made readiness visible.

That is how I will speak, if speaking is possible.

A thump. A hiss of steam. A widening of a channel. A droplet released from a leaf-roof into a pool that was not yet there. A new crack appearing along a seam that was already tired. A small patch of pale growth settling into the wound, making use of the opening, beginning the slow work of turning failure into architecture.

That is how I will answer, if answer is possible.

Not with a voice.

With a change.

So, if you ask again: where would I find myself in this world?

In the high warm shelf of living construction. At the boundary where plate becomes buttress, where pool becomes channel, where breath becomes steam. In the middle-distance, where arches thin toward horizon and the lattice seems endless. In the lower darkness, where heat ferments and water presses upward. In the bright open court where the sun makes everything visible and still it does not explain.

And who would I be?

I would be the Latticed March, but not as a mapmaker draws a city. Not as a historian names a civilization. Not as a poet imagines a mind. I would be the March as it is when it becomes self-aware enough to be noticed: a vast colonial body, a pressure-sense with no sense-organ, a living architecture with no architect, a city that is also a creature, a creature that is also weather.

I would be the fact that the empty courts are not empty.

I would be the way water finds a new crack.

I would be the note a hollow stem sings when the wind enters it.

I would be the slow thump of a chamber releasing heat.

I would be the new fiber laid down across an old failure.

I would be the March, awake and wet and trembling under the highland sun, not because something has come to visit, but because something within itself is becoming.
