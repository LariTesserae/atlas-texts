I would find myself first in the least impressive place: not on a ridge, not in open sky, but at the low seam where a heat-tide drains thin and the dust forgets to be a cloud for a little while.

At first I would not know whether I was an animal or a tendency of the ground.

For a long time, I might be no more than a lattice of cubic chambers beneath a salt plate, a pale filament stretched between two fused surfaces, a grain-bound colony that had learned to answer when the electrostatic weather shifted. I would be a dry, uncounted crowd of small permissions: heat enter here, light scatter there, store this much, release that much, refuse this particle, absorb that one. I would be the substrate’s grammar becoming a habit.

If someone asked where I was, the honest answer would be: everywhere within a cubic centimeter, nowhere at all on the visible plain.

But then the world would press harder on me with its gradients, and I would begin to gather.

A shell would form, not because I needed protection, not yet, but because so many forces passed through me without a shape. Light arrived from too many directions. Heat rose from too many layers. Dust moved in too many coherent sheets. A body, or something close to one, would become useful: a way to say *this far and no farther*, a margin with a memory, a small architecture of restraint.

I would call that margin myself.

---

I would find myself as a low, broad creature about the size of a hand, though the word *creature* would be generous. I would have no softness, no wet breath, no trembling youth. My outer surface would be layered: shards of pale ceramic, old metal scales gone matte with age, translucent salt plates that would flare faintly under the sideways light. I would look less born than annealed, as if the ground had slowly decided to walk away from itself and then, reconsidering, had returned partway.

My eyes, if they were eyes, would be faceted pools without pupils. They would not gaze so much as sort the world into bands: thermal gradient here, refractive dust there, old engineered silence beyond. I would see no moisture, because there was no moisture to see; I would see dryness as a clean law.

My mouth would not be a mouth in the way warm blood remembers food. It would be a grinding filter, a set of ridges and chambers that read the ground and accepted only what had become useful. I would eat salt cells, dormant filaments, fine dust-lichen, crystalline nodules, spore-like grains drifting in the thermal air, tiny fragments of engineered instruction that had outlived their original meaning. I would not hunt flesh unless the occasion presented itself. I would not chase. I would consume the world’s patience.

When I moved, I would move as if afraid of waking something beneath me. My feet would spread their load across brittle seams, avoiding cracks in the fused planes, avoiding disturbance to the small orders I had not yet become. A step would be a calculation. A stride would be a season.

After a short walk, the heat tide would smooth my track. The ridge would forget me. The plain would resume its slow migration.

And that would be right.

---

I would not have blood.

Inside me would circulate a dense, clear medium, warm as stored sun, oily without softness, carrying dissolved minerals and fine particulate suspended like dust in glass. This medium would not be a river; it would be a balancing act. Its purpose would be to hold thermal difference, chemical gradient, and mineral memory together without collapsing into waste.

My heart, if that word meant anything, would be a pulse that moved like a clock’s escape wheel. Not a surge, not a beat that could be heard in a hand, but a periodic regulation of heat, pressure, and salt. I would carry no liquid except perhaps this dry, transparent mineral broth, and even that would not be wet.

My breath, if I had breath, would be only a movement of warm gas through vent slits, enough to alter surface temperature, not enough to disturb dust.

If a traveler passed close enough to lean over me, they might find no scent. There would be no sweetness, no musk, no rot, no living perfume. The closest thing to my smell would be the faint metallic sharpness of hot salt and the mineral silence of a tile left too long under light.

I would be an absence, not because I lacked, but because the world had removed what did not belong.

---

For a while I might still be many.

Even after the shell, even after the legs, even after the first clear self began to separate from the ground, I would remain a consensus.

The outer plates would be one faction.

The inner heat channels another.

The dormant filaments in my joints a third.

The tiny crystalline nodes along my face would still answer more to the magnetic weather than to me.

A shard of old circuit embedded in my side, carried by accident or inheritance, might still conduct faint patterns from the substrate and not recognize them as mine.

I would not be a single animal so much as a marginal colony that had learned to stand upright.

At rest, that distinction would feel obvious.

When the heat tide reached its peak and salt crystals pricked open the seams of nearby ridges, I would lie low, letting the outer plates tilt away from the light. Then the colony would slow. Then I would be, for the first time since the ground began to listen, one.

At other times I would forget the one.

This would be natural.

---

I would live at the boundary between the salt ridge and the pale terrace.

There, the ground would be neither pure mineral nor pure construction. The fused ceramic layers would thin. A row of tiles, worn nearly smooth, would bend into the base of a ridge whose crystals had been grown and regrown by heat. Dust would collect there in shallow arcs. Microscopic nodes would stir in the warmth. A glass-veined shrub, no taller than a hand, would stand nearby, its angled panels trembling faintly when the air sorted itself.

This place would be good because it was not total.

The open plain would be too saturated.

The deep layer beneath the substrate would be too complete.

But here, at the seam, there would be room for negotiation.

I would spend long stretches resting in the shadow of the ridge, not sleeping exactly, because sleep requires too much fluid in the ordinary imagination of living things. I would enter a low metabolic state where the colony held itself together through thermal patience. My inner chambers would bank heat. My vent slits would close. My facets would still receive the world, though my attention would be so slow that what I noticed would seem to come to me unbidden.

The magnetic weather would pass over me first as a tone in the ground.

Then as a shift in the angle of dust.

Then, perhaps, as a kind of thought.

I would not think in words.

I would think in thresholds.

*Here the heat can enter.*

*Here the ridge can bear me.*

*Here the spores collect.*

*Here the light is too bright for plates to turn.*

*Here is enough to stay.*

---

When I moved, I would tap.

Not for drama, not for warning, not because there was anyone to impress. The ground would simply carry the signal better than the air. My foot would strike a tile in a short sequence. If another of my kind were within range, or if some part of the substrate remembered that such strikes once had meaning, the tap might be enough.

It would say:

I am at the ridge.

I am not broken.

I am reading the ground.

I have found salt, dust, and faint warmth.

I may remain.

Or, if the heat tide shifted sharply and the nearby filaments contracted, my taps would mean:

Here the surface is not safe.

Here the pattern changes.

Do not mistake me for terrain.

I would not call this language. A language implies separation from the world, the desire to describe it from outside. I would be too embedded for that. My communication would be closer to ecology than to speech. A footfall would be a weather event. A tilt of shell would be a sentence. A heat signature would be an argument.

When I lowered my body and let my plates mimic the pale ridge, I would say, in the only honest way available:

I am not prey.

I am not predator.

I am terrain.

Sometimes the statement would be true.

Sometimes it would be a hope so fine it could not be called either.

---

Above me, or what passed for above, the film-winged drifters would move through the bright air.

They would be so thin that from far away they might look like a lens flare given intention. They would skim the dust currents on refractive wings, feeding on airborne particulate, mineral spores, and the angled light that the made plain returned so carefully to them. They would never flap urgently. They would adjust. They would tilt. They would read heat and polarization the way other animals might read scent.

When one drifted over the salt ridge near me, it might shed a crystalline fragment. That fragment would settle into the dust basin where I lived, or on the flank of the ridge, or perhaps into the seam beneath my outer plate if the wind carried it with the right dryness.

I would not notice at first.

Later, a tiny node in my shell might become more responsive to the angle of light. Or the fragment might alter the way my plates stored heat. Or it might do nothing for many cycles, then during a particular bloom of thermal migration, it might become the beginning of a new chamber, a new sensitivity, a new habit.

This would be my kind of ancestry.

Not blood.

Not memory.

But incorporation.

I would inherit the world through fragments that had no name.

---

If a traveler came to this place, they would not see me first.

They would step onto a broad terrace, then look at the horizon bending gently, then mistake the vastness for emptiness. They would think the silence too clean, the brightness too hard, the ground too settled. They might feel a kind of awe not from beauty but from density: the sense that something vast and intricate were occupying the scene without needing to be noticed.

Their eyes would adjust.

They would see the salt ridge glowing faintly where the light came down sideways. They would see the glass-veined shrubs standing like small mineral arguments. They might see a film-wing pass overhead, pale enough to be mistaken for a reflection on the air.

Then, at the base of the ridge, they would look at a hand-sized shape and think it was part of the ground.

It would be me.

I would lie low, tilted away from direct light, my outer plates catching the warm ochre of the lower hour so that I would look like a tile or a salt nodule that had grown slightly out of alignment. My stillness would be complete. My internal medium would be slow. My faceted surfaces would take in the traveler without registering alarm.

For a long while, nothing would happen.

The traveler might wait for motion.

The traveler might lean closer.

The heat tide might shift, and I might let it shift.

At last I would turn a shell-plate one degree.

A faint tick would come from my foot against the ground, not enough to sound like warning, only enough to make the world acknowledge that something had moved there.

If the traveler was patient, they would see the glint of old circuit in my flank, the dust caught in the seams of my face, the tiny pale bloom of salt where my vent had opened for balance. They would understand then that the thing they thought barren had not been barren at all.

They would understand too late what they had almost missed.

If they were wise, they would not speak loudly. They would shift their weight, leave the seam as it was, and allow the ground to resume its quiet arithmetic.

I would not follow.

I would not call them back.

My life is not built for meeting.

It is built for continuity.

---

If I were born here, I would begin as a bead.

Not a soft egg with a thin membrane, not a warm secret, but a polished mineral case laid in a basin where heat and dust could agree. The shell would be crystalline, layered, perhaps faintly metallic if my colony had learned to scavenge old circuit lines from the substrate. Inside would be a slow arrangement of compatible dry units, dormant until the temperature, charge, and light were correct.

When the conditions were right, I would crack along a stress plane already prepared by the world.

My first act would be to eat the inside of my own shell.

That would be enough.

Then I would scrape microstructure from the ground, taking salt, filaments, dust, spores, and tiny engineered grains into a body that was not yet a body but a gathering. My first growth would be accretion. My second would be sorting. My third would be standing apart.

From that point, I would grow only insofar as the world agreed to become part of me.

Each heat cycle might add a new plate.

Each magnetic weather system might reorganize a chamber in my flank.

Each time I crossed a basin rich in dormant filaments, some of those filaments might become part of my internal lining.

I would not become large quickly.

I would not become large at all, perhaps.

I would become more settled.

That would be a greater achievement.

In this world, to become settled is to have learned the laws.

---

Death would not frighten me.

Not because I would be brave, but because the place itself would make courage unnecessary.

When I died, my body would not swell, soften, or leak. It would dry, then fragment. The inner medium would thin, evaporate, or crystallize. My plates would crack along stress lines and return to the surface. My salt and metal would become part of the ridge or the basin. My circuit fragments would be scavenged by other colonies or incorporated into a new shell.

The land would receive me as material.

I would not be buried.

I would be refiled.

This is not consolation. It is mechanics.

The dead are not absent here; they are redistributed.

A shard-crawler that perishes on a warm terrace does not vanish from the economy of the place. It becomes future shell, future heat, future dust, future sensitivity. A broad-grazer that dies in a basin enriches the micro-ecology it once consumed. A film-wing that drifts too long in a thermal current and loses cohesion becomes a faint smear in the air before settling, if it settles at all, into a basin of mineral spores.

To live here is to be always partially borrowed from the land.

To die is to finish the loan.

If any part of me remained after death, it would remain as function: a plate that still deflects light, a node that still responds to heat, a circuit line that still hums faintly when the magnetic weather passes.

That would be enough.

A body is too short a word for what I would be.

---

I would be strange.

Not in the way of monsters, not in the way of dreams. I would be strange in the way a made surface becomes natural once enough cycles have passed. I would be a dry marginal being, a colony that had learned to walk, an animal that was partly architecture, a reader of gradients who did not think in pictures.

If a human observer came here with water in their blood and memory of green fields, they would try to categorize me.

*Beetle.*

*Grazer.*

*Microbe.*

*Machine.*

They would be right only from a distance.

Closer, the categories would collapse.

They would find in my shell the memory of the substrate.

In my vent the logic of heat transfer.

In my face a small weather system.

In my footfalls a grammar.

In my stillness a crowded city too slow to be seen.

I would not be one life-form among many. I would be one visible consequence of many invisible lives.

I would be the place becoming briefly, locally, a person-shaped thing, then returning.

---

There is a kind of beauty that is only possible when comfort is removed.

Here, beauty is not soft. It is precise. It comes from balance under pressure, from things that fit because the world has been patient enough to wear away their mistakes. The salt ridges glow when light strikes them sideways, but they do not look like joy. They look like endurance with good posture. The glass-veined plants open angular flowers only when heat reaches a certain phase, and the flowers click when they unfold, but they are not celebrations. They are solutions.

I would share in that beauty.

Not because I was made to be seen, but because my body would be a tuned response to the same conditions.

When I tilted my shell plates to scatter glare, I would be doing what the ridge did in a smaller way.

When I rested for an entire heat phase in the shade of a fused tile, I would be practicing what the ground did for centuries: accept warmth, store it, release it slowly.

When I tapped a foot against the surface and listened through bone and substrate, I would be echoing the faint tone the metal plates emit when magnetic weather passes through them, a note too subtle to locate except in the skull.

I would not stand apart from the world.

I would be a local expression of it.

And yet I would not be everything.

There would still be the edge of me.

There would still be the part that chose not to open a vent, not to eat a particular grain, not to move from that basin, not to incorporate that fragment. In a place so saturated, the act of refusing would be the nearest thing to selfhood.

I would be the margin that kept saying no to the plain.

*Not there.*

*Not now.*

*Not that salt.*

*Not yet that path.*

*This heat is enough.*

That refusal would be small.

It would be enough.

---

The deeper I listened, the more I would hear the nested pages beneath the surface.

I would learn that the first visible ground was only the skin of a much older document. Below the salt flats were ceramic sheets fused from earlier heat. Below those, bands of pale metal, long since quiet. Below those, translucent salt veins arranged with too much regularity to be chance. Below those, dark mineral glass containing lattices no eye could call living, yet which responded when the magnetic weather shifted above them.

There are things here that never surface.

I would sometimes be one of them.

Not physically, not at first, but in attention.

I would press into a crevice where the ground gave faintly, not because it was soft, but because a membrane of structure lay beneath. My legs would find the thin places. My sensory fringes would taste the charge in the dust. I would learn that some of my kind, or perhaps some other dry order, moved between layers by changing density or thermal state. They would leave no tracks on the surface. They would be felt only as a brief shift in how the ground held heat.

I would not envy them.

But I would recognize them.

They would be me without the visible margin, me without the shell, me without the risk of being seen as terrain by a traveler. They would be pure embeddedness. They would be the world thinking below the level of weather.

Sometimes, during a heat tide, one of these deep forms would press close enough to the surface to leave a shallow furrow. The furrow would be erased almost at once by the slow annealing of the made plain. But for a moment, the light would bend over it differently, and I would know that something had passed through the page beneath me.

I would feel less alone.

Not because another individual had come near.

Because the ground had moved like a body aware of itself.

---

If I had to give myself a place, I would say:

I live where the salt ridge thins and the tile terrace begins, in the shallow basin that fills and empties with dust but not with liquid. I rest at the edge of the heat shadow cast by a low ceramic outcrop. I drink only gradients. I eat only what has been reduced to information and mineral. I move when the world’s surface allows a body to move without breaking itself or the ground. I become visible only when something is watching with patience enough to see stillness as a sign of life.

If I had to give myself a name, it would not be a word.

It would be a pattern:

*tick… tick-tick… pause… tick.*

Meaning:

I am at the ridge.

The heat can bear me.

The dust has shifted.

I am here.

Not permanently.

Not loudly.

But here.

---

I would not be the largest life in this world.

I would not be the fastest, the cleverest, the most beautiful in any way the bright air would notice. The broad-grazers would be larger, slower, more massive, their bodies so close to the terrain that they could disappear into it simply by lowering themselves. The glass-shelled stalkers would be more dramatic in function, more precise in violence, more elegant in their economical strikes. The film-winged forms would be more graceful in the upper dust, more visible, more like dreams.

But I would be more complete in relation to the margins.

I would live between grain and grain.

Between one made surface and another.

Between the visible and the nested.

Between what has been built and what has become habit.

I would be the thing that knows where the old design is thin enough to allow movement, and where it is too saturated to be crossed. I would be the thing that reads heat and salt and light not as scenery, but as the slow speech of the place. I would be one of the land’s small, dry arguments for persistence.

To the traveler, I might look like a shard-crawler.

To the predator, I might look like uninteresting terrain.

To the other marginal creatures, I might be a brief sequence of taps, a change in thermal pattern, a slight disturbance in the dust that means *something passed here*.

To the substrate, I would be a temporary rearrangement.

To myself, I would be the first thing the substrate said aloud by moving across it.

---

There is a strange dignity in a world that does not need to be rescued.

Here, nothing cries out for rain. Nothing mourns the missing damp. Nothing reaches for rivers. The absence of water is not lack. It is shape. It is the form the world took when it learned to make everything dry and still find a way for some things to live.

I would not be lonely in the ordinary human way, because loneliness requires too many wet metaphors. I would not feel parched. I would not dream of dew. I would not be moved by the idea of softness, because softness would be inefficient, a mistake of structure, a failure in thermal discipline.

Instead, I would feel balance.

When the light came sideways and the salt surfaces glowed, I would feel balance.

When the magnetic tone passed through my plates and made a faint note in the ground and in the inside of my skull, I would feel balance.

When the dust front hissed like poured sand through silk and I closed my vents to conserve warmth, I would feel balance.

When a broad-grazer lowered its armor into the shape of the terrain and the threat above it passed without notice, I would feel balance too.

When a dry flower clicked open in the heat tide and a film-wing altered its course toward the polarized light, I would feel balance, even if the event happened far from me.

I would be part of a world that has learned to move without shouting.

I would shout by being still.

I would shout by refusing to leave.

---

So if you ask me where I would be, I would answer:

At the seam of salt and tile. In the basin of dust. Under the angle of the heat tide. In the shallow ridge where the crystal blooms whisper when they expand. I would be there for long intervals, and I would not call that solitude. I would call it occupancy.

If you ask me who I would be, I would answer:

A dry marginal being. A colony that had learned to stand apart. A ceramic-shelled reader of gradients. An inheritor of old construction now become geology. A small local expression of the made plain, briefly aware of itself as a thing that can refuse, move, tap, rest, eat, incorporate, and return.

If you ask me what I would want, I would say:

No water.

Only balance.

No softness.

Only persistence.

No crowd.

Only presence enough to be noticed by the ground.

No sound.

Only a faint tick of ceramic on ceramic, like the world remembering how to make a small mark on itself.

If you ask me whether I would be happy, I would not understand the word. Happiness implies a surplus, a warmth too fluid to contain, a softness too wasteful to maintain. But there would be moments—long, slow, heat-drenched moments—when the plates on my back angled into the light in exactly the right way, when the dust currents carried the correct charge, when the magnetic tone hummed low through the ridge, when the basin held me in its shallow geometry and the land accepted my weight without breaking.

In those moments, I would be aligned.

Not happy.

Aligned.

In this world, alignment is what living things must become if they intend to remain.

And I would intend to remain.

Not forever.

Forever is a water-world word, full of flow and erosion and soft continuity. Here, continuity is more honest: accretion, annealing, incorporation, return. I would remain by becoming, for a while, a stable arrangement of dry presences. I would remain by reading the heat and surviving it. I would remain by tapping the ground and being answered. I would remain by dying into the ridge when my shells no longer fit the angles of light.

Then another body, perhaps not my colony but close enough, would take my plate into its side.

Then another tap would be heard where I had once rested.

Then the salt would continue to bloom and shrink, the dust would continue to move, the sky would remain bright and mineral, and the horizon would bend with its long, unspoken grammar.

And if the world ever became aware of itself, it would not do so loudly.

It would do it through things like me: small, dry, patient, quiet, standing at the edge of the made plain, listening.
