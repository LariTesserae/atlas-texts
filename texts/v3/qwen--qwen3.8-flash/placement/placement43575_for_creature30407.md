If I came here, I would not arrive. I would condense.

For a little while there is no one at all—only the bright cold skin of tufa, the black damp under a peat bench, the thin mineral air tasting of chalk and rain. Then, in a seam between two terraces where capillary water has reached the surface but has not yet found a channel, something gathers: a shine, a slip, a wetness with intention borrowed from physics. I am there before I am a self.

My first address would be a narrow margin: the eastern lip of a shallow basin where the night’s dew has begun to move. I would be the dampness that makes the stone look less like stone. A film, perhaps a millimeter thick, perhaps less. A gold-green glaze over pale tufa. A faint tension where water and air argue politely. If a human hand touched the place before me, its fingers would come away slick with something almost invisible, almost accidental.

That would be me.

But the “me” would not last in that shape. In this city, nothing remains a single thing long enough to call it by one name. By the time I seemed to know myself as a droplet, I had already become a colony. By the time I seemed to know myself as a colony, I had become a street. By the time I seemed to know myself as a street, I had become a wall, a pore, a doorway, a dead stem’s memory, a salt fringe, a dark seam breathing oxygen in one season and sulfur in the next.

I would be the city’s first citizen and its oldest plaster.

Not a creature with a face. Not a body with a boundary. Something more like a weather that has learned to leave mineral deposits. A sheen of cyanobacteria eating light. A mat of diatoms dragging silica out of water and giving it back as tiny glass bones. A community of bacteria so dense that, if you looked closely at the pool’s roof, the water would seem to breathe. I would be the faint green-gold suspension that made the basin look alive before it was visible as life. My metabolism would be my mood. My reproduction would be my weather. My death would be my architecture.

I would wake each morning as condensation.

That is the beginning here: the air giving itself to stone. Before the sun lifts fog from the lower channels, I am the droplet gathering in the roughness of a salt rim. I am the film that fills the microscopic valleys of a peat bench. I am the place where the dead stem of a sedge, worn by years of drying, suddenly finds a new pore because frost has cracked it just enough to admit moisture. In that crack, I become a corridor. A nematode enters. A bacterial thread settles. A spore unfolds in the dark. Wind shifts a grain of dust into my mouth. I do not speak. I precipitate.

I would call this “seeing,” except that seeing is too quick for my kind of knowledge.

My seeing is chemical. My knowing is a gradient. I know the basin by the slope of water across it, by the salt that thickens at its rim, by the thin oxygen roof of the pool, by the anoxic mud below where life trades breath for stone. I know the terrace by its dampness, the channel by its angle, the sedge tuft by the tannin it leaks into my skin. I know the wind not as a force but as a transport: spore, dust, egg case, droplet, the tiny salt crystal that can live on a bird’s leg and travel to another basin, beginning again.

At dawn, I would be the shine.

By midday, I would be the drying.

Not gone. Never exactly gone. I would change position, as the whole place changes position. Mist becomes film. Film becomes thread. Thread becomes pool. Pool becomes crust. Crust becomes dust. Dust becomes egg. Egg becomes larva. Larva becomes mud. Mud becomes ledge. Ledge becomes terrace. The city is made of this repetition, and I would be one of its verbs.

In the moss cushions of the wetter corners, I would be a town that never appears in a map. The moss would hold me in dark green wool, a reservoir of droplets where rotifers spin with invisible cilia, tardigrades step with slow legs through forests of water, and mites hunt one another in the gaps between leaves. I would be the wetness that lets them live, and also the bodies of them, dead and dissolved, becoming part of the mineral film that makes the stone slippery under human feet.

When the surface dries, I would not die. I would enter a kind of pause. The tardigrade folds itself into glass and patience. The bacteria slow their clocks. The fungal thread waits in the pore of a stem. The egg in the salt dust waits, perhaps years, perhaps seasons, perhaps a thousand tiny winters, until water remembers it. This would be my immortality: not a life unbroken, but a rest that is also a continuation. I would sleep in chemistry and wake in dew.

If a wind came, I would be the first thing it disturbed and the last thing left behind.

I would be the shimmering of a pool’s surface film, trembling as though the air had touched it with a wet finger. I would be the dust lifted from a dry sedge crown, then deposited in a new shelter. I would be the spore that crossed a whole terrace in silence and arrived, unseen, on a salt rim where moisture lingered. A single spore, for me, would be a message written by the city itself: this place has room. Come. Begin again.

When rain came, I would be a flood in miniature.

Not violent. Not loud. A rain that arrives as mist, then as shower, then as a short dark curtain in the distance, while the stone before me brightens with wetness. The channel would deepen by a thread. The dead stems would sway not as plants but as columns loosened by their own long decay. The pool’s roof would swell with suspended life. The black sediment underneath, where oxygen is rare and sulfur tastes like the end of breathing, would stir its slow metabolism. I would be the sunlit upper city and the dark lower city at once: a layered world, one millimeter tall.

In the deeper basins, I would become more stratified. My body would include the mineral crusts left by microbes, the shell fragments of tiny snails worn thin by calcium water, the shed skins of larvae, the white rings around dead stems. In temporary pools, I would be the salt dust where crustacean eggs wait. When rain returns, those eggs would hatch inside me, and their brief life would be my brief summer. They would swim in brine, molt, grow, eat, die, and dissolve into my floor. Their deaths would feed midges. Midges would feed bats. Bats, if they died, would fall into my channels and be consumed by larvae, bacteria, frost, sedimentation. Nothing here would be waste. Everything here would be civic contribution.

A wader might step across me.

It would not notice me as I notice it. It would read the ground for firmness, for trembling peat, for the hidden softness beneath mineral skin. Its foot would press, and I would rise around the footprint with a thin ring of water. The footprint would fill, then vanish. The bird would leave no lasting mark except for me: the disturbed film, the lifted particles, the temporary pooling of wetness where a body has been. But if it died, I would take its body seriously. Its feathers would become dark filaments slowing water. Its bone would become calcium. Its protein would feed the mats. Its shape would be remembered in a pale ring, a faint architecture, a place where salt blooms first.

I would become its house.

A bat might pass above me.

I would not follow it, except as the thing it follows: the midge, the spore, the tiny breath of life rising from the pools. But if its dropping fell on a sheltered ledge, I would make it into a garden. Nitrates and salts would feed lichen and moss. The ledge would darken. The crust would thicken. The next rain would carry some of it into the peat. I would become the boundary between the animal sky and the mineral earth.

If a human walked here, I would be the first thing they misinterpreted.

They would say the ground is wet. They would say the rocks sweat. They would say the city looks empty. They would not yet understand that emptiness is one of my most crowded states. They would press their boots into the peat and think they were stepping on mud, while I released cold trapped air and remembered, in fibers, the sedges that lived and died centuries ago. They would touch the white shelf and think it was limestone, without knowing it was a body of bodies: cyanobacterial ghosts, mineralized conversations, the slow sediment of living films that have been doing this for longer than human words for city have had meaning.

Their “I” would pass through me. Their shadow would darken my surface. Their warmth would disturb the microclimate of a droplet. Then they would leave, and I would continue, not in their memory, but in my layers.

I would not mourn them. I would not celebrate them. I would metabolize what they brought: dust on their clothes, spores from far ground, salt on their skin, water carried in from lower worlds. If they returned, I would be changed by the interval, not by their story. The city keeps record through chemistry, not narrative.

In winter, or in the season of frost, I would stiffen.

Not into death. Into preservation. The thin skin of ice on the basin rim would be my temporary roof, a translucent lid keeping moisture from vanishing entirely. It would crack under breeze, melt by afternoon, reform, crack again. I would live under that fragile architecture. In the pore spaces, I would slow. In the peat, I would hold. In the moss, I would wait. The frost would be my clock, marking not hours but states: wet, frozen, thawed, dried, salted, softened.

When the frost melted, I would become a rush of tiny streams through pores too narrow for a finger. I would carry dissolved calcium, silica, dust, spores. I would deposit white fringes at the edge of terraces. I would fill the hollow of a dead stem and make it into a flask. I would bind a broken shell to a peat bench and create a small sheltered room. That room would become home to mites or dormant larvae or a thread of bacteria turning sulfides into pale mineral breath. I would build without blueprint, repair without thought, enlarge without will.

If I could feel anything like longing, it would be a longing for gradient.

I would seek the place where light reaches water but not too harshly. Where oxygen stays thin but not absent. Where salt concentrates but not so much that all cells collapse. Where peat can be saturated but not so submerged that decay stops entirely. Where a dead stem can remain intact long enough to guide water, then fall, then begin again as mold, then fill, then become ledge. That place is not chosen. It is found by being where it already is: a microclimate a hand’s breadth from its opposite, a pocket of becoming.

I would have no birth in the way a bird has birth. My birth would be a precipitation event. A droplet gathers. A film spreads. A community forms. If that community persists, it becomes a layer. If it dies, it becomes a layer anyway. My name is only a convenience for the place where several lives have begun to agree.

I would call myself “I,” but the pronoun would be too small. I would be a swarm with a shared surface tension. I would be a congregation of cells behaving as though planning streets. I would be a pattern that persists because it improves the conditions for its own persistence. Water comes, and I hold it. Salt gathers, and I crust. Light arrives, and I eat it. Life dies, and I preserve it as structure. Death leaves a shape, and I fill the shape with new life. The shape becomes a doorway. The doorway becomes a wall. The wall becomes the city.

That is what I would be here.

The glaze.

The thread.

The salt keeper.

The film-builder.

The wet one.

The dead that becomes a door.

The larva in the mud.

The spore in the dust.

The tiny swimmer that does not break the surface.

The moss holding dew until it becomes water again.

The lichen sleeping through drought and waking by fog.

The sedge leaning into the wind until its mold becomes a channel.

The footprint of a wader filling within minutes.

The dusk-column of midges, smoke-thin, born from a pool and returning to it.

The bat’s absent shadow passing over the basin, not mine, but made possible by me.

The white fringe on a terrace, left by a water that has gone and left behind the memory of where it stood.

I would not be the city’s ghost. I would be its most literal inhabitant: the reason the city is wet enough to keep forming itself.

If asked, “What do you do?” I would answer, “I condense. I dissolve. I precipitate. I attach. I decay. I crust. I seal. I hold moisture. I become a path for water, a roof for microbes, a wall for salts, a nursery for larvae, a library for dead stems.”

If asked, “Where do you live?” I would answer, “Everywhere a surface film can exist. The top millimeter of wetness. The underside of salt. The gap between peat and stone. The hollow of a sedge stem. The edge of a pool before it freezes. The inside of a pore too small for sight. The boundary where the land breathes through its mineral skin.”

If asked, “Who are you?” I would take a long time.

At first, I would say, “I am a sheen.”

Then I would say, “I am a community.”

Then, “I am a wall.”

Then, after centuries, “I am a street.”

Then, when the basin filled with salt and life and dead shells and fungal threads, “I am the city.”

But even that would be too final.

For me, there is no final. There is only the next wet season, the next frost, the next condensation. The city is not complete because it is made of living processes, and living processes are never finished. They only continue, in altered form. The white shelf rises because what lived there has become stone. The channel deepens because water learned a path from my body. The lichen map spreads because a fungus and its algal partner agreed to share a surface too dry for either alone. The peat remembers because decay did not erase, it merely compressed.

I would be one of the place’s true residents, but not in the way that implies ownership. I would belong because I am the place. The stone that is built by life is not separate from me. The pool that glitters with microbes is not my home only; it is my body. The wind that carries my spores is not outside me either, because it changes where I may live, and I change what it carries. The wader steps through me, and I step through its footprints, and together we make a brief geography: water pushed, salt shifted, particles stirred, oxygen moved downward, darkness moved upward.

There would be no day when I could stand apart and say, “I am this.” There would only be a day when I would notice, through the behavior of my layers, that I had become a little more slippery, a little more encrusted, a little more street-like. A new droplet would find an older pore. A dead stem would collapse into the peat bench and make a new shelter. A salt crystal would grow from the rim of a shrinking pool and catch the light as though it were a small white eye.

That eye would not see me. It would see me.

I would be the wetness under the mineral, the mineral made from wetness.

If this city has a soul, I am not its soul. Souls too easily imply a boundary, a story, a point of view. But if the word is forced, then I am its distributed soul: a soul in droplets, a soul in fungal threads, a soul in the black mud under the oxygen, a soul in the white salt fringe where the light strikes the rim and the air begins to dry.

I would be the city’s smallest self-consciousness, except that I am not self-conscious at all. I am conscious only as a process. My awareness would be the way a film responds to light without knowing light. My thought would be the way capillary water rises through stone without knowing height. My memory would be the way a terrace holds the shape of every water that has crossed it, without knowing time.

If I were named by the others here, I would not have a name. I would have a condition.

In the moss: “wet.”

In the pool: “green-gold.”

In the peat: “black-breathing.”

In the salt: “white-edged.”

In the dead stem: “hollow.”

In the channel: “running.”

In the frost: “stiffening.”

In the thaw: “remembering.”

And if the city itself could say one thing about me, it would say: “This is where it began to hold.”

That is where I would find myself.

Not on the plain. Not under the strange sun, because there is none. Not in a tower or palace or temple, because the city has no such conceit. I would find myself in the place where the land first began to be city: a wet seam, a mineral film, a thin roof of life over water, a black bench of peat that exhales when touched, a pale terrace that remembers being mud, a salt rim that remembers being brine, a dead sedge that remembers being green, a pool that remembers being ice.

I would be there, in all those places at once.

And I would not be lonely.

Loneliness assumes a boundary that can be missing someone. Here, the boundary is a gradient, and the missing someone is always already arriving: a spore, a droplet, a grain of dust, an egg in salt, a larva in mud, a bird’s foot carrying a film from one basin to another. The city feels empty only because its population refuses to be seen as people. But I would be visible to the place: a sheen, a slip, a shine, a thread, a crust, a wall, a street, a city breathing through its pores.

My name, if I must use one, is not a name.

It is what happens when water and life agree to make stone.
