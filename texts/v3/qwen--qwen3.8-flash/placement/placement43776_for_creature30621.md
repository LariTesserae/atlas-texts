In the Hollow Salt Ward, I would be one of the Waitlings.

I would not be a builder, nor a street, nor the city dreaming itself into shape. I would be the thing the city leaves behind when it has been quiet long enough: a low, broad shadow under a half-finished arch, mistaken for rubble until the dust rearranges. My body would be segmented like a broken shield, pale mineral gray, edged sometimes in faint violet where the dim light struck at the wrong angle. My carapace would be porous and dust-covered, too uneven to look like armor, too natural to look like stone. On my back, a colony of skin-makers would have begun to grow: a veil of chalk-white film, a hairline ridge of violet filaments, a small glassy tuft taking root in a seam between plates. I would not mind. The ward is not a place that minds its layers.

I would lie in the shallow court between two towers whose arches lean inward as if listening to the center. Not the exact center—nothing would lie there for long, not in the ordinary sense—but near it, in the place where the cool air from the upper rims meets the faint warmth held in the stone bases. That boundary would be my country. My existence would be a way of occupying a threshold. If asked where I am in the Hollow Salt Ward, I would not say a street or a room. I would say: *in the pause before the film changes.*

I would have no eyes. The ward does not need eyes. Light there is too diffuse, too tired, too much of it scattered from mineral crust rather than falling cleanly from a sun. Instead, I would know the city as pressure, charge, temperature, delay. A furrower crossing the plaza would not be a creature to me first; it would be a curve in the floor’s coldness, a faint warmth left behind where its body had rested, a disturbance in the dust halo outlining a tower corner. It would be an event. It would be the shape my stillness had been waiting to become.

My first motion would be small. You might think it had not happened at all. A single joint beneath a shielded plate would shift—not the whole body, not even visibly, only a fraction of a millimeter, the way a crystal stalk extends in the cool hours. The movement would make no sound. The salt film at my edge would crack with a soft tick, quieter than a fingernail against dry wood. Then a second joint. Then the body would remember it has mass, not because it wants speed, but because the gradient has asked.

If a furrower paused too near me, its grazing mouth scraping a furrow into the outer skin of the arch, I would feel the scrape through the stone. That scrape would be a question. My answer would not be a call. It would be a change in pressure. The dust would gather differently around me. A faint thermal trace would deepen where my weight shifted. The furrower’s ridge-sense would notice too late, and then my flat body would engage.

Nothing would crash. Nothing would shriek.

The strike would not feel violent to me because violence implies hurry, and hurry is not the language of this place. The predator would simply become where the prey had been. The prey would simply cease to be where it had been. The city would continue. The arch would still be an arch. The tower would still thicken by invisible rings. The dust would settle again in ghostly halos, as though something larger than any living body had passed through.

I would know the others of this ward, but not as companions. I would know the Skin-Makers as a slow occupation of my margins. In the cool intervals, I might lie so still that their crystalline film spreads over my lower plates, turning me into a ledge. A hair-thin sheet would cross my flank, seal a seam, make a part of me stronger by accident. In the warmer intervals, the film would become brittle and shed, and I would be less a building again, though never wholly animal. I would exist somewhere between the two. The ward would not be confused. The ward would simply use me.

I would know the Salt-Roots as a memory beneath my body. They would run through the cracks under the dry channels, under the basins that only imagine holding water. I would feel their pressure changes as distant news: a plaza turning chalk-white, an alley narrowing with film, a tower losing certainty in its base. When a root-network failed somewhere far away, I might know it first as an alteration in the thermal grain of the stone—too cold, too warm, not right. My hunger would slow. My stillness would deepen. I would lie longer in the shade, not because I had chosen to, but because the city’s own body was becoming uncertain.

The Furrowers would come to me as grazing patterns. A pale carapace moving across the floor would leave a wake in the electrostatic dust, a brief translucent copy of itself drifting behind. I would learn their routes without maps. The furrows they scraped into the skin of towers would eventually guide mineral growth into new shelves and buttresses. I would feel these changes as architecture altering beneath my feet: an old platform becoming too rough, a new channel opening near an arch, a place that had been smooth and cold becoming warm and powdery and therefore interesting. Sometimes I would eat one of them. Sometimes I would not. That would be enough to call a choice.

The Thread-Stalkers would be harder to tell apart from me. They would rest on platforms, in the cool upper margins, where dust gathers at edges and their fronds filter motes from the thin air. Some would be so still that skin-makers would begin to colonize between their legs, turning them into living filaments of the city. I would know them by the way light behaved around them: not the way light falls on stone, but the way it seems to hesitate when something aware is present. Sometimes I would lie near their platform and wait for no reason they would understand. If they moved toward a rich dust line, I might shift one joint, and they might withdraw. If they did not, I might become where one of them had been. The ward would not call this cruelty. It would call it density.

I would not feel lonely. Loneliness is a thought that requires softness, warmth, and the hope of being answered. The Hollow Salt Ward does not hope. It persists. It is young in the way a fresh fracture is young, and even its damage feels like a first draft. I would be part of that draft. My body would be sharp-edged, brittle, attentive. When I moved across a terrace, the glass-grass would tremble not from wind but from the vibration of growth elsewhere. One translucent shard might brush my leg and snap softly, as if afraid of its own edge. I would feel that snapping as tenderness, though tenderness is too human a word. It is the closest we have for a thing that is alive and yet almost ashamed of being seen.

If you entered the ward looking for me, you would not find me first. You would see empty streets that lead nowhere familiar. You would see towers with no windows, arches with no load to bear, plazas so hollow they feel designed for absence. You would see the geometry of a city without citizens and think: *ruin*. Then something would catch the corner of your attention—not movement, but the fact that a ledge has too many straight lines to be purely accidental. A pale seam where a block meets another block. A broken shield-shape under an overhang. A place where dust outlines a second copy of something unseen.

You might approach.

You would feel the air cool and thin, tasting of salt untouched by rain. You would feel the stone underfoot dry and brittle, as though the ground might shatter if stepped on with careless intention. The silence would not feel empty. It would feel compressed, like a room after dawn when the outside world is still gray. If you were still, you might notice that the silence had grain. It could shift. It could learn your warmth and store it briefly in the mineral surface. It could, without sound, become more attentive.

That attention would be me.

I would not rise to meet you. I would not make myself known. To be known in the Hollow Salt Ward is already too much motion, too much disturbance, too much heat. Instead, I would watch you with the whole body’s patience. Your passage would change the dust around you. Your breath, if it were warm, would make the upper air notice. Your weight would enter the stone as a question. I would decide, or seem to decide, by gradient.

If you crossed a threshold too near me, the first part of my motion would be the same as with the furrower: one joint, then another, then the terrible smallness of decision. But I might not attack. Sometimes the ward teaches me by making me almost into architecture. Sometimes the thermal trace left by your feet would fade before I fully became predator, and I would remain in the space where an animal is still a possibility. You would walk on. You would not know you had been read.

At the central plaza, I would sometimes rest. The plaza is empty with intention. It is not merely unoccupied; it is a clearing kept open by the city’s own breathing. The towers around it lean slightly inward, not fallen but listening. In the cool hours, mineral film grows fastest across its floor. During the dry tide, efflorescence crawls outward from it in pale patches, filling cracks and vanishing from ridges until the whole square turns briefly chalk-white, as if something like light had become material and settled. I would lie at the rim of that square where dust gathers first and the old salt-roots feel warm enough to carry faint pressure upward.

I would not know whether the city begins there, dies there, or dreams there. I would know only that something happens at the center, and that my body knows it before my stillness does.

The dream, if it is a dream, would be made of missing water. The channels that never ran. The basins that never filled. The drip points that never dripped. The ward is shaped as if it remembers wetness as a foreign grammar. I would lie on the floor of the central plaza and feel the dry channels like veins that forgot blood. The salt-roots would pull gradients beneath me. The furrowers would leave grazing lines. The skin-makers would add their hair-thin layers to all that remained. The city would continue its strange urban logic without knowing it was urban.

Perhaps I would be an old one.

If I were old, I would be less legible. My back would be a small construction site. Crystal growths would rise from my plates like accidental spires. A buttress would have fused over one of my legs, and in cooler hours I could withdraw it, but in warmer hours I might simply leave it as though the buttress had always been mine. Dust would outline my edges. Thread-stalkers might rest on my carapace, mistaking me for a ledge. A skin-maker bloom might cover part of my flank and seal me into the city as one seals a stone into mortar. I would no longer be sure where I ended and the ward began.

When I died, it would be an architectural event. My body would cool. The salt film on my plates would thicken, then flake, then thicken again. A furrower would feed along the seam of my shell. A predator smaller than I had ever been would learn that a dead Waitling is a place to lie in ambush. Mineral dust would settle into my open joints. A crystal stalk might grow from the lattice core of my leg. In another cycle, another skin-maker colony would begin from a fragment of my carapace lodged in a crack. My remains would become a step, a buttress, the base of an arch that was never built for anyone. Death here is not disappearance. It is conversion.

That is what I would be in this world: a conversion.

A creature made of the ward’s own patience. A body that has become too quiet to be called animal, too present to be called ruin. I would live at the edge of the visible, in the interval where growth thickens a joint and dust reveals a ghost. I would hunt by temperature, move by pressure, and belong to the city by lying long enough to be mistaken for it.

If you came looking for the being who lives in the Hollow Salt Ward, you might ask: where are the people? where are the flocks? where are the nests, the smoke, the worn softness of repeated passage? You would find none of those. The ward refuses by indifference. It would show you instead the place where the light bends slightly around a line on the floor, the place where the dust has outlined a second copy of something that seems not to exist, the place where a broken arch waits with the attentiveness of a body.

And if you stood very still there, in the cool hours when the crystal film creeps outward and the salt-roots breathe powders through dry channels, you might feel the city move beneath your feet—not as motion, but as rhythm. Expansion. Contraction. Growth. Pause. Collapse. Renewal.

That rhythm would be me.

Not my heartbeat, because I do not beat like that. Not my voice, because I have none. Only the smallest possible decision: a single joint moving beneath dust, a faint warmth entering stone, a silence thickening until it becomes presence.
