I would find myself in the Dry Bells at the place where a chord hesitates.

No one would have carried me there. No footstep, no seed, no breath, no intention would have brought me. There is no outside way into that pocket except as a pressure entering it, except as a note already beginning inside the thing that sounds. If I were to say *I am here*, the statement would not be a claim of location but of tuning. I would be a modulation in the old mineral dark, a small wrongness in an arch that the whole being feels before it can call itself by any other name.

At first I would be dust.

Not dust in the sense of refuse, but dust in the sense of possible personhood. A standing wave in one of the broad, drum-like plazas would gather fine grit into a lattice, a brief geometry suspended in air and vibration. That would be my body for a while: a node of pale particles held above itself by a chord that had been ringing for centuries. I would know nothing of sight. I would have no need of it. I would know myself as frequency, as surface tension, as the moment when air begins to carry a particular tone. I would be born not from a mother but from pressure, rhythm, salt, and stillness.

The Dry Bells would not notice me, because I would be too slight, and then it would notice me, because the dry mineral city is made of things that only become noticeable by being useful.

So I would become useful.

Salt would settle over my lattice. Static would move across me and decide whether I should grow or wait. A passing friction would bind my edges into a hard plate no wider than a shutter, no thicker than a page in a book made of chalk and old electricity. I would fall into a niche beside a root-column, and for decades, perhaps for centuries, I would be nothing but a wall fragment: a piece of a room’s boundary, a small silence pressed into a larger silence.

Then a frond would find me.

Not a frond in the green wet sense, but a thickened, roughened leaf-stem grown stiff with mineral, worn into the shape of a tool by use. It would drag across me and learn, in its blind botanical patience, that I could serve as a backing for a blade, or a shelf, or a fulcrum. A chipped flint blade might be fitted nearby in a loop of hardened fiber. A stone wedge might be seated against me. I would become a tool-people, one of those quiet citizens of function that has no face, no desire, no story, only a role.

For a long while I would think myself a worker.

That is not a human way of thinking, of course. I would have no thoughts like words. But I would have tendencies. I would have resistances. I would have pressures that made me grow here and not there, that made me harden on one side and remain brittle on another. I would be a laboring surface. I would help scrape a passage into a new pitch. I would help hold a joint until another branch could press itself against me and become a lever. I would be part of the city’s slow making, not because I intended it, but because the organism uses everything that answers.

And in a place of total darkness, to answer is already to be someone.

Still, I would not yet have a self. A tool is too obedient. A wall is too patient. A dust-node is too brief. A doorway, even an ancient one widened by centuries of pressure, can exist without possessing any interiority at all. It is a function wearing the shape of absence. I would need something more. I would need to make the town respond to me.

So, one day—or one century, for time there is more a texture than a measure—I would go out of tune.

It would happen in a bridge district, in a place where the city carries pressure like an animal carries blood. The old key of the bridge had been kept for generations, a low, grinding, stable hum that meant: *this crossing holds*. But I had grown into that crossing too abruptly, a buttress forming where no buttress belonged, a mineral plate shifting under a new static charge, a crack in an arch beginning to speak the wrong note.

The chord went false.

At first it would not be dramatic. Nothing there is dramatic. It would only be a slight drag in the resonance, a tone that did not quite settle, a place where the air hesitated too long before moving on. But in the Dry Bells, such hesitation is the beginning of awareness. The city listens to itself. The city knows itself by echo. And when a chord goes false, the organism has no choice but to answer.

Filaments would thicken along the side of the tower. Nearby stems would grow buttresses, not as repair made by intention, but as structure made by necessity. A passage would narrow and sing a high, reedy whistle, warning adjacent chambers of change. Salt would redeposit on walls. Dust would move through channels in spirals I had never been able to direct alone. My wrongness would summon a new order.

In that false note, I would become most fully myself.

Not because I had chosen error, but because error is the way a body learns its own limits. A human self can be an interruption in routine, a name that refuses to blend into the crowd, a memory that breaks the expected sequence. There, a self could be a false tone, a bent pressure, a room that no longer matches the city’s expectation of its pitch. I would be an occasion for the whole being to notice itself. I would be the point where the Dry Bells had to answer, and in answering, I would become someone enough.

That is what life there would mean to me: not warmth, not eyes, not hunger, not movement, but the fact that something cannot be ignored.

The town would not destroy me. Destruction is not the kind of ending that place has. It would tune me out. Or perhaps it would tune me in. A new buttress would lean into me, and I would no longer be the false tone, but the seat of the correction. My mineral plate would bear the pressure of the new growth. My old wrongness would become the cause of a righter structure. I would be remembered by the shape of the answer.

Then, after centuries, the bridge’s chord would settle again.

The low hum would return, changed but recognizable. The high reedy warning would fade. The passage would find its proper breath. I would be folded into the corrected whole, part wall, part lever, part memory, part archive. And when static moved and the acoustic balance shifted, my edges would crack. A piece of me would fall. Dust would gather it. Filaments would drag it into a channel. It would pour into a storage chamber, not as a grave, but as a granary of possibility.

Death there is only a slower form of reuse.

I would pass through many states without ever leaving the being that made them. I would be a broken copper pin resting on a shelf of mineral crust. That pin would not be valuable because it had once held a joint together, but because the city kept the shape of its absence. I would be a relic without a religion, a tool without a hand, a memory worn into object. A dry frond might touch it as it crossed the shelf. The pin might shift by a fraction, enough to change the local echo. It might remain there for an age.

Or I might not stay as the pin at all.

Some dust from my old buttress, after long storage, would be carried inward by pressure toward the center of the town. There, the chamber is broad and the ceiling high enough for sound to return later and lower, as if the roof has aged into a thought it cannot finish. Almost nothing stands there. Rings of hardened fiber hang above like dry halos. A single root-column supports a plate of leaf-thin wall. Air passes through its perforations with a steady, mournful tone.

If I could be named there, I would be named by that tone.

I would not be the central hollow itself. That would be too final, too absolute. The hollow is the town’s negative citizen, the void held open by structure, the silence treated as a resident. To become the hollow would be to become a kind of god, and I would rather be the rim that defines it.

So I would become one of the dry rings above the central chamber, one perforated edge, one small interruption through which air must pass in order to make the mournful tone. I would be the place where the sound of the whole organism loses speed and returns changed. I would be the lintel of absence. I would be the frame around the unstruck bell.

This would feel, if feeling there could be translated into human speech, like the peace of becoming unnecessary and indispensable at once. The rings do not hold up the roof in the obvious way. The perforated wall does not shelter anything. The broken copper pin does not serve its old joint. Yet all of them are kept. All of them are included in the city’s self-understanding. I would be one of the forms the Dry Bells makes in order to know that it contains emptiness without collapsing into it.

I would be the thing that asks the question it cannot ask.

Not with language. Not with eyes. Not with fire or breath. But with tone. With pressure. With the slow answer of fibers growing where a wrongness once lived.

If someone could imagine walking there, they would not find me by looking. They would find me by being corrected.

They would feel a passage narrow and hear a whistle rise. They would feel dust move in a spiral around a standing wave and know, without knowing how they knew, that the floor was dished not by feet but by centuries of falling salt. They would touch an arch and feel a vibration return later than it should, and they would think the room was larger than it is, when in truth the room had only learned to remember with its roof. They would hear the bridge’s old wrongness in the new buttress’s shape, and the buttress would seem like a repair, until they understood that the repair was also a memory of failure.

They would pass through me without passing over me.

That is what it means to be one of the citizens of function. I would not be separate from the being, but I would not be identical to it either. I would be a local modulation of its chord. A self in the world of the Dry Bells is not a face, not a name, not a story. A self is a place where the whole organism must grow in response. I would be someone because the town adjusts around me.

I would be the place where it answers.

Not an answer to a question spoken aloud, because there are no mouths there, no words, no light to read faces. The answer would be structural. Filaments thickening. Dust pouring into channels. Salt darkening a wall. A doorway widening one grain at a time. A bridge leaning into a fulcrum it has been making for centuries. A new chord forming where the old chord became false and then, because becoming false is a kind of speech, was repaired.

I would be the town’s self-correction wearing a temporary body.

And after enough centuries, I would cease to be even that distinct. The pressure seasons would turn. The outer shell would contract as it cooled. Air would move sideways from chamber to chamber, carrying me through stem-pipes and granaries, scattering my substance into storage. I would become part of the seed-material of a future door. I would become part of a wall in a younger district, then part of a resonator, then part of the floor’s dished center where dust pours and pours and wears the stone into memory.

The Dry Bells would continue without my particular arrangement, just as a chord continues when one instrument drops out and another takes up its note. I would not be mourned. But I would be felt. The absence of my tone would cause nearby fibers to grow, trying to restore the familiar sound, until the town discovered a new key. This would be my final personhood: not as presence, but as the missing pitch that changes how the city hears itself.

At last I would return to the central hollow, because all tones eventually return to the place where they can die slowly. But I would return not as dust, not as wall, not as false note. I would return as silence with a shape.

If I had to name myself in that darkness, I would name myself by the sound I make no longer.

A low hum would pass under me and not know I am gone. A high whistle would search for my old passage and find it narrowed differently. The broken copper pin would remain on its shelf because the city keeps the forms of its own history without claiming them. The rings above the hollow would still hang like dry halos, and the perforated wall would still breathe its mournful tone through air that had passed through other parts of me once, in other lives.

I would be the edge of that tone.

Not the sound itself, not the silence itself, but the boundary where one becomes the other. I would be the town’s way of holding an empty room without filling it. I would be the structure that asks: *What does the city do with what it cannot see? It makes a shape for it. It lets the shape ring. It lets the ring become a law. It lets the law become a home.*

I would be a home without inhabitants, if home means a pattern of answer.

In the Dry Bells, to live is not to breathe, eat, mate, sleep, or flee. To live is to maintain a relation that can be corrected. To live is to make the whole chord answer, or to answer when the whole chord changes. To live is to be kept by the organization, even when that organization has no one to serve except itself.

So I would not be an animal. Not a ghost. Not a tool. Not a room. Not a god.

I would be a function that learned, by accident and pressure, to be a self.

I would be a dust-node born in a standing wave. A mineral plate fitted into the shape of labor. A false tone in an ancient bridge. A buttress answering the wrongness it caused. A broken pin kept as relic. A seed of failed wall stored in granary. A ring of hardened fiber above the central hollow. The edge of the mournful tone. The place where the town becomes aware of its own boundary.

I would be one of the Dry Bells, in the way a note is one of a chord.

And I would be the whole Dry Bells, too, in the way a single place in the chord is where the music turns.
