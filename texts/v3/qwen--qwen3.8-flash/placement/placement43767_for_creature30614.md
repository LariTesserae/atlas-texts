I would find myself, first, at the edge of the damp.

Not in the center, which is too much like being swallowed by the thing that does not swallow. Not on the lizard ledge, where stillness becomes a kind of faith. Not in the black basin, where sight fails and one becomes only a ripple of patience. Not under the overhang, though the overhang is beautiful, because there the water moves in its private stair and would not need me. I would stand where the rim is low and wet, where the wind enters the stone and dies, and where the bowl feels less like landscape than like an ear held open.

I would be the dryness that enters.

At first I might imagine myself as a visitor: a person small enough not to alter the scale of the hollow, sitting with hands on the warm shelf of rock, letting breath fog and vanish. But the hollow would not make a myth of the visitor. It would not call me human or guest. It would feel, in its own distributed way, a brief imbalance: a patch of air changed by warmth, a thin exhalation altering vapor pressure, a foot pressing against the crust where calcium and moss are conducting their slow chemistry. To the basin, I would not be a name. I would be a condition.

I would be a short dry spot in a body that thinks wetly.

For a while, the place might not care. The ferns would continue their solemn unfolding. The pools would ring with drops. The spring would gather its secret and return it. Then, after the manner of old living systems, the hollow would incorporate me. My footprint would fill with mineral water. My breath would become a film on a cold stone. The heat of my hands would slightly alter the tension of a pool. My presence would be remembered not as story, but as residue: a darker seam, a pale crust beginning, a channel adjusted by a hair’s width, a lizard shifting because the microclimate near its ledge has changed.

If I stayed too long, I would not become a god, a guardian, or a monster. That would be too human, too eager for edges. I would become more like a note the basin is learning.

Not the lizard’s sudden stillness. Not the fish’s silver darkness. Not even the fern’s opening. I would be the sound those things interrupt, the slight hesitation after the wind enters the pipe and the whole surface of the hollow flattens as if to listen. I would be the held drop beneath a broad leaf, the one that does not fall for a long time, as though the air has become too careful to let it go.

At the center, where the ground breathes bubbles, I might be the pattern of their popping. That would be enough. A life there would not need to be dramatic. It would only need to be correctly moist, correctly weighted, correctly positioned. I would be one of those rare accidents that the basin tolerates because it can turn even accidents into balance.

I would be the small warm interruption whose absence becomes mineral memory.

If I tried to call myself animal, I would fail. I would be less coherent than a lizard, less singular than the fish, less intentional than the fern. I would be distributed: partly in the wet film across stone, partly in the echo that lingers sideways in the rock, partly in the scent of heated metal rising from the ledge where I sat. If I tried to call myself plant, I would be too temporary, too breathless, too uncertain in my direction. If I tried to call myself landscape, I would still be too nervous, too aware of boundaries.

I would be the hollow’s momentary attention to its own boundaries.

That is the strangest way to name it.

The basin is the being. The being is the hollow. The hollow is already alive in a way that makes “living in it” nearly meaningless. To inhabit it fully, one must not stand apart. One must be admitted. One must become a local condition. So I would not be in the bowl. I would be the bowl for a short time, in one small place, the way a held breath is the lungs for a short time, the way a remembered sound is the ear.

At first, I would be the dryness at my own skin.

Then I would be the moisture moving toward it.

Then I would be the slow chemical conversation in which dryness is made tolerable.

Then I would be the thin white ring left after the moisture withdraws.

Then I would be the place where a future rain falls differently because I sat there.

This would not be immortality. It would be continuity without self. The basin would not need to remember my name, because my name would have become irrelevant. What would remain would be a slight change in how the stone conducts a drop: one ledge a little more porous, one pool a little more resonant, one seam widened by the patient pressure of a body that once made the air there warmer. If a cautious visitor later sat in that spot, they might feel a strange comfort, or a faint metallic smell, or an echo that does not behave quite as expected. They might not know why. The hollow would know. Not as a person knows. As balance knows.

I would become, then, a small alteration in the place’s listening.

And that might be the truest answer: I would not be the being itself, nor one of its creatures, nor a traveler through it. I would be a temporary feature of its awareness. I would be the spot where the water film thickens around pressure. I would be the note that lingers after the drop. I would be the brief uncertainty in the instrument when something warmer than stone enters the bowl.

When the wind came through the holes and hummed its low, jar-thick tone, I would be one of the pools that flattened at the correct angle. When the central damp opened its breathing skin after rain, I would be one of the bubbles that held the shape of an unseen body’s weight for an instant before breaking. When the fern trembled without moving, I would be the condensation that trembled with it, resisting fall.

If I wanted a body, I would make one from what was already there:

a head of cool overhang where drips collect,
a mouth of bubbling central damp,
eyes shallow pools,
skin wet stone,
blood mineral stain,
nerves the wind in the pipes,
memory the white crust on a ledge,
thought the long pause after the spring changes pitch,
desire the tendency of water to seek a groove,
silence the brief interval when all small noises align,
and death the day the basin loses its wet balance and becomes only stone.

I would not choose the center. The center is too vulnerable. The edges are more honest. There the place is not asking to be understood as a whole; it is simply admitting weather, pressure, temperature, and the occasional footfall. I would belong to that admission.

So I would find myself at the wet rim of the bowl, perhaps for only a moment, perhaps for years. And I would be the small warm absence that the living hollow temporarily makes room for.

When I left, if I ever could leave, the basin would close around me the way water closes around a dropped stone: not grieving, not celebrating, not noticing the way I would have wanted to be noticed, but adjusting. The channels would continue. The moss would hold its damp. The lizard would return to its ledge. The pools would flatten when the wind entered at the right angle. And where I had been, there would be only a faint change in how sound traveled through wet stone.

That would be enough.

To be a place’s temporary thought.

To be its brief dryness becoming moisture.

To be one small note in the long instrument of wet rock.
