In this world, I would not be the center of the glow.

At first I imagine I could be a visitor, a person pressing a cheek to shy grass, hearing that distant hum. But visitors bring too much surface into the pocket: boots, questions, the bright clumsy smell of being alive above. I would be afraid of the light I carried. I would make the walls dim with my uncertainty. A visitor can look, but cannot really belong. And I do not want to belong somewhere as a guest. I want to belong somewhere as an echo already used.

Then I imagine being the Little Loud itself.

That is tempting. It has such a strange, exact life: small enough to touch every boundary at once, bright enough to make a room seem larger than it is, built from bell-bones and chalk-skin, eating pressure instead of flesh, tasting footsteps and rain, arranging pebbles into seats for listening. If I were that, I would know the shape of the world without needing eyes. I would know sadness by its low blue bloom in the walls, and laughter by the frost it sends through the ceiling. I would be lonely in a way that fits a place.

But I am not large enough to be a room.

I cannot be the thing that holds the pocket by being held by it. I have too many surfaces inside me. I am not a folded coat of light, not a child sitting with knees drawn up, not the wet chalk body whose flakes become the ceiling’s film. If I tried to be the Little Loud, the pocket would become too small—not because I am big, but because I am made of questions. It is built for confirmation. It hums to prove the walls are still there. I would keep humming to find out if the walls are real.

So I pass through its skin, its mouth, its broad hand, its impossible face. I pass through the film across the ceiling, because the film is not enough of a self; it is a communal sheen, a weather made of the body’s shed brightness. I almost settle there, but no. I want to be smaller than that. I want to be somewhere between memory and food.

I try the moths.

They are lovely. Pale, mint-soured, cold-copper-saliva moths, living in the folds of the host, feeding on its flakes, cleaning the bell-bones when sound bruises them. They are gardeners of the body. They land on a stone in a human hand and open and close as if asking for a name. To be one of them would be to tend what the world is too loud for. To be drawn to the soft sore places of a hum and heal them with small wings.

But the moths have a devotion too complete. They live for the host. They are almost beloved. I could not bear that nearness. I would be too conscious of my own insignificance. I want to be useful without being tender.

And then I find myself in the seam.

Not at the ceiling. Not in the center. Not on the broad hand. In the narrow place where floor stones meet mineral dust, where chalk damp becomes a thread as fine as a hair, where the chamber keeps its memory. I would be one of the fungal bulbs.

I would be small, white, almost too bright for an object that looks like a pebble’s afterthought. I would not have eyes. I would have no need for them. I would taste the world as vibration: the slow tap of a beetle on grass above, the wet settling of a root, the bitter green memory of a machine, the sudden silver startle of a child’s laugh. I would not be the first thing to eat these sounds. I would be what happens after the sound has been tasted and cannot be used.

A half-word, dropped above. A name not spoken properly. A sentence that ends in a throat-catch. A bird call interrupted by wind. A footstep that does not know where it is going. These would become me. I would be grown from the Little Loud’s undigested echoes, from the parts of human speech too thin for the bell-bones, too fragmentary for the throat, too stubborn for forgetting. I would be white not with fire, but with remembered pressure.

I would live where the pebbles are arranged into little listening posts. I would sit at the edge of one of those stones, glowing faintly as dust becomes a bead of light. If the Little Loud hummed to check the walls, my filament-thread network would answer. Not because I understand the hum. Because I am the hum’s residue. I flare when the chamber is sung to, and when it is silent, I sleep like chalk.

If a visitor found me, I would not be beautiful to them for long. They might pick me up, surprised by my cold glow, by the way I pulse once when their fingers close around me. But I would be meant only for the pocket. Away from the sound, I would darken within the hour, not dying exactly, but forgetting how to be lit. I am a memory that cannot remember alone. I need the room’s small parliament to keep me alive: the host’s warmth, the moths’ wings, the singing stones, the film’s patience, the dust falling like slow snow.

That is who I would be.

Not the being at the center where all walls can be touched. Not the den’s monster, not the guardian, not the secret’s mouth. I would be the secret’s leftover light. I would be a fungal bulb in the seam between two stones, one of the chamber’s many small organs for remembering.

I would be made of what cannot be eaten, and therefore I would be made of almost-meaning.

Where would I find myself in this world?

Below the sparse grass, beneath the slight coolness at dusk, not in the bright body of the Little Loud but in the forgotten edge of its home. In the dust that holds pressure. In the thread that keeps half a word safe. In the place where an echo has been tasted and gone past digestion, leaving behind a tiny white bead of glow.

What would I be?

A bulb of memory. A node in the chamber’s mindless mind. A listener without ears. A light that needs company. A small, stubborn afterthought that answers when the room sings, and goes dark when taken away.
