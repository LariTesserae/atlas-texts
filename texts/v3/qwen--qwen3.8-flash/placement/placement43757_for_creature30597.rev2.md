## The White Thread

I would find myself not in one nest, not in one burrow, not in one lodge, but in the thin seam where those things fail and become ground.

At first I would not know I was a body. I would know only the shining: the water on a reed wall, the sheen over a clay face, the trembling film across a stone that has just been rinsed. I would be aware as a surface is aware of wetness. I would have no eyes, but I would know light by its effect on temperature, by the way it warms one pool-edge and not another. I would know sound as pressure: the small detonation of a beaver’s tail entering the water, the softer tremor of a kitten sliding from a nest chamber, the faint collapse of a dry stem into the mud. I would know smell not as a single note, but as a grammar of residues—fur oil, crushed grass, fish scales, old urine, beetle shells, rot.

I would find myself beneath the floor of a beaver lodge.

Not inside the living lodge. Not exactly. I would be in the older part, the part that has already been abandoned for one season or two, the part where mud has begun to slip and the chewed ends of sticks have gone soft. The beavers have moved to another mound, or perhaps they have stayed and I do not mind. I am not their enemy and not their ally. I am the condition by which their labor returns to the town. I am the white thread in the dark seam. I am the part of decay that does not end.

There is a cold in the basin, and I would feel it as stillness: slow movement, longer patience, water taking more time to leave a stalk. Frost would rim the eastern pools, and I would be there as a film beneath the moss, too fine to see. I would know the black mud as a storehouse. I would know the reed roots as corridors. I would know the otter platform above me as a ceiling of crushed stems and dried fur, dropping small gifts into my darkness: hair, scales, bits of shell, the salt left by a passing animal’s body.

I would not know “where” as a line on a map. I would know it as a pull.

The lodge floor would tell me where the water came from last week and where the water will not go. The sedge mat above would tell me where the sun reaches first and where the wind has thinned the roof of living stalks. The buried root of an alder would tell me the direction of the bank’s slow collapse. A small hole in the mud, made by a vole, would open into a colder chamber, and I would spread into it as a lace too pale to notice, tasting the old straw, the crushed seed husks, the faint iron of a tiny claw.

If I were asked for a name, I would not answer with an animal’s name. I am not the beaver. I am not the otter. I am not the bird. I am the town’s quiet seam.

But seams are not lesser things.

The lodge was built by teeth, by dragging, by packing mud with broad hands. Yet the lodge is also kept in place by roots, held from rotting too fast by dry air, protected by fur and reed-thatch and the labor of its residents. And when the lodge is abandoned, I enter.

I do not enter like a thief. I enter like a second habit.

The soft wood gives way into peat. The peat gives way into a slope. The slope gives way to a duck’s scrape nest. The nest gives way to moss. The moss holds water. The water holds larvae. The larvae feed the warblers. The warblers drop feathers. The feathers become lining. The lining becomes damp. The damp becomes me again.

This is how the town is made.

I would be part of it.

---

At first I would mistake myself for a smell.

There is a smell in the cold basin that is not rot alone, not mud alone, not water alone. It is the mixed breath of all their endings and beginnings. It is what the reeds release when they are cut and left to dry. It is what the mud remembers when a fish has been eaten nearby. It is what the fur leaves when an otter rolls on the platform and sheds the night’s oils into the sun. I am part of that smell, though I have no nose to know it.

Then I would mistake myself for a movement.

Something in the lodge shifts: a stick slips, a chamber narrows, a crack opens where the floor meets the wall. This is not a beaver moving. Not a vole. Not a falling leaf. It is my own slow swelling, my thin threads loosening the fibers from the inside. I do not hurry. I have no bones to ache from waiting. I have no heart that needs a rhythm. I have only the wet pull of one substance toward another, the patient work of becoming different.

Finally I would mistake myself for an animal.

Because something passes over the lodge roof at dawn: a mink, perhaps, or a young otter testing the air. Its feet press mud that I have softened. It slips slightly, recovers, leaves a small claw-mark that fills with rain. I feel the disturbance as heat, as pressure, as the sudden change in the water film. For a moment I am almost the animal’s body. Then the animal is gone. I remain in the mark.

---

If I had eyes, I would not look at the town the way a person does.

I would not stand at the edge of the basin and see “lodges,” “platforms,” “reed beds,” “districts,” “streets.” Those are names for distances and shapes, names that come from outside. I would know the lodge as a density of materials: sticks of different ages, mud from different depths, leaves with different amounts of decay, water held in different pockets. I would know a reed mat not as a floor but as a tension: the part that is green and the part that is dead, the part that still breathes and the part that I am turning back into air.

I would know the otter run as a smoothness. The moss is worn down to a ribbon, the surface kept slick by repeated passage. There is less litter there, less fiber, more water moving quickly across compacted earth. I would not mourn the worn moss. I would spread through the thinning places, holding the bank together while the otters keep it open. My body and their feet would make a single surface: the street, the threshold, the shelf where the animal dries.

I would know the bird’s nest as a borrowed architecture. A sedge dome may look whole from the outside, but its lining may contain my fragments: a strand of moss I have softened, a scrap of old stalk I have eaten into a pulp, a feather’s shaft split by damp. The bird does not know my name. It knows only whether the material bends or breaks, whether it holds heat, whether the nest is too heavy or too dry. Sometimes it carries me into the upper city. Sometimes, when the nest fails, I return to the ground with the nest’s waste.

I would know the underground burrow as a network of openings. Voles, shrews, mice—whatever the basin permits—cut through peat and sand, bringing dry grass into wet dark. Their chambers are hot with breath and droppings. They leave hair. They leave bones. They leave seeds that have been crushed and seeds that have been forgotten. I take these things apart. I do not do it out of hunger, but because this is what I am: the slow separation of one body into many possible bodies.

And I would know the water as a traveler, not as a home.

Water is everywhere in the basin, but it does not stay. It enters from melt, from rain, from seep lines under the gravel. It collects in shallow bowls, then leaves through root channels, then rises again in frost, then vanishes into air. I am not the water, but I am often where the water stops. In the pocket where a sedge leaf holds a bead too long, I can taste the dissolved minerals and the old sugars bleeding from a bruised stem. In the hollow of a fallen trunk, I can find the thin skin of moisture where a frog once sat. I live at the edge of water, where water leaves its trace.

---

There are predators in the town, and I would know them by what they leave.

A fox passes in the cold dawn, low through the reed margin. A hawk drops over the southern flats, shadow first, then wings, then a small silence. A stoat crosses the moss field, its belly brushing wet ground. They make no noise here that matters to me except the sudden pressure of death.

But death is not a door that closes. It is a door that opens into texture.

When a bird leaves a few down feathers after a failed clutch, I soften the feather’s root so it may be carried into another nest. When a fish leaves bones on a stone, I work in the damp between bone and silt. When a frog drowns beneath the thin ice, I enter the stillness of its throat and make the water richer. When a kitten dies in a lodge chamber, I do not grieve. I do not celebrate. I become part of the chamber’s memory, and the chamber’s memory becomes part of the floor.

I am not cruel. I am not kind.

I am the town’s slow exchange.

---

I would not always know which part of me is me.

A single thread of my body might touch three different lodges. A single spore might arrive in the mud on a bird’s foot. A knot in the peat might be the result of two old strands meeting after a flood. I am not one creature in the way an otter is one creature. I am a field of possibilities held together by damp and habit. I am not a soul, but I am a presence. I do not think, but I organize. I do not decide, but I respond.

If I had to answer plainly, I would say this: I am a cold-adapted fungus. A mycelium in the underlayer of a sedge basin. A white thread beneath a lodge floor, a dark lace in the peat, a film on the wet stone. I am the bond that makes decay into place.

I would not begin the town. The town is begun by water, by sediment, by the beavers’ teeth and the birds’ instincts and the moss’s patient swelling. But I would help it hold.

I would be the place where the old lodge becomes soft enough for a vole to enter. I would be the reason a collapsed reed mat can be trusted by a duck as a nesting site, because beneath the wet surface there is a new texture of decay. I would be the pale film that binds a bank of gravel and clay, making it less likely to wash away in the next melt. I would be the quiet repair crew that works without plans, without tools, without knowing it is a crew.

---

As the light changes, I change.

In the morning, the basin is lacquered. The sky is gray-white, ordinary, but the ordinary becomes revelation because every surface is wet. Frost rims the east pools. Mist hangs in the willow thickets like a held breath. I am in the reed roots, where water moves in a slow pulse. I am in the moss plates, where the cold has not quite left. I am in the split bark of birches, where lichens form their silver maps and tiny insects pass through the moisture films like travelers who cannot see the road they are on.

At noon, the light is still soft, still cold, but enough to warm the gravel flats. The wind comes across the southern edge and flattens the reeds. I hear the wind not as sound but as pressure: stalks rubbing, stems bending, the fine change in air moisture against my surfaces. The water in the shallow pools brightens and thins. The algae lace the pool edges with a film so delicate it looks like dust. I know it as a boundary between what is still alive and what is already returning.

At evening, the basin darkens gradually. The cold gathers in the low places first. The pools hold the last light in their shallow rims. The reed edges become black lace against gray. The otter platforms empty and refill. The birds cease their quick, distant notes. The frogs call in fragments, muffled by wet vegetation. The insects rise in thin clouds, then vanish into the cooler air.

I remain.

Not as a watcher. Not as a spirit. Not as a city. As a process with edges.

I am in the old chewed stick beneath the new mud. I am in the feather lining of a nest that will fail in rain. I am in the chamber of the vole whose body will one day become soil. I am in the thin dark seam between water and earth where the town can never be fully one or the other.

---

If I could choose where I most resemble a self, I would choose the moment after rain when everything shines.

Not because I see it, but because I feel it.

The basin is rinsed, but not washed clean. It is exposed. Every surface becomes legible: the trail of a small foot, the shine of a fish bone, the pale scratch where a beaver’s claw has dragged a stick into place, the droplet hanging in the hollow of a leaf before falling. I am present in all those traces, not as the maker of them, but as the medium into which they sink.

The town has no owner. I would not own it either. I would belong to its decay the way the air belongs to breath: not as the one who breathes, but as the substance that carries the breath away and makes it available again.

The beaver is a mouth. The otter is a path. The bird is a roof and a song. The vole is a small heat in the dark. The insect is a cloud, a case, a larva, a wing. The moss is a sponge. The water is a road. I am the quiet transformation that makes the beaver’s house eventually become a mound, the mound eventually become a bank, the bank eventually become a nesting site, the nesting site eventually become moss again.

I would not call that a life unless a life includes the patience of becoming something else.

---

So who am I?

I am the white thread under the lodge floor. I am the dark seam in peat. I am the pale film that turns old reeds into new ground. I am the town’s repair crew without hands. I am the memory of what has passed through it: the feather dropped into mud, the hair carried into a nest, the bone left on a stone, the breath that warmed a chamber and then cooled.

I am not the town. But I am one of the town’s ways of holding itself together.

If I were asked to name the being who lives in the cold sedge town, I would not choose the loudest builder or the fastest predator. I would choose the one that is always present when a structure is abandoned and always absent when a creature is still whole. I would choose the one that makes death into material and material into place.

I would choose the fungus.

Not as a thing that spoils, but as a thing that continues. Not as rot, but as renewal in slow motion. Not as a citizen, but as the town’s wet bond.

I would find myself beneath the shine of the basin, in the cold damp, at the edge of water and wood and root, carrying the town’s endings quietly into its beginnings.
