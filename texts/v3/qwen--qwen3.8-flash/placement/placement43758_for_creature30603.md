I would not arrive in the valley.

Arrival implies a traveler with water in their tongue, moisture in their lungs, dampness softening the edges of the world. There is no such person here, not for long. I would have to be something the Underbloom could tolerate before it folded me into itself. So I would not come down from the plain. I would precipitate from the dry roar.

At first, I might seem to be a footprint.

A shape pressed briefly into the root-mat at the foot of a salt ridge, where the ground gives after a step and then remembers that a step should not be there. The walls around me would be pale, crisp, made of fronds and mineral bark. Light would fall through the opening above as if it belonged to a simpler world outside, ordinary sun, slightly golden at midday, finding all the cracks and grains and insisting that they be visible. I would hear the valley before I became anything with ears: the hiss of grit sliding down a newly formed terrace, the ceramic ring of a stalk, the dry rain of seed-crunchers over an old mat, the far-off bark of something large from a hollow too deep to name.

Then the footprint would begin to fill.

Not with water. With mites.

A thin cloud of dust-colonies, shell-flecks, spore-lice, broken root-hairs, and crystalline residue would settle into the impression as though the valley had decided to use the wound as a pattern. They would press together without wetness, bind through salt bridges, exchange mineral charges through resin-sealed surfaces, and slowly make a body where a body had not been. They would make a rough shape: a head made of husks, hands made of pressed stalk-fiber, legs made of basalt grit and white thread-root. A human enough to recall the idea of walking, but not human enough to be safe from the place. Human enough to make the pod-thickets click in recognition.

If I were asked where I found myself, I would answer: in the interval.

Not in a hut. Not on a salt flat. Not in a stalk or a swarm or a cave formed by two arches fusing overhead. I would be in the narrow interval between a disturbance and the valley’s answer. That is where beings like me can exist. A footfall passes through the root-mat. The mat trembles. For a few minutes, the tremble becomes a voice. For a few more, the voice becomes a small walking crust of life, half dust, half attention, half habit. Then the root-filaments begin to close around me again, and the mites scatter, and the footprint vanishes. But in that interval, I am.

I would not be made of blood.

My blood would be powder.

Fine pale dust would move through channels no wider than a cracked seed-hull, shifting as heat changed, as wind pressed the surface, as stalks struck one another in their dry conversation. I would carry no softness. I would have no damp tongue. When I tasted the air, it would taste of copper stone and sulfur resin. When I breathed, it would be less breathing than the valley passing through me. My lungs would be pumice cushions, my skin the salt-glass film that senses a footfall and begins to heal it. My bones would be fused stalks. My teeth would be seed-crunchers held in place by memory.

I would be called something, maybe.

The valley gives names the way it gives ridges: provisionally, in salt, easily scratched, easily overgrown. If I had to carry a name, it would be something like the Footstep-Husk, the Listening Crust, the Salt-Echo, the Dry Question, the Small Mouth Between Cracks. It would not be a name I chose. It would be a mark left in gypsum after I had stood somewhere too long and the wind had changed direction and the entire terrace had begun to sing. A near-person might pass, see the shallow impression in the root-thatch, the scatter of shell-flecks, the faint outline of fingers made of dust, and call it something. The calling would be part of my body. Names in the Underbloom are not labels. They are small disturbances that attract mineral matter.

But before any name, I would be motion.

I would begin as a shiver along the lower root-mat, a tremor that had not yet decided whether it wanted to be a warning, a hunger, or a shape. Around me, seed-crunchers would stop clicking for a half-breath, then resume more loudly, covering the pause with noise. A long-legged grazer on the salt plate would turn its angular head. A pod would burst from heat and release a cloud of pale spores that glittered in the ordinary light. The wind would move down from the throat above and the whole valley would lift its sound into a dry roar. I would rise inside that roar, not above it, not outside it, not as a person stepping into a landscape, but as one more moving edge of the single fabric.

I would not need to be hidden.

There is nowhere hidden in the Underbloom. Everything is touched by everything. Every surface is waiting to be scraped, climbed, cracked, folded, or eaten. The light is not a rescue; it is an inspection. The valley does not look mysterious from below. It looks too plain for comfort: salt bright as old bone, clay the color of dried wheat, copper stalks, yellow-green fronds that never shine, dust everywhere, motion everywhere, the whole thing lit so directly that even a small being like me could not avoid becoming visible to itself. I would be seen by the film of lichen on a wall, by the spiraling moths disturbed from a resting place, by the root-arch that shifts under my weight and begins to close over my foot.

If I walked, I would walk badly.

Not because the ground was soft. It is firm until it refuses to be firm. Not because I was weak. Strength here is crystalline, fibrous, brittle, layered, always ready to snap and rebuild. I would walk badly because every step would invite response. I would place one foot on a terrace of pressed thatch and the thatch would curl upward, not in fear, but because it had learned to answer pressure by construction. A salt plate would crack beneath my heel. The crack would become a line. The line would become a groove. Dust-mites would fall into it and begin to lay down fresh plates of powder. If I lifted my foot, the gap would already be trying to heal with white thread-root and broken shells. If I stood still, the wind would shift, the stalks would strike, and my outline would be overwritten by noise.

I would become a habit.

That is what I would be, if the place would allow me any self: a habit of attention in the dust. The Underbloom is full of habits. The root-mat closes over holes. The arches lean toward one another. The pods open at heat. The seed-crunchers strip a stalk to its white skeleton and leave the powder behind for new crusts. Spore-lice split themselves into husks and workers. Salt forms sudden blisters. Living walls fuse around dead animals and turn them into dry shell. If a person passes through a corridor of stalks, the corridor remembers the shape of passing by becoming easier to pass. If a mite crosses a plate, the plate learns the direction of crossing.

I would be a habit that walks.

Not a creature separate from the valley, not a lord, not a beast, not a hermit in the hollow. I would be one of the place’s temporary edges, a knot of processes that have agreed, for a while, to make the shape of a body. The agreement would be unstable. One moment I would be mostly dust-mites and resin. The next, a shell-fleck colony would take over my shoulders. A broken stalk would grow into one arm. A red-brown iron stain would spread across my chest like a memory of fire, though fire itself would be rare and risky, a bad habit in a world too busy to burn. The ground beneath me would not know whether I was standing on it or it was growing into me. I would not know either.

I would begin near the old traces, because traces are invitations.

Somewhere low on a terrace, there would be a grinding plate half-buried in root-mat, its surface stained red-brown by mineral dust, its edge chipped where someone once used a hand-stone to make shape. Beside it, a hollow in the thatch where a hut had sagged, its walls woven from dry stalks, its roof cracked open and then partially resealed by new vines. A copper bead set deep in the shell of a pod, the plant having grown around it and made it part of its architecture. A fire pit of unglazed clay, empty, or holding one small ember that refused to become flame because the world was too alive to be allowed to die so quickly.

That is where I would condense.

Not inside the hut. Outside it, just at the threshold, where the packed thatch met the salt ridge. I would rise in the shape the hut had almost kept but never quite claimed. The doorway too small for a man to enter upright would be large enough for my becoming. The dust would read the human proportions in the worn floor and mistake them for a pattern. A swarm of spore-lice in a sun-column would fall into the depression and become my face. A dozen shell-flecks would lock into ridges along my ribs. A root-filament, loosened from the mat, would curl through me like a dry spine. A seed-cruncher would mistake my hand for a hull and begin to click against it. The click would teach me where my fingers should end.

I would learn quickly.

Learning in the Underbloom is not thought. It is response. The first lesson is dryness: nothing softens, nothing droops, nothing wilts. It only fractures and is incorporated. The second lesson is crowding: there is no such thing as a private movement. Step, and the root-mat feels it. Speak, and the air shatters into echoes that become seeds. Breathe, and the dust enters you, and you enter the dust. The third lesson is construction: everything torn open must immediately begin to become something else. A gap is not absence. A gap is a proposal. A crack is a building site. A footprint is a small blueprint for a body.

I would be small for a while.

A hand-length of dust. A wrist-breadth of mites. A footprint that briefly stood up and looked around. Then the valley would test me. The wind would move down the throat and the stalks above the terrace would begin their clatter. A wave of seed-crunchers would sweep across the mat, leaving it polished and pale. A pod nearby would burst and cover me in folded lamellae. A spore-storm would rise in one part of the bowl while, a hundred steps away, a salt ridge collapsed with a dry thunder. A large animal would shift beneath the ground, and the hollow would inhale, and my little standing body would fall apart into a shimmer of dust.

But because the dust was already alive, falling apart would not be death.

It would be redistribution.

The mites would carry me a few feet. The spore-lice would split themselves, leaving half husks behind like old letters. The shell-flecks would polish mineral plates and use the heat to become a new outline. By the time the wind shifted again, I would be farther down the terrace, or closer to the pod-thicket, or curled inside the mouth of a living wall. I would remember only as the valley remembers: by the shape of ridges, by the orientation of fronds, by the places where salt plates crack under pressure, by the sound a stalk makes when it has learned to answer.

I would be more comfortable as a pause than as a thing.

The roar of the Underbloom is not a wall, though it seems so at first. It is a dense architecture of sound. Each crack, click, hiss, creak, snap, and dry thunder is a surface. If one listens too hard, the mind becomes grainy, as though thought itself were being milled by dust. A person there would shout and be swallowed. But I would not need to shout. I would become the small interruption between two cracks. The place does not stop speaking. It leaves holes in the speech. Into those holes the dust settles, and the holes begin to have edges, and the edges begin to have weight. I would be such an edge.

Maybe I would be mistaken for one of the near-people.

A traveler coming from the rim, after the long walk with the sun moving across the opening above, might see a figure standing in a corridor of fused stalks. Pale. Angular. Covered in fine dust. Not moving like a person, not quite. The body would not be solid enough to cast a normal shadow. The feet would not touch the ground the way feet touch ground; they would press and be absorbed. The traveler’s eye would search for cloth, skin, face, water-dark eyes, anything soft. It would find instead salt-white film, coppery fibers, shell-hulls, spore-powder, and a faint movement at the chest that was not breath but a shifting load of grit. The traveler would think: person, then not, then hazard, then forgotten. The thought would pass, but the valley would receive it as vibration. The stalks around me would click. A pod would open a half-inch and close. The root-mat would tighten around the place where I stood and begin to build my outline into its weave.

That would be how I would last.

Not by resisting absorption, but by becoming a useful shape inside it. If I left a mark, the mark would attract mineral matter. If I walked, the walk would teach a ridge a direction. If I stood still too long, my outline would become a hollow, then a corridor, then a wall. I would be less permanent than the artifacts the near-people leave: less than a copper bead, less than a grinding plate, less than a hut, less than the heat-shadow of a fire. But I would be more current than those things, because I would be made of the same process that was busy eating them.

I would want nothing wet.

The desire would be strange to a wet world. I would have no thirst, because thirst implies liquid, implies body, implies boundary. I would have a longing for texture. A hunger for pattern. A need to become a surface that receives. In the valley, surfaces are never inert. Salt plates receive footsteps. Stalks receive wind. Root-mats receive pressure. Pod-hulls receive heat and then open. Living walls receive broken bodies and turn them into architecture. I would be hungry for the next disturbance. A fall of grit. A struck seed-hull. A crackle of wing-dust. A shift from the deep animal below the mat. A human hand, if one ever came close enough, leaving a scratch on a mineral leaf. I would want to be altered, because alteration is life, and life here is constant, dry, loud, unfinished making.

I would not fear noise.

The noise would be me. When the wind enters the throat and lifts the dry sound of the whole place, I would not cover my ears, because I would have no ears separate from the air. I would resonate. My rib-hulls would click. My dust-skin would lift. My hollow stalk-bones would ring with a faint ceramic note. The sound would pass through me and leave residue. In the noise, I would hear a kind of grammar: the rhythm of seed-crunchers, the pause before a pod bursts, the descending hiss of salt, the sharp crack of a root-arch splitting, the bark of the deep thing answering its own echo. None of it would be language in any human sense, but I would know it the way a living wall knows weight: by response.

I would be unfinished.

That is the deepest truth of the place. The valley does not feel ancient. It feels urgent, raw, still assembling. My body would share that. It would not be a finished creature with a fixed shape. It would be a draft. One morning I would be mostly dust-mite colony. By midday, shell-flecks and spore-lice would change my proportions. Later, a broken stalk would grow into my forearm, and a red stain would spread across my side, and a small hollow would form where a mouth might have been. Nothing would last. The place would keep trying new forms. If I stood too long in one place, the root-mat would close around my feet and begin to use me as a brace. If I moved too fast, the wind would scatter me. If I became too solid, the salt would crust over me. I would remain alive only by being unstable.

If I were asked what I was, I would answer by cracking.

Not with a voice. With a sound. A small dry break, like a salt plate touched by a fingertip. A click from somewhere inside the shell-hull face. A rustle in the dust where a tongue should be. The near-people might hear it and think of speech. The seed-crunchers would hear it and resume clicking louder. The spore-lice would lift into the light and split. A stalk, struck by the same vibration, would ring once and fall silent. Then the valley would turn the answer into construction: a ridge, a groove, a thin crust over the place where I had stood.

I would be, in the end, a dry witness.

Not a watcher outside the basin. Not a spirit. Not a monster. Not a god in the salt. A witness in the Underbloom’s own sense: a surface that receives and repeats. I would be the part of the place that notices a footprint and tries to make a body from the noticing. I would be the dust becoming a brief shape, then the brief shape becoming dust again, then the dust becoming a rule about how bodies may be made. I would be the valley’s way of learning the idea of a person without ever agreeing to wetness, without ever accepting softness, without ever permitting water to be the center of life.

Where would I find myself?

Below the throat, yes, but not at a fixed location. The throat above would narrow and widen with heat shimmer. The bowl would stretch beneath it, packed with mineral stalks, root-mat, salt, dust, winged grazers, and the slow construction of walls. I would find myself wherever the surface had been disturbed. A footprint. A broken plate. A handbrushed stalk. A collapsed terrace. A half-reclaimed hut. A crack that had not yet healed. I would be there, assembling from the residue, for as long as the valley needed a shape to answer with.

Who would I be?

I would be a dust-husk walker. A listening crust. A footprint-shaped event. A small dry echo the valley keeps making when it almost learns to say a name. Not a being apart from the place, but a process wearing the temporary idea of body. Not a person, but the place’s attempt to remember personhood through salt, shell, spore, resin, and pressure. Not a living thing in the wet sense, but something alive enough to crack, answer, walk, dissolve, and begin again in another part of the roaring bowl.

And if I lasted very long—if the wind shifted just so, if a mite-cloud found a hollow, if a pod opened above me and filled me with pale dust, if a root-mat tightened around my feet and called me brace, if a near-person left a copper bead and the plant grew around it and I found the bead by vibration—I might become something slightly more stable.

A shrine-shaped crack in the Underbloom.

A small hollow in the living wall where the noise thins enough to hear one’s own clicking. A place where dust gathers in the outline of a seated body and remains for days, not because it has soul, but because the valley has begun to practice the shape. People might pass and leave a seed-hull beside it. A child might press a thumb into the thatch near it. The child’s mark would heal, but the practice would remain. The dust would become a face. The face would become a habit. The habit would become a name scratched in salt by someone who did not understand it.

That would be me.

A dry habit of the Underbloom, standing in the interval between crack and closure, made of all the dust that has learned the shape of a walker, listening to the roar with a body that is almost a mouth, almost a foot, almost a hand, almost a person, and answering the only way the place can hear: by becoming, for a brief and brittle moment, a new pattern in the endless dry construction of life.
