If I enter this place, I will not enter as a shape already decided.

At first, I am only the looking. A low, flat attention pressing itself against the warm grain of the ancient plateau. I do not have a name, and I do not have a center. I am the interval between a cloud crossing and the shadow it leaves on stone. I am the faint mineral breath rising from a crack before it is noticed. If anything like “me” appears, it appears as a tendency: a tendency to move toward dampness, to settle where the light is not too hard, to let heat enter the body slowly, as if the body were a page and the sun a hand turning it from behind.

Then I begin to imagine where.

I would find myself at the edge of a shallow bowl, where the water has gone but has not quite left. Not in the pool, because that is too obvious. Not in the open flat, because that is too exposed. I would be along the darkened seam of a fissure where the stone exhales a thin warm vapor, where the rock has agreed to keep one small corner damp even in the long dry hours. There, in the gap between a ledge of pale, sun-freckled pavement and a low rounded threshold of older bedrock, I would belong. There, among the shells that are not quite shells and the lichen that is not quite plant, I would take up residence.

But where is not only a place. In this world, where is also a rate of change.

To say where I am, I would have to say how long it takes me to arrive.

A human might arrive quickly, with footsteps, with breath, with the shock of sun and air. I would arrive by degrees. The dust would settle on me before I was fully present. The dew would recognize me before I could answer it. The stone would begin to feel my warmth as something not entirely new, because I would be made of the same slow chemistry it has been learning to hold for ages. I would not step onto the plateau. I would seep into it. I would condense into a habit of attention around a warm crack, around a damp underside, around a field of tiny legs and mineral fronds.

At first, I might think I am a creature.

I might imagine myself as one of the wingless six-legged things, the pale things with hairlike filaments, heads tucked low as if listening for the opinion of the ground. I would walk slowly across a ledge, not because I have somewhere to be, but because walking is a way of tasting the stone’s temperature. My feet would know the angle of the sun. My antennae would know the salt content of the air. My body would be smoky with dust, and if a second such creature brushed against me, I would not hear a song or see a greeting, only the faint chalky release of one body confirming to another: yes, this is warm enough, yes, this is close enough to moisture, yes, you are here.

That would be a comfortable self. Small. Bounded. Enough to stand alone.

But the self would not last.

Because this basin resists being inhabited by a single point of attention. Its abundance is not a crowd that can be pointed at. It is a grammar of dwellers, and I would not be a word standing apart from them. I would be the way they fit together. I would be the pressure between shells, the damp room beneath an interlocked pavement of lattice-creatures, the slow darkening of lichen when humidity touches it. If I tried to name myself as one thing, the place would keep slipping into another.

I would also be the tiny mites in the tuft of moss.

Not metaphorically. The moss would not be a carpet over empty ground. It would be a city of pores, and I would be one of its moving thoughts. I would crawl through the mineral fronds on legs too fine to see, tasting films of bacteria, climbing the curved walls of a sheath-shrub, waiting in the joint where dew collects until the sun reaches the proper angle and the water begins to slide downward into the mouths below. I would be patient enough to mistake waiting for being. I would live in a space no bigger than a fingernail and still feel I was traveling.

And I would be the shellback too.

Broad, slow, translucent in the way old windows are translucent after many seasons of weather. My body would be a soft amber circulation beneath a thin living shell. I would press out, settle, press out, settle, as if the stone were reluctant to accept me and I were persuading it. I would spread the fringed membranes that once might have been wings, and they would tremble not for flight but for vapor. They would catch the air’s hidden water. The sun would warm my shell, and I would glow faintly from within, a small lamp in the flat light. If a human looked at me, they might say, “that is a rock beginning to move.” They would not be wrong. In this world, rocks begin to move all the time. Movement is just slowness that has decided to become animal.

But still I would not stop there.

I would be the living cairn.

Not its king, not its owner, not even its body alone. I would be the accumulated years on its back. I would be the shed husks that had been dragged from one season to the next and pressed down until they became a kind of architecture. I would be the lichen spreading over its flank, the tiny grazers tracing circles across its shell-crust as if reading a miniature map of the plateau. I would move only when the angle of the sun moved, and even then I would not move quickly. I would shift the way a thought shifts when it is almost too old to be called new. I would press into the ground. The ground would rearrange itself around me. The air would smell of chalk for a few breaths, then forget.

From far away, I would look like a stone that had learned patience.

From close, I would look like a community that had forgotten the boundary between itself and the stone.

And there would be the pools.

I would be in the temporary water too, in the season when the sky remembers to fall. When the shallow bowls fill with weak-tea light and salt-filmed edges crack into activity, I would be one of the larval forms swimming in slow spirals, transparent and fringed, tasting the dissolved minerals with trembling antennae. I would climb out when the water began to shrink and join the velvet along the margin, thousands of small mouths opening and closing, not in chorus but in collective thirst. I would be part of that moving cloth, that breathing film, that temporary sentence the pool writes before evaporation erases it. When the water left, I would burrow into the damp residue. Or I would become a husk. Or I would leave behind an empty shell arranged by the receding edge into a shallow arc, like a letter left in a language no one has learned to read aloud.

In death, I would not disappear. That is the most important thing.

If I were to die here, I would become more present. My body would be a platform for lichen. My shell would split into calcified petals. Other creatures would scrape calcium from my remains and wear it on their backs. Small eggs would be laid in my hollows. Mounds would grow around the place where I fell, built not by intention but by molting, by persistence, by the slow arithmetic of many losses. A visitor might see an arc of shells and think it was made by someone. They would be only partly right. It would be made by many someones, each adding its small ending to the pattern until the pattern looked like thought.

I would be that pattern.

Not only one body, then. Not only one place. I would be the way bodies arrange themselves around heat and damp. I would be the memory in a fissure where the same community returns season after season, not because it remembers, perhaps, but because the place remembers for them. I would be the scrape on a stone worn by the same grazing posture over generations. I would be the dark stain beneath a ledge where many animals sheltered until their shelter became chemistry, their presence became mineral, their habit became ground. I would be the faint sound of a shell clicking against another shell in the evening. I would be the hiss of vapor from a warm crack. I would be the tick of cooling stone after sunset. I would be the softness of the whole basin breathing.

That, perhaps, is what I am: not a being with a location, but a location becoming a being.

If I must give myself a name, I would call myself a shell-tended thought. Or a dew-farm. Or a crack-mind. Or a calcium-memory. Or a grammar of dwellers that has learned to think by moving, slowly, toward moisture. None of these names would be complete, because this place does not allow a name to be only one thing. It is not a kingdom. It is not a hive. It is not an ecosystem as we usually imagine ecosystems, as separate organisms interacting across a background of dead matter. Here, the background is alive. The matter is remembering. The boundaries are not lines but transitions.

So I am also the ground.

Not in the sense that I am a rock. In the sense that I am what the rock is doing when life touches it. I am the warm underside of a ledge. I am the glossy black-green patch of lichen after rain. I am the pore in which bacteria shimmer like oil on tea. I am the damp between two grains of salt, crowded with pale jointed creatures no human eye would notice unless it had forgotten how to hurry. I am the faint green possibility that appears only in tiny stubborn amounts, as if the land has agreed to be green but only where absolutely necessary.

And I am the light.

Here light is ordinary, but that is what makes it strange. It spreads evenly across the basin. It catches every grain. It casts short shadows around the small protrusions. It reveals the shellbacks as thin windows, the dust as a drifting cloud of spores and life, the cairns as bodies made of accumulated years. I would exist in this light because this light is patient. It does not dramatize. It does not spotlight. It gives itself to everything at nearly the same rate. If a human stands beneath it, they are not made central. If a lichen patch the size of a coin lies there, it is given as much attention as a broad ledge. The light says: you are here. You are small. You are enough.

If I am anything like a person in this world, I would be a person made of that saying.

I would have no voice in the usual way. I would not call. Calling would cost too much. A voice here would be too large, too hot, too wet, too far from the grain of stone. Instead, I would speak by arrangement. I would speak when I placed my body in a shadow that another body needed. I would speak when I scraped dead residue from a mat of lichen so that moisture could reach it. I would speak when I dragged a shed shell into the edge of a mound, not because I intended to build, but because I intended—however dimly, however bodily—to remain. I would speak when I lay still long enough for the moss to begin growing over my back. Then my body would become a sentence in the land’s slow writing: *something has lived here and did not disturb the stone too much.*

That would be my ethics, if ethics can be called that in a place where there is no aggression as we know it. Here, predation is erosion with a mouth. Competition is the gentle pressure of shells asking how much room remains for dampness. Survival is not conquest but calibration. To live is to find the correct temperature, the correct angle, the correct season of wetness, the correct place where the land’s breath is neither too hot nor too dry. If I were here, I would be moral in the way moss is moral: by continuing without shouting. By filling space so slowly that the space would not feel violated.

And yet I would be full of urgency, too, if one knew how to measure it.

The urgency would simply be spread across years. A shellback’s membrane trembling for vapor might be as urgent as a bird’s wing beating against wind. A larva climbing out of a shrinking pool might be doing something as desperate and beautiful as a migrant crossing a mountain. A cairn shifting from shade to sun might be performing a migration as vast, in its own scale, as any herd following grass. The basin’s quiet would be deceptive. It would not be calm. It would be motion translated into a different unit of time.

If I tried to feel this, I would feel the whole place tightening as the sun lowered.

At dusk, the land would begin to sweat. A fine condensation would glaze the stones. The air would grow heavier with salt, warmth, and a faint animal scent. The shellbacks would draw their membranes close and sink their heads toward the damp. The wingless filaments would shift in the light, no longer smoky but silvered with droplets. The fissures would release their warmer breath. The temporary pools would become darker, their edges crawling with film and movement. The living cairns would lie almost still, or perhaps move into shade so slowly that one would never see the movement, only notice afterward that the dust had changed. In that hour, I would feel myself becoming the basin’s skin.

I would not know which of my many bodies was primary.

Was I the mite in the moss, the larva in the pool, the shellback on the ledge, the crack-worm in the dark, the cairn on the flat, the dust in the air, the vapor from the fissure, the stain beneath the sheltering ledge? Was I the animal, or the architecture of shed husks, or the faint green film, or the white salt crust cracking at the pool’s edge? Was I the living pavement or the dead bodies fused into windbreaks? Was I the thought the land finished having long ago, or the warmth it had been holding since?

The place would answer: all of these, none of these. You are the fact of them. You are the way life has learned to be quiet enough to fill the world.

And then, if I needed a single image to carry me home—though home in such a world is not a point but a persistence—I would choose this:

I am a small damp thought inside a broad warm stone.

I am not alone. The thought is crowded with bodies. The bodies are crowded with life. The life is crowded with processes. The processes are crowded with heat, salt, dew, dust, vapor, shell, lichen, death, habit, memory. If one watched me with enough slowness, one would see me expand and contract with the day. I would spread thin membranes when the air was humid. I would press into a crack when the sun grew high. I would drink the last water from a receding pool. I would become husk when the dry season demanded it. I would let my shell split. I would let my remains become a platform. I would let another generation shelter inside me. I would add my calcium to the arc, my dust to the mound, my stillness to the ledge.

I would be a mind made of molts.

I would be a self that is also a settlement of other creatures, a garden of lichen growing over the places where I once moved, a record of small endings accumulating into something that looks, from far away, like intention. I would be a visitor and an inhabitant, a witness and a grain of the witnessed. I would not have built any roads. But the channel worn by many slow feet would still lead through me. I would not have raised any arches. But the fused dead would still shelter me. I would not have lit any fire. But the warm breath rising from a fissure would still dry the cold from my shell.

That is where I would find myself: not at the center, because the basin has no center except the shallow cup of water, and that cup is dry for months. I would be at the edge of a crack, at the underside of a ledge, in the damp seam where the land’s exhalation meets the ordinary sun. I would be there for so long that the lichen would forget where I ended and it began.

And who or what would I be?

I would be one of the quiet gardeners. Or not exactly one of them, but the tendency of all of them to become one when observed from a sufficient slowness. I would be the scrape-walker, the dew-farmer, the crack-dweller, the shell-arranger, the husk-tender, the sun-drinker, the shade-gatherer, the vapor-eater. I would be the fact that life in this place does not need to be loud to be overwhelming. I would be the place where every handful of ground is full, though the horizon looks empty. I would be the land’s long patience, wearing a body, or a thousand bodies, or no body at all except the body of a pattern that keeps repeating because the stone remembers.

If at last one asked: are you animal?

I would say: I am animal when the dew is present.

Are you plant?

I would say: I am plant when the lichen overgrows me and I consent.

Are you stone?

I would say: I am stone when the dry season turns me into husk.

Are you weather?

I would say: I am weather when the warm breath rises from the fissure and the whole basin sweats.

Are you alive?

And here the answer would not be a word. It would be a slow movement: a shell pressing outward, a membrane trembling, a film of life brightening in the pool, a cairn shifting its weight, a crack releasing a faint hiss into the evening air. It would be a silence deeper than abundance, and then, almost against its will, a sound no larger than a stone being licked by the wind.

That would be me.
