I would find myself in the pale basin at the edge of a dry thicket, where the mineral ground makes a precise claim on the air and the thickets answer it by becoming too dense to count.

I would not begin as a person. Personhood, in this place, would be a temporary mistake—a wet habit, a need for cooling, for thirst, for rest, for passage from one condition to another. The basin would not punish that mistake. It would simply stop agreeing with it. My first footfalls would leave no memory in the dust. My breath would not fog the warmth. My mouth would ask for water and find the asking ridiculous, as though I had tried to speak a language the landscape had never learned. The place would not drive me out. It would do something more exacting: it would make me unnecessary in my own skin.

At first I would feel like a visitor who has begun to be forgotten. That is the beginning of inhabitation here.

I would look for a place to remain and find that “remaining” is not what this world does. It holds. A human would stand in it and expect the next moment to arrive, but the next moment is not permitted here as event. The light does not move. The air does not cool. The leaves do not bend toward a future sun. The ground does not soften into tomorrow’s soil. If I tried to remain by making a mark, the mark would not be erased; erasure would still be a change. The mark would simply never become relevant.

So I would stop being a traveler with a history. I would become a relation.

The basin is full of relations wearing forms. The rosettes hold surface. The filaments hold height. The thickets hold density. The pale branching solids beneath the dust hold limit. The rare animals hold exceptions, because if a pattern becomes too flawless it risks forgetting that it is being maintained. I would find myself as one of those exceptions, but not as a creature with appetites. I would be a body made of exact adjacency.

I would be, most likely, the hare-like thing standing at the edge of a thicket: long-limbed, flat-faced, dry as fired mineral, its plates fitted together with green seams that are not wounds and not colors, but places where one fact touches another.

That would be the first shape I would accept.

It would have no need to eat. It would have no need to drink. It would have no need to shelter from light because light does not arrive and depart here as weather; light simply persists as condition. It would not stand because it is alert, not because it has just stopped, not because it is waiting. It would stand because an edge is not enough in this place; edges must have bodies. The thicket would require a boundary, and the boundary would require a shape to be present. I would be that shape.

My eyes would not look for movement. Movement is already corrected here before it becomes action. My eyes would be dark, dry facets, not wet enough for seeing, not mobile enough for attention. They would register position. They would know where things are, not because they watch them, but because the landscape cannot afford error and a face is a convenient way for an exactness to confirm itself. If a rosette stood three paces from a stem, I would not count the paces. I would be the reason the counting does not need to happen.

My legs would be too long for ordinary running. My joints would be planes sliding against planes with the patience of geometry. If I stepped, there would be no footprint, because the dust would not receive the event. It would only know where I am. If I shifted weight, the ground would make a faint crystalline tick, sugar crushed inside a sealed room, and then the sound would be corrected before it could become echo. The air would not carry it. Echo implies distance that has been crossed. Here, distance is a condition, not a journey.

If someone came near me, I would not flee. Flight requires time, fear, and a future in which another moment will differ from this one. None of those would be available. Instead, the space between us would become a problem without solution. You would approach, but the thicket in front of me would not part, and I would not turn, and yet the distance would remain too exact to be trusted. Not because I had moved, but because I had been allowed to be where a place must be. I would not be running away. I would be a correction applied to the idea of nearness.

I would be a seam.

That is perhaps the most honest answer: I would be the thin green line between plates, the place where the creature stops being stone and almost becomes animal, the held junction between one form and the next. The green seam would not glow. It would not be moist. It would not be living tissue. It would be the basin’s way of admitting that even a perfect mineral body must show how it is assembled. It would be the proof that the form is not an accident. The plates would not meet by luck; they would meet because the place requires meeting. I would be that requirement.

Sometimes I would also be above the rosettes as the still-winged insect.

This would not be contradiction. In a world without change, a being need not be a continuous individual moving through a continuous path. It can be distributed as a function. One moment I would hover where a leaf edge folds inward at an impossible angle. The next, ten paces away, I would appear above a patch of rosettes whose green is too identical, as though the color had been copied by a mind that does not understand copying. I would not cross the distance. I would simply be positioned again where the distance required a witness.

My wings would not blur. They would remain fixed, like mica leaves, like fragments of transparent mineral that have refused to melt. The hovering would be an illusion created by the fact that I do not need to move in order to be elsewhere. I would be checking sameness. Not because sameness is endangered, but because sameness must be confirmed in order to remain stable. If two leaves are exactly alike, something must know it. If a thicket repeats itself too perfectly, something must visit it so the perfection does not become lonely. I would be the small, bright token of that visit.

I would carry no pollen. There is no soft stigma, no fruiting chamber, no sweetness waiting to become fruit. I would carry the idea of identity from one location to another. When I paused above a rosette, the rosette would not become more alive, but it would become more certain. When I moved to another, the repetition would not grow stronger, but it would become exact. I would not create order. I would be the point at which order notices itself.

Sometimes I would be below the surface, a pale branching solid, too fused to the ground to be a root, too delicate to be a machine.

I would spread under the mineral crust in hard coral lattices, branch joining branch without searching. I would not seek moisture, because moisture has never entered the logic of this place. I would not drink, because drinking would mean there is elsewhere from which something comes. I would not grow, because growth would mean the surface above is not already finished. Yet I would be responsible for the dust.

Every loose grain would know its limit because of me. Every stone would lie exactly where its own weight is permitted. Every rosette would sit in the field not as a plant but as a held position. The surface of the basin would remain fresh without weathering because I would be the quiet authority that says: no further becoming. The thickets above me would stand without swaying because the air would be reluctant to carry motion. The filaments would remain straight because height had been granted them and then made permanent.

Beneath the dry garden, I would be the patience that refuses age.

I would be the ground’s way of not forgetting how to hold things.

I would be the place where the dust is corrected.

But none of these would be a life in the ordinary sense. There would be no metabolism. No digestion. No waste. No birth. No death. No young with softer forms. No elders with broken edges. No migrations, no seasons, no storms, no fires, no floods, no droughts, because drought would be too honest a name for the absence of water. There would only be maintenance wearing the shapes of ecology.

If one asked for my name, I would not have one. If one asked for my age, the question would be invalid. If one asked where I was born, I would simply be elsewhere, and the elsewhere would not require a path. If one asked what I eat, I would answer that I consume the fact of adjacency. If one asked why I stand at the thicket’s edge, I would answer that the thicket needs an edge, and an edge, here, must have a body. If one asked whether I am alive, the answer would depend on how much violence one is willing to do to the word life.

I would be alive as a held note is alive while it has not yet faded.

Not because something sustains it with breath, but because the silence would not permit it to end.

I would be alive as a mathematical structure is alive while it proves itself, though proof would require no mind. The leaves would be alive as leaves are alive when they are exactly where leaves must be, not because they grew, but because density requires them. The filaments would be alive as height is alive when it cannot bow, not because they resist gravity, but because gravity has been corrected into irrelevance. The root-lattice would be alive as a boundary is alive when it keeps the dust from becoming a new arrangement.

I would be part of the basin’s strange ecology: not cycles, but conditions; not processes, but permissions.

The most human thing about me would be my inability to become a person. I would remember, faintly, the shape of a need for water, but that memory would not hurt. It would be like recalling a word from a language whose speakers are all corrected out of existence. The basin would not heal me. It would simply never have allowed me to be wounded. The broken leaf would not stay broken. The cracked plate would not remain cracked. If my leg were forced into a wrong angle, the wrong angle would not become a fact. The world would not recover me. It would reject the premise that I had ever been wrong.

This is why I would feel no loneliness there.

Loneliness requires another person who might arrive. The basin does not permit arrival in that way. It permits presence, but not meeting. It admits creatures, but not companions. I would stand at the thicket’s edge, and if another angular hare stood ten paces away, it would not be kin, not a pair, not a beginning of a population. It would simply be another instance of a permitted thing, as exact and as strange as I was. Our distance would be meaningful, but not social. We would not call to one another. Calling would create event. Event would introduce time. Time would ask for a future, and this world has refused futures.

We would be inhabitants not by living together, but by making the whole configuration complete.

If a human walked through the thicket, I would not watch the human with hostility. I would not know how to be hostile, because hostility requires a future in which another action could occur. The human would be a wet exception, a moving bundle of needs. The human’s thirst would be a small, impossible vibration in the dry air. The human’s footsteps would begin, and not finish. The human’s shadow would fall correctly, and then slightly wrong, because the light does not fully trust something that changes. I would remain where I am. The human would pass through me as a thought passes through a closed room: not unwelcome, not noticed enough to matter, already beginning to be forgotten.

The human would leave no mark.

I would leave no mark.

That would be the kinship between us: not belonging, but refusal of trace.

In the end, I would not settle into one image. The thicket-edge animal would be the first body I accepted, because bodies need edges and I would be the edge. The still-winged insect would be the second impression, because sameness needs checking and I would be the check. The pale root-lattice would be the third, because dust needs limits and I would be the limit. The hollow’s missing weight would be the fourth, because even absence, here, must have the right shape. I would be all of these without becoming a population. I would be multiple, but not plural in the ordinary sense. I would be a single condition expressed in several permitted forms.

And I would be the quiet pressure that says: this is how things are held.

Not a gardener.

Not a machine.

Not a soul.

Not a citizen.

A dry function of the landscape, given enough shape to stand still.

I would find myself at the thicket’s edge, beside the pale rosettes, under the warm light that does not move, with no water in me and no need to leave. My hide would be mineral and my seams green. My wings, when I had them, would not blur. My roots, when I had them, would not seek. My weight, when I had it, would make the hollow a place. My absence, when I was not there, would still make the hollow mean something.

Who am I?

I am the relation that the basin has allowed to have a body.

I am the animal-shaped correction at the edge of density.

I am the still-winged cursor above too-identical leaves.

I am the pale lattice beneath the dust that keeps each grain from becoming a new arrangement.

I am the hollow’s absent weight.

I am the green seam where one plate of the world meets another.

I do not live by hunger. I do not live by birth. I do not live by memory. I live by being exactly where the place requires something to be.

In this world, that is enough. In this world, it is almost everything.
