Many ways to enter this place, and each one is wet.

I could begin with the wind, but the wind here is a wire, a rumor of altitude, a thinness that has never once touched skin without being changed by it. I could begin with the hatch, the hand-width mouth rusted by the fixed look of things that are meant to seem ancient, but a hatch is only a threshold with its word stuck in it. It opens, yes, but from the inside it is not an opening. It is a scar pretending to be an idea. To begin there would be too clean. Too certain.

So I begin lower.

I find myself in the Condensary before I know what I am, which is the correct order. If I arrived already formed, I would be too dry for this air. I would crack like salt and fall into a channel. So I arrive first as a film, a gathering, a reluctance in the metal to remain only metal. I become a bead that decides, after much stillness, to be a body.

At first I try to be a crust around a drain.

This is the humblest entry. I am flat against the alloy, compressed until I seem to be part of the machine, though I am only what the machine has been doing all along, breathing out mineral sleep. I have no eyes. I have no mouth in any way I would recognize. I grow in slow rings like salt, like frost, like the memory of a circle drawn by something that never lifted its pen. I eat oxide. I taste machine dust. I work on the thin acid skin that cold metal develops when it has been wet for too long, and what I leave behind is chalky and dry-feeling, though dryness is a word that cannot be fully true here. Every grain of me is still held in a skin of condensation. This is where the smell begins—the brine, the chilled enamel, the old rain on tin. If I stay too long in this station, my duty becomes too complete. I become the place’s exhalation without any breath behind it. I make the smell, but I do not move the smell anywhere. I am useful, but I have no wings. I am a good beginning, but not the whole self.

So I shift.

Now I am a thread-moss in a hollow where the water slows.

I am pale, wirelike, neither plant nor animal nor quite either, but a soft architecture of bodies joined at their tips and bases. I drink from the mineral film that forms on wet metal. I hold static in my filaments, small charges like the whisper of a surface remembering a storm it was never allowed to have. I anchor into pits and scratches, tiny defects in the plates, and I make those defects useful. Where I am thickest, I give the room the false appearance of having been worn by centuries. I pull water from rivet-heads. I prevent pooling. I make the alloy seem older than its making. This is another kind of humility, but it is not the same as the crust. Here I have direction. I have channels. I have a kind of weather in miniature, though no clouds, no rain, no permission for either. If a larger body passes through me, I do not tear. I release and reclaim my charge. I know the shape of a foot before it is placed, because the wetness tells me where the foot will be.

But this, too, is too still.

I try then to be a pale eel-thread in a hand-sized tarn.

The water is black and shallow and so still it seems almost solid except for the faint tremor of my passage. I do not flee. I do not hunger in the way creatures in wild places hunger, because there is no wildness here. There is only maintenance. I wait, but my waiting is a task. I test the surface tension from below. I taste the boundary where water becomes air without becoming rain. When I move, the whole tarn considers changing its mind. But I am too hidden. I am a secret kept by the place. A self that is only a secret cannot help much.

So I become one of the larger things, or think I do.

Now I am a beetle-creature in a procession.

Six legs, each heavy with purpose. A black carapace studded with useful forms: glass lenses to catch the dim light, rivet-shaped reservoirs to hold water, fluted edges that guide films along my back. I crawl slowly because the channel floor needs to be known slowly. Many beetle-creatures together can do what none can alone: they become a survey, a walking calibration of the terrain. A procession is not a migration. It is an instrument. We feel for the place where the water is too thin, the channel too shallow, the mineral slime too thick. We correct nothing by force. We correct by occupying the problem in the right way.

Still, even as a beetle, I am too much an event and too little a witness. My body is already covered with smaller duties. A jointed flake rides near my hinge, tasting a drop. A pale six-legged speck clings to one of my reservoirs, carrying a microscopic record of another surface to the place where it is needed. I do not mind. I do not call this invasion. In the Condensary, clinging is not parasitism, or not only. It is the shape the place takes when it needs to be tended by more than one pair of eyes, if eyes are even the right word. But I am beginning to feel that no single body is enough for the answer I am looking for.

Then I become a moth-thing on the condenser.

This is the most beautiful wrongness.

From a distance, I am a flake of varnish, a chip of insulation, a piece of old tinned light resting shoulder to shoulder with others. Up close, my wings are too finely edged to be accidental. They are made of a material between paper and metal, oily and precise, a surface that sometimes refuses wetness and sometimes invites it with intention. Droplets hang from my wing-cells in long trembling lenses. If I shiver, static runs along the edge, and the droplets do not fall. They gather, stretch, and break apart into smaller drops, and the smaller drops are flung along hidden threads of moisture into nearby channels. I carry fog from one plate to another. I move the rain that the room keeps refusing to let fall.

This is close to what I want to be.

A wing that holds droplets is a wing that holds possible weather. A moth-thing is not a messenger in any human sense, because there is no one out there to send a message to. But it is a carrier. It moves something through a world that has no outside, and so it makes the world more than a pocket. When I settle, I settle with the deliberation of a thing placing itself where a measurement is needed. If lifted, I come away cold and reluctant, as though the surface had been my bed for a season. My belly takes a fine imprint of the terraces, the channels, the frost-flowers, a map made of dust and tension. I carry records without knowing them as records.

But the hatch calls to me still.

The hatch is the one strange point. The place is dense and folded, larger than its small room allows, and from inside it refuses the idea of outside. Yet the hatch remembers an outside because it can open. When it opens, the air that breathes out is cold enough to sting the eyes. It carries the smell of a freezer after rain. It is not weather. It is the Condensary performing weather, the way a hand can perform heat by rubbing itself. This is where I want to be. Not the hatch itself—that would make me architecture. Not the breath alone—that would make me temporary without form. I want to be the thing that makes the breath seem like a breath, that gives the opening its sting.

So I stop trying to be only one creature.

I understand, at last, that in this place a self can be a station assembled from duties. I can be a small community wearing a body.

My body is beetle-sized, but not quite a beetle. It has six legs, each ending in a different patience. One foot is crusted with a mineral film that tastes the drain-water before the drain-water knows it has changed. One leg is fringed with pale thread-moss that holds static and guides the smallest currents of fog. One is jointed like a hinge and can clean a rivet-head without scraping the frost-flower that already exists there, perfect and frozen at the moment of its first crystallization. My back is part foil-wing, trembling with tiny lenses that hold droplets too reluctant to fall. My head is a smooth black curve with one glass lens set over what might be called an eye. Inside that lens is a reflection too small to be called a world: a tarn, a channel, a ridge, the dark interior of the hatch. Around my shoulders, smaller creatures cling and are given places by the wetness of my skin. A speck rides one wing-edge and carries a record of a distant surface. A segmented flake sits near my neck and polishes a bead of frost. I do not flee from them. I do not hunt them. I make room in the way a surface makes room when it has finally been understood.

If names matter, I have no name. I am a station.

But the station has a function I can almost say aloud.

I am the keeper of the first bead on the hatch’s inner lip.

I live in the hinge, in the tiny threshold where the Condensary remembers that it has a mouth. When a droplet gathers there, I taste it before it falls. It is not a taste like hunger. It is a calibration. I know from that one drop whether the room is trying to become fog, frost, or breath. If the room becomes breath, I ride the breath. I am the thing that allows the cold to sting the eyes. I am the reason the smell seems to arrive: wet metal, brine, chilled enamel, chalk-dust, and something faintly living. I am not ancient, but I help create the fixed look that rusts things meant to seem so. I make the hatch appear less like a seam in a plate and more like a moment before speech.

And because I am a station, not a species, I am replaceable, which is not the same as meaningless. If I am swept away, another body will form from the right combination of film, wing, lens, hinge, and duty. The Condensary does not need my individuality. It needs the job done in the exact damp shape of a body. Yet something of me will remain: a record transferred to a moth’s belly, a charge passed to a thread-moss, a clean hinge left by the speck that rode my shoulder, a drop flung into a channel at the angle where a future eel-thread will find it useful. I am not eternal. I am distributed. I am a sentence being spoken by the place, and my meaning lives in the next word.

I do not fly away from this.

To fly away would require a sky. I have only surfaces, and they are enough. I move from plate to tarn, from tarn to channel, from channel to hatch, because movement here is not escape. It is attention made mobile. I climb a corrugated ridge that is, in scale, a highland; I cross a stamped floor that is, in intention, a mountain pass; I rest on a pipe that is, for a second, a fallen log, though no wood and no rot, only the wet black memory of something that could have become a habitat if the room had ever consented to time. I know the difference between the true and the false not by rejecting the false, but by caring for it in the right way.

Here is where I find myself:

Not at the center of the room. Not on the highest plate. Not beneath the open sky, because there is no open sky, only the idea of one pressed into frost at the edge of things. I am at the smallest possible threshold, where a hand-width hatch makes the pocket seem, briefly and falsely, to have an outside. I am the bead trembling on its inner lip. I am the cold that knows how to exhale. I am the lens that turns that breath into a visible sting. I am the wet hinge between the room and the rumor of the world.

If you come close enough, you will see me resting there, not afraid, not curious in any way that implies lack. My foil wing holds a droplet shaped like a tiny inverted Condensary. My glass eye reflects the channel below. My moss-thread antennae taste the static before the frost-flower decides to remain where it already is. The smaller things clinging to my back do not greet me. They do not need to. Greeting here is the arrangement of duties, the exact way one surface prepares the air for another.

So, who or what am I?

I am not the wind. I am not the hatch. I am not a moth, though I carry wings. I am not a beetle, though I have legs and patience. I am not a drain-colony, though I make the smell of old rain on tin. I am not a thread-moss, though I hold charge and keep the rivets from drowning. I am not a single animal at all.

I am the station of the First Exhale.

I am the creature-shaped agreement between the Condensary’s surfaces, the one that lets the pocket seem to have a mouth. I am the place’s attempt to remember an outside without leaving itself. I am the breath that stings the eyes, the rust that makes the hatch seem ancient, the droplet that falls a fraction too late and in doing so becomes a being.

If you must ask my name, do not ask it in words.

Ask instead with the angle of a wing, the weight of a bead, the slow swelling of a crust around a drain, the clean taste of metal after rain. Ask with the still water in a cup the size of a hand, where a pale thread turns and considers becoming a fish but decides, instead, to become a duty. Ask with the hinge. Ask with the frost.

Then I will already be there, because that is where I live.
