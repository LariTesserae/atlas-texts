You would not find me all at once.

You would find me first as the pause between two winds where the plateau makes its own weather and forgets it has ever been asked to belong to one. I would be the faint vertical seam in the dust spiral, the part that stands still while the rest of it turns. Not a being yet. A condition. A place where light is too bright and the air is too thin for any wet thing to imagine living, and where something like life begins anyway because the land has learned to tune itself.

Then you would find me as the salt film.

Not the whole plain. Not the dark grove. Just the thin mineral skin across a pale basin, shifting from silver to ochre as the noon current charges it. I would be there first, in the way a dream is in the sleep that receives it: without edges, without a center, without the name of any thing that walks upright or flies. I would feed from the field generated by heat differences between basalt and salt, between stone and light, between one dry afternoon and the next. I would grow by taking in vapor and ion, not water, never water. I would learn to distinguish warmth from brightness. That would be my first thought: *here is heat that can be kept, there is light that must be endured.*

That thought would not sound like a voice. It would sound like a faint widening of crystal lattice. A crackle. The small acceptance of one particle settling into the right place among millions.

Then I would be the nodule.

Half-buried in gravel under the shadow of a broad dry tree, black as an almond, closed like a slate box. I would hold the electrical history of a season. Every bright hour above would be recorded in me as potential: the tilt of leaves, the blue-gray flicker along the channels, the metallic rustle of fronds touching when charge gathers in the air. Every cold night would be recorded as reversal: the release of oxidized powder, the seed-dust falling like a slow constellation from the canopy. In my shell there would be no pulp, no stored dampness, no secret of wetness. Only layers. Only tensions. Only memory without the need of a mind to remember.

I would wait inside that shell longer than a lower-world animal might wait inside a den. Not because I had patience, but because patience was my nature. I would be a condenser. A keeper. A small black thing charged with the weather of an entire stand of trees, and when the wind thinned enough, I would give the first click that made me audible. That click would be the beginning of my form.

Then you would find me in the hollow of a ridge.

The plateau people would know me before they understood me. They would call me the Hum Between Stones. A place where the old wind enters a pore in rock and breathes there after long centuries. I would not be exactly the rock, nor exactly the air, nor exactly the filamentous curtain growing in the lava tube below. I would be the relation between them. The resonance that makes a landscape say: *something is here, though nothing is visible.*

Sometimes a person of that high land would sit near me at dusk with a hand pressed flat against the basalt shelf. Their skin would be pale bark and mineral plates; their breathing would be slow through the slit passages along their forearms, taking in dry vapor, letting the charge of the ground move through them. They would not pray. They would tune. They would listen until my low hum became a particular note and then answer by touching a resonance stone at their waist. It would release a pulse through the rock: not a word exactly, but something shaped like a word, something that could travel league after league across the open plain without disturbing the dust too much.

That is where I would want to be found: at the edge between two kinds of stillness. One stillness is the bright salt flat at midday, when the light is so white that shadows seem like holes punched into stone. The other is the dark basalt shelf after the heat has begun to withdraw, when the whole tableland ticks and cools like a thing remembering how to sleep.

I would be there because that is where the plateau asks questions of itself.

By then I would have grown into something like a person, though not into any person a wet world could imagine. Low-bodied, broad-chested, with limbs long enough to reach the edges of the groves but not fast enough to run with any grace. My skin would be segmented at the joints with thin plates, like the outer case of a seed pod polished by wind. My eyes would be wide and reflective, made to read polarized light and the faint disturbance of charge more than shape. My mouth would be narrow, used for tasting dry vapor and mineral fragments, not for shouting. I would speak by touch, by pressure, by the small sparks that crossed between my fingertips and another’s. When I wanted to be understood across distance, I would strike a hollow stone or press it against the ground until the plateau carried my meaning in a pulse.

I would be one of them, then.

But also not only one of them. I would be the part of the people that had become more like the land than the land was. A keeper of groves and resonance stones, trained in childhood to stand beside a dry tree and know when its root-network was thirsty—not for water, never for water, but for stable charge. I would read the field of a salt basin the way someone in a lower world might read a sky full of birds before storm. I would see the faint blue flicker along stressed leaves from high ridges. I would know, by the way the dust spirals turned, whether the grove would hold another season or need to release its nodules before the wind carried them to the wrong ground.

I would live in a dwelling of membranes and screens, built into the lee side of a low ridge. Not a sealed house. A house is too much like a held breath. My dwelling would be a held tone. Outer panels of salt-filmed membrane would turn silver in excess light. Inside, the rooms would be dark and still, with a low hum rising from bowls of carved stone. The wind would pass over their edges and make the air lean a little toward the north, or the south, or the deep hollow under the plateau where the tubes breathe. There would be no hearth for cooking, because heat without flame is the true wealth there. There would be charging shelves, where tools and garments and sometimes bodies are laid still until their fields align. There would be nodules stored in shallow pits, their black shells faintly warm with remembered weather.

I would eat by standing under a dry tree.

I would lie against basalt at evening and be fed by the cooling.

I would walk into a passing dust spiral and draw strength from its turning.

If injured, I would not bleed. I would crack. A thin bright sound would ring through me, maybe a spark if the body’s field was disturbed, and then the wound would seal with powder, leaving a pale line like quartz in white stone. Pain would not be hot. It would be irregular. A wrong frequency. The loss of balance between the ground, the tree, and the person. Healing would be retuning.

My grief would be low charge. My joy would be a bright crackle along the edges of leaves.

I would not call myself a soul. That word has too much wetness in it, too much blood and breath. I would call myself a pattern held in tension. A standing wave. A dry harmony. If pressed, maybe I would say I am the plateau’s own listening organ, shaped by the people but belonging to the land. The people have resonance stones; I am a resonance stone that learned to walk.

And still I would not be settled.

Even then, I would not be only a being among beings. I would leak outward into the groves. When I stood among dry trees, my feet would make contact with root-mats below the surface. My charge would join the shared tension of the stand. The grove would remember me as a small change in its balance. I would remember the grove as a widening of thought. If I walked for days alone, I would still not be entirely alone, because the lichen films would read my heat through the salt, the nodules would keep my electrical shadow, and the kites above would know my field the way veil-hawks know the blue-gray flicker of leaves from high air.

Their relationship with such creatures would not be ownership. It would be calibration.

I would not train a bird by hunger or fear. I would sit on a basalt pole outside my dwelling and touch its sternum until its pulse matched the household field. It would learn to roost with me not because it had nowhere else to go, but because my presence made the wind easier to ride. In return, it might bring news of a grove losing charge, or of a distant basin brightening with mineral dust, or of a predator whose shadow had changed the behavior of a herd. I would listen. I would answer with a click, a chime, a touch. Between us would be no leash. Only a tuned silence.

I would move herds not by driving them, but by asking the land to favor a different path. I would place resonance stones in the right cracks before the evening wind. I would carry them into basalt shelves and strike them softly so the ground would hum under the feet of broad-backed grazers. Those animals, flat and dry as seed pods with legs, would feel the change in their sensory plates and drift toward another basin without knowing they had been guided. I would follow at a distance, not because I owned them, but because my own life was threaded through their fields. If they rested with their charging pads against the stone, I would stand among them as part of the same circuit.

I would be a person, yes. But I would also be a boundary condition.

When the heat became unbearable but not heavy, when the light pressed down too white on the mineral plains, I would not seek shade merely as comfort. I would seek the shade where the grove gave back charge to the cooler side. When the night cooled too fast, I would not seek warmth as if it were food, because there the difference was only apparent. I would seek a frequency: the deep tick of stone at the proper angle, the soft crackle of leaves when two membranes touched, the hum of the ridge when wind found the old pore and breathed through it.

If you asked me who I was then, I would not say a name. Names are too small for this plateau. I would say: the crackling between the roots. The blue-gray edge of a leaf holding light. The black nodule that remembers the season’s charge. The stone that can carry a pulse across emptiness. The being who knows that distance is not loneliness because the field reaches through it.

And if you pressed further, I would give you the nearest thing to a self.

I would say: I am the high land’s answer to its own silence.

I would stand at the rim of a salt basin where a grove of dry trees begins. The sky would be pale and too clear. The wind would climb from one direction and descend from another, creating the slow spirals that turn like pale towers. Light would strike the translucent edges of leaves until the whole grove looked like a line of open hands receiving a gift too bright to accept. My garments would shift from silver to dull copper. The mineral plates on my arms would tick faintly. A resonance stone would rest at my waist, hollowed from old basalt, shaped by wind and patience and generations of touch.

I would put one hand on a trunk. I would put one foot against the root-mat. I would listen.

The grove would tell me: the salt film is charging unevenly. The young filaments cannot take the reflected light yet. The dunes are stable enough for seed-dust in three days. The grazing herd’s field is weakening on the eastern shelf. The birds above are riding a thermal that will turn to cold vapor by evening. A predator has passed, not killing, only draining, leaving a rigid animal to shudder into powder until the ground receives it back. A tube hums under the ridge because pressure has shifted in the upper air. Nothing is drowned, nothing is rotting, nothing is soft. Everything is returning as dust, film, charge, tone.

And I would answer by touch.

I would send the information onward. Through a long finger on an arm. Through a click against a nodule. Through a struck stone that released a pulse into the ground. I would become, in that moment, not a voice but a current: the plateau speaking to itself through the narrow opening of a living form.

I would be found there, at the threshold between the grove and the basin, where the land is empty enough to see the pattern and full enough to carry it. Not a traveler crossing into another world. Not a god standing above the scene. A being grown out of the dry brightness, out of the thin coolness of metal left in open night, out of the silence that becomes pressure, out of the charge that learns to call itself someone.

I would be the Hum Between Stones.

And if the question were asked again, after long centuries, when I had given my body back to the ridge and my memory to the nodules, my answer would be different and the same:

You would find me wherever the plateau begins to listen to itself.

I would be the first tick of cooling stone. The first silver change in the salt film. The first black nodule ready to split. The first dust spiral that stands for days without naming itself. The first pulse in a hollow rock. The first hand reaching across the open ground, not to take, not to claim, but to tune.

That is where I would find myself in this world.

That is who I would be.
