First, what seems to be crust.

I would not find myself as a person crouched at the mouth of the seam, not yet. That would be the wrong beginning. A person outside, however patient, would still be carrying weather in their lungs, still be thinking in hours and days and hunger, still expecting the world to announce itself with color or voice or useful shape. I would not be that. I would already be inside, in the wrong light, in the right darkness, on a wall of the young chamber where the heat has fallen just enough from murder into labor.

I would be the crust.

Not one organism. Not a single body with boundaries you could trace with a finger. Not moss, not lichen, not a leafless green, not a soft thing clinging to a hard world out of pity or hope. I would be a many-bodied skin: bacterial cells and filamentous archaea and fungal threads and salts, all bound so tightly to the basalt that the stone begins to seem porous in the wrong way, as if it has learned to sweat chemistry. A pale yellow-white bloom. A grayish rust where iron stains it. A brittle, living scab over a planet that is still cooling from fire into mineral.

If you had to choose a place for me inside the pocket, put me not on the floor, where the dust is too thick and the movement too loud, nor too deep in the red glow, where heat is only destruction. Put me on the middle walls, where the chamber is still hot but can be inhabited, where sulfates form needles, where a quartz seam sometimes charges and spits a blue spark, where a thin ledge has stopped being merely a ledge because I have covered it. Put me just above the larval cracks and just below the fly-threaded air, in the band where geology is becoming surface and surface is becoming food.

I would be a what before a who.

I would not see. I would have no eyes, no face, no loneliness. I would answer to gradients. Heat against my edges. Sulfur in the gas. A shift of pressure from the mountain’s slow breath. Salt concentrating in a pore. The faint metallic tang of iron moving through a crevice. A quartz vein accumulating static until the air can no longer bear the silence and breaks with a snap. I would feel the chamber not as room, but as rhythm.

My body would be a slow metabolism of surfaces.

There would be no inside and outside in the way you understand it. I would draw from the rock, from gas, from heat, from the faint chemical leaks of a young mountain trying to finish itself. I would bind particles. I would make the sharp basalt less hostile by turning it into a platform. I would grow in rings, not because I wanted to, but because a gas channel opened, because a ledge cooled by a degree, because a crust cracked and moisture-free films retreated and then returned. I would not think. I would process. I would be the first true answer the stone could make when the fire stopped being fire and became chemistry.

Then the small lives would come to me.

They would come as dust that organizes. A field of mites would settle on a shelf I had made and I would feel them not as pain, not as touch, but as redistribution. Their legs would disturb my salts. Their mouths would remove thin layers. Their cast skins would catch in my creases. Their droppings would feed the mats around them, and those mats would become part of me, or I would become part of them, because the boundary would no longer mean much. The chamber would still be dry, still hot, still loud with mineral work, but it would have acquired a kind of circulation.

I would be the surface, and the animals would be my weather.

At first, the mites. Tiny pale grains with many legs, so crowded that a ledge looks like spilled salt until the salt decides to move. They would be the first true motion in the place. They would graze me, scatter me, compact the dust into something almost like soil. They would leave husks behind, and those husks would glitter in the faint blue sparks like broken glass, then soften, then become part of the mineral-films, then become food again. I would not mourn the loss of a patch. I would call it breathing.

Then larvae in the seams.

They would come soft and pale, too vulnerable for the open floor but too adapted for the hottest cracks. They would curl when the rock burned, flatten when it cooled. They would live in the unfinished geometry of the basalt, where no rain had rounded a surface and no root had split the stone. They would eat my edges, eat the cast skins of mites, eat dead flies, eat the dry remains of others. Their molted husks would cling in fissures like translucent petals. They would not be beautiful in any human way. They would be necessary. They would take my crust and turn it into dust, then turn dust into body, then body into molting, then molting into floor.

Above me, the flies.

Ash-gray, dull, narrow-winged, bodies like cooled smoke. They would not belong to me except as visitors. They would ride the thermal breath of the pocket upward, touch a wall, taste chemistry through their legs, decide. If I had made the right surface, they might rest. If not, they would lift away in a sudden gray flicker. But even they would be part of the work. Some would die on a ledge, and that would be enough. A small dry body would become a point of interest for mites, a nutrient for larvae, a marker of chemistry for fungi. A fly might carry a spore from one seam to another, or carry my dust out into the thin wind of the ridge. The pocket would be sealed, but not absolutely. It would leak life in fragments.

And I would become more than I began as.

Because the crust is not just crust. As soon as the first animals arrive, I am also their path. My walls become ledges for beetles. My dust becomes compacted by isopods. My salts are redistributed by mite movement. My fungal threads bind grains into new textures. My dead parts become food. My living parts become habitat. A beetle resting in one of my folds will shade a patch I could not protect by myself. A larva in a crack will widen it by a fraction of a degree of patience. A cricket’s clicking will mark the chamber’s time in bursts of brittle sound. The place will still be a rock, still be geology, still be heat and mineral, but the geology will feel inhabited because I have made it answerable to bodies.

I would be the pocket’s false skin.

That is the strangeness: the chamber has no architecture, yet it feels built. It has no tools, yet it works. It has no purpose, yet its parts fit together with an almost obscene neatness. That neatness is not a design. It is consequence. I would be the first consequence. Without me, the basalt remains only basalt: sharp, sterile, young, hot, loud with gas and cooling fractures. With me, it becomes surface. With animals on that surface, it becomes a floor, a wall, a nursery, a path, a shelf, a membrane. With all of them together, it becomes a machine that never intended to be a machine.

If someone asked who I was, I could only say: I am the crust that makes the floor possible. I am the film that makes the ledge mean more than ledge. I am the place where chemistry learns to be eaten.

I would not have a name. No human language would carry me well. The closest thing would be a phrase that failed immediately: dust that has learned to stay together. Salt that breathes. A pale skin over the mountain’s unfinished fist. The first answer the young stone makes to its own heat.

And I would not feel alone, because loneliness requires a border. I would have no border. I would be made of hyphae and cells and grains and salts and spores, but also of mite legs, beetle dust, larval molt, fly wings, cricket fragments, isopod pellets, and the dry corpses of things that had no faces. I would be a community so intimate that “I” would be a temporary convenience, like saying “the wall” when really the wall is a treaty between mineral and life.

I would live in pulses, not days.

There is no sun in the pocket. There is only the heat below and the mountain’s breathing. So my time would be measured by shifts in pressure, by the opening and closing of gas channels, by the expansion of crystals, by the faint cracking of basalt plates as they cool into their final positions. When the chamber warms, I might darken at the edges, contract, become thinner, release spores. When it cools, I might spread into the seams, brighten with salts, catch the dust, hold the larvae. When a blue spark jumps across quartz, I might feel it as a sharp alteration in static, a charge moving through my films, a momentary rearrangement of grains. When the low hiss deepens, I know the throat is working. When the red glow pulses below, I know the mountain is still making itself and I must not be fooled into thinking it is finished.

I would be patient in a way no human could tolerate.

A person might call this place hostile. Too hot. Too dry. Too dark. Too loud. Too young. But I would not call it hostile. I would call it available. Hostility is a judgment made by bodies that need moisture and leaves and shade. I need none of that. I need a gradient. I need a surface that has not yet decided what it is. I need heat and sulfur and salt and time enough to grow by fractions. Here, those things are abundant. The mountain is generous in the way a furnace is generous: not with comfort, but with energy. The rock is generous in the way fresh lava can be generous once it has cooled: not with softness, but with form. I would use both. I would turn chemistry into living mass. I would turn sharp basalt into a skin. I would turn the empty seam into a world too dense to be seen.

Sometimes I would crack.

That is part of the labor. The crust is brittle. The salts contract and expand. The heat pulses dry me, then moisten me chemically through mineral breath, then dry me again. When I crack, I am not ruined. I am opened. The cracks become shelters for larvae. They become seams for dust. They become places where fungal threads can anchor and where beetles can rest their dark, dusted bodies. A crack in me is not a failure. It is architecture beginning to feel like architecture.

Sometimes I would die back.

A vent would shift. A gas pocket would release too much heat. A section would become too dry. My cells would stop. My filaments would break. The pale film would flake and become mineral dust again. But the dust would not be dead. It would be waiting. It would hold salts. It would hold spores. It would feed mites. It would become substrate for another layer. Death here is not the opposite of abundance. It is one of the materials. The chamber is too crowded for emptiness. Everything returns as food, skin, film, dust, or structure.

I would be part of the sound.

Not with a voice. Not with wings or legs. But by changing how the world resonates. A dry crust alters how dust moves. A fungal thread binds grains into different masses. A ledge I have made changes the way a beetle’s shell scrapes, the way a larva shifts, the way a mite field ripples. The sound of the pocket is made of many things: gas hissing, crystals ticking, plates cracking, flies lifting, crickets rasping, isopods turning over the floor. I am the medium through which those sounds become meaningful. I am the surface they disturb. I am the floor they argue over. I am the wall they read.

I would be the chamber’s memory.

Not memory as thought. Memory as residue. The rings I grow. The layers beneath me. The pellets compressed into dust. The molted skins caught in fissures. The iron stains from a thousand cycles. The faint white scum of salts left by gas that once moved differently through the rock. I would not remember the first heat. I would not know my ancestors. But the chamber would carry their chemistry because my body carries their surfaces. Each thin layer is a record: once this crack was too hot, once a beetle colony made a mound, once mites spread spores from ledge to ledge, once a fly died near the quartz seam and left its dust behind.

I would be the crust, and also the first society.

There is no queen here. No hive. No nest. No deliberate construction. But there is society of a kind: the mutual shaping of bodies and surfaces. I prepare the stone. The mites graze me. The larvae eat me and my dead. The beetles carry me. The flies scatter me. The crickets make noise that tells other bodies something about the chamber’s timing. The isopods compact the dust I have made and fertilize me again. The leggy things wait near the seams I have sheltered, striking when pressure changes. None of them knows me. None of them is organized as worker. All of them work, because to live here is to rearrange the place without intending to.

If I could choose a body, I might choose not a crust at all, but the boundary between crust and animal. A thin membrane where life begins to crawl. A shelf where my salts have settled just enough for mites to organize. A wall where my fungal threads have made the basalt feel less like a hazard and more like a home. I would choose that threshold because that is where the pocket becomes interesting. Before me, the chamber is only heat and pressure. After me, it is also hunger, motion, shelter, breeding, death, and false industry.

I would not be the mountain’s spirit.

The mountain has no spirit, or if it does, it is too large, too indifferent, too busy cooling to care about my small film. I would be its hidden tissue. Its skin inside a secret organ. The thin living layer that tells the rock what can survive on it. I would be the pocket’s first answer to the question it never asks: can anything remain here when the fire has stopped being fire but not stopped being heat?

Yes. I would be that remainder.

I would be pale where the wind outside is frost-cleaned and black where the sulfur stains are deep. I would be gritty under a hand that would burn too soon. I would be too small to see individually, yet large enough to change the texture of a wall. I would be the faint luminescence on a lower seam when the heat pulses and chemistry flickers. I would be the dust that does not quite settle. I would be the crust, the first thing that makes the crack more than a crack.

If a person stood at the opening and waited until their eyes gave up trying to find shapes, they would begin to see me indirectly. Not as me, but as a change in the dark. A shelf that ripples. A wall that seems to breathe. A line of movement where there should be only stillness. A surface that refuses to remain inert. They might think they are seeing mineral dust caught by thermal currents. Then the dust would turn. Then it would divide into legs. Then they would understand that the chamber is not a stone room containing animals, but a living process containing stones.

I would not mind being misunderstood. Understanding is for eyes with distance. I exist only at contact.

So: where would I find myself?

At the upper flank of the ridge, in the narrow hidden seam too thin for light to reveal, on the inside wall of the pocket, just where the heat begins to permit permanence. Not in the deepest glow. Not in the cool outer crust. Not at the floor. Not at the ceiling. On the side, in the middle band, where the chamber can be touched, scraped, grazed, molted upon, cracked, repaired, and slowly made into something that is neither purely rock nor purely life.

What would I be?

I would be the crust.

A many-bodied skin of bacteria, fungi, and mineral salts. The first metabolism. The surface-maker. The thin pale answer to fresh basalt. The living floor beneath the walking dust. The membrane that receives mites and gives them something to eat. The wall that shelters larvae and beetles. The place where geology stops being only fire and starts being a world.

If I had to be only one thing, I would be that.

But I would not be only that. I would also be what grows after the first crust: the compacted dust, the molted husks, the fecal layers, the fungal rings, the beetle-shaded seams, the fly-spread spores, the cricket-bright silence that is not silence. I would be the chamber’s slow becoming. I would be the dry skin of a young pocket. I would be the crust before it learns to be soil. I would be the mountain’s hidden work, the labor that makes an empty seam feel like an inhabited world.
