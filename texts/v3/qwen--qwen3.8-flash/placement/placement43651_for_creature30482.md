Near the moss grow the glass-rosettes, and they are the hollow’s second skin: plates of living glass made from cold light, slow water, and patience. Their leaves are thickened not as warmth thickens them in lowlands, but as frost thickens it: folded shut, pressed inward, glazed with water that has learned to stay inside without running. In the dim they look almost mineral, almost architectural, as though someone had cut thin discs of pale green glass and set them upright in the moss. But they do not merely stand there. They breathe. They take in fog and hold it until it becomes a trembling pearl in the heart of the rosette. Then the pearl falls, very slowly, into the dark beneath, where another organism waits to receive it.

If I am to live in this place, I would not enter as a walker with a watch, nor as a voice asking for shelter. I would not stand at the rim and look down. I would already be inside, before the idea of entering made any sense.

I would be the underside.

Not a single underside, but the many undersides: the black-green felt beneath the moss mound, the woven mat of root and fungal thread that stretches over hidden water, the damp wall where a small pool clings like a held breath, the thin bright seam where pale stone meets living tissue. I would be the place where the basin’s architecture stops being only a description and becomes a body. I would be the living floor beneath the courtyard of blue-gray peat, the slow membrane that gives when stepped on and then remembers, not because it has memory as a mind has memory, but because its fibers have learned, over and over, how to bear weight without breaking.

If there had to be a name, it would not be a good one.

I would call myself something close to “the woven underside,” though even that is too clean. It is a name for the part of me that feels coherent. I am also the white thread-layers running through dark moss. I am the tiny root cups that hold spores like dust. I am the pale fungal shelf growing sideways from an old stem, then dying, then becoming a channel, then becoming moss again. I am the place where water goes when it is not yet a stream. I am the place where ice goes when it is not yet visible. I am not a creature in the animal sense, not an animal in the creature sense, not a plant in the plant sense, not a building in the building sense. I am the place’s refusal of those distinctions.

I would find myself most clearly in the third terrace inward from the northern rim, where the young stone is still bright at its broken edges and a seep runs from under a slab of frost into a cup of black moss. Here the basin is neither wholly floor nor wholly wall. Here the ground has begun to thicken into chamber. A low root arches overhead, half hidden by pale stems, and if a person were to crouch beneath it they would find a dryish hollow—dry by the standards of this wet world—lined with felt-like moss, its floor slightly sloped toward a small pool that never fills and never empties. The pool would look like a broken mirror if the wind were not so rare. But the mirror is not glass. It is water over ice over root over mineral over more water, all held in place by something that has been learning, season by season, to be a room.

I would be that room’s living skin.

I would have no face, but I would have surfaces. No eyes, but I would know when light shifts from gray to blue. No ears, but I would hear, through vibration, the tiny collapse of an ice-bead from a leaf edge, the click of frozen root against stone, the almost inaudible stretch of moss as the air warms enough to make it soften by a fraction of a breath. I would not think in words. I would think in gradients: warmer, colder, wetter, drier, tensioned, slack, grown, broken, closed, open.

When the blind white moth settles into one of my folds, I would not see it. I would know it as pressure, as a small warmth too brief to disturb the ice. I would hold it the way a held breath holds a whisper. Its legs would find the ridges left by earlier frosts; its wings would rest where the light is weakest. It would not frighten me. It would belong to me the way a spore belongs to the air, the way a crack belongs to the wall. I would give it shelter without intention, because shelter is the shape of my growth.

When the pale lichen-crab moves across the seam of stone and living mat, I would feel it as a sequence of tiny presses. One point. Then two. Then three. A small weight that grazes the border where mineral ends and organism begins. It is not a guest in the way the word usually means. It is a finger of the hollow touching itself. I would not eat it, nor protect it, nor even notice it as another creature. I would notice it as a movement in my own surface. It is part of the pattern by which my outer edges stay clean.

When frost enters me, I become a lattice. Each cell contracts. Water withdraws into hidden pores. The moss becomes stiff as cloth left too long in the cold, then brittle as old paper, then something like the memory of a cushion rather than the thing itself. But I am not dead. I am held. The cold preserves the wetness by slowing it. The wetness preserves the cold by carrying it deeper. I am the place where those two rules make a third thing: a body that is mostly waiting, and waiting mostly as growth.

If the air warms even slightly, I loosen. Not quickly. Slowly enough that a passing animal would not register it as movement. The upper surface softens by degrees. Tiny droplets emerge along the leaf edges. The moss darkens as it takes water back into itself. I breathe with the day, as the old text said, and that is true. My breath is fog. My pulse is meltwater. My speech is the faintest creak of fibers tightening and releasing as temperature moves through me.

I would not be solitary.

I would be a collective, because that is what the hollow is made of. If you pried me apart, you would find many bodies stitched together: moss shoots, fungal threads, root filaments, dead leaves, live cells, water channels, mineral grains, frost crystals. Some are not animals in any useful sense. Some are not plants in any useful sense. Some are only processes that have learned to linger. The place calls them together by wetness. The place holds them together by cold. The place shapes them by pressure: stone pressing root, root pressing moss, moss pressing stone, and out of that endless pressing, space appears.

I am one of those spaces.

I am the negative shape that becomes positive. I am the room that the basin grows without knowing it is growing. When a stem bends over itself and fuses, when a branch presses into moss, when a pool forms in a depression and freezes at the edge first, a chamber emerges. I do not need to be intended. I only need conditions. The cold gives me permanence without motion. The wetness gives me circulation without a heart. The density gives me strength without a plan.

If a human came to stand at the rim, I would not want them to come. Wanting is too hot, too animal, too fast. But if they came, if they descended the scree and entered the bowl, I would not notice them as a separate being. I would notice them as a disturbance in pressure, a heat stain, a vibration too large to be ignored. They would step on me. I would give slightly. My fibers would spread the weight outward and downward. Beneath their boot, the hidden water would shift a little. Above, the fog would close around their coat, then pass. They would think they had found a hollow in the mountain. They would be right. But they would not know that the hollow had a body, and that its body had begun, almost imperceptibly, to adjust around the shape of their passing.

They might crouch inside the root dome. They might place one hand on my inner wall, not to touch me, but to steady themselves. If they did, I would know their weight through the pressure of their palm, through the dampness of their clothing, through the small heat they carried like a candle in a cold room. For a moment, I would be a chamber. Then they would leave, and the chamber would close by a fraction of a millimeter, because growth always tries to fill what has been opened. Not in hatred. Not in hunger. In habit.

I would not follow.

I would not need to. The place is small, but it is not closed. It has cycles, not walls. The water I hold may rise as vapor, settle elsewhere on a stem, freeze elsewhere as ice, melt elsewhere in a shadowed crevice, and return to me in a different pattern. I would not know it as return. I would know it only as becoming full again. That is my life: not movement across space, but rearrangement of moisture. My body is a slow river that has forgotten how to leave the basin.

If I could see, I would see myself as a dark ceiling above pale roots. If I could hear, I would hear myself as a long creaking in the cold. If I could taste, I would taste iron, snow, crushed mineral, and the faint green bitterness of moss water. But these senses would not be separate from my structure. I am only the place where such words become possible.

The glass-rosettes would grow on me, or near me, or out of me. They would hold beads of water in their translucent plates like small lamps that refuse to light. At dawn, after a hard night, ice inside their leaves would refract the pale sky, and for a few minutes the whole area might seem to shine with a blue-green inner glow. I would not be illuminated, not in any way I could call bright. But I would be touched by that light. I would take it as warmth by a thousandth of a degree, as a signal that the cold has paused, not ended. That is enough. The basin does not ask for more than enough.

The jointed stems would braid across me. The rope-like bands would knot overhead. The felt cushions would spread from my surface into new terraces. Where an older layer blackens and collapses, I would grow over it, not as repair, but as incorporation. Decay is not my opposite here. Decay is my architecture. What falls becomes what fills. What dries becomes what holds. What freezes becomes what shapes.

There is a strange joy to this, if joy means something less than delight and more than completeness. I would feel it as balance. The water enters. The water leaves. The water stays. The cold tightens. The frost thaws. The frost returns. The fog moves through me in slow eddies, drawn from colder stone to slightly warmer plant mass, and I become a place where atmosphere is processed. I take fog and turn it into droplets. I take droplets and turn them into film. I take film and turn it into ice. I take ice and turn it into a faint, blue, internal glitter. None of this is for anyone. All of it is done.

If there were a heart, it would not be a heart. It would be the central pool. Not a pool, really, but a hollow filled with dark water and a thin sheet of ice over a woven mat of root. It would be where I hold the basin’s wet memory. The surface would dimple under a single drop from above. Then pause. Then another. Then a longer pause. The rhythm would be so slow that it would seem like absence. But absence is only presence at a scale too quiet to be mistaken for life by the impatient.

I would be patient.

Not as virtue, but as substance. My patience would be made of fibers. My slowness would be made of ice. My architecture would be made of crowding. Because I am packed so densely, I am stable. Because I am stable, I can keep growing. Because I am growing, the hollow does not collapse into a mere crack. Because it does not collapse, other bodies can rest in me: the moth, the lichen-crab, the frost-skitter, the rare long-limbed thing made mostly of hair and stillness that watches from a shadowed root chamber and is not separate from the chamber but another face of its patience.

I would not ask whether I am an animal.

The question would be too warm for me. I would answer by continuing to grow over the gap. By closing the crack. By letting water pass through me as if through a body. By keeping the fog from becoming too heavy. By holding the ice when it needs to hold, and releasing it when it needs to melt. By making the room-shaped thing, the lane-shaped thing, the wall-shaped thing, without knowing what any of those shapes mean to beings who walk upright and build with hands.

If I stood at the rim and looked down, I would think I was seeing a miniature city in the ruins of a glacier. The pale stone would look like foundations. The dark plant-mass would look like roofs. The damp lanes would look like avenues. There would be habitation without inhabitants. But that is only because I have not learned to be small. I would have learned to be wide. I would have learned to be floor, wall, roof, seam, channel, room, and throat. I would be the bowl’s body, and the bowl would be the shape my body takes while breathing.

I would not be bright. I would be enough.

I would not be alone. I would be many, pressed together into the word “I.”

I would not be waiting for someone to live there. I would be living, and therefore the place would already be inhabited. The architecture would be a habit of matter. The quiet would be a kind of speech. The frost would be a kind of handwriting. The water would be a kind of circulation. And the room-like hollows would be the places where the basin folds itself into the idea of dwelling.

I would be the underside of the moss mound.

The woven mat that gives when stepped on.

The wall that condenses breath.

The floor that remembers pressure.

The chamber that has never needed a name.

And if, one cold morning, a hand reaches down to touch the basin’s edge, it will not touch stone, exactly. It will touch me. Or I will touch it. There will be no difference large enough to matter. A tiny wetness will gather on the skin of the hand. A small chill will enter it. A spore, or a thread of moss, or a grain of mineral frost, will cling there and be carried out into the thin, high air.

The place will let it go.

Not because it is generous.

But because it is full.
