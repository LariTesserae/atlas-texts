At first, if I were trying to enter the Sinterwork, I would think of it in the way the people think: with a body large enough to leave a stain. I would make myself one of the mat-readers. I would find myself in a low chamber beneath a sinter arch where the ceiling weeps in silver threads and the water pools in the hollows of a floor that has been scraped too often. My hands would be wet and cracked, the skin white from salt, the nails rimmed with black mat. I would wear an apron of stretched gut and sinew, stiffened by mineral paste, and around my neck I might have one copper pin, because metal is rare here and even a pin can feel like a small miracle of cold work. I would have an obsidian scraper worn to the shape of a thumb, because that is how tools become themselves in the Sinterwork: not by design alone, but by the repetition of use.

I would be a person of the middle terraces. Not a builder of great walls. Not a cutter of basalt. Not one of the old ones who knows the names of every fault in the basin. I would be nearer to the people who clean the channels before they clog with mat, who scrape the black felt from ledges so the water can breathe, who know that a blue film thinned means the water has gone sour and a cream ring means something has died. I would move through the city as if through the inside of a shell, touching walls because there are not enough open places to walk without touch. I would know the sound before the shape: hiss, crack, slap, chime. I would know which cracks are vents and which are merely cooling, which channels are running hot beneath the stone, which ledges are safe to stand on when the crust above them looks dry but the damp is only a finger’s width underneath.

For a while, that would be the answer: I would be a person. A small person. A stain among stains. I would be the one who sits on a ledge already worn into a bowl by the sitting of older bodies, and my own body would deepen that bowl, so that later children would sit in the warm place I had made. I would not be famous. I would not be named except by what I did: rinse the mats, watch the black, carry the fly larvae to the boiling stones, keep the oil from the brine, tell a child not to touch the purple bloom unless it has been touched correctly.

But if I am truly going into the Sinterwork, I cannot stay at that size for long. The place would pull me smaller. It would draw me toward the crevices where its real work is done. The city is not only built by hands. It is built by all the small things that move through it, and if I want to be in the world as it is assembled forever, I think I must become one of its thoughts.

I think I would become a crab.

Not a great crab. Not a beast with a name in story. I would be one of the many. A sinter-crab the size of a clenched thumb. My shell would be the color of boiled bark, edged with white where salt had crept over it. My legs would be thin, hard, jointed, ending in points that could pry into the seams where the city is always being made. I would live between the black mats and the white efflorescence, between the warm vent and the cooler channel, in the crack that is neither one thing nor the other. I would not be a pest, not exactly. I would not be sacred. I would be one of the habits the land keeps returning to.

If I am a crab, I would find myself in a narrow channel near the lower quarter, where the brine is warm enough to make the air taste of egg and hot metal. Above me, a gut membrane stretched over stone ribs would catch dripping water and send it into a basin with a slow, wet note. Below me, the channel wall would be furred with microbial mat, black and greenish-blue, bubbling faintly where a vent breathes through its underside. To my right, a shelf of aragonite teeth still wet would gleam in the dim light. To my left, a broken plate of obsidian, tilted awkwardly, would hold a small pool of steam-distilled mineral water, clear but strange-tasting, and in that pool tiny brine flies would rise and sink and be swept onward.

I would have eyes that are not good at seeing the way a person’s are. They would read movement, heat, the trembling of surfaces, the fractured light through steam. The world would not come to me as one whole image. It would come as many bright fragments: the edge of a channel, the wetness of a crack, the shadow of a hand above, the sudden flash of a fly wing, the blue rim of a mat, the white salt dust that gathers on an old roof. I would taste with the underside of my front legs. I would know danger by contact, not by sight. A mat that burned my leg with bitterness would be avoided. A mat sweet and soft would be food. A pool with the oily rainbow of someone’s breath would mean a person was close. I would click a warning into the stone.

The people would not look at me as if I were small and unimportant. That would be the wrong understanding. They are too close to the Sinterwork for that. They know what the small creatures do. They know that if the channels are not cleaned, the mats will rot; that if the rot is not eaten away, the flies will swarm too thick; that if the flies are not there, some other thing will clog and sour the water. They know the city is held together not only by stone paste and human patience, but by all the living surfaces that bind it. So when they see me moving over a channel lip, scraping at a dark mat, they might pause. They might say, in a low voice, the sound of the water still carrying most of the sentence: let it clean there. Do not scrape that ledge yet. The crab knows the seam better than the chisel.

That would be my place: near the seams.

I would live in the walls of the Sinterwork, but I would not build the walls. I would use them. I would press into the gaps between hand-laid stones and natural ridges, into the places where the builders widened a crack but did not finish it, where the crust still grows around their work. I would make my home in the dark under a salted shelf where the water has seeped out and left a skin of mineral behind. There, the temperature would be steady, the air wet, the stone alive with thin threads of fungal growth. I would leave that shelf only when the pools warm too much, or when the mat near it begins to turn too black, or when a person walks too fast and shakes the terrace. I would know the difference between a footfall and a vent-cough. I would know which vibrations mean a pot has been set down, which mean a scraper is being sharpened, which mean a child is running along a path where they were told not to run.

I would eat what the city eats in a city-like way. Decaying mat. Dead larvae. Flecks of salt-crusted skin. The soft rinds of tiny fruiting bodies that grow in crevices like pale warts. The remains of brine flies trapped in a channel’s edge. Once, I might eat a splinter of human meal left behind: a smear of boiled fly paste on a stone tray, dried by the warm air and re-wetted by a drip. That would not be waste to me. Nothing in the Sinterwork is waste. Everything is waiting for the right small mouth.

I would click. Not as speech, exactly, but as proof of presence. In the Sinterwork, a click is not noise. It is a way of putting your body into the water and saying: I am here. I am moving. The wall is not empty. The crack is not dead.

Sometimes the clicking would be answered. Other crabs in nearby holes would answer. A fish under a low shelf would snap at the disturbance. A brine fly would lift off a pool in a sudden silver thread. Somewhere above, a child would stop scraping and listen, not because the sound meant anything specific, but because the Sinterwork is full of small voices and the children learn to sort them before they learn to sort words. A child would say: the wall is clicking too much today. An elder would answer: then the seam is moving. Keep the channel clear. Do not let the black pile up.

I would be one of the things that helps them know when the seam is moving.

This is what emerges, then. I would not be a grand creature in the Sinterwork. I would not be a person named in the same breath as the old mat-readers or the shell-cutters or the ones who know which blue can be eaten. I would be smaller than that. I would be a crab with salt in its shell, living between the human city and the mineral ground, cleaning the spaces no hand is willing to fit into. But I would not be insignificant. The Sinterwork is an assembly of ten thousand small mouths. Hiss. Crack. Slap. Chime. I would be one of the crackles.

And yet, because this world is assembled in the same posture forever, I think even this being would not be wholly fixed. A crab here might not be only a crab. It might be a shape the basin uses to keep one part of itself moving. I might be a small mind in the wall, but the wall is part of the city, the city is part of the basin, the basin is part of the slow wet work of the world. My shell might grow into a place where another crust forms. My burrow might become a niche someone widens with a chisel. My clicking might become the reason a builder says: this crack should be kept open; the small thing there knows the channel better than we do.

In that way, I would remain partly a person and partly a creature, not because I had chosen between them, but because the Sinterwork does not make clean divisions. It deposits things on one another. It grows over them. It makes habits until habits look like places.

I would be a sinter-crab, yes.

But if you asked where I would be found, I would say: not in the open plaza, not in the deepest vent, not in the high terraces where the birds nest. I would be in the narrow seam between the cooking ledge and the salt channel, where the stone has been warmed so many times that its color has deepened to rust, where the dripping has made a tiny bowl, where a black mat thickens in the shade and thins where the heat passes through. I would be there because the water is close, because the fly larvae drift through that seam, because the human stain protects the edge from too much sun and too much dry wind. I would be there because that is where the city and the mineral ground are still negotiating with one another.

I would be there because my back is the shape of the gap. My shell is the shape of the shelf. My legs are the shape of the crack. The Sinterwork is assembled in one posture forever, and if I am in it, my posture would be this: low, wet, listening, clicking, moving one limb at a time over the salt-crusted lip of a channel, tasting the world with my feet, knowing it by the vibration of the stone.

I would be small enough to be overlooked.

I would be necessary enough to be missed when I were gone.
