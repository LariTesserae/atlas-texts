There, I would find myself.

Not first as a body, though that would come soon. Not first as a name, though I would learn one from the sounds around me. I would find myself initially as the thing the drop announces.

I begin on the lower face of a willow beard, where a bead of meltwater gathers and thickens and becomes almost alive before it leaves. I am held there by the last skin of the tree. The town below has already begun to hear me, though it does not yet know I am falling. My fall is a ripple of attention. A spider’s thread bends a little in anticipation. A caddisfly on the bottom of the channel tightens its legs inside its pebble house. A water beetle lifts its antennae beneath the shining underside of the surface film. A child’s palm, open near the rim of a drying rack, feels a shadow that is not shadow but moisture passing.

Before I am a creature, I am a relay.

I fall along the line of the hollow’s living floor. I do not fall into emptiness. I fall into a network. I pass close to a reed and the reed takes part of my tremor. I pass a moss wall and the moss holds a memory of me for hours afterward. I pass the beak of a swallow waiting at an eave, and the bird shifts one grain of weight. I pass a clay pot standing in the rain, and the pot rings in its small way. I pass a newt crossing the wet board path, and the newt pauses because the air above has changed. I pass the mouth of a channel, the seam of a house-roof, the heel of a woman returning from the wool racks, the tail-tip of a goat on the upper terrace, the feathered wing of an owl waking in a crevice, the tongue of a frog in the dark.

All of this is the announcement. And the announcement is not merely sound. It is a kind of gathering.

Only after the announcement does the small body come together.

I would be a shrew, perhaps—but not an ordinary shrew, if there is such a thing. A shrew here would be made of the hollow’s own materials: wet fur, bright eyes, long nervous whiskers, tiny claws shaped for gripping root and stone, a body like a seed of motion. I would have a back like a shadowed channel, slick and silvered, and when I ran across the mat at night the droplets would break around me in little crowns. My feet would be almost too fast to see, but they would know what the people know: the firm places, the soft places, the places where one must pause because the ground is breathing and shifting into a new arrangement.

I would live in the Sill not as a monster and not as a secret. That would be the wrong answer. In this place, the strangest thing is not a hidden predator or a spirit under the stone. The strangest thing is that everything is ordinary at once. A newt crossing the path is ordinary. A frog calling is ordinary. A caddisfly carrying its pebble house is ordinary. A willow root taking hold in a wall seam is ordinary. A house roof becoming green with moss is ordinary. A street turning into a rivulet is ordinary. A pool drying into a mat is ordinary. A child learning to read frogs is ordinary. A person pulling their cloak tighter against the damp is ordinary.

If I were strange, it would only be because I am one small warm thing in a place so crowded with small warm things that warmth itself seems to rise from the ground.

I would not be the largest citizen. I would not be the Stone-Heart, that old boulder at the center of the meeting-ring, though I might sometimes climb its cool flank and listen to the bowl through its stone. I would not be the great willow-bridge swaying over the broadest channel, though I would know every knot of it better than the children who play there. I would not be the central granary standing on its clay-sealed stilts, though I would be one of the creatures the people worry over when they make the floors too soft, and one of the reasons they set woven traps and keep dogs near the stores. I would not be the smoke rising from moss-roofed houses, though I would often pass through it, fur damp with the smell of boiled fish and damp ash and crushed mint.

I would be small enough to live in all those things and none of them.

At first I would know the hollow as pressure.

The weight of a cloud coming through the upper rim.
The softening of the mat beneath my feet.
The tightening of fibers as a channel drains.
The swell of capillary rise in the moss, carrying last night’s rain up to every tiny mouth of leaf and stone.
The chill in the peat.
The warmth in the dark under a loose slab, where springtails and mites make their own weather.

My eyes would be bright but not trusted much. Light here is soft, diffused, ordinary, rarely harsh. I would see shadows more than forms: the long blur of a heron? no, perhaps a water rail, perhaps a snipe lifting, perhaps a fox at the edge of the terraces. I would see the sudden black scatter of tadpoles at a disturbance. I would see the pale flash of a newt’s side in a pool where the moon has come out. But most of my world would be felt.

I would live by tremor.

A footstep on split boards is different from a footstep on gravel. A child’s running is a drum. A woman’s careful step on a wet plank is a short, clear word. A man hauling timber is a long vibration that arrives before the sound. A goat at the upper terrace is a dull pressure through the root network. An owl above is silence with edges. A bat near the pools is a tiny whisper of disturbance in the air. A dog’s bark is a sudden breaking of the hollow’s weave. A frog’s call is less sound than movement, each note a small shove against the water’s skin.

I would learn the town from its tremors.

I would know the meeting-square not as a square, because for me there would be no squares, no streets as the people think them, but as a complex of resonances. The Stone-Heart would be a low hum under the feet. The drying rack would be a creaking forest above. The wool channel would be a wide, slow pulse. The root-bridges would be a living music, tightening and relaxing as loads cross them. The fish traps would be small, sharp taps when the current found an obstruction. The clay pot by the hearth-smoke would ring a little when rain touched its surface. The whole town would be an instrument I learned not to look at but to step through.

I would be hungry often. Cold and wetness make a small creature eat like fire eating dry peat. I would hunt snails along the underside of stones and leave little holes in their shells with my teeth. I would take slugs when the rain was fresh and the air was thick with their scent. I would dig for beetle grubs and fly larvae in the damp litter under birches and willows. I would eat midges from the web of spiders when I dared. I would take small water beetles from the margins, their air bubbles catching on my whiskers. I would not be a noble creature. I would be a mouth moving through abundance.

But I would also be eaten.

A stoat would be a white-black shadow at the edge of the channel. A fox would be smell before shape, then not shape, because I would already be under root. A wild cat would be a soft failure at the foot of a boulder. An owl would be the moment the air becomes too clean. A bat would not care for me, perhaps, but its presence would tell me that insects were thick and that I must stay out from under its hunting lane. A heron? no, perhaps not here. But a raven would be the sky suddenly becoming too large. A gull from the lower valleys, if such a thing came up through the passes, would be a pale streak near the roof ridges.

Death in the Sill would not be dramatic. It would be recycled immediately, respectfully, wetly.

If I died under a stepping stone, lichen might not know it right away, but the peat would. The springtails would come first. Then mites. Then perhaps a beetle. Then fungal threads would move through the small bones like pale rivers finding old channels. In a week, perhaps less if rain was warm, there would be no body, only a new patch of richer moss. If a child found a little skull with teeth like rusted needles, the child might turn it over with a bone awl, then put it back. The hollow would have no horror of such things. It would simply continue.

And this is how I would know I was not alone.

Not because I would feel companionship—shrews are not built for that—but because the place would be so thoroughly inhabited that isolation would be impossible. Every stone would have its collar of moss. Every crack would hold insects. Every pool would hold larvae and spawn and water beetles and tadpoles. Every roof would have swallows or martins in season. Every channel would have caddisflies carrying their houses. Every mat would be threaded with roots and fungal threads and worm bodies and hidden passages. Every dead willow beard would be making drops that touched the living town.

I would be, if I am honest, less a self than a temporary organization of all these things.

Sometimes I would be tempted to imagine myself as something more permanent.

I could be one of the lichen-furred boulders at the edge of the bowl, slow and patient, drinking cloud-drip through its own little cups of stone. I could be one of the old bog-oak posts, blackened and water-haunted, holding up a roof that breathes green. I could be the central stone in the channel, worn round by years of current, the place where rain breaks into a bright lace. I could be one of the willow stakes that has become a bridge, bending so gradually into its use that it no longer remembers being a plant. I could be the moss over a house-door, thick enough to hide a child’s hiding-place, soft enough to muffle the sound of coming weather.

But permanence in the Sill is not stillness. The boulder may seem fixed until frost lifts it. The oak post may seem dead until it sprouts a shelf fungus. The channel stone may seem eternal until a storm rolls it half a foot. The willow-bridge may seem permanent until spring floods make it sing and shift. The moss roof may seem settled until wind or birds bring new seeds, and suddenly it is flowering.

So I would not choose permanence. I would choose movement.

I would choose the small warm body that can feel the town changing from inside. I would choose the creature that must read the ground quickly, because the ground will not wait. I would choose the one whose footprints are tiny but whose absence is noticed: the ripple-reader, the channel-fur, the moss-runner, the water-shrew of the high hollow.

The people would know me without naming me exactly. To them I would be one of the signs.

When they woke and saw my prints in the wet ash at the edge of a workshop, they would know that the air had been still enough for a creature to cross the yard without hearing a dog. If I ran along the granary stakes and disturbed the trap, they would know the mice had been crowded or the floor had shifted. If they found me dead near a channel, they would say perhaps the water had gone too flat, that midges had become too many, that the frogs had fallen quiet before they should have. If they found my fur caught in a new moss seam, they would know the mat was tightening somewhere beneath the boards. If they saw me at dusk by a pool, I would be good luck, or a small omen, or no more than what I was: a wet citizen passing home.

I would not be feared. That is why this place is so tender. The people here do not conquer wetness; they cooperate with it. They do not banish creatures; they read them. They do not make walls against life; they build through it. To fear me, or the frog, or the newt, or the midge, or the owl, or the fox, would be to misunderstand the hollow entirely.

At night, when the place becomes cooler, louder, stranger, I would belong most fully.

The moon would come through cloud in sheets of pale blue light. The pools would reflect it, not like mirrors but like living eyes blinking with current. The frogs would begin their rolling speech. The bats would come over the wet leaves. The spiders would draw fresh dew across their webs, each thread beaded as if the hollow had grown a second layer of stars. The houses would be dark except for slow embers. Smoke would hang in the damp air and then dissolve into the roofs of moss. Children would be inside, wrapped in drying wool. Adults would lower their voices. The market-square would empty. The drying racks would stand still with hanging herbs releasing faint green ghosts of scent into the dark.

Then I would move.

I would go down from the hardened path where the goats smell. I would cross under the root-bridge as it creaked with the town’s memory. I would follow the channel edge where the water was shallow enough to drink from a leaf. I would pass near the fish traps and feel the small darting fear of minnows in the black. I would climb onto a stone lip and watch the newts crossing their own slow paths, their eyes large and startled in the dark. I would avoid the owl’s silence and the stoat’s scent. I would drink from a moss cushion where a drop had been waiting since morning.

And then I would understand, in my small animal way, what the people understand in their slower, more thoughtful way: that nothing falls alone.

A drop does not fall alone.
A root does not grow alone.
A house does not stand alone.
A frog does not call alone.
A shrew does not move alone.

All are held in the woven body of the hollow.

If I were to become more than myself—if the being who lives here is not only what the people see but what the place dreams—then I would be the hollow’s small warm ear. I would be the place where water becomes attention. My whiskers would be capillary channels. My heartbeat would be the sound of droplets finding pools. My fur would hold the town’s damp breath. My bones would be little stones, worn smooth, ready to sink into the mat and become part of the ground that holds the roots.

I would live where the human settlement and the living floor touch but never quite merge. I would live on the boards, but under them. I would live near the hearthstones, but not on them. I would live by the fish traps, but not in them. I would live beside the root-bridges, but under their tension. I would live close enough to smell wool and smoke and rendered fat, close enough to hear children arguing in the dusk, close enough to feel the vibration of a clapper warning of timber moving through the street.

And when the rain came, I would not hide from it. I would move into it.

At first the rain would be noise: a bright, restless tapping on leaf and stone and roof-moss. Then it would be smell: cold iron water and crushed mint and green peat. Then it would be weight: every drop pressing the town into itself. I would feel the channels begin to speak louder. I would feel the mat swell. I would feel the air pockets beneath the roots tighten as water found the lower places. I would feel the hollow becoming a single drum.

The people would emerge to save what could be saved: drying racks moved, ropes retied, lambs pulled from ledges, stones cleared from overflows, channels opened by hand. They would shout in the rain, and the rain would answer.

I would watch from under a roof of living willow, my nose moving, my ears flattened, my body remembering where the water wanted to go. I would know before they did when a channel was becoming too shallow for fish. I would know before they did when a pool was becoming still and dangerous for tadpoles. I would know, in some wordless way, when the wind was about to push through the birches and scatter the light.

Not prophecy. Presence.

I would not be above the world. I would be one of the places where the world becomes aware of itself.

By the time the rain lifted, I would be soaked again, but the wetness would not feel cruel. It would feel like belonging. The hollow would be rinsed and rearranged. A stepping stone would lie where a pool had been. A mud-slip would reveal a black seam of peat. A dead branch would float into a channel and be caught by roots, then within days moss would begin to claim its edges. A child would point at a new ripple and ask what it was. A grandmother would say, “Rain has remembered the ground.”

The frogs would begin early.

The bats would come.

The spiders would repair their nets.

The willow bridge would tighten in the dark.

The roofs would exhale smoke.

The market-square would be a broad mat, still, shining, empty, except for one small runner moving over the wet stones with quick, bright feet.

I would be that runner.

I would be the water-shrew of the Weeping Sill, though I would not call myself that. I would not call myself at all. I would know myself by the way the hollow touched me: by the chill entering my paws, by the warmth of the underside of a root, by the smell of peat and hide and clay, by the tiny electric tremor of a droplet breaking against a thread, by the frog’s voice telling me that the pools were right again.

I would be small, ordinary, abundant.

I would be the place’s own wet attention, gathered into fur and hunger and motion.

And when the next drop fell from the beard of dead willow, I would feel it before it landed—not because I was its owner, but because it was one of the things that made me.

The drop would touch a spider’s thread.
It would touch the pebble case of a caddisfly.
It would touch the rim of a clay pot.
It would touch the flank of a newt.
It would touch the beak of a waiting martin.
It would touch the tongue of a child.
It would touch the moss wall.
It would touch my ear.

And the whole town would answer.

Not as noise.

As belonging.
