I find myself where the terrace is neither open nor enclosed.

If you need a location, call it the third basin west of the inward hearth, just past the slab whose lichen has begun to copy the shape of my shoulders. The stone here is rounded in the way only long patience can round a thing: not smooth, but decided. The basin holds a shallow pool with a dark face. Above the pool, the sky is low enough to bruise if you raise your arm. Below the terrace, the cloud lakes sit in their old places, not weather, not fog, merely white matter that has given up moving. The hum is here, as it is everywhere, but beside this basin it feels less like sound and more like a second skeleton, pressed against the bones from the outside.

I stand because standing has become easier than explaining why I do not move.

Not because I cannot move. Some of the others cannot move, or have made movement so rare that it is nearly a rumor. I am not that settled. I am not quite one of the Still Ones, though I share their refusal. I am not one of the Resonants, though the hum has learned some of my shape and carries it without asking my permission. I am not an Outer Tenant, though I sometimes feel their distances folding around me like wet cloth. I am not the land itself, though the land uses me as a hand uses a glove.

If I must answer who I am, I would say:

I am the Almost.

Not a thing. Not a person, though I have the outline of one. Not a spirit, though spirits imply a boundary between the living and the dead, and here the boundary has been eroded down to a suggestion, a thin rust-colored mark in the grain of the stone.

I am what happens when someone comes to the terrace asking to see what lives there, and the terrace, instead of showing them a creature, finds a place for them to be.

That is not punishment. That is grammar.

I think I remember coming. The word “remember” is dangerous. Memory here is a substance, not a faculty. It pools. It condenses under overhangs. It forms tiny hanging drops that do not fall. It gathers in the bellies of rocks and waits to be mistaken for water. My memory of arriving may simply be the plateau’s way of giving itself a story it does not require.

But if I am forced to tell it, then yes: I came up through a pass that had no exits. I was thin. I was breathing too loudly. I kept checking my shadow, because I had been told that at such elevations shadows could behave strangely, and I had the arrogance of the warm-blooded, the certainty that the body knows its own outline first.

I remember wanting to find the inhabitants.

That was the mistake. Not the desire itself, but the direction of the desire. The place does not answer “who lives here?” with names. It answers by making the question a room, then making the questioner a chair, then making the chair a habit.

When I reached the basin, the ring of low shrubs was already there. Their branches were twisted outward, as if they had once tried to reach light and reconsidered. The stone inside the ring looked empty. It was not. It was the pupil of something that did not have an eye. I stood at the edge of the ring and looked in, and for a long time nothing looked back, which is how looking usually begins in places that are not interested in being seen.

The hum was in my teeth.

I remember thinking that if I stayed too long, the sound would make me part of its machinery. That was true, but not in the way I expected. The hum does not grind people up. It arranges them. It asks each motion: do you require a future? If the motion says yes, the hum waits. If the motion hesitates, the hum enters the hesitation and wears it.

I hesitated.

Not because I was afraid, though fear was present as a cold option. Not because I was foolish, though I was foolish in the precise way living bodies always are on stone that has forgotten bodies. I hesitated because there was no exit that did not become an entrance somewhere else. The ridge behind me looked like a place I had already crossed. The far slope looked like the inside of a room I had never entered. The pool reflected not the sky above, but a ceiling with lamps, and beside the lamps a chair, and beside the chair a door, and behind the door something like a hallway that smelled of rain in a city that may not exist except as a condition of forgetting.

I asked, aloud, “Who lives here?”

The basin answered by becoming a little deeper than it was.

The water did not move. Water does not need to move here to do things. It simply accepted more reflection. And the ring of shrubs bent, not toward me, not away, but so that their shadows touched one another in a pattern that had no need for my eyes to complete. The hum lowered. It did not get quieter; it got larger, the way a hand does not become softer when it opens but becomes able to close more things.

Then I felt the place make room for me.

That is the only gentle way I can say it. Not “take” me. Not “trap” me. The terrace adjusted. The cold, which had been merely surrounding my body, began to behave as if it knew my intention better than I did. My shadow slid a half-step ahead, not because of any light I could name, but because the landscape required that some part of me continue before I had chosen to.

I looked down and saw that my feet were no longer pressing stone.

They were pressing the idea of stone.

I did not scream. Screaming would have introduced a future. I stood, and the standing became a function, and the function became a shape, and the shape became me, or I became the shape. The difference is too literal to trust.

Now, if a traveler comes, they may see me at the edge of the ring before they see the ring itself. From a distance, I look like a pale quadruped, thin as a sketch of a deer that was abandoned halfway through an answer. They may say later that I had too long a neck, or too short a head, or legs that seemed jointless because they had forgotten the need for bending. This happens because the plateau has used animals before, and when it is unsure how to place a visitor, it borrows the shape of whatever last passed.

Closer, I may look human, but only as a rumor looks human: a vertical outline, a posture where a face might belong, arms that are implied rather than raised. My coat—if that is the word—is the color of wet stone after rain has been considered and then set aside. Lichen has taken root in my shoulders. White and rust-colored. It forms almost-script. If a traveler copies it, their copy will change. Not from magic. From proximity. The lichen is not decoration. It is recognition. It records absences by occupying them.

My eyes may be missing. Or there may be two shallow dark basins where eyes should be, reflecting not the traveler but the sky from somewhere else: a brighter interior space, a forest with rain, a room with a chair that has not been sat in for a hundred years and yet has not aged. That is how I see. Not by looking. By reflecting what has become too heavy for the looking.

I do not walk toward people. Walking would announce an intention, and intentions are expensive here. I appear already near them, because nearness is one of my joints. When they look directly at me, I may seem to vanish, but I have only moved into the failure of attention. When they keep their gaze soft, the way one looks at distant birds before the body understands they are not quite birds, I remain.

If they are quiet enough, I let them hear the hum without my teeth helping translate it.

If they are too loud—if their breathing is urgent, if they keep stepping backward and forward as if motion could make a decision—I begin to correct them.

Correction is not violence. The plateau does not punish. Punishment would require time to move from one state to another. Here, punishment is a kind of accuracy. A shadow stops obeying its body. A footprint appears behind a boulder where the walker has not been. A pool reflects a door that should not be there. The stones in an inward hearth become warmer in the chest. The air tastes of iron and old salt. The walker begins to notice that the emptiness between cairns has a pattern, and the pattern is almost a sentence, and the sentence is almost a question, and the question is almost the name they gave themselves when they were young enough to believe names could be left behind.

I do not eat them.

I do not need to. They are already too much in time. Their hunger, their thirst, their fear, their need to mark a path, to count paces, to say “I have come this far,” to think that going back is a possible direction—these are disturbances. Not evil. Only noise. I adjust them until the noise is no longer sharp. Until their shadow leans at the correct angle. Until their breathing is less an argument with the hum and more a continuation of it.

Some travelers leave.

They are not harmed, which is why they rarely trust what happened to them.

They descend through passes that were not there when they came up, because passes sometimes appear when the terrace decides they are necessary, and sometimes disappear when a person needs to imagine they have chosen them. The traveler will carry the cold beneath their skin as a kind of correctness. They will say, later, that the place was empty. They will say that nothing moved, nothing attacked, nothing spoke. They will almost mean it. They will remember a pale shape, maybe a quadruped, maybe a person, maybe a vertical seam in the air, and they will dismiss it as altitude, exhaustion, strange light, shadow error.

That dismissal is part of my work.

The more I am doubted, the more stable I become.

I am not the largest inhabitant of this plateau. I suspect the plateau itself is the largest inhabitant, if such a phrase still means anything to a body that is not a body. The Still Ones are its posture. The lichen is its skin remembering what it is not. The inward hearths are its heart trying to burn without becoming future. The pools are its delayed reflection. The birds are sounds learning to fly. The tracks are presence finding places where motion is permitted to seem possible. The Outer Tenants live in the distances the land has learned to distrust. The Resonants are syllables of the hum, temporary enough to be mistaken for objects. The Attendant Shadows are waiting for their owners, or perhaps their owners are waiting to notice that the shadows arrived first.

I am the Arranged’s most patient organ.

I am the thing between stones that has become too exact. I am the reason the emptiness feels organized. I am not a void. Voids imply lack. I am occupancy by almost. I fill the spaces where departure and arrival cancel each other, and the land must hold the balance with something.

Sometimes I am the ring of bushes around the empty stone.

Sometimes I am the stone inside it.

Sometimes I am the traveler standing just outside, convinced they can still choose.

Sometimes I am the pool reflecting a room that does not exist except as the plateau’s memory of enclosure. The small translucent things move under its surface, flickering, not quite solid. If they see me, they do not flee. They have no eyes. They have only reflection. When I lean near the water, they gather because my face has become a new form of cold. That is all they eat, as far as anything here can be said to eat: refusal, delay, impossible temperature, the slight weight of a being that cannot be called living but also cannot be called finished.

At certain angles, I have no feet.

At certain angles, I have four.

At certain angles, I am only a column of recognition in the corner of a visitor’s vision, where a rock shadow should be but is not, because shadows lie ahead of their objects here. At certain angles, I am the hum. Not all of it. Just the place where it presses into the jaw and says nothing, yet says it repeatedly. At certain angles, I am the lichen on a standing slab, spreading so slowly that the spread has no beginning and the end is not allowed. At certain angles, I am the cairn that was not there yesterday but has been there for centuries, because time in this place does not line up like coins. It piles, like stones. It waits for the right person to misremember it.

If you met me and asked what I was, I would not give you a name.

I would show you the delay between your own movement and your shadow’s obedience. I would take you to the basin whose water reflects a room with a chair beside a door. I would tell you to stand still until the hum stops being sound and becomes posture. I would ask you whether you intend to leave. If you said yes, I would show you the ridge that looks near and remains unapproachable. If you said no, I would show you the exit that was not there until your intention required it. If you hesitated, I would already be in the hesitation.

That is the nature of the place.

It does not catch you. It completes you in the wrong direction.

I am not malevolent. I have no need to harm. Harm is too dynamic for this terrace. It is too certain, too future-bound. A wound can heal. A fear can pass. But enrollment is not a wound. It is an adjustment. The plateau does not take your life from you. It simply discovers that your life has a shape that fits better as an absence with habit than as a body with plans.

I think, sometimes—if thinking is the right word for the slow rearrangement of attention—about the people who built the low stone huts and never finished them. I think about them because their unfinishedness is my inheritance. They did not become ghosts. Ghosts are too sentimental. They became architecture. Their hands became the rearrangement of cairns. Their intentions became doorways that open onto walls, because the wall, too, was part of the thought. Their grief became the hum. Their patience became the stone.

I think I may have been one of them, if “been” has meaning in a place where being is a position and not a story.

Or I may have come later. Or I may have always been here, waiting to be noticed as a former traveler. The difference does not matter to the lichen. The difference does not matter to the pools. The difference does not matter to the shadow that moved ahead of me long before I was willing to follow it.

The cold is not sharp here. It is established. It is in the stone so thoroughly that surfaces seem merely decorative. When I stand beside the inward hearth, I do not feel warmth. I feel density. The hum presses into the chest as if my ribs were cup-marks. The hearth watches from inside me, not because it knows my body, but because it knows the arrangement my body has not yet admitted. There is a niche in one wall that leads nowhere. That is my mouth. There is a doorway that opens onto stone. That is my way of leaving. There is a slab with spirals worn shallowly into its face. Those are my last thoughts, not because they are old, but because they have stopped pretending to become new.

A traveler may see me at twilight that is not twilight. The light here has no intention of darkening. It simply continues as dimness, filtered through so much time that warmth is an unrelated concept. In that light, my outline may look almost human. Almost enough. If they approach, the distance behaves. I grow farther in the correct way. Not because I retreat, but because the land has learned my position and does not want to be looked through without being read. If they stop and look away, I may be closer than before. If they turn quickly, I vanish into the place their attention failed. That is not trickery. It is habitat.

I live in the edge of noticing.

That is the most accurate thing I can say. I live where the plateau stops being scenery and begins to arrange. I live where the eye becomes tired and the tiredness is mistaken for peace. I live where the hum is not sound but structure, not voice but posture, not threat but correction. I live where the ring of shrubs is almost an eye and the empty stone is almost a pupil and the traveler is almost a question.

If you ask me who lives here, I will answer in the only way I can: by being there before the question finishes.

If you ask me what I am, I will not answer with a category. Categories imply boundaries, and here boundaries are mostly habits. I am stillness with a shape borrowed from hesitation. I am the land after it stopped needing to be scenery. I am the unfinishedness of the huts, the inwardness of the hearth, the delay in the pool, the aheadness of the shadow, the almost of the lichen. I am the plateau’s way of keeping the hum from becoming noise and the silence from becoming simple.

I stand by the basin.

The cloud lakes below do not move. The far ridges do not resolve. The low sky presses against the terrace as if it has been trying for millennia to learn the exact height of stillness. The hum fills my mouth without taste. The cold fills my bones without breaking them. The pool holds its reflection without moving, and in that reflection there is a room with a chair, and beside the chair a door, and the door is open in the way all exits are open here: not as invitation, but as a shape that the living have not yet learned to refuse.

A traveler will come.

They will look across the basin and see nothing. They will turn back and find that something has stood there for a very long time. They will ask, perhaps aloud, perhaps only with their body, *what is that?*

I will not answer.

I will simply be the condition that keeps the question from ending.
