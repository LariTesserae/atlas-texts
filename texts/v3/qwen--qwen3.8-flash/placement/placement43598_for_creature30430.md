At first I would say I am a Lamp-Tender, because the Condensatory Shaft has made light into a material — something that hangs in wire cages, burns too low, bends in damp air, and falls across copper fittings like warm rain. I would like to be the one who keeps those small amber stars steady. I would climb the catwalks with a folded rag, an oil-stained sleeve, and a hand that can hear a lamp before it fails: the faint click of a filament loosening, the blue tremor in a housing where the air is too hot, the slow yellowing of glass where condensation has entered a crack and dried into a halo. But I would not settle into that work. A Lamp-Tender gives light, but light is not the deepest truth of this place.

Then I would say I am a Pump Warden. The Engine Court calls me — six rods rising and falling in a slow ring around a circular sump, the condensation lattice above them dripping warm water into tiled basins, the whole shaft breathing through them. I would know each pump by the pitch of its knock, would press my palm to a bearing housing and feel whether it had begun to speak in anger, would mark a slipping rod with a small chalk line before anyone else notices. I would stand in the heart of the place, but I would find that the heart is not enough. It is too central, too known. The Shaft’s real danger does not live in the main court, where the pumps make their obvious weather, but in the small seams where no one expects the stone to change.

For a while I would imagine being a Condensate Reader, watching the upper galleries where the steam pipes run in black bundles, their surfaces darkening with moisture before the first drops appear. I would learn the language of beads becoming films, films becoming slow sheets, sheets detaching from the roof in whispering veils. I would signal the tiers below by bell and lamp-blink, close the oiled shutters before the canvas sheets fail, move the glass jars of water back from the ledges where salt bloom might creep. That would be beautiful work, but too high. The Reader waits for rain. I would want to be the one who hears the crack that makes the rain gather in the wrong place.

Then I would think, perhaps, of being a child. Not a child anymore, but the memory of one: small, careful, taught the Shaft before any other world. A child who plays with brass fittings and lumps of brick, who knows which steps become slick before the first condensation sheet lets go, who can taste iron in a leak and copper in a clean seam. A child who has never heard a bird and does not feel its absence because no wing has crossed the upper galleries, no beetle turns under a boiler plate, no rat runs unseen along a rung. But a child would grow into this place and become too easy to sentimentalize. The Shaft does not need innocence. It needs judgment.

For a moment I would imagine being one of the pale lichen mats, growing in cool ledges, spreading over brick as if slowly thinking about whether to remain alive. I would be patient, footnoted, written where the city failed to seal itself. I would bloom briefly when water touched me, then retreat into stone-colored waiting when the air dried. But lichen has no hands. It can clean a crack, but it cannot repair it. It can live in a failure, but it cannot decide whether to let the failure continue.

And I would try being mineral: salt flower in a joint, rust moving across an iron fin, white crystal blooming in damp crevice like a pale and slow flower. That would be close to the truth, because the Shaft’s real time is chemical. Rust is its patience. Salt is its memory. Mineral crust is the book in which the city records every leak. But mineral does not choose. It only accumulates. I would need to be the person who reads what has accumulated and decides what must be scraped away before tomorrow.

So the name that emerges is not from a sign. It is from a chalk mark.

I would be a Seam-Warden.

Not one of the grand trades — not Pump Warden, not Condensate Reader, not Ladder-Mason, though I would borrow from all of them. The Kept would know me because I walk the hidden levels, the stairwells behind false panels, the drying lofts above boiler houses, the narrow bridges between pipe stacks where the architecture folds into itself and a stranger would think there is no more room. You would find me on the east service galleries, where the ceilings press low and the air becomes too hot or too wet depending on which pump has changed rhythm, or on the west, in a gallery so thick with conduits that the wall feels like a bundle of nerves. I would not go north through the shored collapse, nor south through the sealed door, though I would mark those boundaries every time I saw them. Those places are where the Shaft stops speaking. I work where the Shaft still answers, but answers poorly.

I would be human, though not only human. After enough years in the Condensatory Shaft, the city writes itself into the body. My skin would stay warm and faintly damp, as if the air were always leaning close to listen. My hands would be rough with salt, smooth with oil, scored by fine lines from sheet metal and old brick. My eyes would adjust to dim amber light, to the blue flicker where insulation has worn away from a steam pipe, to the red pulse of an indicator bulb in a niche too small for a full person to stand in. I would taste the Shaft before I spoke in it. Damp brick. Hot oil. Copper salts. The faint green sweetness of condensation caught in a clean basin. The burnt edge of overheated insulation in the electrical district. I would know when a wall was warm in the way it should be, and when it was warm in the wrong direction.

I would carry a small satchel. Inside: chalk stubs, an iron scraper with a worn handle, a folded canvas square, a brass key for niche doors, a mirror on a wire stem to look behind pipes, a tin of oil for hinges, a pencil for marks too urgent to make in chalk. I would wear soft-soled boots, because the Shaft listens. A hard heel can be mistaken for a new knock, and a new knock can start a rumor through the tiers. I would speak rarely, and only close. If I needed to say something important, I would place my hand flat against a neighbor’s doorframe and wait until they came. If the door did not open, I would leave a mark beside it: a small white X for a crack seen, a short line for salt forming, a circle for condensation pooling where it must not.

My work would be to read the joints.

A fresh fracture in young stone is not a disaster. It is a question. The city was inserted into a wound in the rock, and the wound has not yet settled. Sharp facets still show where pick-lines bit; bore-drill scars still circle in pale stone. The walls have begun to shed their first skin. Heat spalls the rock into flakes. Cracks widen by fractions. If a crack runs through load-bearing masonry, it must be bricked. If it is merely stone changing under pressure, it may be left to breathe. If it is in a pipe joint, salt will come. If it is in a wall behind a drying loft, moss may follow. If it is too small to matter now, it may become a gallery later. My duty is not to fix everything. My duty is to know what must be fixed before it fixes itself into a ruin.

Each cycle would begin with listening.

Before the pipes darken, I would stand in the lower workshops and press my cheek near the cold metal wall, because cheek reads temperature better than hand. I would listen for the regulating valve’s slow knock, for the hiss that has shifted in pitch, for the tick of heated metal cooling somewhere above me. I would smell the air at the top of the stairs. If the smell changed toward clean and metallic, I would climb. If it changed toward iron and burnt pitch, I would not. If it changed toward faint green, I would know a leak had reached dust or moss and was beginning to wake. Then I would go to the upper galleries and watch the black roots of the steam pipes. I would wait with my palm over a joint, not touching, only sensing the thickness of moisture in the air. When the first bead formed, I would raise my hand as if catching a secret. Then I would go to the Market Gallery and tell the vendors not by shouting, but by lifting one finger: close the jars. Cover the dried fruit. Move the brass fittings away from the gutter line.

When the internal rain came, I would not celebrate it as a visitor. I would treat it as a guest with many teeth. Thin, warm, mineral rain would patter on copper roofing and vanish into iron grates. The Shaft would seem briefly like weather. I would walk under the grates to see where water returned. Too little and a basin is blocked. Too much and a cistern is being fed from somewhere it should not be. I would count the drip-stops. The quiet between sheets is not silence. It is the Shaft thinking about whether to fall again.

Between rains, I would scrub.

The Salt Scrubbers would know me well. They scrape the crust from pipe joints before it seals a valve too deeply, but I am not cleaning only to clean. I am reading the crust. A white bloom near a flange means slow leak. A brown streak beneath a copper fitting means water has traveled along an unseen seam before returning. Green-black film near a stone base means heat and moisture have met for long enough to make a small world. I would scrape enough to keep the machine honest, but not always all the way down to bare surface. Some lichen patches I would leave. Some moss seams I would protect with a small stone ledge so the water could feed them instead of washing them away. These plants are not conquerors. They are footnotes, and sometimes footnotes hold the text together. If a seal is failing, lichen may grow into the failure and become a temporary stitch. If the Shaft is hot enough to dry it out, then the stitch is not yet needed. My judgment would be this: let the plant be a symptom until it becomes a structure.

I would mark walls with chalk like a mapmaker of small failures.

The Kept are not rulers. They are corrections. A pipe bursts and a scaffold rises. A crack appears and damp mortar seals it. A pump slows and a technician walks the whole circuit, listening to rods. I would be part of that rhythm. I would not own the repairs. I would invite them. My chalk mark on a stairwell arch would summon a Ladder-Mason before the crack becomes an arch too tired to hold its shape. My oil smudge near a valve wheel would tell a Valvekeeper that salt had begun to eat the turn. My folded canvas left on a drying-loft beam would warn a resident that the next condensation sheet would fall too early and the clothes would need shelter.

If outsiders came, they would not notice me at first.

They would enter by the main shaft elevator and see a modest cavern and think: small place, hot place, dense place, finished enough. They would see the red valve house and mistake it for a landmark. They would hear the Engine Court and think the city is simpler than it is. They would smell damp brick and hot oil and assume it is only industry. They would wonder, after a while, why the light seems to bend around objects instead of cutting through them. They would ask where the birds are, where the insects are, where the dogs bark. They would be told: none of these things live here. Then they would notice that everything moves anyway. Water travels through channels too small to see. Steam rises in visible columns. Flywheels turn under grates. Salt precipitates and returns. The whole Shaft operates like a body without animal life: fluids, pressures, cycles, repairs, and waste.

I would be one of the body’s senses.

I would not sleep in the Engine Court. It is too bright, too rhythmic, too much the public heart. I would sleep in a small room on the residential tier, built into the curve of the shaft, sharing walls above, below, and sideways. My room would be plastered in faded green or ochre, not because the colors are cheerful, but because they remind the mind of light that does not fall from windows. A thin cot, a shelf for tools, a basin under a pipe for emergency condensation, a shutter of oiled fabric. The walls would breathe moisture. My hand would rest on them in the dark and know whether the room was too dry, too wet, too hot, too still. If the pump changed rhythm in the night, I would wake not startled but attentive, because in the Shaft a changed rhythm is the city clearing its throat.

I would know solitude, but not loneliness.

The Shaft’s solitude is specific: standing alone on a narrow catwalk above the Engine Court, watching steam curl through an amber lamp, feeling heat press into clothing, hearing the slow knock of a valve that no one else is listening for. It is not empty solitude. It is full attention. The absence of animals does not make the place empty. It makes it focused. No one chews a wire. No one fouls a cistern. No one disturbs a condensation sheet before the basin catches it. I would not speak to a beast, because there are no beasts. I would speak to a wall. I would press my ear to stone and say, softly, in the language of breath, “Do not open yet.” I would speak to a pipe: “Give me salt, not water.” I would speak to a crack: “Show me what you want to become.” And sometimes the Shaft would answer in the only way it can: by not leaking, by holding, by turning the red bulb once, by letting a drop fall into the right basin at the right hour.

If I had to name myself in the registry of the city, I would not put Seam-Warden. I would put Kept.

That name is enough. It means maintained and maintaining. It means I belong to the Shaft not as property, but as function. I am not here to enjoy it. I am here because the Shaft requires correction and I have been corrected into requiring it too. My hands have learned the work. My breathing has learned the pumps. My taste has learned the rain. My quiet has learned the architecture. I am the human answer to the place’s question: how long can heat cook air and call it weather? how long can stone be cut and call itself home? how long can a city survive with no animals, only people, only machines, only lichen in the mistakes?

I would be found where the answer is neither yes nor no, but not yet.

You would find me where a fresh crack runs too cleanly through old brick, or where a joint has begun to weep, or where a basin fills a little too slowly and the delay is already an accusation. You would find me in a hidden gallery above a boiler house, kneeling on an iron grate, holding a mirror to see behind a pipe, listening to the hiss that is almost but not quite steady. I would have a chalk mark on my wrist and rust in the creases of my fingers. I would look at the ceiling where rock meets copper bands and read the condensation not as rain, but as a page turning.

I would be part of the Shaft’s grammar.

When the upper pipes darken, I am the hand closing the shutters. When the first drops fall, I am the eye watching where they land. When the rain stops and the drip-stops, I am the footstep beginning again. When the salt settles, I am the blade scraping before the valve forgets how to turn. When the moss blooms into dark velvet, I am the one who decides whether it is a blessing or a warning. When the steam rises through lamp beams, I am the breath inside the breath. When the young stone changes its mind, I am the one who asks gently, “Where are you going?”

And when the Shaft’s quiet deepens, when no human voice is near, when the lamps burn low and the heat waits near the floor in slow blue eddies, I would stand in the Engine Court’s periphery and feel the city listening back.

Not with eyes. With rhythm.

With the way a valve changes note when I approach.

With the way the basins fill at the same hours.

With the way the flywheels turn as if they have known me for years.

With the way my own pulse falls into the slow knock of the pumps.

I would not be the Shaft.

But I would be one of the places where the Shaft becomes a self.
