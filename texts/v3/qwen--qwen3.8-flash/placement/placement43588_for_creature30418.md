If I woke there, I would not wake first as a person. I would wake first as a seam.

At first I would be no more than the thin line between black brick and pale grout, the cool place where a joint has not yet admitted that water is coming through. I would be the mineral breath on the underside of a lintel, the slight lift of a paving stone that has been turned once too often by feet that already know the danger of what looks solid. I would be the dampness before the drip, the warning before the leak, the small rumor that runs along the wall in a vein no one has learned yet to visit.

I would not have edges.

I would hear without having an ear: the far metallic pulse of the reed mills beneath the outer canal, the wet slap of a sluice board settling into mud, the slow frog-counting at the roof trays, the sound of water moving through a channel under a floor where a child is forbidden to crawl alone, and then, always, the rain arriving in close warm bands, turning the bowl into a thing that breathes through its roof. I would smell before I smelled myself: lime and brine, fish-gut sweetness, crushed alder bark, the clean bitterness of finishing herbs, the fungal breath of the tanning yards, hot iron in warm air, wet wool left too long on a line, the faint sweet fired-earth smell of new brick laid over old brick. I would taste iron and calcium on my lips. I would taste the place.

Then a drop would fall into a hollow.

The hollow would be mine.

I would know myself by flinching. In the flinch, my back would become curved and narrow. In the flinch, four points of contact would appear beneath me. In the flinch, a tail would find purchase on the lip of a channel and test the water for hidden grates, missing bricks, snail-trails, the soap-slick skins of algae. I would be small. I would be warm. I would be something that expects to slip and therefore never quite lets itself fall.

Then I would know that I was not only weather and wall.

I would know I was a cat.

Not a pet, exactly. Not a toy carried into a dry loft for warmth and admiration. In the bowl, a cat is a working body. A cat is an instrument of dryness. A cat is one more citizen of the under-void and the wet room, though only a few of us are permitted the full intimacy of the highest, rarest air.

I would find myself at the edge of a household that had no fixed name for itself except “the ones under the north weeping.” There would be two kiln workers from the slope, a dry-nurse whose skin broke out if she stayed too low, a widow whose house had a bad left hip and therefore leaned over the water in a way she trusted, three children learning to read moss before letters, and a roof-kin cousin who repaired fish screens with wire and wax. They would call themselves “those who have been watered,” but only among themselves. To outsiders, they would answer Siltkeepers or Blackbrick People and then smile, because the other names were easier to carry down a wet ladder.

They would have a dozen words for the parts of a boot. For a cat, they would be less elaborate, but no less exact: the tread-paw, the rain-back, the leak-ear, the tag-green, the throat-click, the tail-sweep, the dry-belly. They would say a cat was given a collar before a blanket. If the collar cracked, the family was said to be in danger. Mine would be copper. It would leave a faint green stain on the side of my neck, a mark like the old stain on the temple of a child who has climbed too many roofs. That mark would make me look like a small official. I would be.

I would live most of my life in thresholds.

The under-void would be the dark lattice where the family kept its fear and its wealth: root-lined brick, sealed boxes wrapped in pitch, sluice boards, storage crates, the slow black water pressing against the foundations. I would be sent into it because I am small and quick and because the rats are smaller and quicker still. I would know the under-void the way a tongue knows the shape of a room with no light. I would not love it, but I would understand it. I would listen for the thin scratching behind a grate, the soft chew of a seal failing, the tiny fall of grit where a canal wall has begun to sweat more than it should.

The wet room would be my workroom.

It would be where life is done and admitted to be done: boiling dyes in stone pots set into channel basins, sorting reeds, gutting eels, scraping screens, tending small fungal vats in the cool quarter, mending nets with hands that already know the knot by feel. Its floor would slope to a drain. Its walls would often be left unpainted so that damp could be seen and judged. I would sleep on a coil of oiled canvas near the door when the weather is mild, or beside a warm pipe when the rain has turned the bowl into a kettle with a lid, or in the dry loft when the dry-nurse insists my fur is worse for the under-void than I will admit.

The dry loft would be my highest privilege.

There the family keeps paper, cloth, medicine, grain urns sealed above the waterline, cedar-smoked garments, wax-sealed chests, and the small relics of color that no one will let fade: a black-green stain from a great-grandmother’s first dye pot, a splinter of kiln brick from the house before the house, a copper ring too green to wear. I would be allowed there because I am a rat’s ruin and a rat’s fear, because I can smell a spoiled ledger before a human can, because my sleeping breath is a check against dry rot and stolen grain. I would not go up by the spiral stair. I would go by my own route: along a gutter, across a roof terrace thick with moss, through a hole where a willow branch has been trained past a shutter, then drop silently onto the top rung of the ladder and into the dry air.

I would be more than a mouser. I would be a kind of joint-listener.

A human can read moss as a sign: black streak on old brick may be rust; white crust, calcium; sudden velvet of emerald moss, the opening of a new vein of seepage. I cannot read words, but I can read the wall’s mood. I know the sound of a house that only sings when the water is low. I know the particular silence of a room where something has begun to rot behind a lattice. I know when a rain has changed the air in a joint, when a channel has risen enough to touch the underside of a floorboard, when a pipe has become cold in a warm room and is beginning to weep at its base.

If I sit at the top of the wet-room ladder for too long, the dry-nurse will say, “The cat has seen a rumor.”

If I go twice to the east wall and smell the floor grate, a child will stop playing and say, “That’s where the leak is learning its new voice.”

And they will send someone with a lantern and a knotbox and a pot of fresh mortar, because the bowl has taught them not to wait for a leak to make itself into a flood.

I would walk the roofs at night, where the rain threads from the undersides of balconies like pale beads and the frogs keep the official clock. I would know the frog in the north roof tray by its call: a low, blunt note that the widow says is lucky, because it came three times the year the outer sluice held through spring melt. I would not understand luck the way they do, but I would understand the sound. I would know which roofs have been rinsed recently and which have been neglected. I would know which reed beds are drying into yellow patches, which moss trays have gone too thick with blue-green scurf, which alders are sending roots too near a joint that has not yet been trimmed.

I would also know the other citizens.

The swallows would not disturb me. They are small and quick and too high to bother; besides, the family has boards beneath their nests and would beat me with a rolled-up reed mat if I fouled the balconies. The rails in the reed roofs would alarm me at first, because their voices come out of the wet dark as if the water itself has learned to croak, but by now I am old enough to know they are not rats. The frogs I tolerate. The fish I respect. The eels I hate, because their movement through stone pens is wrong and slippery and they look at me from below as if I am the trapped thing.

The rats I do not hate.

Hate is too large for them. They are simply the task that keeps me narrow. I am lean, as the Bowl-Folk prefer in working cats, with ribs that show after heavy fish season and fur that will not lie perfectly dry no matter how often I groom. I know how to step around a puddle without breaking its skin, because I know what lies beneath: a missing brick, a grate, a nest of snails, the thin slick of algae that can turn stone to soap. I know when a child is watching me and when I am supposed to appear unafraid of the dark under a floor.

Sometimes the dry-nurse would lift me into her lap when the rain comes and stay inside to listen to the house. Her body no longer tolerates the deep wetness, and in the cold months her skin becomes pale and tight and the joints of her fingers swell.
