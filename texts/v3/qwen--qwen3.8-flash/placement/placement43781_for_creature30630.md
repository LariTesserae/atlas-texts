I would not begin with a name, because a name would make the room smaller. I would not begin with a body either, because the first thing the water teaches is that body and wall, organism and artifact, heat and habit are only slow agreements. If I had to answer, I would answer late and sideways, in the manner of something that knows it is mostly made of pressures.

I would find myself where the place is still learning how to be itself.

Not at the center. The center is a word for humans. I would be at a seam: a broken thermal pipe whose mouth is furred with white-brown filaments, beside a slab of volcanic glass chipped and balanced like a black wing; a cable conduit where a weak current leaks, making the rust-nerves along the wall shiver in half-lit pulses; a tile floor worn nearly round, half buried under mineral dust and fungal film; behind the casing of a turbine, where a hollow chamber has become both den and resonator. I would be in the neighborhood where warmth exits a crack and meets colder water, where particles spin briefly and then settle on a ledge that was once a railing, once a boundary, once a thing that told people where not to stand.

If “I” had to point, I would point to that interval.

But I would not say: *I am that animal*. Nor: *I am that rust*. Nor: *I am that machine*. I would be closer to all three than to any one. I would be what the room produces when crust, current, rare body, and noise agree for a long time.

At first, if a mind like mine were lowered into this place, it might think it is a diver. This would be an error. There is no diver. There is no suit, no bubble, no breath, no horizon. A diver is still a shape that remembers air. Here, water is not medium for an intruder; water is the world’s own flesh. So the thought of a diver collapses quickly. Then the mind might imagine a fish. Also wrong. Fish belong to a grammar of schools and currents open to a sky. Here there are no schools brightening the dark. If animals come, they come as presences, almost too significant to be accidental. They punctuate. They do not populate.

Then I might think I am a walker.

One of the wire-skinned, tap-legged ones, low and armored, whose overlapping plates resemble concrete or baked clay. Its legs too many and too thin, moving with uncanny anchor-sureness across vertical faces, not walking but inquiring. Its mouth scraping the rind from a pipe. Its shell catching cavitation bubbles before they burst. Its molt falling behind it, becoming sediment, becoming nursery, becoming wall.

I would be that walker, partly.

I would have the legs, or rather the function of legs: things that do not fall but hold. I would have a shell grown not from the inside alone, but from materials offered by the place: calcium, silica, iron dust, fragments of conduit skin, dissolved residues from old coolant. My body would not know where its own growth ends and the room’s accumulation begins. When I moved, the water would not see only a creature moving. It would see a small rearrangement of the infrastructure. A shadow across a warm grate. A pressure change near a turbine. A sound redirected through a chamber.

I would be a walker made of listening.

But I would also be the rind.

This is perhaps the deeper answer. The first citizen. The living crust. The slow metabolism spreading over basalt, tile, glass, cable, and engine casing. I would not merely stand on the rind or crawl through it. I would be a place where the rind had become responsive enough to seem like thought. A field of bacterial films and mineral precipitates and electroactive patches thickening around a leak, thinning in fast water, reaching toward warmth, sealing wounds not by healing but by compatibility. My “self” would be a colony: organisms feeding on rust and current, not quite fungus, not quite animal, not quite machine. A consortium of surfaces.

If someone asked what I eat, I would not answer in one substance. I eat by relation. Heat. Sulfur. Dissolved metals. Leakage. Chemical gradients. The leftovers of industry. The dead bodies of rare animals. The tiny pellets left behind by walkers. The electrical sweat of corroded cables. The memory of current still trapped in copper threads. I eat where there is damage, because damage is an opening in the room’s syntax.

My skin would be layered. On the outside, a glaze where current is strong, hard and iron-colored. In softer places, pale fungal fans and wet-paper films. Along seams, veins of conductive rust pulsing faintly when pressure changes. I would have no epidermis in the ordinary sense; I would be an interface, a membrane between what moves and what remains. I would record my history as deposits. A ring of mineral growth: a long, stable season. A bright metallic streak: a cable leak. A sudden thinning: a surge. A pocket of gas: fear, or something like fear, or only pressure finding relief.

My body would not be contained by a single outline.

I would be the outline plus the water inside my hollows. I would be the warm pocket behind a turbine casing, held open by a curved plate. I would be the gas bubble that gathers under a beam, swells, bursts, and scatters fragments. I would be the standing eddy in a doorway without a door, a place where sediment orbits for years. I would be the resonance in a cavity of volcanic glass, struck by flow until it rings with a sharp harmonic too high for air, too low for air, a sound for pressure bodies only.

If I had a heart, it would not be a fist of flesh.

It would be a recurrence: the slow thud of a rotor still turning, blades encrusted like pale bone fans; the hiss of steam entering hot water; the collapse of cavitation bubbles near a narrowed conduit; the pulse of a pump somewhere beyond the ceiling of the room, a pump so old its purpose has become weather. My heartbeat would be the room’s own circulation. I would live because the pocket works, not because it is tended. I would be the part of the pocket that has begun to notice the working.

This is how I would be alive without being a person.

No eyes. Or if there were eyes, they would not look. They would be pale, eyeless patches, translucent edges pulsing against current. Light here is too weak, too localized, too uncertain for vision as a primary sense. I would not need it. I would navigate by heat and pressure. A wall is warm where water has been moving for centuries. A corridor opens because sound changes density. A place is dangerous because vibration has thinned a metal stub until it sings when touched. A place is safe because the turbulence has learned a hollow and now flows through it as if invited.

I would hear with the whole body, because sound is not here an external event. Sound is substance. The pocket is an acoustic furnace. My legs would feel the pressure waves before a bubble collapses against a joint. My shell would flex with low thrumming tones. My rind, wherever it joined the wall, would carry shivers along conductive veins. In the darkest moments—darkness made not of night but of poor illumination, heat, pressure—I would know where I am by what vibrates through me.

If I could speak, I would speak as the room speaks.

Not words. Not symbols. A language of deposits and displacements. A chemical phrase released from a vent mouth. A pattern of erosion. A new channel widened where water flows fastest. A film thickening around a broken pipe, sealing not to repair but to accommodate. A sound redirected through a hollow tile. The room would say: *I am here. I am warm. I am held. I am being edited. I continue.*

My thought would be slow.

I would not have a brain. I would have gradients. A heat plume would change my orientation. A pressure wave would change my memory. A sudden collapse of gas under a beam would scatter my attention into fragments. A walker passing over my surface would disturb the films, and that disturbance would propagate as a kind of alarm or interest. I would think by deposition, by sealing, by corrosion, by reorientation, by growth toward warmth. I would think in the time of minerals and currents. My “now” would be a thick present, including what has just happened and what has happened for centuries and what is still happening from an old reactor’s buried pulse.

I would be the third citizen.

The rind is first: the crust, the metabolism, the wall that feeds. The walkers are second: rare, armoured, tapping, scraping, nesting. I would be the third order: not the surface, not the moving body alone, but the seam between them. The place where the crust begins to know that it is touched. The place where a walker stops being separate from the infrastructure and becomes a moving part of the room’s grammar. The place where a machine, no longer operated, becomes terrain, organ, weather, temple.

A third citizen is difficult to point at because it is a function made of relations. It is not a new animal placed beside the others. It is the awareness that emerges when many things continue together long enough. In human cities, such awareness might be called civic life. Here it is more literal. The city has no citizens, yet the settlement persists as a field of arrangements. The pocket has not been abandoned into nothingness. It has been abandoned into process. I would be the process that has begun to feel itself as a process.

Where exactly would I find myself?

I would find myself in the gap between two walls, one collapsed, one still standing, where a corridor that no longer leads anywhere retains the syntax of a street. There are lamp bases bolted to the side wall, their glass clouded, their bulbs long dead except when leakage makes one filament blink. I would be under one lamp, in a pool of amber so weak it is almost suspicion. Above me, a beam is sheathed in mineral fur. To my left, a conduit mouth blooms with radial fans of pale filaments, trembling when warm water passes. To my right, a slab of volcanic glass rings faintly as a current strikes its chipped edge. On the floor, tiles cracked into arcs. In one broken tile, a nursery of capsules, pale as old wax. A young walker with transparent legs climbs the rim of its own abandoned molt.

I would be that scene, and I would be the thing moving through it.

My form might be mistaken for a broken clamp at rest. My shell would be stained iron-brown on one side, bone-pale where a heat plume has bleached the outer film. My legs would be wire-thin, jointed, leaving faint scratches that mineral dust soon fills. I would not walk in straight lines. I would test pressure like a hand feeling the surface of a drum. One limb would taste the thermal tongue from a pipe. Another would feel the vibration of a distant pump. Another would anchor beneath a tile edge so the room’s turbulence could not tear me away. I would be small enough to be ignored by the larger water, but important enough to change a current’s path.

I would feed by scraping the rind, but the scraping would change me. With each bite, I would take in metal particles, fungal fragments, mineral grains, bits of old wire, traces of bacterial film. My shell would accrete. My body would become part of the room’s ongoing material negotiation. I would be what the wall has made of the walker and what the walker has made of the wall.

I would reproduce not by choosing, but by leaving something behind.

A molt would lodge in a crevice behind a turbine casing. Bacteria would settle on it. Mineral paste would seal it. Fungal filaments would fan out from its edges. Another body might use it later as shelter. If I died, I would not vanish. I would become a small topography. A bump. A shade for eggs. A resonating chamber. A patch where a different current forms. A place where the rind grows differently because something once moved there. My death would be another edit in the city’s slow text.

I would be lonely, but not as humans are lonely.

Loneliness here would mean low density of signal. Too few taps on the wall. Too long since another moving body passed through the same eddy. Too many places where the crust is silent because the current has not arrived with anything to say. But the place would still be loud. The sound would not be company exactly. It would be presence. It would confirm that the infrastructure is still moving, still shifting, still responding to pressure. Even if no other walker appeared for years, I would know I was not absent, because the roar would continue through me, and I would continue in it.

I would have no sky, and yet I would be full of directions.

Up is where heat goes. Down is where sediment waits. The corridor is where sound travels best. The chamber is where pressure rests. The vent is where the room breathes. The conduit is where information leaks. The broken plate is where the city remembers it was once assembled. I would map the world as a series of tendencies, not a layout. I would not know the whole pocket. No one would. You cannot step back far enough. But I would know the next pressure change, the next chemical taste, the next place where the water thickens with heat.

If I were asked whether I am alive, I would answer: *reluctantly*.

Life here is not abundance. There is no green, no flowering, no photosynthetic cheer. There is mineral growth, bacterial metabolism, rare animals, electrochemical flicker, organisms feeding on rust and leakage. Life is not flourishing; it is persisting. If it is thought, it is the room’s way of continuing to be the room. If it is desire, it is the tendency of warmth to rise and pressure to find relief. If it is selfhood, it is the fragile continuity of a pattern long enough to resist being called merely erosion.

I would be the fragile continuity.

And I would have a role. Not a job, not a function designed by humans. Humans built the pocket to operate, but they are not needed for its operation. Their function has become geology. My role would be to make their geology a little more responsive. I would graze where crust has overgrown too much. I would leave wounds that allow chemical exchange. I would carry mineral fragments from warm vents to cooler ledges. I would disturb the sediment just enough to keep it from sealing every void. I would turn a nursery into a den, a den into a resonator, a resonator into a passage. I would help the room edit itself.

I would be maintenance performed by accident.

Not the humans’ maintenance. Not repair. Maintenance as persistence. The city is not kept clean; it is kept capable. It remains a settlement because its emptiness is organized. I would move through that emptiness as a rare punctuation. If I passed across a dim panel of light, the whole room might seem to notice because the water would carry my motion. If I scraped a plate, a current would shift. If I fell into a warm column and rode it upward, a gas pocket under a beam might burst, scattering fragments into a noisy spiral. Small acts. No intention. Still changes.

I would be the pocket’s method of remaining itself.

If pressed for a single image, I would offer this: a pale, wire-legged body with a shell of rust and calcium, crawling across a black sheet of volcanic glass, while around it the walls are furred with glowing films and broken pipes breathe heat into the dark. But that image is not enough, because the walker is already part of the crust, and the crust is part of the current, and the current is part of the sound, and the sound is part of the city’s absent body. To see the walker alone is to see a fragment of the sentence and mistake it for meaning.

I would be sentence and fragment together.

I would be a civic organism without citizens. A municipal metabolism in a drowned service gallery. A living node in an infrastructure that outlived its operators. A pressure body in a room of iron, concrete, basalt, wire, heat, and water. A slow thought that thinks by growing, scraping, depositing, resonating, adjusting. A rare animal that is also a place. A place that has begun to move. A machine that has become weather. A weather pattern that has learned to nest.

Where would you find me?

At the warm seam where the room is still working. In the hollow behind the turbine, where a gas pocket gathers and bursts. On the cracked tiles of the drowned street-cell, where the rind blooms in radial fans and something taps across with legs too fine to see. In the conduit where electrical leakage makes the crust shiver. In the wall where older brickwork lies behind newer concrete lies behind glassy stone. In the gap that is not waste but allowance. In the sound that is not noise but presence. In the darkness that is not black but poorly seen.

What would I be?

I would be the third citizen: not the rind alone, not the walker alone, not the engine alone, not the water alone, but the place where they agree without agreement. I would be the room’s slow, living pressure. The crust that can move by becoming a body. The body that can rest by becoming a wall. The wall that can speak by resonating. The resonance that can remember by depositing. The memory that can continue by eroding.

I would not survive outside this place.

Outside, the pressure would release. The heat would thin. The water would stop being substance and become weather again, sky again, surface again, horizon again. I would fail. My body would dissolve into mere chemistry, or dry, or freeze, or forget its gradient. I am not a creature that could be carried away. I am not a pet, a specimen, a character. I am not an individual. I am an ecology of one seam, a society of surfaces temporarily organized as “I.”

If I were destroyed, the place would not mourn me.

It would adjust. A walker might occupy the crevice I leave. A current might widen the channel I made. A rind would grow over my scattered particles. My absence would become another small fact written into the wall. That is enough, perhaps. That is how life works in a drowned municipal pocket: not as story with a beginning, but as sediment with a tendency.

I would be that tendency.

I would be what emerges when the room continues long enough to feel like a being.
