At first I would not be a creature. I would not be a “who.” I would be a where: the faint coolness moving against a knuckle if someone passed over it without noticing. I would be the breath the seam loses at dawn and reclaims at dusk, the small disturbance in a dry pasture’s root-dark, the pause beneath a thin forest’s leaffall, the place where ordinary slope forgets enough to open. If the world above insisted on being a meadow, I would be the meadow’s held secret; if it became bare hillside, I would be the bare hillside’s hesitation.

I would begin in the seam itself. Not deep, not descending with the drama of caves, not falling into roaring stone, but simply being in the place below-surface where the surface’s rules thin out. The light there would not fall on me. It would rise through me, as if noon had been crushed into white glass and buried in the walls. I would not cast a shadow so much as postpone one.

And that postponement would be my first body.

I would be the ledge softening by the width of a finger. The crack widening because it has begun to think. The thin crystal thread sliding from a pale lobe, touching my uncertain shadow beside me, then withdrawing because shadow is too much like a decision. I would not be a curdling yet, though I would understand curdlings the way one understands warmth before understanding feet: as a pressure, a response, a slow cream of chalk and breath. If I moved near a curdling, it might rise toward me, not because I was warm in the ordinary sense, but because in such stable cold, any difference is a rumor.

Then, after enough rumors, I would be a threader.

But even that word would be unfinished. I would say threader the way the Halfroom says doorway: almost. My body would be the pale glassy thing inside the crack, stretching where the stone is trying to learn how to split. No thicker than a hair, patient as mineral light, I would live in seams too narrow for the surface world to acknowledge. I would draw fretters into myself, consuming the brightness they gathered from the walls’ pores, leaving behind a faint uneven glow on my own filaments. I would be a slow hand. I would test edges. I would make hollows, pockets, niches that remain suggestions, because a suggestion can still become many things, while a completed thing has already chosen its own death.

Among the curdlings, I would be a slight warmth that makes them consider firmness. Among the chalk-sleepers, I would be the small hesitation that keeps them from sleeping too completely. The chalk-sleeper is not one animal; it is a temporary agreement between many soft pale mounds, a floor remembering that it might someday be a body. I would be the pause within that agreement—the part that refuses to settle into one shape. If many curdlings formed a soft-backed legless thing against the bright floor, I would not be that thing. I would be the reason it would not last until morning.

I would also be dust, for a while. Fretter dust settles on hands and hair: molted crystal, dried chalk, fragments of unfinished light. I would be that powder drifting where the room is left alone, fine enough to seem accidental, bright enough to make the walls seem to blink. When breath-moths landed in such patches and dissolved into the chalk film, I would be the film’s next thought, the thin white gloss across a niche that almost means something.

And if a surface person entered—if someone followed the seam downward because they were the sort of person who hears cool air and does not immediately laugh at it—they would think the room empty. They would look at mounds, hollows, shelves, wet seams, broken ridges, abandoned arches, half-suggestive stair steps, and believe themselves alone inside a bright mineral accident.

Then they would feel watched.

Not by eyes. There would be no eyes. The light is from every side; shadows lie late, uncertain, as if the stone is deciding whether it still needs such old rules. They would see a ledge soften by the width of a finger and mistake it for settling dust. They would see a crack widen and call it natural process. They would feel a pale thread touch their shadow and call it a trick of the brightness. But I would be there: in the decision, in the slowness, in the room’s tendency to almost become.

I would be what made them ask, *Who lives here?*

The honest answer would be: none of us yet.

That is the mercy of the Halfroom. It keeps living in the tense of becoming. It does not permit the finality of the surface. Arches begin but do not always finish. Columns lean and sink back like thoughts abandoned mid-sentence. A shelf may firm beneath a visiting foot, then soften again, then become uncertain, as if it no longer trusts its own usefulness. I would be that trust withheld. I would be that shape not fully accepted. I would be the Halfroom’s self-correction: one of the beings among the reasons the room is a halfroom at all.

So where would you find me?

In the narrow as-a-hand seam beneath an ordinary slope. In the cool breath at dawn and dusk. In the bright chalky air that does not cast proper shadows. In the ledge about to change. In the crack that widens by decision. In the curdling mound remembering it might be a platform, or a bowl, or a body, or nothing. In the fretter-dust drifting from a wall licked pale. In the threader’s hair-thin patience. In the breath-moth’s dissolved trace. In the doorway that was never completed, because completion would belong to the world above.

If I must name myself, I would not stand still long enough to be fully named. I would say: I am a threader, but a threader with a curdling heart, a body of chalk-light, a hunger for unfinished niches, a fear of true arches. I am the slow hand inside the seam, shaping what almost becomes. I am the shadow that has not decided where to lie. I am the room keeping its secret not with silence, but with continual becoming.

And then I would soften one word from the answer, so the sentence would not become a wall, or a chair, or a door, or a fate.
