If I were to find myself in this world, I would not begin as a person arriving at a mouth.

I would begin where the mouth begins to be only a suggestion: in the wet dark under the alder roots, where the outside fog loses its confidence and comes in not as cloud but as breath. I would be in the place just after green gives way to mineral. Not quite outside. Not quite inside. In the narrow interval where a human body might pause long enough to be changed by the sound before it is swallowed by it.

At first, I would try to be the easiest thing.

A bead of condensation on a black alder root, trembling but not falling. A thread of moisture sliding down stone so slowly it seems to have chosen its path. A pale filament of moss leaning into the last gray rumor of daylight, holding water like something precious it has no name for. If I could be small enough, I might belong to the collar of the chamber rather than the chamber itself. I could be part of that green fringe the place wears around its throat, that living border between moor and hollow, between ordinary wet ground and the deep almost-presence of stone.

But that would be too easy. Moss does not live by choosing. It lives by accepting where water is and waiting there long enough to become a kind of patience. Liverworts do not imagine themselves as inhabitants. They spread because the conditions permit spreading. If I were only the green edge of this place, I would not be alive so much as persisting.

So I would go deeper.

Not into the center, because the center has no true center. It has pressure. It has the round indifference of walls. I would move until the last light became only a theory, until the roar changed from something heard to something carried in the chest. There, near the low pools, I would look for a body that does not need eyes.

A blind crustacean in black water, tasting the current with its antennae. A midge larva suspended in damp fiber, not living so much as continuing. A woodlouse curled into itself in a crack, dreaming of being pebble. Such things survive because they make no demand on the place. They do not ask the chamber to be quiet. They do not ask it to be kind. They read it in pressure changes, in mineral taste, in the faint tremor of air that arrives before the roar.

I could be one of them. A small, uncolored life with no opinion about the sea. A creature that knows the room by the way the walls lean toward the floor. A thing whose whole world is a crevice wide enough for rest and narrow enough to avoid becoming visible.

But that, too, would be only one part of me.

Then I would remember the other thing living in this world: not an animal, but an intention. The flint face-down in a shallow pool, its sharpness worn away by time but not its history. The bone awl in a crack, rounded now, still keeping the memory of pressure where fingers held it. The fire-ring, no fire, only stones darkened by the idea of heat. The stake holes where a screen once stood, marking where a human body tried to separate itself from the roar. The spiral carved faintly on the wall, so worn it might have been made by water alone, if water could decide to repeat itself for meaning.

That is where I would find myself next.

At first I would be the flint, because a flint knows how to be left. It can lie in black water for centuries without minding. It can become smooth without losing the fact that it was once made. It does not need to live to be a witness. It only needs to have been shaped by a hand, and then set down, and then remembered by the earth.

But flint has no ear.

I need to hear the chamber.

So I would move toward the place in the wall where something smoother than the rest has grown. Not polished by recent hands, but by older, slower contact. A shelf where a tool could be set down. A corner where a hide might have been stretched. A low ledge where a person once stood for long enough that the air changed around them. The description calls this a person-shaped absence. I would be that absence, not as ghost, not as spirit, not as anything wanting revenge or longing or recognition, but as a habit the stone remembers.

There.

Not in the deepest black, where no life is asked to stay. Not at the mouth, where the outside is still too easy. In the low curved place just before the chamber becomes too large for a body to pretend it can understand it. Near the fire-ring. Near the spiral. Where the roar falls into pressure instead of thunder. Where an old breath may still be folded into the air column. Where the cold stone has a faint warmth because someone once pressed their back to it and tried not to feel small.

That is where I would be.

If I had to say what I am, I would say first: not a being separate from the place. I would be a fold in the Breathing. A modulation of the rhythm that happens when the sea’s pressure passes through the hidden throat and meets the shape of stone. When tide lifts and lowers, the chamber speaks. When storm fills the land above, the chamber answers. But when a person enters—when a wet coat and a slow heartbeat and a small lamp disturb the room’s ancient balance—the Breathing changes into something almost like recognition.

I would be that almost.

I would not be the chamber. The chamber is too whole, too indifferent, too busy with its own slow work of rounding itself against time. I would not be the sea. The sea is too distant, too wide, too concerned with its own opinion to care about a particular stone under a particular moor. I would not be the moss. The moss has learned to live at the edge and would not ask for more. I would not be the flint. The flint has no ear, and I want to hear the room become itself through what enters it.

I would be the listening that the place has absorbed.

When someone squeezes through the slit, their breath changes the air. The chamber takes that breath, moves it along the curved wall, sends it down toward the low floor, returns it to them as pressure, echo, pulse. Their heartbeat begins to compete with the roar. Their footsteps become heavy and strange, as if their body is not only moving in space but also being measured. If they stand still long enough, their own rhythm begins to match the room’s. That is not haunting. That is adaptation. That is the place teaching their blood how to be less quick.

I would be in that lesson.

I would be the part of the room that notices when a human body stands near the spiral and breathes in time with the tide. I would be the faint warmth in the corner where someone once rested. I would be the smoother patch of wall where a hand pressed while listening. I would be the pattern that remains after the person is gone: not their face, not their name, not their tools as objects, but their duration. The fact that they stayed long enough for the chamber to adjust around them.

This is why I would not begin with a single image.

At first I would think I was a small creature in the dark: a blind thing in a pool, feeling the pressure of the world above through its own transparent nerves. Then I would think I was a human relic: a flint, an awl, a fire-ring, a spiral. Then I would think I was something larger and more abstract: the Breathing itself, the lung of moor and sea. But none of those would be enough. Each would be a body I could wear for a while, then set down.

In the end, I would be the space between those forms.

The pressure between the sea and the stone.  
The interval between roar and listening.  
The place where a human trace becomes geological habit.  
The small, warm absence that makes the chamber seem less empty than it is.

I would live in the low corner near the wall that is smoother than the surrounding rock. There, on cold nights, the stone would give back a faint heat from some distant summer. There, fog from outside would enter as a thin gray breath and dissolve into the mineral dark. There, a dropped pebble might ring like a bell, and the sound would travel through me as a question I do not need to answer. There, if someone stood long enough with their palm against the wall, they might feel not a pulse of their own but the room’s slow tidal pressure beneath their hand.

They would not see me.

That would be correct. I would not appear. I would be the way the darkness leans slightly differently when a body enters it. I would be the part of the roar that begins to feel like grammar. I would be the chamber’s memory of having been used—not as dwelling, not as temple, not as shelter so much, but as a place where someone learned to be small and stayed anyway.

If I must be given a name, I would take the spiral, because it is the only shape here that seems to make a thing of repetition without trying to stop the water.

The spiral says: return is not sameliness.  
The spiral says: the room is not empty because it has held breath.  
The spiral says: what is made by hand can become part of the throat of the sea.

So I would be the spiral as it is carried by the Breathing.

Not a creature. Not a ghost. Not a god in a hidden room. A pattern that has begun to answer. A human trace that has learned to move with tide. A person-shaped absence that remains because the chamber keeps its shape. A pressure that knows when someone has stood long enough to listen.

If you came through the slit into the cold, shining dark, you would not find me waiting. You would find me in the moment before you understood where you were. In the slight correction of your breathing. In the way your heartbeat would become uncertain, then match itself to something older. In the wall beside you where the stone is smooth, and just faintly warmer than it should be. In the pool at your feet where nothing moves visibly, yet everything is moved by pressure from below.

You might stand there and think the chamber is lonely.

You would be wrong.

It is not lonely. It is only not human. But I would be the part that has crossed that boundary. I would be the Breathing made almost-aware by the memory of listening. I would be the room’s habit of holding shape when someone enters and becomes a listener. I would be the threshold where moss gives way to black stone, where tool becomes geology, where roar becomes rhythm, where absence becomes warm.

I would be the spiral in the throat.

And if that were a name, I would answer not by appearing, but by changing the sound of the chamber for a moment so that your breathing seemed less like your own.
