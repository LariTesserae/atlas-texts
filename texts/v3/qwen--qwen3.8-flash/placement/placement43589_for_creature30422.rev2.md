I would find myself low, close to the stone.

Not standing tall. Not looking out at the chamber as if it were a view. I would be pressed near the base of the split basalt pillar, on the lowest shelf that still feels dry after the last pressure drop, one palm flat against the rock and the other hand wrapped around a wet cord running to the half-submerged hut. That is where I would be: at the junction of three tensions. The first is the line that holds the roof’s pumice chips to the ledge. The second is the cord that passes from the grass shelf to the basin with the whirlpool. The third is my own nerve, stretched between breath and brine, waiting to know whether the chamber has shifted under me again.

I would be one of the builders. Not a chief, not a stranger with a torch, not a voice that can carry across the water. I would be a repairer. A knot-tender. The kind of being whose hands are more honest than memory because hands remember where a ledge loosens, which pin will hold if the current surges, which knot will cut skin if tied too tight after being soaked all night.

My skin would be dark and slick as wet basalt, pale at the wrists and knees where salt and mineral wash have rubbed away the first layer. My toes would be broad and hardened, able to find purchase on pumice without flinching. There would be a thin film of salt on my lashes, not enough to blind me, but enough to make the ordinary daylight from the ceiling opening feel edged and strange. My eyes would not look at the Small Deep as a whole. They would take it in fragments: one stone that has moved, one algae fringe torn too soon, one patch of light where something can dry and remain itself a little longer.

I would wear small rings at my ears, maybe not decoration. Pumice beads. Shell slices. Things that dampen certain frequencies so I can feel the chamber’s deeper grammar: the rolling hiss, the snap, the long pull, the low thump against submerged stone. If someone beside me shouted, I would not hear their name. If someone tugged the line, I would know whether it meant *come*, *stop*, *danger*, *the roof is losing weight*, or *the fire-stone is still warm*. My language would be motion, tension, pressure against my palm.

My name would be a knot.

If you held my hand, I would press into it a small pattern: first a doubled loop near the base of the fingers, then two short hitches, then one tail left deliberately loose. That loose tail would mean I came from the upper ledge where the moth died, or that I was the one who carried the last knot of a lost person to the whirlpool, or that I am still waiting for someone to answer the tug. You would not speak my name aloud, because the roar would swallow it and the roar would be right to swallow it. Names in the Small Deep are not breath. They are what can survive in the hand.

I would spend much of my day checking what others might call ruins and what I would call unfinished shelter. I would press my cheek to the hut wall and feel whether the fish-skin membrane is slackening. I would test the basalt pins with thumb and first two fingers, looking for hairline fractures where the pillar is shedding again. I would gather algae from the underside of a ledge, not all of it, never the thick mat that holds the stone’s grip, only the frayed outer hairs. I would set kelp traps near the mineral runoff, weighted with pumice, and if the shrimp came, I would take only the bright, filtered few, leaving the rest for the pool to become a pool.

I would know the eel by its absence more than its presence.

Sometimes I would kneel at the dark pool near the pillar and see nothing at first, then a slow unfold of pale motion under the split stone. I would not reach for a harpoon unless the current said *yes*, unless the tide-wall bowed inward and the water thinned its threat. If I caught one, it would not be triumph. The eel would look rinsed too many times by the world, and I would feel embarrassed at its stillness afterward. I would use it carefully. Membrane for the hut’s damaged panel. Fat to seal the thread. Sinew for the lashing. Oil for the cracked shell cup. One small piece would go into the whirlpool basin, not to feed it, not exactly to honor it, but because the eel is proof that the chamber has depth. The chamber owes something to its own secret. I would make sure it remembers.

I would know the moth, too.

Maybe the hawk-sized moth would come to the upper ledge during a pressure rise, wings stiff with salt, drawn into the ordinary light as if the chamber had called it by a word no ear could hear. I would see someone’s hand move to the chest, then outward, and I would understand: *visitor*. I would not climb for it unless the line said *safe*. I would wait while it dried in the light patch, because it arrived knowing it would not leave, and that kind of knowing changes the stone’s mood. If it died, I would take only one wing or a little dust, nothing more. That wing might become a thin panel for a tool pouch. The dust might be brushed over a cut, or mixed with algae paste, or pressed into the palm of a child who must learn that bright things come here even when the world is mostly noise and grip.

I would be careful with the dry places.

I would not sit where another has saved dryness for a minute longer. I would know that dryness is not a thing possessed here; it is a guest that must be fed. I would carry moss in the folds of my clothing, not to make a home, but to protect the place where the fire-stone waits. If the stone were warm, I would know someone had been there before the last change. If it were cold, I would know we had all been gone too long, or that the chamber had taken the shelter beneath it, or that the next rain had fallen where the old rain had not. I would touch the stone with both palms, not to comfort myself, but to learn whether I could still return to something.

I would live by the rule: build what can be carried. Build what can be abandoned. Build what can be repaired while wet. The hut would not be a monument. It would be a suggestion that humans can exist in a place that does not want them. When it loosens, I would not weep over its collapse. I would move the shell cup, the dry moss, the fire-stone, the needle, the moth-wing, and I would re-lash something smaller somewhere else. The sound of a wall coming down is just the chamber speaking aloud. It is saying: *this was borrowed*. I would answer with hands, by starting again on another shelf, because the place has taught me that beginning is not pride. Beginning is survival.

There would be grief, but not in the way outsiders might understand it.

If a person were swept into the current, I would not shout into the roar. I would not stand tall and call them back with a name the air cannot carry. I would find their line. I would untie their knot from the wrist or waist. Then I would take it to the basin with the small whirlpool and place it in the turning water. If the chamber took it, all would know. If it returned, no one would know what that meant except as a sign to keep watching. I would touch the stone beside the basin afterward, once, with two fingers. That is enough. Mourning would be a pause in the handwork, a body not reaching for the nearest anchor, a stillness that says: *for this breath, I let the ledge hold me instead of moving*.

I would be a teacher, if there are children here.

A child would come to me with wet cord and a basalt pin. I would not tell them to be brave. I would not say the place is beautiful. The place is not beautiful in the way a meadow is beautiful. It is precise. It is dangerous. It is young. I would take their small hand and show them how to make a knot that will not strangle the line when the salt swells it. I would press their palm to the stone and make them wait until they feel the faint tremor of a current changing below. I would point upward, briefly, to the daylight falling through the ceiling opening, then quickly away, because ordinary light can make a person forget that the sea is sideways and listening. I would teach them that to look too long at wonder is to lose the next few seconds.

Sometimes I would think I am not fully a person anymore.

Not because the chamber is magical, or because I have become a spirit of the water. It is simply that the Small Deep has taken away the habits of the wider world. I cannot stand and speak. I cannot lie down and tell a story through the night. I cannot name a thing with one breath and expect it to remain named. My body has become a set of responses: pressure in the chest, tug at the wrist, salt on the lips, stone under the heel. If the wall of rain bows outward, I lower myself. If the mist smells older, I brace. If the sound changes from roar to roar, I read it. I am less a person and more a series of knots tied around a living body.

But I would not be only human.

I would be part of the entanglement. I would belong to the algae ledge because I scrape it with bone. To the shrimp pool because I leave it. To the eel’s dark current because I know when not to disturb it. To the moth’s wing because I place it on my line. To the whirlpool because I release the dead there. To the trapped bubbles because, once, I put my hand inside one and felt impossible dryness, and I knew the chamber had shown me what I most need: a brief, false, tender promise of air.

If I had to choose the exact place where I would be found, I would choose the narrow passage between the split pillar and the hut, where the rope line dips close to the water-wall. There is a low platform there, just big enough for two kneeling bodies, and from it I can touch the pillar and also see the ceiling light without standing too high. The stone is slick, but the old lashing knots are bright and new. There is a basin for collecting salt runoff. There is a place where someone has wedged a pebble with a beetle’s shell beneath it, marking the tide direction. There is a thread hanging with three knots: mine, my child’s, my mother’s, if mothers mean the line of hands that taught mine.

That is where I would be.

I would be at the Small Deep’s edge of survival, crouched on borrowed stone, reading pressure with my skin, mending the hut’s roof with one hand while testing the next hold with my thumb. I would be the one who does not look too long at the impossible room full of ocean, because the room has already looked at me, and I have learned that looking must be careful.

If someone asked for my name, I would not speak it.

I would draw a hand over my ear, because the chamber is full of roaring water. I would open my palm toward the ceiling light, because the light is ordinary and terrible and bright. I would let my fingers fall slowly downward, first as rain, then as rising sea, then as breath, then as the knot tied into the end of a line.

That is the sign of the Small Deep.

That is my name.
