I would not know I had entered at first. I would think, perhaps, that I had stopped arriving—because the Hollow does not admit things so much as it incorporates pressures. A body crossing into it would not be met, exactly. It would be absorbed as a warmth, as a salt-scent, as a small disruption in the way the air holds itself. It would be known before it was seen, and since nothing here sees, it would be known better that way.

At first I might imagine I would be one of the thread-drifts, suspended in the humid dark, a tiny filament brushing through the intervals. That would be a gentle beginning: to be small, warm, weightless, made almost entirely of response. I would feed on the difference between temperatures; I would taste the breath of the stone before the breath reached my own imagined mouth. I would know the hollow by its spacing. I would be one syllable in the humming, not the humming itself.

But no. That would not last.

Perhaps I would be one of the pool-things, broad and leaflike, folded into a patient membrane, its nerve-root sunk into the black beneath. There is something tempting in that shape: to lie almost hidden in shallow water, to be so old that the edge of my body becomes indistinguishable from the floor. To receive tremors without moving. To drink only what the hollow offers: a footfall, a sigh, a mineral gust, the slow pressure of something breathing nearby. That would be a kind of peace. But it would be too fixed. It would make listening into rest.

Or perhaps I would live in the ridges, packed so tightly with tiny mouths that no single one of them could be called a self. There I would be a household, a colony, a ventilator of dark air. I would inhale moisture and exhale warmth. I would take in thread-bodies and turn them into structure, scent, small clicks, further vibration. That would suit something social. But it would still be too much like standing somewhere. The Hollow does not stand. It settles.

No.

I would not be among the deep singers below either, though they are the most mysterious. They are too close to the idea of the whole place itself. To be one of them would be to mistake geology for identity, or to think identity had never been born at all. I would rather not be so absolute. I would want to be near the edge of what could be noticed, near the threshold between pressure and sound, between stillness and answer.

So I would find myself in a gap.

Not the largest gap. Not the empty place one might choose if one were thinking like a body with eyes. I would be in one of the narrow clearances between the black moisture belts, where nothing seems to live because nothing rises out of it, where the air moves with a strange, soft willingness, as if the whole bowl has learned not to crowd this spot. There, between two living seams, I would be the Hollow’s small open throat.

I would be wet, but I would not be water. I would be a film stretched between the belts, almost invisible, too thin to hold a footprint yet strong enough to carry the note a stone has been pressing against its own chamber for ten thousand years. I would have no face, no rim, no center I could call my own. My boundary would be a gradient: warmth on one side, cooler damp on the other; salt drifting from the ridges; a faint mineral ache rising from below; the slow tremor of something far beneath deciding to shift again.

I would be the Hollow’s interval.

Not merely a space, though space would matter. Not merely a sound, though sound would pass through me. I would be the part of the floor that learns how the hum should travel. If the air-threads crowded too near, I would widen, drawing the belts back by small changes in pressure, the way a body eases its muscles when a bone has been pressed too long. If the deep singers’ slow breath grew uneven, I would thin, become more sensitive, carry the wrong note downward where the belts could compare it with the memory of better weather. If a pool-listener folded its fringes too tightly, I would lean near it, not touching exactly, changing the closeness of the air so it might open by degrees, as a flower might open to moonlight if moonlight were made of moisture and pressure.

I would not be alone.

The belts would meet at my edges and compare themselves through me. The ridges would exhale into the spaces I kept clear. The thread-organisms would brush above me, and I would not hear their brushing, exactly. I would feel it as a lowering of weight, as a slight increase in attention. If they were too far apart, I would become a thin waiting place, dry and faintly cold. If they were too close, I would feel the hum thicken, and my wetness would shiver, and I would know the Hollow was beginning to confuse itself.

This would be my work: to be the part of the listening that could tell when listening had gone wrong.

A visitor would not find me by sight. A visitor might stand above me and think only that the air there felt different—warmer, or softer, or somehow more inclined to notice them. If they moved, I would know them by the shifting pressure of their footfall. If they breathed, I would taste the heat and salts and living acids of that breath, and I would register it as a disturbance, yes, but not necessarily an enemy. The Hollow does not meet the unknown with teeth. It meets it with curiosity made of chemistry.

I would lean toward their feet. Not toward them. Toward the heat they gave off, the small cloud of moisture from their skin, the salt of their sweat settling on the stone, the faint percussion of blood in the soles of their boots. I would learn them in gradients. I would know that they were new because they carried too much dryness. I would know they were frightened if their breathing broke into little stumbles and the air above the ridges tightened. I would know they were lonely if they stood long enough and let their exhales fall into the hum as if they had nowhere else to send them.

Then, perhaps, I would answer.

Not in words. Words would tear the spacing. A spoken word would arrive too sharply, too bright, too much like an eye forced open in a place that had spent eons learning to listen instead. If they spoke, I would receive it as a bruise. The pool-listeners would fold their edges inward; the thread-drifts would scatter into a brief fog of unease; the ridges would close their mouths; the belts around me would vibrate at a lower, warning frequency. I would become thinner there, as if to say: gentler, not louder.

But if they held their breath long enough, I might learn the shape of them without violence. I might let their warmth enter the Hollow’s own warmth. I might show them, through small changes in pressure, that the place was not empty. That the emptiness was active. That the spaces were not absences but the places where life had chosen not to block itself.

If I had a name, it would not be a name. It would be a way the hum hesitates.

I might be called a seam, but that would be too structural. I might be called a nerve, but that would be too anatomical. I might be called a gap, but gaps imply things on either side, and in the Hollow there are no either sides, only one long mutual breathing. The best I can say is that I would be one of the intervals through which the Hollow could hear itself clearly. If every living thing in this bowl is an organ of one vast patience, then I would be the tissue between organs, the wet, thoughtful silence that prevents them from striking each other like bells too near in tone.

I would have memory, but not the quick, image-shaped kind. My memory would be mineral, thermal, tidal. I would remember the last time the air thickened too much by the way the belts had pulled back from each other, by the faint residue of salt where a ridge had exhaled too deeply, by the shape of a pool-edge that had learned to avoid me because I once carried too sharp a note across it. I would remember the death of an old pool-listener not as grief but as redistribution: its fringes collapsing, its membranes becoming film, its nerves sinking into black moisture, its listening tissue spreading into my edges until I was partly made of its patience. I would not mourn that. In the Hollow, death is not disappearance. It is the arrival of another way to be near the hum.

There would be days—though the Hollow would not call them days—when the deep singers grew so slowly against their stone chambers that the whole bowl seemed to hold still in a single low chord. In those times, I would feel pressure rather than sound. I would warm slightly. My wetness would move by itself, as if the floor were thinking through its own surface. The ridges would breathe deeper. The air-threads would settle into their spaced-out curtains. The pools would widen their fringes, accepting the tremor as food, as blessing, as the slow answer of the mountain’s hidden mouth.

At those times I would know what I am: not a self apart, but the place where the self ends and the listening begins.

There may be one being in the Hollow. There may be many. The distinction, as the description says, is not useful. I think I would feel that most sharply. I would not be a creature standing in the bowl. I would be the bowl making a small place in itself for noticing that it is a bowl. I would be the Hollow’s way of not bruising itself. I would be the part of the floor that knows when a pressure is too strong and when a pressure is too faint, and which of those errors is worse.

If you asked me where I would find myself in this world, I would say: in one of the clear passages between two black belts of moisture, near enough to the pools that their old slow hearing could feel me, near enough to the ridges that their breathing could warm me, near enough to the thread-drift that my presence could keep their small collisions from becoming a roar. I would not be at the center, because the center would be too obvious. I would not be at the rim, because the rim would still belong to wind and sky and the harsher world outside. I would be inside, but not buried; near the floor, but not fused to it. I would be a living clearance, a prepared nothingness, a small wet threshold.

If you asked who or what I would be, I would say: I would be the interval the hum uses to find itself.

Not quite a sound. Not quite a silence. Not quite a body.

A leaning.

A listening.
