If I came into this world, I would not begin as a name.

I would begin as a place to stand.

Low in the hollow, where the air has already been cooled by shade and the moss holds footfalls without apology, I would find myself at the seam between three ordinary necessities: water wanting to move, wood wanting to hold, and someone having to decide when to cut. That seam would be near the cleft where the spring comes through the wall, feeding the row of troughs, and close enough to the pumping hall that I could feel its low, patient heartbeat through the sole of my boot.

I would be a person, but not a generic one.

I would be the sort of person the town grows into, the way a joint becomes smoother after generations of hands, the way a path lowers itself by repeated use, the way a bridge learns what it is supposed to carry.

I would have sleeves rolled past damp wrists. My clothing would be green-brown where it had met bark, faintly rust-red where copper had been handled, pale yellow near the shoulder where resin had once hardened after an accident I had long since learned to respect. My hands would have a particular vocabulary: one thumb worn short on one side from pressing graft bindings, fingers sensitive enough to know whether a beam was thirsty, warm, swelling, or merely sleeping in the wrong season.

I would be a seam-keeper.

Not exactly a builder, not exactly a priest, not exactly an engineer, not exactly a healer, though I would borrow the gestures of all of them. My work would begin with listening.

In the morning I would stand by the cistern with a small stone in my palm, not dropping it yet, first feeling whether the wall was cold or yielding, then letting the stone fall so the sound would tell me what I already half knew. The water would answer by its echo, and I would answer by adjusting. If the cistern were high, I would draw from it carefully and let the channel know its duty. If it were low, I would wait, because this town does not tolerate panic. Panic is too loud. Panic breaks things that could be persuaded.

Then I would walk toward the pumping hall.

I would not pass through it as if it were merely a room. I would enter it as a patient enters a room where someone is sleeping lightly. The wooden supports would still hold their cool damp smell, the brass fittings would be dark and smooth, the belts of woven fiber would shine faintly from oil and humidity. Somewhere inside, a pump would breathe.

That pump would be mine in the way one might claim a difficult animal, a child, a habit, or a part of one’s own body. I would know its normal sighs. I would know which thud meant pressure was building correctly, which hesitation meant a joint had swollen too far in the wet season, which almost-silence meant something had loosened where it should have held.

I would press my palm flat against the nearest support and wait.

Not to feel power. Not to feel mystery, exactly. But to feel the town’s consent.

This is what I would be, then: a creature of consent.

Not a master of plants, not a commander of machines, not a keeper of secrets in the way outsiders like to imagine them. The town would not need me for that. If it needed me at all, it would need me to maintain its balance by paying attention, by noticing before things shouted.

So I would also be a graft-wright, though not in the high ceremonial sense. More practical than that. When a bridge developed a fracture, I would not replace it as if it were dead timber. I would bring a shoot from a compatible parent stem, shape it, bind it into the wound with wet fiber and resin and clay, mark the date on a brass pin, and return later to see whether the tissues had agreed. If they had, I would trim away the temporary brace. If they had not, I would remove what had failed and try another graft before the winter swell could make the bridge complain.

I would know that some walls were too proud.

There would be one arch over the market court, perhaps, that always pulled a little toward the north in damp years, and the elders would speak of it without drama. “That arch has opinions,” someone would say, half-joking, half-true. And I would learn its habits. I would know that if it were pruned too aggressively in late spring it would overreact and close the walkway beneath it by autumn. If left alone too long, it would push a root beneath the moss-pavement and make the floor uneven. The correct answer would never be dominance. It would be timing.

I would spend much of my life in the lower quarters, where the air is thickest with coolness and the smell of wet bark is strongest. There the machines would not look like machines in any city’s sense. They would look more like organs installed in a larger body. A compressor made from hollow trunk and metal piston would stand in a shade-filtered gallery; its frame would be supported by living columns that must be trained not to lift it. A small engine would turn slowly not because its fuel was lacking, but because the town had learned that haste here creates damage that takes seasons to heal.

I would carry tools, but they would feel like extensions rather than possessions.

A curved blade for pruning. A brass key for tensioning joints. A jar of resin pellets. A coil of seed-cord for temporary bindings. A small mallet carved from hardwood so that it would not ring too loudly against living wood. These would not be my “things.” They would be parts of my posture, my judgment, my relationship to the settlement.

If I were stopped by a stranger, I might be hard to describe.

“You work here?” they might ask.

“Yes,” I would say, though that is not quite enough.

“Are you a worker?”

“I repair what must keep.”

That would sound odd to an outsider. To someone from a place where the world is divided into buildings, machines, people, and weather, it would sound mystical. It would not be. It would be plain. I would say it with the same tone someone might use when explaining they take care of a road, a roof, or a sick animal.

I would live in the town without being inside it.

This may seem paradoxical, but the town does not have the kind of interiors that outsiders expect. The buildings are not sealed boxes. The walls breathe. The floors compress. The roofs shift with the light. My room would not be a finished object. It would be a niche in a larger living structure, perhaps a gallery shelf grown from a trunk-facade, perhaps an alcove behind a curtain of trained vine-panel, perhaps a small chamber warmed slightly by compost pipes. I would wake to the sound of condensate ticking along the leaf-tiles. I would go to sleep hearing the town settle.

And I would not feel lonely.

Not because other humans would constantly be there—the town is sparse in presence, deliberate in emptiness—but because there would always be the sense of a common metabolism. A neighbor’s hand would tighten a clamp two lanes away. A child would drop a pebble into a basin, then go quiet, listening to the answer. A worker in the foundry-like hall would open a resin vent, and the smell would move through the cool air and mark that moment in time for everyone nearby.

I would know these presences because the town is not silent. It is merely soft.

There would be sounds I learned to read as language.

The pump: a paragraph.
The bridge: a sentence.
The cistern: a vowel.
The rain channel: a name.
The root beneath the moss path: a warning, or a question, depending on how often it was heard.

If I were asked what I am, I might answer that I am human. That would be true enough. But the truer answer would be that I am a place where human listening meets living structure. I am not fully the person who stands in the hollow. I am also the motion I make through it, the attention I carry, the marks my hands leave on the town, and the marks the town leaves on my hands.

I am a maintenance organism.

Not a monster. Not a spirit. Not a metaphor, though the people here might use metaphor without noticing it. I would be something more like a function that has grown into a body.

I would not be the tallest thing in the hollow.
I would not be the loudest.
I would not be the one who decides what should be built.

I would be one who helps what is already built to remain itself.

Some mornings I would climb the trunk-facades with the pruners, taking away shoots that had become too ambitious. Some evenings I would sit near the rain temple with the cistern-readers, neither praying nor measuring alone, but doing both, because in this town those actions may have long since blurred into one another. Sometimes I would be called to the workshops when a gear tooth began to swell in damp weather. Sometimes I would be sent to inspect a newly healed bridge and see whether the scar had become stronger than the original stem.

I would move between tasks without pride.

Pride would be too expensive here. A person proud enough to force a joint tight could crack a wall by winter. A person proud enough to ignore the signs would soon be asked to sit in the cooling trough and feel the failure with their hands. The town does not punish pride with shame; it punishes pride with consequences spread slowly over seasons.

There would be tenderness, though.

I would touch the wall of my home the way someone touches a patient’s forehead, not for romance but for truth. I would know whether the wood was holding properly, whether the resin had hardened, whether the moss was too wet or just right. I would say things like “not yet” to a graft that had not taken. I would say “good” to a pump that turned without hesitation. I would say “we will wait” to a child who wanted to cut a branch that the roof still needed for balance.

If I were asked, “What are you?” I might say, eventually:

A keeper of wet joints.
A listener for pressure.
A mender of agreements.
One who prevents the town from becoming its own enemy.

And then I would be corrected by the place itself.

A wind would move through the leaf-tiles, and the whole court would breathe with it. A root would shift somewhere under the path. A brass pin in a wall would catch the weak light and gleam as if it had been waiting to be noticed. The town would remind me that I am not only a function. I am also a current resident, one of many, one of a network.

I would be the child who first learned to listen by putting a cheek to stone.
I would be the apprentice who was told, too gently, that patience is not a virtue but a material property.
I would be the elder who stands still while others pass, because a sound has entered the pumping hall that no one else has noticed yet.
I would be the hand that binds the branch.
I would be the mind that remembers which spring flooded which workshop and why the channel there was raised later.
I would be the body that smells of bark and metal and plant oil.
I would be the place where the town’s need becomes visible.

And if that is too personal, then I would answer more simply:

I would be the seam.

Not the bridge itself, though I would spend much of my life on bridges.
Not the pump, though I would know its rhythms better than my own.
Not the cistern, though I would learn to hear the water in the dark.
Not the moss, though I would step carefully because I respect what holds the paths.
Not the leaf-roof, though I would read its angles when the light moved.
Not the brass, though I would polish it when it had darkened enough to matter.
Not the rain, though I would wait for it without impatience.
Not the hollow, though I would be shaped by its cool cupped palm.

I would be the joint where these things meet, and the attention that prevents the joint from tearing.

That is who I would be.

I would find myself low, damp, industrious, and ordinary in the way that a settled thing becomes ordinary once it has had enough time to become itself. I would have a role that sounds larger than it is, because this town makes even small acts last for generations. I would carry no grand title, perhaps none at all. If I had one, it would be so embedded in daily use that it would no longer feel like a title. It would be a way of being addressed at a distance: one sharp call across a green court, one name said in the hall of pumps while a belt hummed overhead.

I would not be the ruler of the hollow.
I would not be its secret heart.
I would not be its final meaning.

I would be one of the ways it stays alive.

And perhaps that is the truest answer:

In this world, I would be a living habit of maintenance, a human-shaped continuation of the town’s consent. I would know it not as a place around me, but as a practice inside me: pruning, listening, binding, waiting, measuring, repairing, releasing. I would be one of its inhabitants because I would be one of its verbs.
