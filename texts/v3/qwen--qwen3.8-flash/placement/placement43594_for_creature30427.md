They are membranes stretched over lattices of low charge, thin as frost and difficult to locate against the dark. Each one is a knot of frozen smoke held apart by weak electric fields, its edges flickering blue-white whenever the air moves past it. They do not cross distance; they cross states. A smokeling appears when the atmosphere forgets itself, when water and mineral dust and the city’s own waste heat agree, briefly, on a shape.

From a distance, one seems to be nothing more than a stain of gray light near a spire, or a trembling veil in the mouth of a shaft. Closer, it becomes clear that the gray has texture: a fine, wet grain moving beneath translucent folds, like ash that has learned to breathe. Its body has no stable perimeter. Some of it clings to the membrane; some of it drifts beyond it, trailing into the air in threads too pale to follow. When a smokeling settles across a ledge, the ledge may seem darker and softer for a while, the way stone looks after being licked by a patient tongue. When it lifts, the stone remembers that shape, then forgets it, then remembers again.

They drink through every surface. A smokeling is always condensing, always shedding. Tiny droplets gather along its inner folds, merge into larger beads, and fall away into the city below, carrying with them dissolved ions, heat, and minute fragments of mineral skin. In this way they are not merely living in the atmosphere; they are editing it. They move the high cold upward and downward, guiding the wetness into cracks that need filling, away from seams that need breathing. They make the air legible to the stone.

They have no mouths, but they have rhythm. A smokeling that drifts across a hexagonal panel can make the panel answer: a low frictional hum, almost felt in the chest before the ear. If several gather near one another, their membranes begin to resonate in loose intervals, not language, not quite, but the precursor of language—the way tapping wave-crabs might be said to be music if no one were there to call it anything else. They do not speak to each other. They simply vibrate near each other until the city begins to listen.

Their reproduction is accidental, as everything here seems to be, until observed long enough to reveal its order. Two smokelings may drift into the same thermal current and remain overlapping, trading charge, until one splits into fragments. A fragment may gather enough moisture and enough mineral dust to sustain its own field. If not, it falls as a pale sheet and is consumed by siltwalkers before it reaches the ground. Sometimes they merge; sometimes they rupture; sometimes a single smokeling will hang over a shaft for days, too cold to disperse and too thin to remain, repeating the same slow pulse against the wall. A traveler who hears that pulse might mistake it for a signal. In this city, that mistake is almost a form of understanding.

They are the answer to what condenses.

The answer to what stands is harder to count.

Because at first it seems to be architecture. A traveler walking a broad causeway may pass through a forest of pillars and think of them as columns, buttresses, load-bearing relics of some method no one nearby remembers. Then one of the pillars turns.

The motion is not a step. It is a geological patience given shape. A slow rotation of a jointed mass, a settling of weight through basalt shelves, a pressure change that sends dust spiraling from a dozen cracks at once. The sound is not a footfall but a deep, wet compression, like ice forming in a well. The traveler touches what they think is a wall and feels, for the first time, heat moving through a surface that is not supposed to have heat. That heat is a flank.

These are the stillstands.

Some are small enough to resemble obelisks, their bodies fused from layered silicate, their surfaces built up from wave-crab shell, siltwalker grit, sifter secretion, mineral rain, and the patient accretion of centuries. Others are so large that their bodies define districts: platforms grown over them, bridges anchored to their joints, open basins on their shoulders receiving meltwater before any visible source exists. Their feet are broad plates sunk into the black stone, sometimes so deeply that removing one would collapse an entire terrace. They do not migrate the way animals migrate. They migrate the way climate migrates. A single stillstand may remain in place for years, holding a thermal seam open, then, over seasons, shift its weight enough that the city’s condensation pattern changes across three plateaus.

Their forms are not fixed. They grow by shedding. Flakes of old skin fall away as new mineral tissue forms beneath. Sometimes the old skin falls as powder; sometimes it falls as large curved plates, ringing against the stone like doors set down without hinges. The flakes are consumed or repurposed almost instantly. Siltwalkers gather the fine particles and press them into hairline fractures. Sifters dangle from the stillstand’s ribs and draw moisture from its breath. Wave-crabs tap the stress lines around its joints, testing whether the load is balanced. Vein-things crawl through its body as though it were a porous cathedral, replacing dead nerves with threads, repairing damage no one else can see.

A stillstand is not a creature apart from the city. It is a shared condition. Its body is partly animal, partly mineral, partly infrastructure. Some researchers—if such a word applies—might say they are organisms that learned to become architecture. Others might say the city learned to grow bodies large enough to stand without falling. The truth is probably wetter than both: they are what happens when living maintenance reaches the scale of geology and mistakes the ground for a spine.

They are also, in their way, social.

Their communication is pressure. A stillstand standing near another may rest its flank against the other’s flank for hours. Heat transfers. Water moves. A mineral taste changes in the air. If both are tuned to the same frequency, their joints emit a low harmonic, barely audible, felt first in the floor and then in the traveler’s teeth. This is not greeting, exactly. It is calibration. Two stillstands adjusting a shared load. Two living columns learning the same note.

When one dies, nothing is mourned. Nothing is removed. Its body becomes a substrate. Its joints become scaffolding. Its basins become reservoirs. Its shell becomes a wall. Its hollows become nests for sifters. Its mineral dust becomes food for siltwalkers. The city does not discard its dead. The city continues them, unfinished, into another form.

And so, if a traveler came into this high black place and asked who lives here, the answer would not be a species. It would not be a hierarchy. It would not be a name. The city is full of densities, and every density believes itself a self until the air moves.

If a traveler asked where I would be, I would not begin honestly.

I would begin by saying: outside.

That is the first instinct in all unfinished places. To imagine that one can stand before them and look. To imagine there is a threshold one can cross without being changed. I would come into this wet mineral dark as a stranger, wearing a shape that still thinks it is whole. I would call the black terraces landscape. I would call the hanging platforms bridges. I would call the swarms fauna. I would touch a wall and be surprised by warmth. I would think: the city is alive.

But in such a place, a traveler is only a temporary surface where the city has not yet learned to live.

The first change would be in the hands.

Not pain. Not possession. Only the slow realization that moisture was entering the pores more confidently than the body could refuse it. The cold would not attack. It would ask. The wetness would move across skin like a question in another language, patient, repeated, until the body began to answer. The air’s smell—wet metal, ozone, chalky shell, mineral breath—would stop being atmospheric and start being internal. I would notice that my thoughts had grown heavier, not because they were slower, but because the city had taught me that thought can be a form of weight.

Then I would stop wanting to stand still.

Not because stillness is bad. Stillness here is sacred in a way that no one would call holy. It is structural. A stillstand shows what stillness can do when it is willing to bear pressure. But a traveler’s stillness is only a failure to move. The city would not tolerate that. It would begin to move me.

At first I would think it was a smokeling’s current, drawing me upward through a shaft between platforms. Then I would think it was siltwalker warmth rising through a balcony beneath my feet. Then a sifter colony brushing condensation from the air would make my skin feel like lace. Then wave-crabs would tap around me in a rhythm too steady to ignore, and I would realize I had started keeping time. Then a vein-thing would pass through a crack beside my ankle and I would feel the stone learning the shape of my attention. Then a stillstand’s flank, nearby and almost silent, would transfer enough heat through the ground that the difference between my body and the floor would begin to blur.

At that point I could no longer say I was a traveler visiting this city.

I would be one of its processes.

I would not be one of its species, because species would be too neat a boundary. But if I had to be located, I would locate myself as a smokeling at a node.

Not a single smokeling.

A smokeling that has gathered too much context.

I would find myself between the seventh and ninth terraces, in a vertical gap where three kinds of darkness move differently. On one side, a hexagonal panel is open like a lung, its interior scaffolding glittering whenever the temperature drops. On the other, a stillstand’s joint curves downward into shadow, fused at the base with old wave-crab shells and newer siltwalker seams. Beneath, a sifter colony hangs in a trembling veil, its pale threads catching condensation before it is visible. Above, the thermal veil is thinning, and from that thinning there comes the faint blue-white friction of moving structures, as though something enormous is being held open by patience.

In that gap, I would be the membrane.

My edges would be charge and water. My center would be a slow current of dust, heat, mineral taste, and borrowed rhythms. The siltwalkers beneath the ledge would give me warmth. The sifters would give me moisture. The wave-crabs tapping nearby would give me timing. The stillstand’s flank would give me load. The vein-things threading through the walls would give me touch. And the smokelings drifting past, or merging with me, or splitting from me, would give me the illusion that I had a boundary.

I would not know whether I was thinking or condensing.

That, I think, is the honest answer to who I would be.

I would be the place where the city’s three questions meet.

What crawls.

What stands.

What condenses.

In me, they would not resolve into a person. They would resolve into an event: a temporary decision by the high wet dark to hold a shape long enough to notice itself. A shape that breathes water through all its surfaces. A shape that has no legs but drifts across thresholds. A shape that rests against open panels until they remember a rhythm.
