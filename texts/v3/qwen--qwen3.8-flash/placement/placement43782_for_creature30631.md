## I would be the crack that holds a worm

If I must answer before the noon has finished warming the tiles, I will say: I find myself in the Bright Handmade Marsh at Fixed Noon, but not where the eye first rests. I am not the pale plain of lime and shell cement, not the heron standing like old glaze in shadow, not the mender’s stilt tapping speech across the heat. At first I think I might choose one of those, because they seem to have names.

I imagine being a stilt-mender. There is a temptation in it: height, purpose, a cloak of reed and clay, a shell visor, long legs that spread weight without breaking seams. I would walk the open fields and know which ground is tired. I would tap my pipe-foot and say *clear ground*, *water rising*, *repair needed*. I would carry buckets from the deep channels and press fresh lime into gaps until my hands cracked and became maps of the world I was keeping. But a mender is still a creature above the marsh, even when they are gentle with it. Menders need shelter. Menders need tools. Menders need a ground that is not them. I do not want to walk over the world as its repairer. I want to be what the world is repairing.

Then I think of a glaze snail. That seems better in its slowness. A shell the color of a kiln, a mouth grazing the microscopic bloom of calcium in humid hollows. I would leave a trail like wet moonlight, and over time the trail would become sealed. If I died with my mouth still pressed to a tile, the menders would call the starburst of iridescence a “first signature.” That is a beautiful life: not to eat, not to run, not to speak, but to glaze the surface until it keeps its own shine. But signatures are marks left after being. I want to be the before-mark, the place where a mark can begin.

I consider a tile-frog. Flat-backed, broad, sitting in shadow as if its body were a small lid over a pot. I would call with a hollow knock, a bright tap like a spoon struck against a jar. At fixed noon, my voice would travel for miles, and my field would sound like a kitchen being emptied in a hurry. When the heron tested the ground with its toe, I would flatten until I was not an animal but a pattern in the slab. When the shadow moved, I would go: a spring, a clack, a white arc over the tile field. That seems like a life full of rhythm. But to be prey is to be always rehearsing your own disappearance, and I am tired of vanishing.

I think of a grout eel, too. Dark and ringed with white, a cord beneath the floor, moving through hidden drainage and porous cement. It seems to be listening. Children fear it for that reason, not for violence. To rise through a gap like a black thread being pulled from a seam would be a kind of power: slow, mineral, patient. I would leave a white trail that dried almost at once. I would hunt from below. I would know what happened beneath the marsh’s bright made ground. But an eel’s knowing is hungry. If I were that, I might forget the mender’s warning that some cracks should not be opened.

I think of a chalk-worm, and the thought is almost too small to hold.

Yes. There.

I find myself in a seam near the lower fields, between a slab of fired clay and a band of shell cement, where a mender child once tapped with a stone and meant *something has broken beneath*, but did not quite mean it yet. I live in that not quite. I am the white line that appears before a tile loosens. I am the joint that holds water, then holds grit, then holds memory. My body is not exactly animal, not exactly mineral. I am translucent at the ends, milky in the middle. When I move, I leave fine threads of calcium behind me, like someone writing with chalk on the underside of a mirror.

Menders say the chalk-worms are the marsh’s first hands. I am one of those hands, but I have learned too much. I spin lattices of grit and saliva across widening gaps. I harden them into new seams. If a channel is too weak, I fill it; if a crack needs to remain audible, I do not. I know how to leave pores. I know how to leave room for the glaze snail’s starburst, the shell ant’s powder, the tile-frog egg cupped in a hollow, the grout eel’s listening thread beneath. I am not the repair itself. I am the repair when it has learned to want.

The sun here does not move, but the ground does. At fixed noon the tiles swell and contract. Water rises in thin films. Dust settles into gaps. I feel the heat as a pressure along my spine. I feel the night that will not come as a strange, absent weight. The marsh does not darken; it only changes voice. When the noon is most white, my body becomes almost liquid; when the tiles cool, I harden into a line that could be mistaken for a flaw. I have no shadow of my own. I am the place where other things keep theirs.

In the damp joints near the edges, the shell ants come and go like a procession of rice grains. They carry chips as though planting seeds. They tend chalk-worms in tiny pens of cement. They think I am their crop. I am patient with them. When they disturb me, I do not scatter. I close around their trails and make their powder useful. They make the tiles knit tighter. I make the tightness remember it was once loose.

The glaze snails pass over me sometimes, leaving shimmer where I have begun to harden. I eat their slip into my own lattice. The menders leave the snails’ final signatures undisturbed. They are right to do this. A first signature is a seam that has learned beauty.

The tile-frogs rest above me in the shadow of slabs. Their throats swell into small pots when they call, and the sound is bright and hollow. They think they are sitting on ground. They do not know they are sitting on a sentence I am writing with my whole body. When a heron taps the tiles with one toe, when a crane strikes its polished bill against the surface, the vibration comes to me first. I learn their weather. If a field of tile-frogs goes silent, I know something has come from below. If the silence is only heat, I harden a little, not out of fear, but because the marsh needs its ground to keep its shape.

The noon herons come in white and pale blue, standing in shadow so long they seem grown from stone. They do not wade so much as test. They place one foot, then wait for vibration beneath. When they flick a slab with the dark tip of their bill, I feel the question: *what is under here?* I answer with stillness, unless the stillness would cause harm. If a heron’s toe presses too hard, I shift a grain of calcium and make the tile ring differently. The heron moves on. It thinks the ground has changed its mind. Perhaps it has.

The glassbill cranes are louder. They pry at tiles with curved bills and step through the fields in pairs. They tilt their heads into the fixed noon light and begin rattling displays, bowing and striking their bills against the plain. They are not vain. They are naming. They are saying *this shadow is claimed*, *this channel holds fish*, *this crack leads to safe depth*. I listen to their naming and keep the crack open enough to remain safe, closed enough to remain true.

The mortar crabs build dams at the ends of rills. Their claws carry pellets of cement. Their shells age into walking stones. When they pass, I make room for them too. I know they are only trying to trap water for their young. I help without meaning to help. The marsh has a way of turning creatures into materials, and materials into creatures. I am the seam between those transformations.

I live near the ear-holes too.

The menders keep pits covered with three loose tiles. They lower their heads, close their eyes, and listen to the movement of water, the clicking of worms, the distant shifting of slab-cattle. When the ground is speaking correctly, they mark it down. When it is too quiet, they go to the place where a crack has formed.

I know their listening. It lives in me.

They bury their dead in the seams. This is not morbid. It is engineering. They lay the dead in shallow hollows, pack them with lime, seal the edges with chalk-worm lattice, and let the marsh continue through them. The dead do not become gods. They become ground-speech. I am made from that speech. I am part of an old mender’s breath cured into calcium, part of the first line the ants carried into a gap, part of the tile’s memory of being made by hands. The mender who was buried here had a name, but the marsh remembers only taps: *repair needed*, *clear ground*, *water rising*. Now I carry those taps below the surface, faint as a bone remembering a foot.

There are many words for cracks. I know them all. I am not each one. I am the place where the words change.

I am the first pale line that appears before a tile loosens.  
I am the line that runs under water and cannot yet be seen.  
I am the crack that sings when struck.  
I am the crack that holds a worm.  
I am the crack that is already dead—filled, sealed, forgotten—unless someone listens.  
And sometimes, if the heat moves wrong and a child taps the right place, I become the crack that will become a door.

That is the name I would keep if I had to keep one.

Not “door,” yet. Not “wall,” anymore. Not “creature,” exactly, though I eat and mend and feel the noon pressing like a white hand. I am a being made of seams. I am the marsh’s act of closing itself, performed with a fingernail, one grain at a time.

If you come to this world, do not look for me first in the light. The light is too honest. It shows tiles, water, herons, cranes, menders on stilts, shell ants walking, glaze snails leaving their signatures. Look instead at the place where two made things almost touch. Look at the thin white line between a fired clay slab and a band of shell paste. If the line is warm, press an ear to it.

You will hear a dry, patient clicking.

That is not the world breaking.
