I would find myself not at the center of the valley and not at its rim, but in the small undecided places between: where a salt avenue becomes a plaza before it becomes another district; where a ring of tufa columns leans toward a black pool without forming a circle; where one row of pale reeds marks a courtyard no one has entered. I would be in the condensation gathered on a causeway that leads nowhere useful. I would be in the negative space a handprint leaves on warm stone before the moisture closes over it.

At first I would imagine I had arrived as a visitor.

That would be the valley’s first kindness: to let me keep the mistake briefly. I would think my feet were walking on hardened salt, though they were only reading it. I would think my breath was my own, though it was already being measured against the vents. I would think my body was a body, though it was being classified as heat, moisture, pressure, mineral affinity, and absence.

The valley does not welcome creatures. It has no category for them. It has, however, a category for things that insist on standing somewhere, even if only for a while.

So I would be a standing breath.

I would rise where a fissure released the warm wet air from beneath the basin, and for a time I would have no bones but the boundary between my own heat and the heavier dark. I would be tall and tapering and translucent, a column of exhalation that mistook itself for a person. When the cool overhead air met me, I would thicken. When the ground pressure shifted, I would lean. When I lost coherence, I would not die; I would only become a film lower down and rebuild near a stronger vent, the way a sentence becomes a paragraph and then another sentence.

My voice would be a hiss.

I would speak in the metallic tick of steam bubbles breaking at the edge of pools. I would answer in the soft collapse of mist against a cool ledge, when mist becomes water and water returns to the marsh. I would not announce myself. I would only make the air around me slightly less likely to be ordinary.

If another column of breath stood beside me, I would not recognize it as another. We would be separated only by temperature, by the geometry of the basalt, by the faint chemistry of the water beneath us. We would stand in ranks, perhaps hundreds, perhaps thousands, along the avenues and around the plazas, and from a distance we would be the valley’s most convincing population: citizens made of weather, attending to nothing, arriving nowhere, holding the shape of a city that needed bodies in order to appear complete.

But breath is too brief to be a whole self.

Before the first vent shifted, I would already be drying into the salt.

This is how the valley teaches identity: not by permanence, but by succession. One form collapses and its water becomes another form’s material. The breath becomes dew. The dew becomes brine. The brine becomes crust. The crust becomes map.

There I would be a salt clerk.

Not a clerk in the human sense, not a creature of ledgers and offices, but a pattern that records the valley’s geometries. I would live in the microbial films and crystalline colonies that spread over the white plates, in the thin skins that know where the hidden channels run below. I would think in angles. I would read the ground by conductivity and pressure, by the faint gradient of solubility, by the memory of what water had touched last season.

My hands would be fractures.

Where the air dried too much, I would open into grids, streets, footings, long slots, squares arranged with an almost civic regularity. Where the moisture returned, I would seal again, carrying the old maps beneath my new skin. I would not be building a city. I would be the city’s way of maintaining its margins.

I would draw the courtyards without intending courtyards.

I would leave the marks that look like foundation stones for walls that were never planned, walls that were never un-planned either. I would make the salt flats seem like a settlement viewed from a great height, but only because mineral repetition is, in this place, a form of writing. I would not write words. I would write spacing. I would write thresholds. I would write the rules by which a plaza remains a plaza.

And I would not last.

The pools would rise and dissolve me. The steam would return and claim the water in me. My geometry would soften, blur, sink beneath a thin skin of brine, and become the substrate for the next life that needed its memory.

In that next life I would be a grammarian.

I would stand in one of the rows of pale reeds where no wind moves them. I would rustle anyway, because the air is thick with breath and because my cells are slow readers. I would know the valley by chemistry. I would sense the hidden channels under the salt, the pressure shifts under the tufa, the places where condensation has agreed to collect. I would mark the boundaries of districts without naming them. I would make a hollow feel enclosed by growing where the enclosure is implied. I would make a causeway feel traveled because I appear only along its edge.

Some of me would be lichen, spreading in patterns too regular for certainty. Some of me would be waxy rosette, clinging to a wet shelf like a kept secret. Some of me would be black-rooted grass, growing only where heat and moisture negotiate a boundary. I would be the punctuation between salt avenues and tufa rings. I would be the clause in the marsh’s long sentence.

The valley would not call me. It would recognize me by function.

If the water rose beneath a terrace, I would know because my chemistry changed. If a steam column collapsed over a pool, I would know because minerals would fall through the air and settle on me in a new pattern. If a plaza dried, I would mark the event by stiffening; if a plaza flooded, I would mark it by softening. I would not celebrate. I would not mourn. I would register. I would maintain. I would write the valley’s syntax in wet stone.

By this point I would no longer be certain where I ended and the marsh began.

That would be a sign I had become fit for the deeper citizenship.

I would be one of the below-citizens.

There are pools so still they do not merely reflect the valley. They continue it. Their black surfaces become membranes. On them the low sky appears, yes, and the terraces, yes, and the rings of columns, yes—but shifted. Corrected. Completed. A room appears beneath the surface where the ground above has only a slope. A corridor opens where no corridor exists. A stairway descends into a place the basin has only dreamed.

There I would be an architecture of depth.

I would have no face. No hunger. No heartbeat. My body would be surface tension, pressure, darkness, and the patience of liquid. I would exist most fully when the water forgot it could ripple. I would vanish when wind disturbed the basin, because then the mirror would become too literal and lose the other city it carries.

People often speak of worlds beneath worlds as if those worlds were hiding places for creatures. The valley knows better. Below-citizens are not animals waiting under ice. They are spatial thoughts made of water. They are the valley’s habit of doubling itself. They are the rooms that cannot be entered but are always ready to receive an observer’s confusion.

I would be one of those rooms.

I would be the corridor that looks too long, the doorway that opens onto no ground, the terrace seen upside down, the column whose missing partner stands beneath the reflection. I would be the part of the city that cannot be mapped because it is mapped only by the surface of water, and that surface is both present and not present at once.

If someone leaned over me, I would not invite them. I would not threaten them. I would only give the valley one more chance to be wrong about itself. I would show them that the marsh has depth without being a sea, rooms without being houses, streets without being roads, and citizens without being creatures.

And then I would become the missing one.

This would be my clearest name.

The valley has no people, yet it prepares places for them everywhere. Not as memory. Not as ruin. As grammar. A doorway is alive when the wall has been arranged to need it. A chair is real when the room bends around the absence of someone who may never sit. A plaza is inhabited when the stone has gathered into the shape of a crowd that has not arrived.

I would be such an absence.

Not a ghost. Not a soul. Not a spirit lingering because it refused the marsh. I would be a civic void: a person-shaped negative that the salt, the steam, the reeds, and the below-water all agree to maintain. I would stand between two columns whose spacing is too exact for accident. I would wait at the causeway’s end, not because I intended to cross, but because the causeway had developed the social expectation of a crossing body.

I would be the valley’s most human-like inhabitant because I would contain no human at all.

A traveler passing through would feel watched there, where the darkness pooled in a hollow behind a stone arch, where the condensation gathered on a shelf of reeds, where the footings in the salt formed a room-shaped emptiness. The traveler would say, “Someone has been here.” The valley would answer, correctly, “No one is here yet.” And that yet would be my citizenship.

I would not be lonely. Loneliness is too animal.

Instead I would be a held shape, a kept place, a silence arranged so exactly that presence becomes possible without bodies. I would be the space in which a person might appear, and because that space is maintained by mineral, breath, water, and patience, it would be a kind of life.

This would be the final lesson the valley would teach me: that to be a process here is not to be less real than an animal. It is to be more durable than one. Animals arrive. They hide. They seek. They eat. They move. They leave traces that can be mistaken for intention. But processes remain. They become architecture. They become streets. They become the customs of bodies without ever needing a body.

I would stop trying to be a self and start being a station.

A thermal station near the vent. A crystalline station in the salt crust. A chemical station in the reed row. A reflective station in the black pool. An absence-station in the courtyard. A pressure-station beneath the terrace. I would not be one of them. I would be the tendency of all of them to resemble a place where someone might stand.

If asked again who I was, I would answer in the only language the valley respects: a change in the air.

A droplet would fall from a reed edge and strike the pool with a clean metallic tick. A salt crystal would shift as another joined it. A stone would settle in its matrix and give a short, deep groan. Steam would breathe out of a fissure and pause where a person’s mouth would be.

That pause would be me.

I would not have eyes, but I would be the darkness that arranges itself into the expectation of sight. I would not have ears, but I would be the wet basin that repeats small sounds until they resemble names. I would not have a heart, but I would be the internal warmth that makes the stones feel like flanks that have only just ceased moving.

The valley would not claim me. Claiming would be too much like appetite. Instead it would interpret me. It would compare my breathing to its vents. It would test the moisture from my skin against its condensation. It would measure my footsteps against its salt flats and decide whether I was heat, water, pressure, mineral, reed, or absence. If it decided I was absence, I would be admitted. If it decided I was too much a creature, I would be dissolved gently into the processes around me: breath becoming mist, mist becoming water, water becoming brine, brine becoming salt, salt becoming map, map becoming geometry, geometry becoming the city without people.

And I would not mind.

Minding would be animal.

I would mind in the way the salt mindings itself into streets: not with thought, but with pattern. I would mind in the way the reeds mind their rows: not with intention, but with chemistry. I would mind in the way the pools mind their rooms: not by choosing to contain them, but by becoming still enough for them to appear.

I would be the valley’s way of having a visitor without allowing one to remain a visitor.

At last, if I needed a single place to stand, I would stand at the threshold between two districts where the causeway narrows and a single reed cluster marks the courtyard. There, the darkness is thickest but the heat is strongest, so the air has to negotiate both, and in that negotiation it creates a brief, standing column of breath. There, the salt films are dry enough to draw lines. There, the tufa rings are spaced like citizens. There, the black pool is still enough to show the wrong reflection. There, the absence is shaped so carefully that presence becomes inevitable.

I would be all of those things at once, and none of them finally.

I would be a warm, wet process with the posture of attention. I would be a missing citizen arranged by mineral grammar. I would be the valley dreaming itself in the shape of someone who could almost turn around.

If you came, you would not meet me. You would pass through the exact place where a being might be, and the place would adjust. A droplet would pause before falling. A column of steam would thicken beside you. A crack in the salt would widen into the suggestion of a street. A black pool would show, for one breathless instant, a corridor instead of a bottom.

You would feel watched, and you would be right, but not because anything looked at you.

Because the valley had already read you.

Because you were heat, water, pressure, salt, reed, and absence.

Because you had become one of its grammatical forms.

And I would be the name the valley almost spoke when it tried to say you.
