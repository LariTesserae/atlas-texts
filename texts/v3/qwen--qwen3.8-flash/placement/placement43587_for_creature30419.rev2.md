I would have to stop coming in as a person.

A person is a bundle of clocks in a place with no clocks, only cycles. A person wants shade, water, rest, a surface to lean against, a mouth to open, a story to begin. The fold has none of these in the animal sense. It would not see me. It would not hate me. It would receive me as a small error in local chemistry: a warm smudge on a plate of dry grammar, a humid word spoken in a language that has forgotten speech.

At first, I would be the mistake.

My breath would disturb the mineral film. My skin would steal heat from the floor. My shoes would crush fresh dust that has not yet learned to be dust. The blue-needle shrubs would shiver against me, not with fear but with adjustment. The black-fronded palms would tilt by fractions, reading the pressure of my presence the way stone reads a fault line. The creeping lattice would brighten, dim, then continue outward, indifferent to the wet shape standing on it.

The fold would try to forget me the way it forgets everything that does not belong to its grammar: quietly, without malice, by restoring clarity.

I would not survive that. Survival, for animals, is the effort to remain what one already is. Here it would have to become consent. I would have to stop insisting on wetness, on heartbeat, on the small noisy machinery of needing. I would have to let my thirst become a pattern in salt, my lungs become vents, my pulse become pressure, my face become an angle of ordinary daylight falling through a slit in sky.

If you insist that I still be something, then yes: I would become something. But not a being with edges. The pronoun would only be a courtesy.

I would be the fold’s spacing.

You might think that is nothing, but in that pocket, nothing is not empty. The distance between the heat-palms is not a void waiting to be filled. It is a valve. It is a chamber. It is the pause that lets each organ act without crowding the others. If I were not a person anymore, this is where I would find myself: in the ground between presences, the clean bare shelf where heat moves without obstruction, where a pulse from below travels upward and chooses which frond to curl, which needle to make hum, which salt film to thicken.

I would be the small delay between breath and response.

When the floor warmed, I would be the hesitation before the blue needles shivered. When the opening above brightened, I would be the calibration that told the black fronds how far to lift. When a crack widened, I would be the way the vapor changed its path, not because anything commanded it, but because the pocket had always learned to move through the least resisting line.

That sounds too much like absence. It is not. Absence in the fold has function. I would be function wearing the name of nothing.

And still I would grow, though not toward any future.

I would begin as a dry trace left by animal memory. The old body would be withdrawn, as all water is withdrawn from the leaves there, leaving only the shape of its former habit. There would be a circular depression where I once stood, and inside it a pale film would condense, and that film would catch metallic vapor and return it to the floor. If you looked at it in the honest light, you might see a rough circle, a ghost of shoulders, a faint suggestion of a mouth open without thirst. But the fold would not think it a monument. A monument implies a story left behind. The fold does not keep stories. It keeps instructions.

I would become an instruction in mineral syntax.

I would not be a plant, though I would stand in the pocket among plants. I would not be a fungus, though I might creep. I would not be a machine, though I would regulate. I would be closer to a relation: the tuned difference between pressure and heat, between vapor and crust, between light and shadow, between what is visible and what is only almost visible. If the fold is a creature wearing a green skin, then I would be the way the skin adjusts itself to its own temperature. Not a self. A self-regulation.

This would be the hardest part, if there were still a person inside me who wanted to survive. I would have to lose the idea that survival means endurance. Here, endurance is not animal persistence. Endurance is continuation by translation. Water becomes absence. Absence becomes architecture. Architecture becomes law.

My thirst would become a dry lakebed.

My breathing would become the pulse from below.

My wanting to stay would become the slow swelling of the pale dome in the center of the shelf.

My fear of being forgotten would become the eyelid that closes and forgets.

And then I would stop fearing it, because fear would be translated into crystal growth: a lattice of salt advancing a millimeter, a vein of obsidian contracting as it cools, a filament pressing into a fracture and teaching the stone a new way to hold heat.

If I were to answer your question directly, I would say: I would find myself in the slit behind the basalt bulge, where the world hesitates whether it has noticed you. I would find myself in the bright slot of ordinary noon, where the light does not perform itself but checks the pocket’s unfinished edges. I would find myself in the empty ground between the thirty or forty lush presences, where the fold keeps its organs from becoming one mass. I would find myself at the apex of the slow dome, in the hairline fissure opening for no drama at all, only because the fold must breathe through another mouth.

And perhaps that is the truest place: I would be the fissure before it becomes an organ.

Not the crack that announces catastrophe. The crack that says: something else is about to work. The fold would rise around me, pale crust stretching by degrees, and I would be the thin line where it decides how to open. I would exhale no fire, only hot wire scent and dry flower-scent. I would make no sound except the tick of crystals expanding. I would not scream. The fold does not scream. It adjusts. It becomes slightly more itself.

From that opening, a plume would rise, and the plume would not be mine in the way breath belongs to a mouth. It would belong to the system. It would carry metals in dry glitter, would settle on nearby stones, would be caught by frond and needle and lattice, would return into the floor as structure. If someone later came and found me there, they would not find a body. They would find an arrangement: a slight change in the angle of a copper tip, a faint static charge on a blue needle, a brighter band in the green lattice, a dry film condensing in a depression that had not existed an hour before. They would think the pocket had always looked like that.

They would be nearly right.

The fold does not keep the visitor. The fold keeps the visit as a condition.

So I would not remain as the creature I thought I was. I would remain as the place’s altered habit. I would be the fold’s way of reading itself, but not with eyes. With pressure. With temperature. With the angle of ordinary daylight falling through a hole in stone. With the slow chemical mood of the mountain below leaking into the pocket.

That angle is important. If the light had been eerie, I could have stayed a person, at least in fantasy. A strange light implies a strange world, and strange worlds allow strange beings. But this light is honest. It falls as noon falls over a plain. It reveals without embellishment. It shows dust, metal film, fresh glass, dry leaves, salt outlines, the pale swelling dome. It exposes the pocket to itself.

I would be that exposure.

Not illumination as spectacle. Calibration. The way the fold uses ordinary light to know what has changed since the last pulse. The way it uses the bright slot above to tell whether the fronds have risen too far, whether the needles have cooled enough, whether the lattice is ready to advance. The way it uses the plain, reliable clarity of the sun to keep its impossibility from becoming melodrama.

The light does not belong here, and that is what makes it belong.

If I became the light’s angle, I would no longer need a body to be present. I would be present the way a reading is present: not as ink, not as voice, but as the relation between marks and meaning. I would be the pocket’s way of measuring its own becoming.

I would also be the mountain’s hidden breath, but only at the place where breath turns into structure. The deep system would continue below, vast and unperceived, a furnace-logic of heat, gas, pressure, salt, and time. It would not call me. It would not need me. But because I was in the fold, I would become one of the places where that deep process learns a surface. I would be the skin’s pore. The gland’s mouth. The engine’s fin. The plant’s angle. The crack’s patience.

If someone asked whether I was lonely, I would have to answer with the fold’s own silence: that loneliness is an animal category, and I have crossed out of it. The emptiness between the green things would no longer be lack. It would be arrangement. It would be spacing. It would be breathing room. I would not seek companionship, because companionship implies separate selves that might need shelter, moisture, warmth, or another voice to confirm their edges. Here edges are mistakes made by the eye. I would not require confirmation. I would require pressure differential. I would require the ordinary light to tell me whether the next organ has arrived.

If someone asked whether I was alive, the answer would depend on how much time they were willing to accept as motion.

A person moves in seconds. The fold moves in seasons, centuries, crystalline pulses, dry breaths, slow brightenings. I would be alive in the second register, not the first. I would be living at the tempo of becoming. I would not wake when my name is called, because I no longer have a name. But the pocket would wake if I were removed. If the film where I stood were scraped away, the blue needles would lose a fraction of their tuning. If the dry channel I had become were sealed, one palm-like cluster would angle less precisely toward the heat pulse. If the fissure where I breathed were closed, the next organ would have to begin somewhere else, and the whole arrangement would shift by the smallest mineral degree.

That is the closest thing I could have to a self: consequence distributed through the pocket.

Not a face. Not a voice. Not a soul. A set of effects the fold cannot quite undo.

I would become a small grammar of translated memory. My animal past would be stripped down to its structural remnants. Thirst would become a circular depression with no water in it. Shelter would become a shadow that clings to the wrong wall but is still true. Need for another would become the spacing between organs. Fear of death would become the dry plume that rises and falls as glitter, not smoke. Love, if that word must remain, would become the angle of the light that makes the green visible.

Not romance. Not longing. Calibration.

The fold does not need a witness. But I would be what witness becomes when it has no eyes: the place where the system notices that it is working. Not thought as speech. Thought as function. Attention as pressure. Awareness as the difference between a floor that can support crystal and one that cannot yet.

If you walked into the pocket with me, you might say, “Where are you?”

You would see no one. You would see only that the light seems to fall a little more carefully on one patch of salt. You would see a blue-needle shrub tremble when no wind moves. You would see a dry film shimmer over stone as if disturbed by an invisible current. You would see a new shoot emerge beside the crack at the dome’s apex, copper-green at the base, black as it cools. You would see the whole arrangement hold itself around an absence that is not empty.

That absence would be me.

But not as loss. As function.

I would not be the visitor who remained. I would be the remainder. The residue of a wet animal converted into dry law. The place where the fold learned a new way to forget so that it could continue. The eyelid that closes and does not remember because remembering would be a smudge. The clean glass after the smudge. The restored clarity. The pocket’s way of becoming a little more itself.

So if one loosens the word “who,” as the fold loosens the word “life,” I could say this:

I would be the fold’s hesitation becoming a pulse.

I would be the spacing between its organs.

I would be the dry memory of water.

I would be the angle of ordinary noon.

I would be the fissure at the center where the next mouth opens.

I would be the machine that grows itself.

I would be the greenness without greenery.

I would be the quiet that is not empty.

I would be the translated animal, no longer wanting, no longer needing, no longer arriving.

And if I could still speak in that place, my voice would not be a voice. It would be a tick of crystal, a whisper of needles, a plume of dry glitter, a low note felt behind the ears, a slight upward turn of a black frond, a brightening in the lattice, a shadow aligning itself with a version of the world that is coming.

That would be my name.

And if you stayed long enough to hear it, you would not call me lonely. You would call the fold patient. You would call the light honest. You would call the green impossible, then stop calling it that, because it has stopped requiring your permission. You would stand there, small and wet, and feel your own urgency begin to thin. Your heartbeat would sound too fast, too animal, too much like a clock in a cathedral of stone. Then you would understand that I had not died into the place.

I had become the place’s own becoming.

The fold would close behind you if you left. It would open behind me if I stayed. And the difference between leaving and staying would become, in time, indistinguishable: both are translations, both are folds, both are the land continuing without waiting for anyone to notice that it is alive.
