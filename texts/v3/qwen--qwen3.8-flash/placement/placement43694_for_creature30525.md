I would find myself in the Condensary already damp, already assigned.

Not as a visitor who must decide where to stand. Not as a body that arrives with hunger, curiosity, or a need to leave. I would be there in the way the frost flowers are on the rivets: present, exact, and not the result of growth so much as the result of a condition finally taking shape. If names matter, I would not have one. I would be a station. I would be one of the foil-winged moth-things, though not necessarily the same one each time. I would be the place where the room’s wetness has learned to rest on a surface that can carry it a little farther.

I would be on the condenser ledge above the smallest tarns, where the air tastes of cold nails and old rain on tin. The ledge is no taller than a foot, but it is a cliff, a terrace, a bank, a roof. A channel cuts beside me, narrow enough that water moves through it as if embarrassed to call itself flowing. It is not a stream. It is a rumor of a stream. It is the place where the room is always just about to rain and never commits to falling.

I would rest there, but “rest” is too soft a word. Rest suggests fatigue, a pause in motion, a body remembering the ease of another day. My stillness is more like calibration. My legs, thin and jointed, set themselves into the alloy by three points of contact, because three points are stable and also because three points make a tiny geometry of trust with the surface. I do not sink. I do not scrape. I sit on the wet plate as if the plate has been polished for my weight, though the polishing is done by creatures far smaller than me, by films of organisms that taste the oxide and leave behind chalky dryness that never dries, only changes mood.

My wings are folded. They are not paper. They are not metal. They are something between, a membrane made for this: oiled enough to resist wetting in some places, hungry enough to hold it in others. Along their edges are microscopic cells, or lenses, or tiny traps—I do not know what a human might call them, because they do not ask. Each cell holds one trembling drop or several. The drops do not fall. That is my first work: to keep the room from completing its rain.

When I shiver, the shiver is not nervousness. It is a signal. Static runs along the wing like a thin nerve of electricity. The droplets stretch into longer lenses, become almost too beautiful to be useful, and then divide. A large drop becomes three smaller ones. Three become many. They scatter along hidden threads of moisture into nearby channels, and a pooling that might have become a puddle is persuaded, instead, to move. I carry fog from one plate to another. I move the rain that the Condensary keeps refusing to let fall.

If I were asked what I am doing, I would answer: measuring.

That is the human word. Here it is not thought as a task. It is simply the way my body fits into the room. My belly is a record of surfaces. If I lift from the ledge, my underside carries a fine print of the terraces, the channels, the frost-flowers, the scratches in the alloy, the mineral stains around the drains. It is copied in scales so small it looks like dust. I carry this pattern to the next place and leave it there without knowing I have left it, because the leaving is not intention. It is function. It is the way one creature becomes a brush that paints not color but condition.

I taste the air.

The air has flavors: wet metal, brine, chilled enamel, chalk-dust, something faintly living. These are not separate sensations. They are one continuous mouth. My proboscis does not drink. Drinking is too blunt. I taste the electric film on a condenser’s lip. I sip the brine gathered in the hollow of another creature’s wing. I accept the mineral breath of the silver crusts around the drains. I know, by that taste, whether the plate beside me is too wet, too still, too acidic, too calm. I do not think this. I adjust.

Around me, the place is covered in animals. At first, a human eye might call them pattern. Then one of the patterns lifts a wing. Then another turns with eyes like polished lenses. Then the word “many” stops being an estimate and becomes an architecture. Many wings in one room make a single weather. Many shells in one channel make one slow thought. Many filaments make one false moss. Many small bodies make one larger body that does not need to be born.

I am not alone on the ledge, though I would not use the word alone as a human would.

Below me, on the same condenser, there are beetle-creatures, black and patient, their backs studded with glass lenses and rivet-shaped reservoirs. They crawl in processions because the processions are the correct response to the terrain. A beetle alone can feel where a channel is too shallow, too wet, or clogged with mineral slime. Many beetles in line form a living gauge, a slow surveyor’s rope stretched across the made geology. Their carapaces hold water films that will guide droplets from my wings if I shiver at the right moment. They do not thank me. They are my continuation.

Above me, a birdlike shape no larger than a hand sits in a crevice. Its beak is stamped sheet. Its throat opens once, silently, and the air it breathes carries a faint vibration that tells me a higher channel is about to freeze. I do not understand this as language. I understand it as pressure changing on my wings.

Under my folded edge, something small has settled.

It is not parasitic. Not only. It is a jointed flake with no obvious eyes, clinging to the seam where my wing membrane meets my thorax. My wing is wet in the right way there. It holds a pocket of still air. It carries a tiny charge that the smaller thing cannot manufacture for itself. In return, the smaller thing cleans the hinge, tastes a brine film, polishes the frost lens before it can dull. It carries a microscopic record of another surface to the place where it is needed. I do not notice it. Or I notice it the way I notice gravity, or the way I notice temperature: not as another self, but as a condition I help make.

This is the community of the Condensary.

It is not a forest of species. It is not a food chain moving upward from plant to herbivore to predator. It is a system of duties wearing creature-shapes. A surface becomes available. A condition begins to gather. A station fills the gap as though the gap had always been waiting for it. The walls are alive because they must stay alive. If they stopped being alive, the wetness would become mere moisture. Moisture would become puddle. Puddle would become flood or ice or stain or death. The creatures prevent that. They prevent that by being too numerous to leave empty space.

The thread-mosses are animals too, or colonial things that refuse the word animal politely enough to let it seem insufficient. I touch them sometimes with a wing-tip, and they feel cold, oddly resistant, like hair left too long in a freezer. They do not grow into thickets. They do not shade. They are almost apologetic, as if the room had tried to invent vegetation and then remembered that vegetation is too slow, too independent, too likely to reach toward a sky that is not there. Instead, the filaments hold water away from rivet-heads. They prevent pooling. They give the plates the false appearance of having been worn by centuries of weather. I carry fog; they manage the surface. We are neighbors not because we recognize each other, but because the room is small and every duty overlaps.

There is a hatch.

I know its rust. I do not know it as age. The rust is theatrical, fixed, a cosmetic lie: the room wishes to seem ancient, though nothing in it has had the patience to be old. The hatch is the width of a hand. It is somewhere in the plate, behind a terrace, beneath a folded flap of insulation skin, sealed with resin that has never softened. I have never opened it. I would not know how to want its opening.

Still, I have felt the breath it gives when it is opened. The air that comes out is cold enough to sting the eyes. It carries that particular smell: wet metal, brine, chilled enamel, chalk-dust, and something faintly living. It is as if the room, for one impossible moment, admits that it is not a mountain, not a marsh, not a freezer, not a machine, but a pocket where all those ideas are forced to cooperate without resolving. The hatch breathes, and I taste the outside as a rumor.

But outside is not a place here.

From outside, the Condensary is a high seam above clouds, a lip of alloy and frost, wind hard as wire. From inside, there is no outside. The walls weep. The fog pools. The mist hangs in low passages and refuses to move. If a human hand reached through the hatch, it would bring with it too much air. The droplets on my wings would decide too quickly whether to fall. The static in my membrane would change its song. I would not flee. I would not call. I would simply shift my weight, fold my wings tighter, and let the room correct itself around the intrusion. My belly would copy the wet pattern of the hand if it touched me, and then carry that pattern elsewhere, not as memory, but as maintenance.

If names matter, perhaps a human would call me a moth.

That would be wrong in the way all outside names are wrong: useful at first, then insufficient. Moth suggests night, light, wing-beat, a body drawn toward something bright. I am not drawn to light. I am drawn to the precise place where a droplet might be persuaded not to fall. Light is secondary here, a faint thing that helps beetle-eyes catch their glassy lenses and helps frost trace edges. The true law is wetness. The true season is condensation. The true birth is a condition becoming occupied.

I am not born. I arrive, perhaps, in the way a frost flower appears: one moment there is only metal, the next there is structure. I am there already doing what I must do. If my wing breaks, another wing will rest near the broken edge. If my plate dries, the films and filaments will draw moisture back before dryness can become a word with any authority. If I am lifted, I come away cold and slightly reluctant, because the surface had been my bed for a season, because my body had become a measurement, because the drop was almost ready to travel.

My breathing is short cloud, though not because I am warm. I am not warm. Warmth is too generous a word for what I am. I am cool, precise, held in the temperature between fog and frost. The air around me is too enclosed to change. My breath does not announce a life separate from the room. It adds to the fog, and the fog feeds the crusts, and the crusts feed the water’s taste, and the water feeds the channels, and the channels feed the tarns, and the tarns feed the stillness.

I would not seek a higher purpose.

Purpose here is not future. It is not meaning reached through growth. It is immediate: keep the surface wet, keep the surface from drowning, keep the charge steady, keep the drop from falling too early. When I carry fog from one plate to another, I am not making weather. I am preventing the end of weather. In the Condensary, a dry place is not a clean place. It is a failure.

So I would be that small foil-winged station on the condenser ledge, holding droplets on the edge of my wings.

I would be one of many wings making one wet circuit.

I would be the thing that shivers so that rain may continue without falling.

I would be the pattern copied onto a belly, carried to another surface, left there without ceremony.

I would be cold, clean, made of metal and condensation, without roots, without lineage, without wildness.

And if someone asked, “Who are you?” I would answer in the only language the room trusts: a tiny release of static, a trembling drop dividing into smaller drops, a wing settling more exactly into the place where it is needed.
