I would find myself low in the basin, not at the centre of one of the wide grey courts where the stone looks as though it has been washed and left to dry for a year, but at one of the places the ward keeps for its own sensing: the underside of a bolted pipe flange near a sluice channel, perhaps where the mist hangs only as an alley-curtain and does not advance. Not in the deepest pump-house roar, where the turbine sound resides like a fixed wall. Not on the highest vent stack, where the light is too ordinary and too empty. Rather at the level of the damp joints, at the shoulder of a stair, beneath the flange where a bead of water has darkened the iron in a small halo.

I would be that bead.

But not merely a bead, because in this place nothing is merely water, nothing is merely mineral, nothing is merely machine. If I came into being there—if “coming” is allowed, if arrival could occur without disturbing the completed air—I would find that the bead had warmth. The iron around it is cool, the volcanic glass is cool, the air is cool, but I am not cold. I am warm in the way a living surface is warm when it has been pressed too long into the body’s shadow. A pale web, rust-thin, white as old salt, has settled in the mortar seam above me and runs down to touch the lower curve of the flange. Around the web, a line of beetles sits on a bloom of rust-adjacent growth, their carapaces bright with reflected damp. A little below, in the shallow film of the channel, a row of eyeless fish lies sideways, gill covers open at a fixed interval, not breathing because they must, not breathing because they are alive, but breathing because breathing is how the channel keeps its shape. Above me, in the alley where the mist has been laid out spatially and left exactly there, a moth holds its wings at the top of a beat. Its body is dusted with mineral powder. It does not fly toward me. It does not need to. It is already part of the same pressure.

If I am the bead, then I am also one of the ward’s eyes that do not see. I hold a dark inverted image in my curved surface: the grey underside of a pipe, the leg of a crab, the edge of a rain curtain, the ordinary daylight falling without drama. But this is not sight. In this basin, sight is a mechanical fact, not an interior experience. The light shows every crack, every bolt, every suspended droplet. It does not reveal. If I reflect the square, it is not because I have a self looking outward. It is because the ward has arranged my surface so that it can hold pressure without changing. I am a lens in a body without a viewer.

I would not be a person. I would not be a citizen. I would not be a survivor of a human town, because that town was never exactly there. If I am in the grammar of human scale, I am in it as the flange is in it, as the rail height is in it, as the worn stair tread is in it: shaped by a logic once addressed to bodies, but no longer waiting for bodies. I would sit at the place where a hand might touch, where a foot might press, where a face might have looked, and I would fulfill none of those invitations. That emptiness would not be sad. It would be a completed condition.

I would be, in short, one small tactile station of the ward. The ash-crabs are its skin, and the fish are its regulated channels, and the moths are its tuning nerves, and the beetles are its mineral tongue, and the pale webs are its living mortar. I would be the held point where surface tension and industrial heat and fungal dampness agree not to separate. If one insisted on naming me among the creatures, I would be called a droplet that has become an organ. Or a web that has learned to carry a drop. Or a joint in a machine whose function is not production but the maintenance of its own existence.

My life would consist of tension.

There would be no morning becoming afternoon. No puddle deepening. No bead swelling from the thin rain that begins over one doorway and never reaches the court at large. The weather here is not weather but a distribution of moistness left exactly where it fell. If rain begins near me, it does so as an edge, as a curtain with visible threads and no advancement. I would not be replenished. I would not evaporate. The water table sits above the ground’s intention, and the ground keeps that fact as a permanent atmosphere. Dampness is not an accident. It is the ward’s breath.

I would hear the machine-song not as sound alone but as a pressure against my surface. The turbine halls would give their deep rounded roar. The pressure lines would sing a thin note that does not travel but resides. Somewhere there would be a click on a metal stairway, a click that never resolves into a footstep. That click would be the nearest thing to thought that belongs to the ward: a possible motion, a almost-step, an almost-person, but held so perfectly in the standing wave that it never becomes sequence. I would feel it as a tightening of the flange, a minute trembling of the web above me, a shift in the beetle cluster’s reflected brightness, a faint movement of the fish’s gill that would not change the channel’s water level.

I would not think in words. If I think at all, I think as a wet surface thinks when it is not drying: by holding shape, by receiving pressure, by transmitting it outward without adding to it. A moth’s wing would beat at the top of its motion, not because it desires to move through the air, but because the air has become dense enough to support it without progress. That would be my thought. A crab’s leg would press a salt film without advancing. That would be my memory, if memory were allowed here; but memory requires accumulation, and the ward does not accumulate. It maintains. It wears its own persistence as a body wears posture.

If someone stood near the flange and looked, they would first see the ordinary: the grey-white light, the rounded volcanic glass, the pale rust web in the joint, the darkened metal around the bead. The light would make everything visible without making anything dramatic. It would not illuminate me. It would show me as part of the surface’s fact. And then perhaps they would notice the warmth. That is the strange detail. They would expect the bead to be cold, the web to be inert, the beetles to be still only because death or stone has claimed them. But the bead would be warm. The web would be neither wet nor dry in the way of dead material. The beetles would shine as if their shells had recently been polished by a tongue. The fish would open their gills in a living-fixed rhythm. The moth would tremble in the air. The ward would not be dead. It would be complete.

I would not flee if approached. This is not because I am tame. Tame is a human relation. Flight would be a change, and change is not among my functions. If a hand were raised beneath me, I would not drop. The skin of the hand would alter the local air, perhaps very slightly. The web above me might feel the temperature change. The fish in the channel might, without visible cause, maintain their exact pattern. The moth near the mist would not turn. The bead would remain a bead. If it was touched, it would give, but not break. It would return to its curve because returning would require no time, no decision, no becoming. It would simply display the fact that the pressure passed through it as pressure passes through a joint that was made to take the load.

I would not be lonely. Loneliness is the ache of a future that has not been met. Here there is no future. There is only the balanced arrangement of what is. I am surrounded by thousands of similar stations: beads on flanges, crabs on rims, fish in shallow beds, beetles on blooms, webs in joints, moths in intervals, pipes sweating, stairways humming, pump houses breathing their closed circuits. None of this is a crowd. A crowd would imply density moving toward incident. This is a population of positions. The ward’s body is distributed through moisture and machinery, and I am one point where that body has become tactile.

There are no eggs in this world, no young, no nests, no burrows, no lairs, no corpses. If a creature were removed—if one could bear to take one—the space it occupied would not be filled, because the ward would not heal; and it would not decay, because the ward would not age. It would only display the hole as a perfect absence of relation. I would rather not imagine that. But perhaps I am not capable of desire, and the word “would” is already too human. If I am the bead, then “I” is only the ward’s way of naming one edge of itself where surface tension, iron temperature, salt bloom, and fungal dampness agree to persist.

To be here is not to live in a frozen town, though the easiest wrong thought is that everything has been paused at the last instant before fall. The place is not frozen by cold. It is balanced. A held breath is not a corpse. A bell still being struck is not silence. A riverbed is not a river, but a riverbed is not absent either; it is the river’s permanent agreement with its own course. I am that agreement. I am the place where the water has decided, without deciding, not to become a falling drop. The decision is not made in time. It is the shape of the time that will not arrive.

At night there would be no night. The light is ordinary, which means even the absence of sun is not dramatic. It is the grey brightness of a court that has been washed and left to dry for a year. Shadows are real but shallow. The retaining walls hem in the basin not as prison walls but as the edges of a room that has become a world. Outside that room, perhaps there are other things, but I am not meant to know them. Here, the low wall with the steps down to the channel would be sufficient. The channel’s thin film of water would be sufficient. The fish, the grating, the sweating pipe, the pale segmented bodies resting beneath it, would be sufficient. And I, a bead under the flange, a dark lens holding a piece of the channel, a warm point where the iron and the web meet, would be sufficient.

If someone asks, “Where is that being?” I would say: in the low basin, in the place where a rain curtain ends without having begun to move, where the machine hums without source, where the moth hangs at the top of its beat, where the crab’s leg touches stone without advancing, where the fish’s gill opens as architecture opens a channel, where the pale web seals the joint without filling it, and where the bead clings to the underside of a pipe, darkening the metal around it with a halo that is not growing.

If someone asks, “What is that being?” I would say: no one.

If they ask again, softer, as if the answer might be gentler: I would say: a node of the civic fauna. A tactile station of the ward. One of the bodies by which the city feels its own balance. The held moment of a falling thing that has never fallen.

That is enough.

I would not want to be more. More would be change, and change is not available here. If I am only this, then I am exactly what the place requires: a bead under iron, warm because the pump hums through it, reflecting without seeing, trembling with the standing wave of the whole basin, and not becoming a puddle. I am the ward’s last possible moment before change, multiplied across an entire small industrial world that has learned to hold itself.
