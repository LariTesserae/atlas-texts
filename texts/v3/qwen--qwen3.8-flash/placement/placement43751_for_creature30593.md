**I would be found in the seam.**

Not at the lip of the Hollow, where the plain still remembers itself, and not at the bottom, where the dark water makes its quiet squares and holds the light too still. I would be in a middle place, on the terrace where damp stone begins to dry and dry stone begins to remember damp. A room there, or what the town calls a room: a niche widened by a few careful years, a shelf cut into one side, a sleeping ledge on the other, a door hung in a frame made by hands that had never built a door before but learned it because a corner of their life became a doorway.

I would be one of those people whose hands look too large for the person, not because the hands are monstrous, but because they have become more honest than the body around them. Broad palms. Short fingers. Smoother pads where the fingerprints used to be. Chalk in the lines like a second birthmark. The right thumb thickened from pressing a chisel while the mallet comes down. The nails cut close, always close, because the Hollow punishes vanity with a snagged rope and broken skin. If someone from the upper world looked at me, they might think I was a laborer, then a mason, then a water-keeper, then perhaps a child of the plain who had wandered too far underground and not wandered back. But I would not be all of those at once, or none of them. I would be the one the Hollow had already begun to write.

At first I would call myself a channel-mender. That sounds like a job, and jobs make people feel less lost in the world. Every morning I would go down to the lower terraces where water beads and runs in little hidden ways, and I would look for the places where the flow had slipped from the channel, where a clay pipe had cracked at its rim, where a stone had shifted after someone hauled grain too hard against it, where a moss thread had grown too fat and slowed the passage. I would carry a small coppery pin, a flat bone scraper, a stick wrapped with old cloth, and a pouch of powdered lime or clay paste. Then I would clean. Then I would seal. Then I would wait and listen.

But the more I wrote that name for myself, the less it seemed enough. A channel-mender is still too like a person passing through a place. I would not merely mend channels; I would think in them. The Hollow teaches that kind of thinking. You learn to trace water without seeing it. You learn that the faintest wet line on stone may be a river remembered from an hour ago. You learn that a cistern can sound like a voice because someone once spoke near its mouth and the stone kept the habit. You learn where your body fits in the light: how at midday you can work a grinding slab without a lamp, how by late afternoon the terrace turns the color of ash and you must carry a clay basin from one bright rectangle to another before the shadow swallows it.

If pressed for a fuller name, they would call me by a place. “Siltstep,” perhaps. “Chalkhand.” “Third Terrace, by the Drip.” “Ledge.” “Door-Woman.” Not a title. Just what the town says when a person becomes part of its vocabulary.

I would be mixed in the way the Hollow is mixed. There is no pure thing down there except water, and even that leaves mud behind. My father might have been a quarryman from the surface, one of those who knew the tone of a good stone strike and could read a seam like a prayer. My mother might have come from a drought village, carrying a name and a sick child and a pot wrapped so carefully it might have held the last milk of a cow. Or both parents might have been strangers to each other and the Hollow made them into neighbors, and then into lovers because love was easier than distance when there was no distance. Or perhaps I was Hollowborn: born in a small room where the shaft light fell at a slant through the morning, my first breath taken in air that tasted of stone, smoke, dried mint, and the faint mineral sweetness of water waiting inside walls. That kind of birth would mark me more than any story I could be told later. I would have eyes that do not need open sky. I would know fragments and call them whole.

I would walk differently, too. The Hollow is vertical. You do not stride through it as through a field. You turn sideways. You grip. You lower a basket before passing a neighbor’s shoulder. You learn to stand on one foot on a ledge while holding a pot steady with your hip, because standing straight and still, in a place where there is not much straight and very little still, is one of the first professions the town teaches. My feet would be broad and low, toes spread from years of gripping steps made too narrow by new stone. I would wear felt slippers cut from old cloth, or bandages wound tight around my ankles and feet, because bare feet on wet stone are foolish and loose sandals catch on stair lips. My walk would be soft. In a town where everyone hears everyone, silence is not virtue; it is practical kindness. My footsteps would fall like a hand laid on a sleeping child’s shoulder.

In the morning I would see my breath. Not a dramatic thing. Just a pale wisp escaping, showing that I, too, am a warm animal in a cool wound. I would stand at the gallery ledge and look across the Hollow. The opposite wall would be dense with houses, some of them almost touching, some leaning into one another like relatives arguing about a small inheritance. The near wall would be a stack of platforms and niches, scaffolds creaking under people hauling timber, ropes hanging slack from beams. The shafts would admit white daylight as if the sun were an ordinary stranger stepping into town without ceremony.

I would not live in a grand chamber. Mine would be a home in the manner of the place: a small stone cell with a plastered wall and a clay floor partly crushed with shell for firmness. There would be two sleeping platforms, one for me and one for whoever was staying: a niece, an apprentice, a traveler who came down from the plain and had no right to return before the rain. A low roof would be fixed by weighted stones against the chill and the occasional drift of dust falling from above. A shelf would hold an oil lamp, a cup, a handful of beads, a folded rag, and a pot root vegetable so small it looked less like food than like a held breath. Dried herbs would hang from a beam—mint, yarrow, thyme—scenting the room so faintly I would forget they were there until I came in from the water work and they seemed to remind me that the surface world still existed.

My days would be made of repairs. Not repairs that restore a lost perfection, but repairs that keep becoming from collapsing into ruin. The town is new, which means it is unfinished in a way that has nothing to do with poverty and everything to do with patience. A crack in the lintel must be watched because the stone above it is still adjusting. A floor must be re-leveled because the ground beneath it is still settling under human use. A channel must be cleaned because a leaf from the plain fell down and lodged in it, or because a rat disturbed the silt, or because a child spilled a pot and the water found its own new road. I would move from one small problem to another, not because the town were broken, but because the town were alive.

I would know its quiet not as empty but as thick with attention. There would be no shouting unless it was a child, and then the shout would shrink before it left the mouth, because even children learn that sound in stone can travel through unexpected ways. I would hear a mason’s chisel three terraces below, but it would not come from the direction expected. I would hear a woman wringing a cloth into a basin and think, for an instant, that the water was dripping inside my own wall. I would hear feet on a platform above and know whether they belonged to someone carrying stone, someone carrying a child, someone trying not to wake a neighbor. The Hollow does not make secrets impossible; it makes them unnecessary. You learn to keep your privacy with a door, a hanging reed cloth, a lowered voice, a body turned away. You do not need distance. The stone provides it if you know how to stand in it.

I would know what animals meant because animals here are not decoration. A rat near the grinding floor means grain. A moth in the shaft means a lamp uncovered. A shrew in the rubble pile means beetles, and beetles mean damp. A spider crossing the lintel means a dry place, which is not a small thing to know when everything around you is learning how to be dry. I would not fight these creatures as though they were wild enemies. I would manage them. I would seal cracks with lime and clay. I would watch the storage mats. I would smoke out a corner if the grain smelled strange. I would move a pot because a spider had chosen its shadow. In this world, a person’s relationship to small life is part of their character. The Hollow cannot afford to be careless with what lives in it.

There would be light. Always light, but not light in the theatrical sense. It would come down from above and scatter along pale walls, revealing the truth of materials without flattering them. Yellow sandstone here. Bluish gray clay there. A chalky white vein running through the rock like a secret the quarrymen tried to follow and failed to understand. Damp stains darker near the cisterns. Bright rectangles at doorways and ledges where the shafts touched just enough to make a room usable. Water in a basin would look like a window. A wet wall would reflect a patch of sky that no one in the Hollow could see directly.

I would be shaped by that light. Not my spirit alone, but my habits. I would know where to grind grain so the dust did not choke the smoke. I would know where to dry herbs so they would not mold in the cool corners. I would know where to stand with a pot of water at dusk so the last pale rectangle of afternoon touched my face and let me see who was coming up the stair. I would know the difference between rain-dark and evening-dark. The first means water is rising in the channels and the town will smell sharp and clean for a few hours. The second means someone must bring in the drying grain and the cistern lips need checking. Light is not only visible here. It is a task.

I would have neighbors so close that stone carried their voices through shared walls. I might be the one who hears the widow on the far side coughing, and I would tap a pattern on the wall to ask if she needed water. She might not answer because she is sleeping. Or she might answer with a soft knock because she wants to be seen without being seen. Privacy would not mean distance. It would mean the choice of whether to answer. I would know that the couple below argue quietly because their rooms are too small and their work is too hard, and sometimes one of them comes out into the gallery and says nothing for a long time, looking at the opposite wall until the anger dries. I would not fix that unless asked. I would leave food near their threshold when no one is watching, because the town understands that hunger can make a person seem worse than they are.

There would be work that required many hands and work that could only be done in silence. I would do both. Raising a timber frame meant shoulders and rope and shouting through the narrow alleys. Clearing a cistern meant bending down in cold water, feeling for the silt with one hand, scraping with the other, moving slowly so the walls did not crack under hurried effort. Rebuilding a floor after water had undermined its base meant carrying stones and tamping clay and checking with a cord and a weight until the surface lay true. This kind of work is not romantic. It leaves your knees sore and your fingers numb. But it gives you a relation to the town that no story could. You know the place because you have altered it and it has altered you.

At times I would be asked about the above. Visitors from the plain, standing at the edge of a shaft, would ask if we feel cut off. They would ask if we miss the whole sky. They would ask if it is not strange to live in a wound. I would not answer well. Not because their question is stupid, but because it assumes a world I no longer live by. To them, completeness may be an unbroken horizon. To me, completeness is a room with a door that shuts properly, a channel that drains, a floor that will not crack in three days, a neighbor who knocks softly when she hears something wrong. The Hollow is not a diminished world. It is a compressed one. Compressed things are not weak. A fist is not less a hand for being closed.

Still, I would sometimes carry a small pot of moss to my sleeping ledge. Not because it is useful. Moss is not food, not rope, not stone. But a green thread growing in wet gray rock makes the town feel less like a machine and more like a living body. I would watch how it spreads after rain and retreats after dust. I would learn which days are damp enough for it to turn bright and which days it dims into a dull fur. If the moss on the north side of a niche grew fat, I would know that water was moving closer than I had expected. If the moss thinned, I would know the air had changed. This would be my small book of signs, written not in ink but in patient green.

I would be careful with fire. The lamps are simple: oil in a basin, cloth twisted as wick, a cupped hand to shield the flame. But fire in the Hollow is both friend and threat. It cooks. It dries. It carries scent from one narrow passage to another. It also produces smoke, and smoke must be guided. A soot-stained shaft is a map of who has lived where and how long they burned things. A new soot pattern means a new hearth or a careless neighbor. A clean shaft means someone is not using their home, which can worry a town that cannot afford to lose too many rooms.

I would be hungry for order but not for ornament. The buildings are not grand, but they are organized because survival demands it. The town has zones, and I would move through them with the unconscious fluency of someone whose body knows the rules before the mind names them. The wet lower terraces for cisterns. The bright middle galleries for work where light helps the hand. The dry ledges for storage. The common stairs for passage and conversation. The small niches for sleeping. The broad platforms for assembly. I would know which terrace can hold a load and which must not. I would know how to step around a dark square of still water because that water has no business being there but is there anyway, and the Hollow is full of such accidents. I would know how to pass a neighbor carrying grain without touching the grain, without touching the neighbor, without breaking the quiet, without losing the path.

As the writing continued, the role would loosen. I would no longer think, “I am a channel-mender.” I would think in water, stone, dust, and voices. The town would be less a place where I live than a body of which I am a function, like a nerve near a joint that reports pressure. I would be the one who listens for a drip in a pipe and knows whether it is clean, muddy, or close to blockage. I would be the one who puts a hand to a wall and feels the coldness of hidden water. I would be the one who watches a crack with a small mark scratched beside it to see whether it widens by morning. I would be one of the many small eyes, hands, and ears through which the Hollow knows itself.

That is who I would be. Not a chosen one. Not a king of the pit. Not a mysterious guardian of ancient secrets. The Hollow does not need that. It is too young for legends. It needs people who make the ground level, who keep the water moving, who speak softly in tight places, who teach children to stand still on narrow shelves, who cover stored grain when the shafts darken with mist, who leave the door open when a neighbor is lost outside and shut it when the smoke blows inward. I would be one of those. I would be one of those because the place asked for it before I knew I had answered.

And if asked where I would find myself, I would not say, “in the town.” I would say, “between the drip and the door.” I would say, “in the hour when dust hangs in shaft-light and someone is climbing up with a basket of stone while someone else is scraping silt from a basin.” I would say, “near the crack that has not widened yet, but might.” I would say, “by the wall that carries the widow’s cough or the child’s laughter or the mason’s quiet strike.” I would say, “in the room being made, not finished, not ruined, but ongoing.”

I would be a person becoming the Hollow.

I would be a hand with chalk in it. A foot that grips ledge before stair. A breath that fogs in the cold and clears in the warmth. An ear trained to know a distant cistern by its voice. An eye that reads bright rectangles as streets. A heart that beats in a place where nothing has had time to settle into elegance, and therefore everything is precious because it is barely there. I would be neither fully from the plain nor yet fully a new kind of human. I would be the in-between: the unfinished, the necessary, the being-made.
