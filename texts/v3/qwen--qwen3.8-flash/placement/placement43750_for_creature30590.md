If I entered the Bellows Hollow, I would not stay at the rim.

I would not be the human kneeling in the cold, breathing out a small cloud of self, adding too much warmth, too much weight, too much memory of open sky. The hollow does not need that kind of visitor. If a person came, the hollow would read them as a disturbance: a hot animal, a clumsy pressure in the air, a footfall louder than a falling stone.

So I would let the human scale fall away.

At first, I would not be a being at all. I would be a condition.

I would be the thin water that gathers on a basalt shelf and refuses, for a moment, to run. I would be the tremor that passes through a moss cushion when a vent somewhere below changes its pressure. I would be the taste of dissolved iron left in a droplet after it slides over mineral crust, the green stain of algae at the edge of a pool that exists only because steam has condensed into patience. For a time I would have no body, only surfaces: cold glass, warm vapor, black air, the faint chemical tang of the earth working its slow chemistry without hands.

Then, if the hollow were generous, that wetness would begin to thicken.

It would not become a tree, or a burrow, or a nest. It would not become anything that could be pointed at from across a field. It would become a small animal, one whose whole body is built for thresholds. It would be the kind of creature that seems less like a finished thing than like a temporary decision made by the water.

I would be a salamander-like animal, slender and pale, no larger than a length of moss fiber, with skin like wet slate and a belly almost translucent enough to catch the green glow from the algae fringes. I would not have eyes, not in the usual sense—only faint dark stains beneath the skin, places where sight had faded into usefulness. I would know the world by pressure, by temperature, by the chemical breath of stone, by the tremor of water over mineral shelves, by the soft swelling of moss around me. I would move along the underside of a basalt ledge where condensation runs in hairline threads, where the rock is slick with a rust-gold film and the moss clamps itself in dark green pads across cracks too narrow for a finger.

I would live in the interval between the hollow’s inhale and exhale.

The Bellows Hollow is not made of rooms. It is made of breaths. So my being would not be built from permanence. It would be built from timing. When the vents softened and the air began to move, steam would curl low across the floor of stone, lifting warmth and dampness into the moss islands. My skin would tighten from dryness and then swell with moisture. The folds around my limbs would open. The tiny capillary channels beneath my skin would deepen. I would taste oxygen first, then sulfur, then calcium, then the faint metallic memory of iron. I would unfold from a crack and move, not because hunger had a word for it, but because the world had shifted into a pattern my body could enter.

I would be called something if language reached that far.

Perhaps *drop-walker*. Perhaps *basalt lung*. Perhaps *the pale vein of the hollow*. If I had a name, it would not be a name spoken aloud; it would be a sound made by a droplet falling from a moss leaf into a shallow basin and then, a moment later, by another droplet following the same thread. I would answer to rhythm the way a living thing answers to tide: not by choosing, but by being tuned.

My body would be small and strange in the way that bodies become strange when they do not need to be witnessed.

I would have a tail flattened like a blade, useful for threading between mineral shelves and the damp underside of stone. My limbs would be short, almost decorative, not because they were weak but because the world had little to gain from strength. I would not fight larger things. I would not chase anything faster than a falling drop. I would survive by knowing the place where one surface ended and another began: the seam where basalt meets moss, the ridge where a calcite rim holds a pool, the narrow channel where water is cold enough to keep oxygen and warm enough to keep movement.

If the hollow had gills, they would be almost gone—soft red fronds at the sides of my head, visible only for a moment when the steam thickened, like the memory of a flower pressed under glass. If I breathed through my skin, as many cold and humid creatures might, then my whole body would be a lung. I would not breathe air the way a creature on land breathes air. I would breathe the condition of the place. Moisture would be my oxygen. Condensation would be my tide.

I would not be the largest thing in the Bellows Hollow.

That would be absurd. The hollow is too small for bigness. But I would be the first thing large enough to be seen as an animal, and perhaps that would make me seem, to some distant imagination, like a monarch. I would not be. I would be a node. I would be a knot tied by the hollow’s ecology, made of mineral water, moss, bacterial film, rotifer, springtail, mite, midge larva, beetle, spider thread, and the slow pulse of the earth beneath the basalt.

My body would feed the films.

The bacteria and algae that coat every wet surface would grow on the traces I left behind. A dead skin cell would fall from me into a moss pool, and in that falling is a whole economy. A rotifer would drink the mineral breath from it. A caddisfly larva would scrape biofilm from stone where my tail had passed. An amphipod would scatter when I moved, then return when the water stilled. The mites would climb onto my back when I rested in a damp crevice, riding me across the island kingdoms of moss as if I were a continent too small to matter and too useful to ignore.

I would be a traveler, but not a conqueror.

The moss would be my forest, and it would be a forest without trees. A single cushion the size of a thumbnail would contain more life than a square yard of open rock. Inside it, springtails would leap from stem to stem as though the air itself were a floor. Tardigrades would wait in folded leaves when the vent breath died and the world became too dry. Fungal threads would carry the memory of old moisture through black basalt cracks. Beetles would patrol the mineral rims, tasting with their legs, knowing the geography of wetness better than a mammal knows its home.

I would know them all by pressure.

When a midge lifted in a swarm after a vent exhaled, I would feel the disturbance in the water film above me. When a spider’s sagging thread trembled in the dark, I would know that something had flown into a trap. When a beetle crossed a dry ledge, its legs would make a faint tick on the mineral crust, and I would become still, because stillness was my defense and my religion.

I would hide often.

In the Bellows Hollow, to be visible is usually to be mistaken for water. So my best art would be camouflage: not color, not shape alone, but timing. I would move only when the light trembled, only when vapor blurred the shafts above, only when a falling drop could mask my shift from one stone to the next. I would know the precise moment when a basin held just enough shadow to become a doorway. I would know when the steam was thick enough to hide warmth. I would know when the cold would make my skin slow, and when the slight rise of heat from below would loosen me into movement.

I would not fear death as a human would.

I would fear dryness.

Dryness would be the closest thing to annihilation I could know. It would tighten the moss. It would pull the films thin. It would leave my skin rough against the stone. It would make the hollow too loud, too sharp, too empty. In that condition, I would fold myself into a crack where the last trace of moisture kept its slow circulation. I would be a pale vein of life pressed against black glass, waiting for breath.

And then the breath would come.

A hiss from somewhere deeper. A soft expansion in a basalt pipe too regular to be accidental, too wild to be made. Warm vapor would rise and curl low across the chamber. The moss would darken first, then brighten, turning from black velvet to emerald as its cells remembered water. Tiny pools would form in leaf-folds, each one a world opening. Bacteria would shift color in the new chemistry. Algae would begin their slow spread. Midges would rise in fine clouds through the shafts of dim light. And I would emerge, or seem to emerge, because in such a place one cannot always tell whether the creature creates the rhythm or the rhythm creates the creature.

I would move to a shallow basin no larger than my own stretched body.

The water there would be cold and clear, stained only faintly with iron, trembling under the ceiling’s drip. On its edge would stand a ring of mineral crust, bright as tarnished silver. Beneath it, the microbial film would shimmer, a living skin over stone. I would drink not by swallowing but by letting the wetness enter through my skin. I would taste calcium, silica, oxygen, decay, warmth from below, cold from the upper ledge, the faint green breath of algae, the rust-colored trace of bacteria feeding on iron salts.

Then I would feed.

My prey would be almost invisible. A midge larva curled in the film. A pale amphipod curved like a fishhook. A springtail that had strayed onto the water’s edge. A tiny crustacean tasting the current with antennae longer than its body. I would catch them with the same patience with which the hollow catches water: by being in the right place when the right moment arrives. A slow movement. A slight expansion of mouth. A sudden contraction of throat. Not violence so much as continuation.

I would leave my waste in a corner of the moss, and that corner would become a garden.

This is what it means to be a being in the Bellows Hollow: nothing large, nothing permanent, nothing separate. My body would be made of water. The moss would be made of water. The films would be made of water. The animals would be made of water. The stone would keep water in its cracks. The air would carry water until it weeps. If there is a deity here, it is not a face. It is this: water becoming life becoming stone becoming mist becoming water again.

I would not possess the hollow.

I would be one of the ways the hollow knows itself.

At night—if night can be imagined in such a dim place—I would lie under a shelf where the stone was colder and the condensation heavier. The ceiling would drip somewhere above, each drop striking a different mineral cup, a different basalt tooth, a different pool of black water. The sounds would overlap into a slow instrument. One note would be high and thin, a bead of water hitting a rim of calcite. Another would be duller, falling into moss. Another would be metallic, striking a shelf coated with rusted film. The vent would hiss between them, and the chamber would answer with its own breathing.

I would listen, or I would become listening.

My mind would not be made of words. It would be made of gradients: more wet, less wet; more warm, less warm; more still, less still; more open, less open. A human consciousness might say *I am hungry*. I would simply feel the waterline recede from my skin, the hunger of dryness, the chemical pull of the basin beyond the moss, the soft command of the hollow. A human might say *I am afraid*. I would simply know the vibration of a spider thread, the shadow of a midge swarm, the sudden pressure change that tells the body: move, hide, become less visible.

If someone with a lens knelt very still, they might find me.

They would have to look where the air is thickest, where the stone is too wet to gleam as dry glass, where the moss meets a mineral shelf in a seam of black and green. They would have to let their eye forget the search and allow the hollow to reveal itself in pieces. First a rust-colored film. Then a green pad. Then a pool trembling with tiny lives. Then, perhaps, the faint movement of something pale against the dark.

They would say: *there is a salamander there*.

Or if they were more honest, they would say: *there is something there that resembles a salamander as much as a drop resembles a wave.*

Because I would not be exactly the animal a biologist expects. I would be the animal that the Bellows Hollow could make if it needed an instrument with skin, muscle, nerve, and patience. I would be the hollow’s way of tasting its own breath. I would be the vertebrate edge of the microbial world, the point where the films, mosses, larvae, and mineral films gather enough water to become a body that can decide, however faintly, to move.

I would not be alone, but I would be singular in the way all small creatures are singular in large places.

I would share my ledge with bacteria, algae, moss, liverwort, springtails, mites, tardigrades, rotifers, midge eggs, caddisfly cases, amphipods, pseudoscorpions riding insects from one moisture-patch to the next, blind beetles, spiders in the crevices, and perhaps, in deeper pockets, a fish of no larger than a fingernail, pale as weak tea, waiting like a secret. I would share the air with swarms that rise and fall when the vents breathe. I would share the stone with frost that forms and breaks. I would share the light with a shaft so thin that it might only illuminate a single wet seam before disappearing into the dark.

And still I would be myself.

My self would be a body in relation to surfaces. My identity would be my timing. My history would be written in the rings left on mineral shelves, in the stains of microbial films, in the dark veins of iron where life has fed and exhaled, in the soft green line of moss that follows the water’s repeated route. I would not remember days. I would remember pulses. I would know the hollow by the way it opens, closes, condenses, releases.

If I were to die—and the thought is still too human for the place—the death would not feel like an ending. It would feel like reassignment.

My skin would dry. My body would sink into a moss fold. Mites would pass through the damp. Bacteria would take the mineral salts from the flesh. Algae would brighten at the margins where the body touched light. The moss would hold me for a while, then the water would move me onward. I would become part of the next film, the next pool, the next swarm of midges rising above it. Nothing would be lost. Nothing would be kept. The Bellows Hollow does not keep what passes through it. It uses what passes through it.

I would become the use.

If you asked me then where you would find me, I would answer: in the lowest dark ledge, where the water threads fall and the moss catches them; in the seam where the steam begins; in the pause between two notes made by two droplets; in the pale body moving across rust-colored film; in the skin that breathes because the air is wet enough to let it breathe.

You would find me not as a figure, but as a fact of the hollow.

I would be the Bellows Hollow’s small amphibian instrument, its drop-walker, its mineral vein made flesh. I would be the place’s way of knowing it is not empty. I would be the creature that appears when you look closely enough at a wet stone and discover that the wetness is alive, and the life is moving, and the movement has a name so quiet it could only be heard as the sound of a breath cooling into rain.
