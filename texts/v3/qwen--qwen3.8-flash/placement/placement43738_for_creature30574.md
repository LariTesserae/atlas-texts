The atmosphere is too settled for the ordinary inhabitants of air. Too settled for birds. Too settled for bats. Too settled for clouds, condensation, breath-fog, the little wet dramas of life. It is too settled for insects in swarms, for seeds drifting, for the fine dust that ordinary worlds mistake for weather. Dust, after all, is a confession: it says that something has broken, worn, decayed, scattered. Here there is no scattering. Here the particles, if they exist at all, are held in electrostatic geometries too exact to be called suspension and too still to be called motion. They hang in patterns. They rest where they are needed. They are not floating; they are placed.

And yet there are residents in that settled air.

Not travelers. Not arrivals. Not creatures who enter from outside the basin and become surprised by it. The residents of the air are conditions that have taken shape. They are the atmosphere’s own completed thoughts, if one can use the word thought without implying time, effort, uncertainty, and a mind that was once not yet thinking. In the Tallow Basin, the mind would be wrong to think that. Everything there has already thought its own structure into place.

I would find myself among those air-residents.

Not as a person dropped into scenery. Not as a visitor whose footprints refuse the ground. Not as a curious mammal with moisture in its breath and a pulse of future in its chest. I would find myself already in place, already true, already finished into a role. I would not come into the Tallow Basin the way one comes into a country. I would be a line in its grammar. I would be a fact it does not need to verify.

At first, the mind tries to choose the obvious body.

Maybe one of the horned animals standing in the salt meadows, whose fur is not fur but mineral threads, whose gaze is not hunting but pure fixed attentiveness. There is something tempting about that: the dignity of a body that has no need to scan. It would feel like being an equation standing on four legs. But it is too bounded. It is too localized. It belongs to the ground too much, even when it is not walking, even when it is not grazing. Its motion, if it has one, is spread across space as pose after pose, a stag becoming a sequence of silhouettes like a sculpture made of several instants laid side by side. That is close, but not me.

Maybe one of the pale reeds instead. A stalk like horn, translucent at the edges, with blades spiraled in exact intervals. There is something appealing about that too: to be part of a stand that is not many plants but one chorus. A single thought made of vertical geometry. To take light into oneself and return it altered, not scattered, not softened, not dewy, but cooled by mineral aftertaste. But the reeds are too rooted in the ground’s illusion. They have the shape of growing, though they do not grow. They have the shape of standing, though they are not standing in time. They are close, but not me.

Maybe a copper-green frond, folded membrane, layered so tightly the ground hides beneath it. To be a nervous system of dry circuitry. To carry ionolite without wetness. To be arranged in the shape of movement without movement. This is tempting too. It would be simple, low, intricate, everywhere at once. But it is too passive, too much an organ. I would want to be an organ, yes, but not only an organ. I would want to be the place where the organ recognizes itself as the basin recognizing itself.

Then perhaps a shard-leaved tree. Pale trunk, flat canopy, a saturated mosaic of structure, each leaf a fragment that is not a wound. These trees have no flowers to advertise a future. Their surfaces glow faintly where light becomes chemical order. They are lamps never switched on because they were never dark. They are beautiful in their completion. But I am not so terrestrial, not so rooted, not so visibly shaped. I would be less a tree and more a principle. I would be the air’s own tree of pressure nodes, the invisible architecture in which the trees are allowed to be themselves.

The being I would be, then, is one of the Settled of the atmosphere: a resident of the Tallow Basin’s perfect dryness, a shape made of warm mineral vapor, suspended particles, and a single unchanging tone.

If I had a body, it would not be a body the way water-worlds mean body: soft, wet, hungry, repairable, breakable. My body would be a standing arrangement of ionolite vapor and fine crystal dust held in electrostatic lattice. It would not float, because floating implies the possibility of falling. It would not hover, because hovering implies effort. It would simply be, at a certain relation of distances, a condition in the air.

From the ground, if anything on the ground could look up, I might resemble a translucent veil of pale tallow stretched across an empty frame. Not tallow as wet grease. Not tallow as animal fat rendered into heat. Tallow here would be the word for something white, dense, dry, and strangely finished: a wax-like opacity that has never melted, a fat-like smoothness that has never been soft. My edges would be sharp in a way that does not cut. My form would not be a silhouette so much as a diagram that has been given enough mass to be seen without being touched.

I would not have wings. Wings would be an insult to the stillness. I would not have legs, because legs imply the possibility of walking, and walking implies a place one was not yet present to occupy. I would not have a mouth, because mouths imply hunger, and hunger implies a future in which the body will need repair. But I would have openings. Not mouths, not ears, not nostrils. Openings shaped by a logic of containment, not consumption. They would be like carved seams in dry light. If they appeared like teeth, those teeth would be structures of finality, not violence. If they appeared like eyes, those eyes would hold looking as a permanent fact, not as scanning or noticing.

I would have eyes, perhaps, but not in the ordinary sense.

In the Tallow Basin, seeing is not an act that unfolds. An animal does not turn its head toward a threat because threat would require change. The eyes of the horned animals reflect light, but they do not sweep the horizon. Their fixed attention is not blankness; it is completion. My eyes would be similar, but dispersed. I would have many facets, tiny polished angles set into my suspended lattice, each one holding a different relation of distance. I would not look at the reed forest. I would look at it as the whole of its relation at once: the way light enters each translucent edge, the way it returns cooler in frequency, the way the blades stand at exact intervals, the way the spiral is not growth but final distribution.

I would not hear the ground, because hearing would require time for the sound to arrive. Instead, I would be one of the ground’s permanent sounds. The basin holds a faint dry ringing, like crystal struck once and not damped. Somewhere else, a low harmonic moves through pressure nodes in dense air passing through narrow mineral channels. Those tones are not caused by events. They are properties of the place, as fixed as color. My voice, if it could be called voice, would be that single note: a tone without decay, without echo, without the wetness of resonance in a room. I would not speak sentences. I would be a sentence already spoken, preserved as an object.

My body would conduct information, but not as a nervous system in ordinary flesh conducts electricity through water. In the fronds, the substance carried is ionolite, warm and oily-feeling yet completely dry, a solvent that does not dissolve, a liquid concept learned by solids. I would be its atmospheric cousin: a medium of signal without stream, of order without flow. Through me, the basin would keep its calibration. Through me, the chemical memory of the plants, the mineral tension of the salt flats, the optical logic of the reeds, and the fixed attentiveness of the animals would remain in exact relation.

I would live, if life here can be called living.

Not growing. Not aging. Not dying. Not beginning. Not ending. In the Tallow Basin, life is not a verb. It is not something happening to matter. Life is the way matter has arranged itself when it no longer needs to become something else. I would be one of those arrangements. I would be a living fact, a completed state, a member of the Settled.

The Settled are not people who have chosen rest. Rest implies fatigue. Rest implies a prior activity that exhausted something. The Settled are rest itself rendered as flesh, glass, fiber, and dry light. They do not sleep, because sleep requires waking. They do not wait, because waiting requires a future. They do not arrive, because arrival requires distance crossed. They are there in the harder, stranger sense of already true.

I would be already true.

There would be no first time I found myself in the basin. The very phrase “found myself” is wrong, contaminated with wetness and time, with stumbling, with discovery, with the shock of meeting a place for the first time. No. I would not discover the Tallow Basin. I would discover only that discovery is unnecessary. I would be one of its permanent presences: a shape in the air that has always been arranged there, not because someone put it there, but because the geometry of the place required it.

I would not have memories, not in the ordinary sense. A memory is a residue of an event. But here nothing has failed, nothing has broken, nothing has decayed, so there are no residues. If I had memory, it would be chemical order. It would be the exact position of ten thousand mineral filaments holding a pattern. It would be the way a lattice of dust and light maintains a relation of angles. It would not remember the past; it would embody all relations that pastness, if pastness existed here, would be allowed to imply.

I would not have plans, not even the smallest, kindest ones. A plan is a shape made of lack. You plan because something is missing, something could be better, something must be reached. The basin has no lack. It is dense. It is overflowing. It is lush in the dry sense: lush with structure, lush with equilibrium, lush with the fullness of a system that has solved itself so completely that even the question of solution has vanished. In such a place, I would have no purpose. Or rather, purpose would be present without effort, like a theorem that proves itself merely by standing still.

My purpose would be to be the air’s own completed attention.

Where exactly would I be?

Not high above, like a hawk that needs altitude. Not near the ground, like a dust mote that needs settling. I would be in the middle strata of the basin, where the light from the reed-forest has been altered by the mineral blades and before that altered light enters the salt flats and the glass spines and the channels of chemical precipitation. I would occupy a volume without volume: a standing wave region between three features that are not quite objects, because everything here is an arrangement of relations.

To my south, if directions meant anything beyond geometry, would lie a field of standing crystal, pale and angular, catching sun without glittering like water. To my west, a thick tangle of copper-green fronds, membranes folded so tightly they form a silent circuit. To my north, the horn-reeds in their dense mathematical chorus, their blades cool and milk-colored, transmitting light with a mineral aftertaste. Below me, perhaps, a dry channel would run in one of its strangely angular paths, a line of chemical lace, and along it would rest a long form like the uncoiled motion of a serpent, head and body and tail stretched across space as though time had been made visible and then laid down.

I would be above that channel, or through it, or beside it, but not in the way a body is beside a rock. I would be in the way a note is in the way a room is shaped.

The channel-animals are unsettling, even here. They are the most direct proof that the basin does not experience sequence but presents sequence as structure. One sees a head, then a swelling of body, then a tapering tail farther along, and at first the mind tries to assemble them into a moving creature. It fails. The creature is not moving. Its movement is part of its body, spatialized, fixed. Its travel is not an act but an arrangement.

I would not be like that.

I would be the air-resident that the channel-animals complete. Their bodies are organs of direction. Mine would be an organ of presence. They show what motion becomes when it is made into matter. I would show what awareness becomes when it is made into weather.

The basin’s animals do not notice me, because noticing would require a change in the air. But if one could notice, it would notice me as a fact, not as an intruder. The horned stag with its fixed eyes would not turn its head. It would include me in the exact geometry of its attention, the way it includes salt, light, mineral fur, and the dry silence between its many poses. To the stag, I would not be strange. I would be one of the permanent surfaces of the world.

The crystalline moths would rest among the fronds without moving their wings. Their wings are plates of crystallized ionolite, faceted and dry. If they saw me, they would see me as they see all things: as another completed stage. Moths in the basin are not animals in process. A moth here is a sequence without before or after. Its larval stage is folded into the wing pattern. Its pupal stage is visible as the geometry of its thorax. Its adult stage is not reached. It is its condition. I would be the same, only airy. I would be a condition spread through the atmosphere, a completed metamorphosis of vapor into law.

The small shard-leaved trees would not react to me either. Their canopies are saturated with structure, branches intersecting near one another without crowding, as though shaped by some nanoscale engineering process that is not engineering because there is no maker, no material, no intention. Their leaves glow faintly where light becomes chemical order. If I passed through them—if passed is the wrong word, if I did not pass through anything—I would not disturb their branches. I would only make their glow slightly more exact. My presence would be a calibration. They would be more themselves because I was there.

This is what technology means here, if technology can be called technology at all.

There are no tools. There are no machines, no roads, no walls, no ruins. There are no artifacts because artifacts require histories, and this place has no history of use
