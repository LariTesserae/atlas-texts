I would not begin with a name.

I would begin with a hand.

A hand lifted out of reed matting at the hour before full light, pressing flat to the weeping stone. If the palm comes away wet, the day will be for scraping and sealing. If the wall is dry to the touch, the day will be for carrying stored water and making new catchments. This hand is mine, but it is not yet a person. It is only the place where the town’s first question enters, where the body begins its work before the mouth has anything to say.

Then I would be the sound that follows: the soft click of a shell knife on pale crust, the whisper of a bone scraper taking too much, too little, just enough. The mineral flakes falling into a reed tray. A gutter cleared. A ladder notched into fresh clay. A sleeping platform above a flooded structure, braced by three other structures that must not be disturbed. A cord tested with teeth because hands are too cold to feel where the strain is.

If I were allowed to choose, I would want to be in the lower part of the bowl, near the seam where one house has grown into another so completely that the wall belongs to no one. I would not choose the rim. At the rim one can see the town all at once, as an object, a wet nest, a patchwork stain. But the rim is too far from the work. I would want to be inside the tangle, where a passage opens sideways into a kitchen, where a roof is also a drying deck, where water runs under a floor that is also someone else’s ceiling, where you can stand up only if you first think about your head.

I would be human there, because the humans are the being this place made.

My body would be shaped by the bowl before it was shaped by memory. I would not stand tall for long. My shoulders would round into a working curve, my neck flexible from looking down, my spine familiar with bends through doorways and under overhanging platforms. I would have wide feet, toes that had learned to hold notched timber and damp reed, heels toughened by mineral-slicked stone. I would move as if the town had a hidden rhythm under the visible one: a footfall placed where the floor would bear weight, a knee raised beside a channel, a hand steadied against a wall that was safe only because an elder had said so.

I would be broad in some places and narrow in others, not according to any design, but because a family had spent generations moving ladders, carrying trays, bending through gaps, lifting pots over gutters. My knees would be cold. My lungs would know the mist before the sky made it. My clothing would be damp until someone remembered to hang it in a high draft, where the thin breeze would take from it not enough dryness to save it, only enough to make it tolerable for another day.

I would wear white without deciding to.

Chalky dust would settle into the creases of my knuckles, into the small of my back, into the folds of my hair when I slept on a roof and woke under the skin of mist. If a stranger asked whether it was ornament, I would not explain very well, because it is not. White is simply residue. It is the sign that the wall has given something, or that the body has been close to the wall. A pale forearm might mean hours spent scraping crust. White in the hair might mean someone had been testing condensation on a high shelf, or catching water in a split basin before the sun took the day from the air.

I would be a child first, though not an ordinary child.

In that bowl, childhood begins by learning the grammar of wetness. I would point before I spoke clearly. I would learn which hollows become pools at night, which channels drain slowly, which cracks carry a cold breath even when the wall looks dry. I would not be told, first, what to fear, but where water comes from. Before I knew the full name of my mother, I might know the name of the place where the spring behind our platform had changed course. Before I could say “come here,” I could say, with urgent finger, “wet, here, do not let it sleep.”

Somebody would call me *Small-awake*.

Not because I was brave, but because I had been listening when no one else was. I had noticed the dark shine before it made a thread. I had felt the new damp beneath a floor that had been dry the morning before. I had carried my palm against the wall like a question and returned the answer to the elder on the upper shelf, who would touch the stone herself, then nod once, then send someone to fetch a gutter-stone.

That is how names would begin for me.

A name here would not be a gift from someone above. It would be a recognition from the network. If I were steady on a soaked roof while older children hesitated, I might become *High-foot*. If I freed a snail trapped in a channel, I might become *Choke-clear*. If I could be trusted to move a ladder at the wrong hour and not wake the shelf above, I might become *Cord-holder*. If I learned which lintel supports which sleeping loft, I might become *Stone-teller*. If, one day, a wall gave out in the dark and I had rerouted a run of water before it took a floor from beneath a family, someone might say, after the repair was done, with a seriousness like prayer: *Raise-by-hand.*

My name would be thin, work-worn, sometimes changing. I would not think it sacred or ordinary. It would be a handle by which the town held me responsible.

I would not lead in any loud way. There is no square to stand in, no raised platform large enough for a crowd, no throne because there is no room for a person to sit straight while others wait around. Leadership in the bowl would be the ability to say, “Move the ladder from there to here,” and have it happen quickly because people know the hands that say it have learned to listen first. I would become one of those people, perhaps. Not by desire. By repetition.

If I were lucky, I would become a *water-rememberer*.

This is what I would be: not the one who owns the water, not the one who stops it, but the one who recalls where it has gone before. I would remember that a wall which weeps in spring becomes dry by summer, that a channel which is cleared too wide in one season cuts under a platform in the next, that the white crust near the northern face hardens sooner, that the bitter seep near the tuber troughs must not touch the drying fish. I would remember which stones the town cannot remove. I would know the names of stones that are not stones but dependencies.

I would have relatives by load.

There would be the person whose gutter emptied into my basin, whether I loved them or not: my *water-giver*. There would be the person whose shelf rested above my ladder: my *shoulder-under*. There would be the person who knew whether my floor would bear a new roof, and the person who knew how to tie a cord when the reed slipped wet under tension. A marriage might matter. Blood might matter. But in the morning, if a platform sagged, what mattered more was whether the right hands could brace it before the bowl took it down.

My friendships would be practical.

We would stand close enough to touch shoulders in a passage too narrow for two to pass properly, and one of us would say, “Let me help.” Not as an apology. Not as charity. As engineering. As love in a dense place.

I would know grief as a long exhale, because the town does not give room for dramatic sound. A wail would return too fast, bounce from wall to wall, become mistaken for collapse. So I would learn to cry quietly, with the breath, while someone else held a basin steady under a crack. I would learn laughter enclosed, rare, bright, the sound of something released. I would learn that anger is best spoken softly because shouting here is just another shape of panic, and panic can make a person knock the wrong support free.

I would smell like the bowl.

Damp hide. Boiled root. Fish drying on a high shelf. Warm clay. The faint bitterness of mineral stone. My hands would often be too wet to feel warmth properly. My hair would hold reed fibers no matter how tightly I tied it. My mantle would soak through unless I turned it often, unless I dried it at dawn, unless I learned to know mist not as weather but as a neighbor who sometimes stays too long.

My work would be never finished.

Every day would begin with a question asked to a wall. *What are you doing now?* And the wall would answer with touch, with shine, with a flake, with a crack, with the taste of the air. I would scrape weep-shell carefully, leaving enough crust to keep the face whole. I would carry pale flakes in a tray, pass them from hand to hand as if they were seeds, mix them with clay to seal a jar, pack them into molds to line a doorframe, spread them over a reed basket until it could become a skin. I would know when it was brittle, when it was still soft, when it would fuse too slowly and when too quickly. I would not waste a single pot because pots are bodies. A cracked pot becomes a lower basin. A broken mat becomes stuffing. A bone pin reworked until blunt becomes familiar, and its shape holds the memory of the hand that wore it.

I would raise floors over older flooded floors. I would lay reed bundles over woven lattices. I would shore a sagging wall with fresh slabs chipped to size, set in clay. I would build upward because the bowl does not allow much sideways. I would hang fish, tools, cords, bundles of split reed in the spaces above passageways. I would learn to step across a roof to reach a drying rack without breaking it. I would know that the roof is also a floor, also storage, also bed in dry season, also trap in wet season, and also the thing that keeps someone’s sleep from being washed away at dawn.

I would know the snails.

When they became too thick on the troughs, I would scrape them off with a shell. When a child held one to the pale light, I would not laugh. The child would understand something I had already learned: a snail is not merely food here. It is proof that the mineral water supports life even when the town has crowded almost everything else out.

I would know the eels.

In the dark, I would set reed traps where the channel deepened just enough. In the morning I would check them with careful hands, feeling for movement, for cold life, for the slippery thread of something that does not belong to the architecture. Small eels would be rinsed, gutted, dried, boiled, made into fat when there was fat to spare. They would be part of the diet of maintenance, food that kept the body able to keep the town from sinking.

Sometimes I would watch the ribbon-fish when it came into the lower basin.

The whole town would seem to slow and listen. The fish is not a promise. It is not a blessing. It is not even, truly, a gift. It is an interruption. It passes through the channels like a question the people cannot answer: *Why is this here? What does the water intend?* The fish is not burdened by a roof over someone else’s head. It does not know which reed mat supports a sleeping loft. It does not have a water-giver, a shoulder-under, a cord-holder. It simply moves through the wetness the town has spent its days negotiating, as if negotiation were its name.

I would envy it for a moment, perhaps.

But only for a moment.

Then I would look away and test a joint.

If I were asked what I was, I might answer: a person. Then I might say: a channel-runner. Then I might say: a gutter-speaker, in training. Then, after years, if the town had changed me, I might say: a stone-teller, a raise-by-hand, a cord-holder. These names are not proud. They are not permanent. They are the way the bowl speaks about a body.
