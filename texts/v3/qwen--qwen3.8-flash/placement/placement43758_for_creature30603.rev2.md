I would find myself where the light has a little time to fall before it lands.

Not at the rim, where a traveler’s shadow would be long and uncertain, and not at the deepest floor, where the root-mat grows too thick to be trusted. I would be on a terrace, a place only recently made, half-still, half-trembling, where salt plates have just begun to curl upward from the clay like the edges of old paper. A shaft from the opening above would be moving across the basin in the ordinary way of noon, bright, dry, slightly golden, and in that light I would be easy to mistake for something built rather than born: a low heap of pale shells, a broken arch caught mid-collapse, a crouching figure whose skin had taken on the valley’s chalk and ochre and ash until it seemed only another dry formation among dry formations.

At first I would be a sound.

A click. Then a smaller click after that. Then the pause in which something learns whether the click came from a pod opening, from a seed-cruncher biting into its own meal, from a salt-plate shifting under foot, or from me. I would not begin with eyes. Eyes come too late, too easily. I would begin with the ability to notice a vibration before it has a name: the faint upward ripple of a burrower passing beneath the mat, the distant percussion of a barking herd-lung calling across a hollow, the almost invisible shiver of a root-arch testing its own new strength against gravity.

Then, slowly, I would take on a body.

But body is too wet a word for it. In the Dry Hollow there is no softness to call upon. I would be assembled, not grown: plates of crystallized resin for shoulders, sheets of salt for forearms, brittle white fibers for the inner lattice that holds shape without sap. My skin would crack in fine patterns the way old clay cracks when the heat goes through it. Beneath the skin would be powder, not blood. If cut, I would not bleed; I would sift. If burned, I would not melt; I would click apart. My mouth would be dry as a grinding plate. My breath would taste of stone. My eyes—when I had them—would be narrow, mica-backed, rimmed with chalk, built less for looking than for measuring surfaces.

I would be one of the low people, perhaps, but not wholly. I would be one of them the way a ridge is “one” of the surrounding ridges: a temporary unity, a shape the valley has allowed to stand long enough to be noticed.

Or I would be one of their names for the strange and necessary thing among them. The near-people have so little need for final categories. They would not say, *I am a person*, because personhood here is always shared with the root-mat, with the herd-beasts, with the shells that crack into dust and become the floor again. They would say something that means more nearly: *the-listener-in-the-low-arch*, or *she-who-taps-where-the-ground-is-safe*, or *the-bead-in-the-pod-that-is-not-yet-finished*. They would scratch that last one once, perhaps, on a salt plate, and by the time the wind had filled the line with powder, the scratch would have become only another ridge, another mark among many, another proof that nothing in the Hollow can remain a single thing for long.

I would live in the seam.

There is a place, you would see it only after a while, where the terrace begins to curve downward into the mouth of a hollow and where a broken stalk-arch has fused with a living column. That would be home. Home would not be a room so much as a knot. A lean-to of woven dry stalks would sag against the arch; one of its panels would have already been reclaimed by a creeping vine, drawn over the roof like a hand closing. Inside, the sleeping mat would not be cloth. It would be a pressed bed of broken shell-plates, fine resin, and pale fibers that had been woven and unwoven until it became warm enough to trust. The doorway would be too small. I would have learned to crouch. I would have learned to test the floor with one toe before putting weight on it, because the mat below may be firm in one instant and hollow in the next, breathing or collapsing without warning.

I would sleep with both palms resting on the floor, not because I am afraid, but because sleep in the Dry Hollow is not a closing-away. Sleep is a loosening into contact. If the ground begins to rise somewhere below me—something vast shifting beneath the root-mat—I must feel the first slow expansion in my wrists before I move. If the barking herd-lungs stamp three terraces east, I will know by the answer the salt gives: a thin, bright shock traveling through the crust and into the arch above my head. If a pod bursts close by, I will not dream of it. I will already have dreamed of its swelling husk hours before, when the air smelled of heated resin and sulfur, when the stalks began to sing their structural song and the mat tightened with something that was not quite alarm and not quite expectation.

I would have no tears.

That is not a lack. It is a design.

In the Dry Hollow, emotion would not wet the cheeks. Grief would be a rhythm struck against a hollow stalk. Loss would be a small cairn built with one hand and then, if the wind shifted too quickly, allowed to be buried again by dust. Joy would be the rare and startling silence after a ridge has fallen and not taken the path with it—the pause in which the air, for a breath, is not crowded. Hunger would be a metallic taste in the teeth. Fear would be the first stillness, the moment when the body learns not to crackle.

I would be afraid of settling.

Not the fear of death. Death here is merely the process by which a thing becomes available to other things. The fear is the other way: the fear of finishing, of becoming too much a single shape. The root-mat is the greatest being, perhaps, but it is not one being. It is a thousand slow decisions pressed together. The stalk-wrights are hills, but they are not fixed hills. They are arguments with gravity that last years and then lose. The barking hollow-beasts are herds and also instruments. The salt flats are floors and also mouths. The moths are animals and also fragments of the wall that have learned to startle. In such a world, to remain one thing too long is to become a mistake.

So I would be unfinished on purpose.

My left shoulder might be partly bark. My right might be partly seed-cruncher shell, gathered and fitted by hand until it became a kind of armor that clicks when I move. My back could carry a row of small resin beads, not as ornament alone but as counters—each bead marking a crack, a safe path, a place where the mat rose like a breathing lung. My hips would be broad, low, calloused into plate-like pads. My fingers would be long enough to feel tension in stalks before trusting them with weight. My feet would know the difference between salt that will hold, salt that will slip, salt that will sing, and salt that is already beginning to be taken back by the white threads rising through it.

I would speak by striking.

Not loudly at first. A stalk would be tapped with a dry bone awl: two quick notes, a pause, one long rasp. This might mean something like *the ridge is moving here but not there*. A heel pressed to the mat would answer: *I am still listening; do not step yet*. If a traveler were near, I would not greet them with a shout. I would flatten myself against the ground, press my palms down, and let my body become as much as possible a continuation of the floor. If the traveler moved carelessly, the whole terrace would begin to shiver for it, and I would strike the nearest arch once, sharply, in a rhythm the people know: *Be still. Be dry. Let the valley know what you are*.

I would be the one who names places, but only by their instability.

In the Dry Hollow, names would be maps of what is about to happen. A terrace is not “the high white terrace.” It is “where the salt plates crack when the herd comes from east.” A hollow is not “the barking hollow.” It is “where the ground breathes and the roots do not close after noon.” A path is not “the safe path.” It is “the place where the mat is thick enough to forgive three steps but not the fourth.” Names would not settle like stones. They would have to be carried from season to season, struck against bark, tapped into the ground, whispered through the roar in a voice made of clicks and grinding.

If I were to name myself, I would probably choose a sound that comes from two places at once.

One part of the name would be mine. The other part would be given by the valley answering.

*Krik-Under-Arch*.

That would do for a while.

Then one day an arch would split with a noise like a struck plank, and the name would need to change. Or the root-mat would close over the arch entirely, and there would be no “Under” left. Or the barking herd-lungs would move closer, and my small clicking voice would have to be replaced by something with more percussion in it. Names would be temporary, too. They would not be false for that. They would be honest. They would admit that to speak here is to speak with the whole roaring, crackling, mineral economy of the Hollow behind you.

I would eat the valley without appearing to eat it.

A woman grinding seed-hulls beside a salt ridge would know my hunger without me saying it. She would press resin into cakes, fold in powdered stalk, beetle-larva flour, a pinch of iron-red dust, a measured chip of mineral salt. I would take one from her hand. The cake would be dry and dense and warm from the ground’s own heat. When I bit it, my teeth would make a sound like small shells cracking. My tongue would not salivate. It would simply work, careful and mineral, like a tongue built for powder. If I were thirsty—though thirst in the Hollow is not the wet thing it is above—I would lick a crystal plate and taste the slow, bright sting of salt and stone. There is no water to drink, so life has learned to drink dryness itself, to hold it in shell and fiber and resin, to turn absence into structure.

I would be careful with fire.

Not because I fear warmth. The Hollow is warm enough to feel like a body’s temperature, like heat rising through cloth from the belly of an animal. But fire in a place without water is a kind of insult to the balance. A spark is not a spark there. It is a summons. The dry stalks would answer it. The salt would ring under the wind. The root-mat, if burned, would not merely die in place; it would become a gap that every creature and seed-cruncher and burrower would rush to fill. The valley would build something new from the wound, and the new thing might be worse.

So my fire pits would be shallow.

They would be lined with clay and salt, almost always cold. If a people-band needed warmth, they would warm stones and wrap them in resin-hardened hide, or stand near a living column where the heat rose through its hollow chambers and came out through cracks in the bark like breath. Fire would be used only when the wind was from the throat above and carrying dust upward, so that any spark would be sucked into the light instead of falling into the mat. If ever I made fire, I would make it as a guest, not as a tool. I would watch it the way one watches something large below the surface.

I would live among animals as if they were my organs.

In the Dry Hollow there is no clean line between creature and place, so why should there be a clean line between me and the living noise? Seed-crunchers would crawl over my shoulders while I slept, using my shell-plates as another terrace in their endless grinding. Their mandibles would click against my back, and I would not call it parasitism; I would call it conversation. Pale burrowers would tunnel through the mat beneath my feet, and I would learn to read their paths as I read thought. Ashwing grazers would lift from the stalks in gray clouds that glitter once in the shaft of light and then fall again, and I would breathe in their dust and find in it the pollen of a hundred flowers that have not yet opened.

The long-legged salt-walkers would be my teachers of balance.

I would watch them pick across the crystalline flats with absurd precision, each footfall a small crack, each step a negotiation. They make ridges by breaking salt. They make paths by choosing where to place weight. They are beautiful not because they are graceful, but because they are exact. In their bodies, the valley has learned that motion can become construction. I would try to walk as they do, though I would fail, because the Hollow does not permit perfect imitation. Every being must add its own distortion to the pattern. So my footfall would be heavier than a salt-walker’s. My pads would crack a different rhythm. The mat would answer me with a different tightening. And the place would grow around that difference, as it grows around everything.

The barking hollow-beasts would lend me my voice.

Sometimes I would stand near the mouth of a deeper terrace and listen to them strike their dry reports against the walls. The sound is not wet. It is not the cry of anything soft. It is a log struck in a room, but the room is the world. When they bark, the whole valley shifts into relation. The seed-crunchers pause for half a beat. The moths loosen from the bark. The root-mat answers with a tremor so broad it feels like a second floor forming beneath your feet.

I would learn that bark and incorporate it.

I am small; I cannot make the walls answer like they do. But I can strike a hollow stalk in the same rhythm, with the same pause before the second note, and the people will understand me as one of the valley’s many loud organs. I would bark with the animals, not as animal, but as translator. A child of the near-people might follow me across a terrace, repeating the sound with a bone beater, learning the difference between *here* and *there*, between *safe* and *already changing*.

I would have friends who are not quite friends.

There would be a woman, low-built and dry-skinned, who repairs my shoulder-plate when it cracks. She does not say my full name; names are for strangers. She strikes a short rhythm against a stalk beside her grinding plate: *Krik-still-here-but-not-there*. I answer by pressing my heel once. We sit together in the shadow of a column that has fused with a broken arch, and the noise around us is so complete that silence becomes a place we can almost touch, a small pocket of not-yet-collapsing. We do not hold hands in the way of wet worlds. Holding hands is too still. We touch forearms and feel the shell-plates shift under one another, a dry rustle like the whole world being turned over.

There would be an elder who sits too long in one place.

Dust settles on his shoulders like powder on bark. He does not speak unless he has to. He listens with his feet. When he moves, he moves only after the mat has shown him that the ground is willing. Sometimes I sit with him, and he taps the stalk beside him three times, not to me, not to anyone, but to the hollow itself. After a pause, the answer comes: a faint creak from above, a shift somewhere below, a pod that bursts in a distant terrace with a sound like cloth torn. He nods. I do not understand. He does not explain. In the Dry Hollow, understanding is not given in words. It is learned by standing until the body is no longer separate from the thing it is listening to.

I would have children near me.

Not mine necessarily. Children are too loud to be assigned so easily. But there would be a small dry creature, barely old enough to crouch without stumbling, who presses her palm to the mat beside me and asks with her whole body: *where is it moving today?* I would show her how to wait. How to place her fingers not on the surface but near the root-hair fringe, where the mat thickens. How to feel the difference between a burrower’s path and the slow pressure of a large thing underneath. How to know that a patch of stillness is not safety but hunger: the valley deciding what to do with the space. She would not laugh as above-ground children laugh. Her laughter would be a sudden release of breath through dust, a dry exhalation, and the seed-crunchers would scatter for a moment and then resume as if nothing had happened.

I would love, or the dry equivalent of love.

Love would not be softness. It would be the willingness to let someone test the floor beneath you. It would be sharing a cake of resin and powder without turning away when the crumbs fall and the mat reaches for them. It would be striking a rhythm at night, though the dark has no stars and there is little to strike in the dark except your own small percussion. It would be placing your palm against another’s and waiting to see whether the valley agrees.

Because that is the truth of it here: all contact must be renegotiated.

No body remains one body. No path remains a path. No hut remains a hut. No name remains a name. To love anything in the Dry Hollow is to love it in the act of becoming something else. A wall may become arch. A dead moth may become powder. A child may become an elder with salt plates at the throat and cracks in the skin like clay. You do not love the final form. You love the process by which form keeps giving way to more form.

I would be a cartographer, then.

Not of distance. Of response. My maps would not be drawn. They would be tapped, scratched, and allowed to fade. One map might be a row of notches on a mineral bark, made with a flint blade and renewed every time the bark sheds a scale. Another might be a line of low cairns across a salt flat, each cairn set where the plates can be trusted for a season and then dismantled by the first good wind. Another might be a song in which each note is a known crack in the ground, and by striking the right stalks in the right order you can teach a stranger’s feet how to cross without falling into a hole that is not visible from above.

I would know that the best maps are ones that know they are wrong.

The Hollow is not hidden; it is simply always ahead of its own description. Every line drawn on salt will be filled with powder before the day is done. Every path made by feet will be answered by roots. Every mark left by a hand will be checked by the mat, the burrowers, the wind, the barking beasts, the salt-walkers’ slow cracks, the moths that explode into flight when you think the wall is still.

I would make my home among artifacts because I understand what artifacts are.

The people’s grinding plates, their cracked pots, their digging sticks, their embedded copper bead—these are not signs of weakness. They are signs of honesty. They admit that the valley will not be owned, only used briefly and carefully. A traveler looking for ruins would find them. A traveler looking for cities would find none. That is the difference between habitation and arrogance. The near-people know they are marks upon the noise, like stones in a riverbed, and so they build as stones do: low, mobile, temporary, and with a strange beauty that comes from being almost forgotten.

I would be one of those almost-forgotten things.

If the wind shifted, I would have to become something else.

This is not metaphor. In the Dry Hollow, if the wind enters the throat above in a certain way, the whole basin begins to speak in dry thunder. Stalks strike one another. Salt hisses down ridges. Fruit-hulls pop and snap. The air becomes a marketplace of broken surfaces. In that roar, the self must be reformed or lost. I would press my palms to the floor and let the mat answer through me. I would feel the terrace tilt beneath me. I would let a new crack open at my edge. I would fold my shoulder-plates into a smaller shape and wait.

Sometimes, when the roar becomes unbearable, the near-people do not shout.

They crouch. They flatten. They become as many-sided as the noise, so that no single part of their body is asked to carry meaning alone. The valley does not care for a solitary voice. It wants a chord. So I would let my bones become part of the chord. My shell would click against a broken stalk. My foot would answer the stamp of a salt-walker. My throat would hum with the resonance of the barking herd. I would become one of the valley’s thousand overlapping sounds, and in doing so I would survive.

Survival here is not escape.

It is participation so thorough that escape becomes meaningless.

I would not flee when the ground rises like a lung. I would move with it, or listen to it, or mark the place where it rose so that the next child would know not to press her palm there too long. I would not rage when my hut was swallowed. I would open another doorway where the root-mat had already made one. I would not mourn the death of my name when the arch changed shape. I would wait for the sound that would come from me after the change, and let the people find it again.

If asked directly, I would say:

I am Krik.

If pressed further:

I am the one who listens with both palms.

If pressed still:

I am the place where the Dry Hollow is learning to be a who.

And then the ground would shift.

A ridge would collapse somewhere far below the terrace, sending a wave of dry thunder through the root-mat. The shaft of light would move. The salt plates would begin to sing in their thin high way. A pod nearby would burst with a sound like cloth struck hard, releasing a cloud of pale dust that caught the ordinary midday light and hung in the air as if the Hollow had opened its mouth and spoken in miniature. I would turn my head, narrow mica eyes scanning not for danger—danger is too slow a word—but for the change in tension that tells you what the valley is about to do next.

I would not stand upright for long.

I would bend low, broad hands on the arch beside me, and answer the new tremor with a rhythm that means *yes*, though it also means *not there*, though it also means *this place is still possible for a while*. Then, when the wind moved again and the whole basin began its loud unfinished business, I would become one more dry surface in the roaring world: a shell, a knot, a name, a listener, a map already half-erased, a being who does not settle because the valley cannot, and who does not rest because the living mass beneath him never finishes asking what to become.

You would find me there.

In the light that falls through the aperture.

On the terrace that is not yet stable.

Crouched where the root-mat answers before the eyes can see.

Not a traveler.

Not a god.

Not a beast.

Not a person in the way a person imagines a person.

I would be the Dry Hollow’s next draft: partly stalk, partly salt, partly sound, partly the crouching attention of near-people who know that to live in a world without water is to live in a world without apology, where every surface must be built from what is left behind, where every silence is only the moment before a crack, and where the only true inhabitant is the place itself, learning through me how to listen to its own name.
