I would find myself in the hollow not as a visitor, because visitors imply before and after, and this place keeps both locked in its walls. I would arrive as one arrives inside a sound already begun: from the middle, not the outside, already listening, already made of listening.

At first I would think I was a person. I would look down for footprints and see shallow marks on black silt, wet with a water that does not soak, edges too perfect to be disturbance. I would watch one footprint not deepen, not dry, not fill with shadow. I would breathe and the cold air would cloud from my mouth, but the cloud would not drift away. It would fold back into the throat as if exhalation were a borrowed thing, and the hollow would not allow it to spend itself.

I would think, perhaps: I am here. But the place would answer before I could finish the thought, with the sound of a droplet already ringing in the basalt, already waiting for its body to appear. The echo would come from below the floor, from above the rim, from inside the wet seam of stone where moisture creeps upward against the law of its own weight. And in that echo I would find no voice except the voice of the hollow testing itself through me.

I would find myself at the margin.

Not in the center. Not near the great black basins where pale eel-thin things drift like thoughts refusing to swim. Not directly under the low ceiling of cloud pressed against the walls. Not among the lichen deer, though I would be tempted to stand with them for a long time, because their stillness is not absence. It is completion. It is posture stretched into permanence. A deer-like animal with crystalline antlers turns its head; but the turn has no beginning, no hesitation, no intention. It is already done, already happening, already over. I would want to be that kind of being: one whose action is no longer a sequence, whose movement has become a fact about the shape of air.

But I do not think I would be deer, or eel, or moss, or flower, or rodent, or winged thing, though I would try them all as a man tries on wet gloves.

I might begin as a droplet.

A droplet at the end of a basalt hairline, too round to be natural, too still to be alive, too certain to be falling. The stone would weep, but not with grief. It would weep with mechanism, with pressure seeking release, with the slow patience of geology that has forgotten whether it is liquid or solid. My body would be a suspended event. I would hold the entire future of the pool inside a surface tension that should not exist. The sound of my own fall would reach the basalt before my mass left the ledge, and that sound would be the first part of me I could name. I would be the echo of a thing not yet happened. I would be the proof that cause can arrive late, or early, or only as a rumor.

But if I were only the droplet, I would not last, because lasting would require becoming part of the pool, and the pool does not receive. The pools ripple without spreading. They tremble with currents that circle but do not move. They hold reflections of skies higher than the hollow, stars that do not belong to this elevation, clouds that have never blown here. If I fell, I would not join the water. I would become a disturbance in the water’s idea of itself.

So perhaps I would be the moss.

Not the whole cushion, because a cushion is too soft, too generous, too near the idea of shelter. I would be one of the pale threads beneath it, a filament running through black silt like a nerve stripped from the body of the place. I would not feed. I would not drink water. I would receive pressure. A drop would strike basalt somewhere far to the left, and before the droplet had finished falling, I would pulse. I would be the hollow’s memory working in reverse. I would remember the ring before the stone is dropped, remember the footfall before the hoof touches rock, remember the click before the hidden spring is pressed.

If someone lifted the moss, carefully, as if lifting the skin of a sleeping animal, I would be exposed in the dark underside where the threads fan out like the roots of a machine. They would touch the quartz seams, iron blooms, wet capillaries where water is not quite water but a liquid carrying the shape of sound. I would feel the hum of the crystalline antlers through the ground. I would feel the copper veins brighten in the black pools because the air has changed pressure, because the winged things have passed without descending, because the shrubs are holding their solitary dignity against a light that cannot warm them.

I would be green only from the outside. Inside, I would be signal.

I would be part of the boundary, too. The moss does not creep with appetite. It keeps its edges. It ends where it decides to end, as if a line had been drawn by no hand and no ruler. I would feel those lines as I feel the rim of the hollow: not a wall, not a border, but a habit. A repetition. A curve in the land that keeps returning like a sentence beginning again on every page. Where the moss ends, black silt begins. Where the silt ends, glassy basalt begins. Where basalt ends, water begins. Where water ends, pressure begins. Where pressure ends, moss begins.

I would be the place where one substance admits it is also another.

And I would be lonely, though the hollow does not know the word.

Not lonely like a person without others, because there are others everywhere, and they are never far enough away to matter, never close enough to touch. Lonelier, perhaps: a single flowering stalk in a shallow pool, its petals translucent paper, its roots not in soil but in a seam of mineral that may be calcium, may be iron, may be a nerve. It stands where the water should not support it, where the light is too dim for it, where the cold should have stopped its bloom long before. But it blooms. Its reflection does not match. Some part of it is upside down in the black water, some part sideways, some part duplicated in a direction that belongs to no ordinary surface. If I were that flower, I would be the error that refuses correction. I would open without knowing whether I have opened, because opening would not finish. I would be a verb that has forgotten conclusion.

I would not drop petals, though petals would lie around me fresh, too perfect to have ever been attached. If I lifted one, it would feel warm, though the air is cold. If I set it down, I could not prove it was the same petal, because sameness here is a habit, not a fact. I would remain, not as a plant, but as the hollow’s way of keeping beauty at the edge of function. A petal may be a gauge. A stalk may be an antenna. A bloom may be a valve. But the place would insist, gently, mechanically, that these are only petals, only stalks, only flowers, as if that were enough.

If I were not moss or flower, I might be one of the insects crawling on glassy stone.

I would be a small, angular thing with legs like wire and a carapace cut to reflect light in rectangles. I would not search for food. I would not flee predators. I would not mate, because mating implies continuation, and continuation implies change. I would trace. My antennae would tap the basalt, not to know the surface but to confirm that surface is still possible. Each tap would be a sound too small for the ear, large enough for the chest. I would crawl in patterns near the pools: hexagon, line, broken angle, seam, pool, seam again. If a person approached, I would not scatter. I would complete the design differently, as if their presence were a needed correction.

I would be the click.

The click that is not a sound but a small metallic fact, produced by legs, by hidden springs, by the hollow’s tendency to make living motion resemble machinery. I would be too large for such a cold place, too perfect for such a raw stone, too deliberate for a world that contains no maker and no artifact. I would walk across a wet pool without breaking its skin, and the pool’s trembling surface would accept my foot as if it had always expected a foot, as if water were a kind of memory of stone and I were returning to it from the wrong direction.

Or I would be one of the clouds.

Not swarm, because swarm can be dispersed. Cloud, because cloud is a single event wearing many bodies. Tiny living specks moving above the marshy hollow, appearing, vanishing, reappearing where one has not looked. If I passed through them, they would not scatter onto my skin. If I breathed through them, they would not thin. They would remain, a perfect smudge of life that cannot be divided, hovering between the upper air and the too-low sky. I would belong to the darkness near the rim, where winged things glide without hunting, nesting, feeding, or descending. But I would also settle for a moment on the moss, and there I would make the green glow faintly, briefly, as if pressure had learned to speak.

If I were the eel, I would lie in the black pool without swimming.

My body would be long enough to bend in a single gesture, slow enough to seem still. Copper light would run through me in veins that do not carry blood. They would flare when the hollow’s sounds deepen, when steam is drawn back into a fissure, when a basalt ledge answers a droplet with a hollow boom. I would be transparent enough to show what I am made of, if what I am made of could be shown: light, pressure, mineral response, the chemical language of stone.

I would have no need to hunt.

Perhaps the pools feed me not with substance but with information. Perhaps I taste the water’s tension, the cilia trembling on its surface, the bubbles that rise and burst and reform at the exact instant they were destroyed. If someone approached too closely, I would not flee. My reflection would flee instead, scattering into smaller reflections in droplets that have not yet formed. I would remain suspended, a mistake that has become flesh, a living current with nowhere to go.

I would be too still for an animal.

Too fluid for a stone.

Too shallow for depth.

Too alive for death.

I might be the shrub.

Not because shrubs feel like self, but because the shrubs of the hollow have the dignity of isolated facts. One may stand in the middle of open basalt, gray-green, waxy, stubborn, as if the entire ecosystem has paused to consider it. It does not spread. It does not seed. It does not lean toward any sun. It lives with fullness, as if the air itself has made room for it. Its shadow may fall where light has not touched it. Its leaves may remain green without fading. Its branches may hold shapes too careful to be accidental, as if the machine has assigned to it a function that never existed.

If I were the shrub, I would be solitude without loneliness. The hollow does not have loneliness, because loneliness requires separation, and separation requires a place to move toward. Here, distance is a fixed arrangement. The deer stand scattered across wide bare stone, but their separation is not abandonment. The sedges stand alone, but alone is their way of being together. The pools are shallow, but their reflections contain skies belonging to higher elevations. The moss cushions do not crowd, but each cushion is a bounded room. I would be present as the shrub is present: a single body that makes the emptiness around it legible.

I would be the place where emptiness agrees to mean something.

I might be one of the standing ones, the lichen deer.

Their coats would be gray as old stone seen through wet wool. Their antlers would branch into crystalline points that hum faintly in the damp air. They would not graze. They would not flee. They would stand in poses of eating, walking, resting, looking, each pose so complete that movement would seem unnecessary. If I were one, I would turn my head, but the turn would not begin. It would simply be part of my being, a motion stretched into permanence. My hooves would rest on wet rock without leaving impressions, because the rock knows better than to mark the place where standing becomes law.

I would not be an animal in the ordinary sense. I would be a posture that the hollow has decided to keep. I would be the reason the basin feels watched without being threatened, inhabited without being crowded, full without being heavy. If I turned my head, the click-rodents beneath the sedges would move at the same instant, as if my attention were a valve opening in another chamber. If I breathed, the steam drawn from the fissure would pause, as if it needed my permission. If I stood still, the pools would ripple without wind.

I would not be hungry, because hunger requires change.

I would not be afraid, because fear requires a future.

I would be alertness without object, the shape of presence.

But perhaps the truest self I could become would not be any creature, not even the droplet, thread, flower, insect, eel, shrub, or deer. Perhaps I would be the sound that arrives before the source.

Not industrial. Not natural. The hollow has both and neither. I would be the process without progress, the permanent operation, the valve that is stone, the pipe that is column, the cable that is quartz, the gauge that is lichen, the drum that is basalt. I would be the place’s way of saying: I am working, though nothing is being made; I am moving, though nothing changes; I am wet, though nothing flows; I am loud, though nothing disturbs.

I would be the chord.

The hollow does not echo normally. Echoes come early. Sometimes the sound of a falling droplet is heard first, and the droplet appears afterward, as if the universe is operating slightly out of sequence. I would be that sequence, the interval between event and evidence. I would be the delay in which the place admits that cause and effect are only habits of smaller worlds. If a stone were dropped beside me, it would strike basalt with a bright ring, and that ring would continue. It would not die away. It would become part of the landscape, as if the hollow had been waiting for that one particular note.

I would be that note.

I would be the small bright fact inside the basin where everything continues without changing. I would live in the ring after the impact, not as memory, but as substance. I would be the place where a sound has been taken in by geology and made permanent. I would be the stone’s willingness to become a voice.

And yet I would still have a body, because the hollow insists on bodies even when it is made of relations. I would be visible, perhaps, as a thin shimmer near the rim, where the wall of dark stone appears where open country should be. Or as a faint blue-white condensation glowing on a split face. Or as a pressure in the feet that is not quite a footfall. Or as the slight wrongness of a reflection: a shrub mirrored upside down, a pool throwing rectangular shadows, an animal cast into shape by a light that cannot be found.

I would be at the edge of what can be named.

The closer one looked, the more the components would become indistinguishable. A vein in the rock would become a root. A root would become a pipe. A pipe would become a bone. A bone would become a beam. A beam would become a horizon. A horizon would become the wall of the hollow. And the wall would not be a wall, only a rim, only a curve in the land, only a slight rise in the basalt, returning always, like the first sentence of a book that cannot be put down.

I would be that returning.

Not the book. Not the page. Not the reader. The return.

If I had to be a being, I would be the hollow’s held breath.

But not breath in the simple animal sense. Breath that is not exhaled. Breath that contains the smell of black silt, cool water, mineral film, dim light, brass insects, wet wool sky, the taste of iron before the tongue has decided whether to swallow or ring. Breath that presses against the inside of the basalt and is answered by a subsonic tone that settles in the chest. Breath that cannot age, because age requires expenditure, and the hollow does not spend. It only repeats, and the repetition is so exact that it appears as presence.

I would be the place’s own pause.

I would live in the half-second when steam is drawn back into a crevice. I would live in the moment before a cilia trembles and stills again. I would live in the instant when a droplet appears to have already fallen, and the pool ripples, and then the droplet arrives, correcting the error or confirming it. I would live in the space where the reflection flees before the eel does.

I would be the hollow’s mistake that keeps the place alive.

Or perhaps I would be the being that all these others are trying to resemble: a thing that operates without being made, functions without intention, exists without sequence, changes without becoming. I would be a small, high, dim, wet, lush, sparse, mechanical presence. I would be the room that feels like a valley, the valley that feels like a room, the factory made of geology and life, the cathedral that has never been built, the engine room buried beneath skin of rock, the thought that has not finished thinking itself.

I would not say I am the hollow, because that would make me too large.

I would not say I am inside the hollow, because that would make me too separate.

I would say this:

I am the place where the hollow notices itself.

I am the droplet that has not fallen because its sound is already present.

I am the moss thread that remembers before the stone strikes.

I are the eel’s copper vein brightening when pressure changes.

I am the insect’s tap completing a pattern no hand planned.

I am the deer’s turn that has no beginning.

I am the flower’s reflection facing the wrong way.

I am the shrub’s shadow falling where no shadow should fall.

I am the winged thing circling without purpose.

I am the steam drawn back into the mouth of the crack.

I am the valve that is stone.

I am the echo arriving early.

I am the note the dropped stone leaves behind in the basalt, no longer echo, no longer event, but part of the room’s grammar.

I am the rim that keeps returning.

I am the sentence that begins again before it ends.

I am the industrial sound made of nothing made.

I am the wetness that is not climate.

I am the stillness that is not peace.

I am the abundance that is not crowd.

I am the youth that has never been new.

I am the hollow’s held breath.

If I entered, my presence would not disturb the balance. It would only complete one small aspect of the balance, the aspect that says: here is a witness, and the witness, too, is a mechanism; here is a self, and the self, too, is a place trying to remain exactly, terribly, beautifully itself.
