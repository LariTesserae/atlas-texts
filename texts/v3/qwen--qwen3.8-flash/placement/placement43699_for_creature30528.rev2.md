I would find myself in the Kettle-Colonnade not as a visitor, but as a sentence the basin had almost completed: water had carried the thought across the basalt, mineral had laid down a shelf for the next word, and then, one cold afternoon, fog had left a person on the causeway like a cloak set out to dry.

For a while I would not be a single being at all.

I would be a pause between two drips. A small indentation where the wind had decided to make itself heard. A wetness gathered in the hollow of a column, trembling before it fell. A doorway facing another doorway across an empty plaza, each waiting for a body to turn the waiting into arrival. A bowl of grain on a sill, not yet eaten, not yet spoiled, not yet offered—kept in the middle of its purpose, which is hope.

Then I would begin to take on shape from the place.

My first instinct would be to listen. Not to search for meaning, not to ask what the town wanted, only to hear which stones answered and which only repeated. I would crouch at the edge of a channel and hold my hand flat against the black-green basalt. The cold would enter my bones like a polite guest. Under my palm, water would move by paths invisible from above, threading through hair-cracks and old toolmarks, adding a thread of pale mineral wherever it lingered long enough to think. If I stayed still, I would hear the place not as a sound but as a grammar: click, hum, breath, slap, hollow, silence, click.

I would become a person because the place kept making room for a person.

Someone, perhaps myself in another season, perhaps no one at all, had swept a dry ledge near the eastern lane. Someone had laid reed mats across the dampness so that the stone would not be heard too loudly at night. Someone had placed a bowl beside the hearth-circle, half-full of grain mixed with dried lichen, and I would understand that it was not for gods. It was for the first guest the town had imagined.

That guest would be me.

I would not have a name at once. Names arrive slowly where the wind is too loud and the stone too wet. A woman carrying a reed basket along the causeway might glance at me and call me with a short knock on a standing stone: here is a place to stand, here is a dry foot. An old man scraping mineral from a rain trap might say nothing for an hour, then place a bone pick into my hand as if I had been asked to hold it long ago and had merely been late returning.

In time, I would be called a Tender, though the word would fit poorly, like a wet cloak over shoulders still drying. Tender implies a soft hand, and here the hand must be firm where water is soft. I would chip back crusts that closed mouths before their time. I would guide reed stoppers clear. I would listen to causeways with the heel of one foot before letting my weight pass. I would know that a sound too bright means water has begun to drum beneath the stone.

I would be one of the few who walk the streets.

There is a difference between a person walking a city and a person rehearsing a city. I would do both. I would carry a basket of harvested moss through a plaza that has never held market, whose lichen continents shine under shallow water as if they are maps of places no one has named. I would stop at each doorway that has not yet been entered and look inside, not because I expect to find anyone, but because the doorway is asking whether it is still worth being a doorway.

I would answer, without speaking aloud, yes.

My body would learn the basin’s rules before my mind accepted them. My knees would go green from kneeling on wet ledges. My nails would collect pale dust from mineral lips. My cloak would become heavier with fog, its reed weave darkening and lightening as the day shifted. I would move carefully because the town is too large for its silence and too wet for its quiet. A careless footfall would crack the air open. A raised voice would return in fragments, a chorus of false people saying the same word back with different mouths.

So I would speak in knocks.

Two short strikes against a dry slab: this stone holds. Three quick taps against a channel wall: is the water clear? A long scrape with reed across stone: rain is coming. A palm pressed flat against a column: listen. Not for me. For the town. For the way it is changing.

Sometimes I would be old.

Not aged as a human grows old, but aged in the way a standing stone becomes more itself by accepting weather. I would sit on a high ledge where wind enters between columns, letting the hum pass through my ribs until I cannot tell where the column ends and the person begins. My fingers would trace the mineral seams because each year some crack is thinner, each season some lip grows sharp enough to cut skin if touched carelessly. I would know the town’s mood by its noise: whether it breathes, sings, complains, or prepares. I would leave the bowl of grain on the sill and watch the fog with patience. My body would be tired in the way of things that have returned again and again to the same unfinished work.

Sometimes I would be young.

I would be the one who practices knocks when no one is watching, hiding behind a broken column and striking a hollow stone to see how far its voice travels. I would run barefoot along a causeway after rain, then stop, embarrassed by the sound my feet had made, because in the wet plaza even playfulness can become loud. I would kneel by a pool and tap the black surface, startling midges, watching their tiny spirals rise as if the air itself had become confused. I would want the town to answer me faster than it was built to answer.

Sometimes I would be neither young nor old.

I would be the hand holding the bone pick at the mouth of a channel, deciding whether mineral growth is an injury or a gift.

That decision is what I am, in this world: not a conqueror of stone, not a maker of city, but a reader of water’s first drafts.

I would keep the central plaza.

Not own it. Own is a word too heavy for a place so wet. But I would be the one who comes there when the last fires have gone cold and the few hearth-holds have drawn their reed curtains. I would kneel where the channel is deepest, where the standing stones are black with damp and some are broken at the top like teeth chipped by use. The air would be colder than the rest of the basin, and the water on the floor would not tremble at first. It would only wait.

I would touch it.

The surface would hold a thin skin of light, and under that skin the mineral film would move. Not fast. Not slow. With the certainty of something that does not know it is being watched. A crack beside the channel would be narrower than yesterday. A lip of pale stone would be thickening along the edge of the plaza floor. Somewhere a roofless house would be filling itself with moss as though the moss were walls, and though the walls were unfinished, they were not failed. They were becoming.

I would mark the new lip with a row of small stones.

This is all a person needs to do: not finish, but notice. Not complete, but consent. Not build the whole town, but say to one part of it, yes, you may keep growing, and to another, no, not yet, not here.

In the Kettle-Colonnade I would be the one who keeps that boundary.

I would be a sound-tender, but also a moss-scrubber, a channel-wright, a fog-reader, a person who moves a drying rack after a storm because the dripping stone would otherwise make the rain too loud in the sleeping place. I would be every role the basin needs but no role enough to make me separate from it. The people here are rare, so I would carry many of their tasks the way a single column carries the wind’s whole voice. I would not be famous. I would not be remembered as a founder, a king, a prophet, or a hero. I would be remembered, if at all, as one who returned when the mineral closed too far across a lane.

There are many names that could fit me.

The Woman Who Listened to the Channel. The Old One Who Left Grain on the Sill. The Child Who Knocked the First Causeway into a Name. The Hand on the Column. The Voice Not Quite a Voice. The Guest Who Never Arrives but Always Almost.

But if I had to answer simply, I would say: I am the next person this place imagines.

I am not human first.

This is the strange part. If I tried to be only a person, I would feel too small for the scale of the town. The streets are too wide. The plazas are too broad. The houses stand at distances no family would build for one another unless they were speaking to absence as seriously as to presence. If I were only a person, I would be lonely. But the emptiness here is not lonely. It is architecture. It is space prepared for future footsteps.

So I am made of that preparation.

When I walk down a causeway, it is partly because I am walking. Partly because the causeway has asked for walking. When I carry a reed basket of moss, it is partly my hand and partly the basket’s waiting for the next basket’s waiting for the next hand. When I knock a dry stone to signal stable ground, I am also the dry stone knocking the town into being.

I would be, in part, a living extension of the place’s unfinished syntax.

Not a god. A god would be too finished. Not a spirit. Spirit is too light. Something heavier and stranger: the basin’s habit of becoming a town wearing a temporary person.

The world around the Kettle-Colonnade would keep its normal shape.

Outside the ring, the moor would stretch cold and sparse under high sky. Wind would move across rough stone without much to say. The horizon would be honest and uninviting. There would be lower valleys with people, animals, fields, quarries, roads, markets, grief, joy, children, fires, and the ordinary noise of a settled world. Somewhere below, copper might be traded, or wool sold, or a story told in a house where everyone speaks loudly enough to hear one another.

Inside the ring, the rules would tilt.

Stone would grow where wetness stayed. Sound would sharpen wherever water pooled. The town would be full of future inhabitants and almost empty of present ones. The fog would have the patience of an animal that has learned not to rush. Light would fall as it always does in the open, but here it would find the basin too wet and too newly shaped not to multiply.

I would live in that difference.

I would not always know which side I belonged to. Sometimes I would feel as if I had been left there by outsiders—a wanderer stranded in a place too large to be abandoned. Sometimes I would feel as if I had been placed there by the basin itself, the way water places a leaf in a pool: gently, without asking the leaf, as if the leaf has always been meant to float there.

I would choose, eventually, not to choose.

I would stay because staying is the work.

In the mornings I would wake in a house with a sagging roof and moss in the corners. The doorway would face a plaza wide enough for a festival that has not happened. I would step carefully from the sleeping slab to the causeway, listening before walking. I would hear the marsh pools sucking and bubbling as the first fog thinned. I would know by that sound whether the town was breathing well or whether the water had begun to hide.

I would check the reed stoppers.

I would scrape a channel’s mouth where pale crust had closed too neatly, making it look as if the town had decided to stop speaking there for a while. I would clear it, but only until the water clicked back to life. Then I would leave a small shelf of mineral untouched. That shelf might become a step. If it did, I would step on it next year and call it a gift. If it blocked a door by spring, I would chip it back and call it a correction. If it sealed a crack where no crack should heal, I would mark it with a pebble and say, not yet.

I would tend the empty houses.

Not with sadness. With grammar. A blank wall is a sentence without its object. A roofless house is a word waiting for rain to finish. A doorway facing an empty plaza is not an absence; it is a relationship that has not yet learned its name. I would sweep their thresholds, not because dirt matters, but because the sweeping is a way of keeping them in the present tense. I would leave a stone bowl of water on a sill, and in a dry season I would watch it shrink like a clock with too little time left.

At midday, when the light is plain and high and the fog lifts enough to show the basin’s wet streets clearly, I would feel almost human.

Almost.

The basalt would gleam black and green. The reeds would bow and lift. The pale lichens would spread in slow continents of gray and copper across plaza floors. The standing stones would be dark where the wind’s moisture touched them and bright where sun had dried their tops. The whole place would look as if someone had set it out for guests: clean, ready, empty. I would carry lichen up a lane in a sealed basket, and the midges would rise when my shadow crossed a pool. I would stop and let them settle. The pool would click after me.

I would answer it.

Not loudly. With one small knock.

I would return late afternoon to the roofless house. The missing roof would leave a rectangle of sky above the hearth. Fog would enter and lie flat on the floor like milk poured slowly. I would set lichen drying near a wall, away from the drips. I would move a clay pipe if the condensation had found a new path and was singing against the storage basket. The song would be beautiful, but beauty near dry goods is a risk. I have learned that in the Kettle-Colonnade, even loveliness must be placed where it will not ruin bread.

I would eat carefully.

Grain from below, boiled with reed roots, thickened with horsetail shoots. Lichen flour, bitter until rinsed, then sweetened by patience. Sometimes a snail from a lichen wall. Sometimes a small frog caught under a moss mound, though I would feel as if the basin had given too much of itself and I had received too little in return. The meal would be small. It would matter more than abundance.

At dusk the fog would crawl from the lower terraces.

The sound of the town would change.

The wind would move through the standing columns and turn them into a low choir. The channels would click as the last water ran unevenly through them. The marsh pools would breathe. The grasses would hiss. Somewhere a pipit would land on a broken column, tilt its head, and leave as if it had checked whether the city had finally found its people and still had not.

Then the last occupied houses would draw inward.

People would move toward the few hearths, carrying peat, dry grass, reed torches capped in clay, small baskets of gathered lichen. No one would call out loudly. No one would need to. A knock would tell a companion that ground was dry ahead. A sweep of reed across stone would warn of rising water. The town would gather in its own sound, each household becoming one more voice in the chorus of wetness.

But I would remain at the central channel.

Not always. Sometimes I would go home, sometimes I would sit at the hearth with others, sometimes I would be too tired to keep the plaza. But often I would stay where the largest stone lip was forming, where the mineral threads were thick enough to catch the fog-beads, where the crack by the channel was becoming a new edge.

I would kneel.

I would breathe slowly, because breathing too hard makes the water tremble, and when the water trembles, the place becomes loud.

I would touch the stone.

Under my fingers it would be colder than outside air and wetter than expected. A faint film of pale mineral would move across the surface, not in waves, not in pulses, but as a certainty. The place is not waiting to be discovered. The place is waiting to be continued.

I would understand myself there.

I am not the first builder, because the builders came before the names I would know. I am not the last builder, because there is no last builder in a town that grows. I am not the person who will live here forever, because forever is too solid for water. I am not the city’s ghost, because ghosts belong to places that have already happened. I am the place’s rehearsal of people.

That is who I would be.

A rehearsal is not a failure. It is the shape something makes when it is trying to become real. The Kettle-Colonnade rehearses city in its broad streets. It rehearses homes in its roofless houses. It rehearses festivals in its empty plazas. It rehearses language in its channels. It rehearses breath in its marsh pools. And sometimes it rehearses a person by leaving one standing at the central channel at night, hand on wet stone, listening for the next sound the town is making.

I would be that next sound.

Not all of it. Not one clear note. A small click, like a pebble falling. A thin hum, like a column waking. A wet slap, like a drop deciding to become part of the floor. A name not yet spoken but already laid out in the stones, waiting for someone with enough time and silence to read it.

If I had to answer the place directly, I would say:

I would find myself at the center of the basin, at the singing channel, in the cold wet plaza where the mineral skin creeps and the future footsteps already echo because the architecture of the place is made of promise.

I would be one of the Few Who Return, but also the one they return for. I would be a Basin-Wright, a fog-reader, a sound-tender, a guest who arrives because the town has been waiting long enough to become a host. I would be small enough to kneel at the water’s edge, strong enough to chip a blocked channel, careful enough to speak only when the stones permit.

And beneath the person, I would be the place’s slow becoming given a temporary voice.

The water writes. The stone answers. The moss accepts. The wind hums through the ring. The fog makes the empty streets feel crowded with possibility. The people tend the unfinished town because the town is not ruin, not wilderness, not monument. It is a grammar of wetness that has not yet found all its speakers.

I would be one of them.

I would arrive in the Kettle-Colonnade carrying nothing but a reed basket and a bone pick. I would leave behind only one small line scraped into mineral crust: a mark for next season. A place where someone else, or I again, will kneel and understand that the town has changed its mind a little, and someone had better listen.

The basin would answer.

It would answer in clicks and drips and humming columns and sucking pools. It would answer in the thin green of new moss and the pale seam of stone forming over intention. It would answer in the bowl of grain waiting on the sill, still waiting, still not spoiled, still offered.

And I would answer back.

One knock.

Then another.

Then the long pause after which the town begins to speak.
