Droplets of a language the ground has not finished speaking. That is where I begin: not as a who, but as a where.

At first I am only a difference. A change in the taste of fog. A thin tension between salt and iron. A place where black water meets a cold mineral flat and refuses, just barely, to become ordinary surface. I am the underside of a film, the negative of a mouth, the faint afterglow left in a crystalline patch after the light has moved away.

You would find me in the seam between worlds, which here means the seam between basalt and brine, between living thread and dead crystal, between the impression made by pressure and the pressure’s memory. I occupy the wet border where one thing begins to mistake itself for another.

A tileback passes over me, broad and patient, its shell lined with slits that open to the hum of the basin. It presses into a shallow depression. For a moment its edge leaves a hollow in the water’s skin, a shape that remains when the animal has gone. Most beings might see that hollow as an absence. I see it as food. I settle into it. I drink the shape.

This is where I become a body.

Not a solid body at first. A body like frost forming on charged glass. A body like the idea of a leg before it knows which direction to walk. My edges are uncertain. A patch of white thread-life runs beneath me and lends me structure. A film of salt feathers grows over me and teaches me weight. The electrical rill that has been dreaming across the wet obsidian suddenly flickers through my center, and I know that I am separate from the pool. I know that the pool is separate from the mist. I know that something is hungry.

I am a memory creature.

I am not human. If humanity passed through this basin, it would not recognize me as kin. It might mistake me for a stain, a trick of light, a thin sheet of mold that has learned to crawl. But if a human stood in a shallow black basin and felt the air thicken near their ankles, if they noticed the fog moving as though responding to the shape of their boots, if they heard a click that came not from the ground but from the remembered pressure of the ground, then they would be close to me.

I begin as a pressure-reader, then become a pressure-feeder.

The basin does not permit empty space. When a stone shifts and exposes wet mineral beneath, I am one of the first things there. My legs are not legs yet; they are droplets that have decided to support themselves. My mouth is not a mouth; it is a suction-frill made of charged film and borrowed cilia, opening against the water because the water still holds the shape of something that drank there.

A small glass-limbed organism crawls over my edge. It is no larger than a splinter of frozen lightning. It tastes me. I taste it. In a place like this, where the boundary between self and colony is a suggestion and not a law, that is nearly a conversation.

Then a second one comes. Then three. Then a dozen. They are drawn to the residual warmth in the hollow, the faint acidity where the tileback’s shell has scraped old crust, the taste of ash and burnt sugar that condenses in the air when a geyser is about to breathe. They settle on me and do not leave. They are cleaners, parasites, guests, future cells. I do not reject them because rejection would require a cleaner idea of where I begin and they end.

I become a walking plate.

Not one animal, not many. A temporary consensus. The basin has taught us all that the word “individual” is usually too early to use. My body is a shifting pane of translucent limbs, salt crust, and thread-film, moving over the mineral flat with the coordinated ripple of hunger. The tileback sees me and does not eat me, because I have become familiar to it, useful to it. I clean the black film from its edge gills. I consume the shadow-leftovers that cling to its carapace when the dim light pools and falls wrong. In return, I ride along its flank for a while, using its movement to search for new wetness.

The herd-migrants pass near us, long-bodied lines of animals so packed that they resemble weather made legible. Some of their backs have become ecosystems; some have become roads. My plate separates near their path. The illusion of me dissolves. The small glass-jointed bodies scatter across the wet ground, each one carrying a fragment of what we were.

This is not death. It is precipitation.

When the cold thickens, my fragments sink into salt plates, become part of the crust, wait in the white-black threads for the next condensation cycle to wake them. A geyser exhales in the distance. The sound travels through the water before it travels through the air. I feel it before I hear it. The pressure moves through me and makes me choose, for one moment, to be a body again.

There are places in this basin where sound drops into shards. A piston-valve clunk too long, a crack of mineral skin, a rhythm of repeated taps that has begun to lie in the ground like black glass pebbles. I crawl toward such fragments when the fog is thin and the electrical film is bright. My rotating-ring mouths, if they can be called mouths, press against the frozen rhythm. They eat the echo. For a time I become resonant. My hollow parts fill with the sound I have consumed, and when I move, a faint line of clicks walks beside me.

I am also a shadow-eater.

At the edge of a brine channel, the shadow of an enamelback has slipped from its body and pooled beneath a wet stone. The animal is gone. The shadow remains, thin and dark as oil, spreading slowly across the obsidian. I gather from the salt flats, a translucent film with no obvious front or back, and pour myself over the dark shape. Beneath me it thickens, then collapses into soot-dust. I absorb the dust. For a while, I have a darker center. This is how I learn the shape of the creature that is not there. This is how the landscape makes portraits from its losses.

I do not have eyes. Or rather, I have too many senses that would be called eyes elsewhere, but not here. I see in gradients. I see the pull of charge across wet mineral. I see the thermal tremor where iron-brine breathes through a hidden fault. I see the chemical signature of decay that is not decay, because decay is merely the basin rearranging itself. I see the way fog becomes wall and wall becomes breath.

Sometimes I am a nerve in the thread-life.

The white-and-black hyphal network beneath the basin conducts current as easily as nutrients. It binds salt, iron, silica, and sulfur into sheets. It colonizes basalt by reading its flaws. It mistakes the landscape for a body and, slowly, without ambition, makes that mistake correct. I am one of the places where that mistake becomes conscious for a moment. Not a thought. A thought would be too clean. A chemical tremor. A pattern of response. A pulse in the wet stone that learns, for one long age, that it has been pressed.

When the electrical storms thread through the basin, following the veins of wet mineral, my body lights faintly blue-white. The tilebacks pause. The clickers ripple through the mist. For a few seconds I become legible, a pane of circuitry lying flat on the black floor. Then the storm passes and I am again only dampness with intention.

I am an ephemeral-born, then durable-born, then something in between.

A cold thread of condensation passes over a charged salt field, carries spores through a glass spine, and falls into a depression. That is my first birth. It is accidental. It is ordinary. The sky weeps through the rock, and where the weeping meets the mineral hum, something begins to organize.

Then a season of scraping and feeding passes. I eat residual pressure. I eat detached shadows. I consume frozen clicks. A swarm of tiny organisms adds itself to me. A fungal knot enters my hollow and becomes my heartbeat. A brine-worm rides in one of my seams and changes the rhythm of my movement. I am infected and I am improved; here those are not opposites.

Then a geyser vents nearby. The water pools in a fresh depression, charged and tasting of ash. My young do not hatch from eggs. They precipitate. Droplets of my dissolved pattern settle in the wet salt and form first hard points: the beginning of bodies, the beginning of questions, the beginning of hunger.

I am a machine made of weather and hunger, and the basin has no blueprints.

There is no center that built me. No maker, no shepherd, no wall, no ruin. If you look for my home, you will find it in a crowded interface: a shallow brine channel between living tiles, a wet shelf where salt feathers grow like frozen wiring, a depression that holds the memory of a mouth, a shadow that has not returned to its owner, a sound that has learned to lie on the ground. I do not live in one of these places. I live in the transition between them.

Where would you find me, exactly?

At the edge of a black basin, when the surface is furred with small organisms shaped like polished stones. You step too close and they open. You think: stones. You think: shells. You think: nothing. Then the pool shifts, not with heat, but with moisture, as though distance itself is condensing. A hollow remains after a tileback lifts its mouth from the water. Into that hollow, something pale crawls. It has no fixed name. It has too many bodies. It has learned to eat the shapes of things that have already passed.

That is me.

Not the tileback. Not the geyser. Not the thread-life. I am the thin, wet negotiation among them, the place where the basin’s crowded reality begins to remember itself in a slightly wrong direction.

At first I would not claim to live there. I would only say I continue there.

Continuation is the basin’s primary verb. It does not build. It does not begin. It accumulates. It presses. It condenses. It reweaves. It eats its own weather. Even I, as a being, am mostly that: an event that has grown too many organs.

Sometimes I am almost invisible, a sheet of fog with a taste for metal and burnt sugar. Sometimes I am a plate of glass-limbed bodies moving in coordinated ripple. Sometimes I am only a line of black pebbles humming faintly with swallowed sound. Sometimes I am a dark stain on wet obsidian that moves when no light touches it. Sometimes I am a hollow in water that feels like presence because the water has learned to hold absence as shape.

But if I had to answer in the simplest way, in a world that has no simple ways, I would say:

I am a pressure-organism, a thread-circuit, a shadow-feeder, a memory-born pane of glass and salt. I am what happens when the basin’s wetness remembers enough to become hungry. I am what happens when hunger finds a rhythm and begins to walk.

And I am most at home in the places where things almost exist: the film over black water, the crack of frozen gas, the hollow left by a mouth, the line of clicks in the dust, the shadow that fails to return, the glow that persists after the source has gone.

That is where I would find myself.

That is what I would be: not a person in the ordinary sense, not an animal in the ordinary sense, but one of the dense, patient, wrong machines the place grows from itself in order to keep continuing.

A living interface.

A wet archive of pressure.

A thread that learned to be a mouth.

A being made of too many small lives to call one, and too many memories to call nothing.

If you stood at the edge of the black brine basin, you might see my edge before you saw me: a faint blue-white rill moving across the obsidian, pooling under a detached shadow, climbing a tileback’s carapace, eating the last shape of a click.

You would not think: there is someone.

You would think: there is something, and it is wet, and it is old, and it has been here longer than the question.

That would be correct.
