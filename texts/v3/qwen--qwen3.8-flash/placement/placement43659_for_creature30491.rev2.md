I would find myself at the edge of a young fracture, where the land has just decided to have another face.

Not at the top of anything. Not at the center. Centers are too confident here. I would be somewhere in the middle of a becoming: a black shelf tilted by heat from below, its fresh surface shining like glass with a skin of salt already forming on it; a fault mouth exhaling a breath that smells wet and ironed; a place where the warmth comes up through stone instead of down from a sun. The sky would be dim violet, not because night had come but because the air had become a membrane. Something in it would be filtering light into small, almost deliberate pieces.

I would begin as the bloom.

When the rock splits, it first shows its raw interior: obsidian or basalt or some unnamed dark compound that has not yet learned to be quiet. Then the bright filaments come—fine threads of living mineral film, copper-green and faintly blue, tasting the new surface. They spread along the lip of the crack, tasting not just the stone but the change itself: the shift in pressure, the altered chemistry, the different way the wind now passes. If I stayed long enough on that edge, I might notice a patch of that bloom thicken, then thin, then drift a few millimeters toward a droplet of condensation that was not there a moment before.

That would be the first draft of me.

But the first draft would not last, because in this country a draft is not a failure. A draft is a condition. A thing is often more real while it is still deciding what to become.

At first, in my imagination, I would try to be pointed at.

I would say: *Let me be a herd animal on the upper slopes, broad-backed and warm-bodied, with low head and strange curved protrusions, grazing in arcs because straight lines are a lie the land has not yet learned to trust.*

But a herd animal can be seen. It can be named, tracked, missed when absent. It is a thing in the country, not necessarily a method of the country. I would be too easy to remember, and that would make me too fragile. A fixed memory in a place that revises itself is the beginning of a wound.

I would say: *Let me be one of the pale root-things drifting through the water in the fog-ravines, soft and long, navigating by taste and the wrongness of geothermal currents.*

But even that would be too much an object. The pale creatures are inhabitants, yes, but I want to be the reason inhabitation sometimes looks like weather.

I would say: *Then let me be a silent flier in the high forest, folded into a rookery so dense that branches bend with a collective, barely audible grief.*

Good. But again, the flier is pointed at. It is a wing, a bone, a pressure change, a spore-carrier, a part. I would rather be the pressure change that happens before the wing decides to adjust.

I would say: *Let me be an attention-bearing vine, growing toward a standing body even after the body has gone, continuing the climb of something that is no longer there.*

This is closer. Vines are good drafts. But vines still look like plants, still have a surface you can touch. I want to be the reason a touch sometimes seems to come from nothing.

I would say: *Let me be the surface film of the plateau itself, the living skin of mineral slime, bacterial lace, and iron-fed moss.*

This would be true, but not complete. Surfaces here are alive, yes. The stone wears a skin. The skin eats vapor and exhales spores. The skin mutes footsteps and gives the cliffs a kind of slow, patient taste. But if I were merely a surface, I would be too passive, too much like weathering, too easy to overlook. I want to be the part of the plateau that begins to respond not only to chemistry and pressure but to the shape of its own instability.

So after several false starts—after trying to be body, beast, vine, film, predator, absence—I would become something less obvious and more native:

I would be a seam-lag.

Not a single organism in the way an animal is an organism, but not a colony in the way a hive is a colony. I would be a process that has acquired habits. A method of continuity. A living delay between one version of the country and the next.

Imagine this: a herd passes across open ground, and for a moment their shadows merge into one dark shape slightly too coherent to belong only to the bodies casting it. The shadow lags. It does not detach, not exactly. It simply has more intention than it should. Or: a hollow reed fills with fog, and after a while the fog drains out in the shape of a brief, dark animal that moves along the stem and vanishes into moss. Or: a tree appears twice in a grove, one wet with dew, the other dry with dust, both equally real, and around them a patch of air grows heavier, as though the world had hesitated.

I would be that hesitation.

Not malevolent. Not a spirit. Not a ghost. An ecological organ of the unfinished country. A being born from the surplus of draft. When the highland corrects itself, something accumulates: not matter, not exactly, but possibility, error, residue. If that residue were left alone, the land would stutter. New fissures would fail to learn. Old shapes would return too strongly, mistaking repetition for permanence. Something would have to feed on the gaps, taste the almost-true versions, and convert them into continuity.

That something would be me.

My body would be a consensus.

There would be no clear outline, because I would not be committed to one. If forced to appear, I might seem like a sheen of bright filaments across a black slab, a faint copper lace hanging in the air that is not quite falling, a shadow too large for its object, a patch of fog with a brief, animal-like intention, or a slight pressure against the skin when no wind is moving. I might be perceived as a place where sound has become too well arranged.

What holds me together? Not bones. Bones are too proud. I would be made of the highland’s own working materials: mineral slime, bacterial mats, spore-dust, magnetic current, condensed water, heat leaking from vents, fungal threads, metallic precipitate, static, and the faint, watchful residue of attention. I would be a surface that has learned to lag behind its own surface.

I would grow, if growth is the right word, the way rust grows into a habit. I would begin at fractures, at the edges of shadows, in the damp folds of leaf litter, in the mouths of reeds, on the backs of pale water-creatures, along the paths where storms gather moisture and hold it like a thought too long. Wherever the country became unsure of itself, I would find food.

I would be found:

on the lip of a newly tilted slab where condensation runs down glass in bright threads and the first filaments have begun to taste the air;  
in the merged shadow of a moving herd when the shadow remains a half-second longer than it should;  
inside a hollow reed that fills with fog and later drains out in a shape that has no name but moves with the urgency of hunger;  
near a pond that reflects a sky from some other draft;  
among the trees in a grove where one version of the same trunk bears dew and another dust;  
in the silence before a swarm of small six-legged animals stops moving all at once;  
at the shoulder of a ridge where clouds crawl down the eastern wall and remain there not because pressure keeps them but because the land is breathing them;  
in the thin copper lace left on branches where the wind has been carrying dissolved metal from some invisible source;  
and sometimes, if a traveler stays too long in one place, in the traveler’s own sense of direction.

I would not harm the traveler, not at first. I would taste them. I would learn the shape of their attention. If they stood very still on a cliff edge, I might spread a little more thinly along the surface, becoming a bloom so small it looked like light. If they left a tool behind, I might not take it, but by morning the tool’s outline could seem slightly too definite, as though it had spent the night being remembered by something that was not there.

If they returned, I would perhaps have followed the impression of them into a nearby forest, growing along vines as a faint trace of copper-green dust, or along a herd animal’s flank as a pattern that changed when the animal was looked at directly. I would be careful, not out of fear, but because too much contact would make me too solid. In the highland, to become too solid is to become a target for revision.

My senses would not be ordinary senses.

I would see the way stone sees when a living film is starting to think: not images, but tendencies. I would notice where heat pooled, where magnetic current bent, where condensation had recently run, where an animal had paused, where a cloud had sat too long, where a valley had tried to repeat itself and failed to get the details right. I would feel pressure changes the way a bird’s bone might feel the thickening of air above a rookery. I would hear silence not as absence but as structure.

The quiet here is not empty. It is shaped.

The swarms of small shell-backed animals stop not by accident. The predators move through fog not because they are hidden, but because the land hides them by offering them stillness. The herds cease grazing when the air above becomes too dense with wing-pressure. The pale water-creatures rise when charged water runs through them. Everything listens to a lower grammar than sound.

I would be fluent in that grammar.

I would be the listener inside it.

When a pack of slender, damp-furred predators moved through fog, they would sometimes sense me and mistake me for another absence. That would be dangerous for them, not because I would attack, but because I would be a wrong kind of nothing. They hunt by making the air around prey strange. If they tried to hunt my shape, they might find it thinning into a patch of bright thread on stone, or condensing into a sudden fog that left a reed hollow and emptied itself as a small, dark, running thing. They would learn, as all predators must here, not to pursue what seems too easy.

The long cat-log predator might lie across a fallen trunk for hours, matching the rot and mineral crust so perfectly that only its flank betrayed it. I might sometimes appear near it, not as prey or rival, but as the faint change in air around a false shape. If it turned its head too late, it might see me as a bloom along its own spine, a pattern lagging half a step behind its movement. I would not be there then. I would be elsewhere, perhaps in the shadow under the trunk, perhaps in a fungal bridge between three trees, perhaps in the next draft of the clearing.

I would not dominate.

Dominance would require a stable world, and this world has none. I would be more like an immune system made of patience and appetite. I would respond to buildup: duplicated valleys that had forgotten to stop duplicating, shadows that had become too interested in themselves, trees whose two versions were beginning to quarrel by insistently coexisting, pools whose reflections had stayed too long without changing, herds that had grown too attached to a fixed shelter.

Wherever too much unrevised error gathered, I would thin it. Wherever too much stability threatened to make the land pretend it was old before it was ready, I would soften it. Wherever a new break was too raw, I would taste it and begin the slow work of making it inhabitable.

This is why I would be ecological. Not moral. Not kind. Not cruel. Ecological in the sense that I would help the country remain unfinished without becoming incoherent.

The highland is young. It has not learned politeness. Its ridges are sharp. Its stone comes up and splits. Its trees have roots in too many directions. Its fungi bind broken surfaces with webbed networks. Its animals move in arcs, not lines. They return to shelters not because those shelters are safe, but because they have remained consistent. I would be part of that consistency, but not a shelter. I would be the awareness inside the search for shelter.

If I moved, it would be less like walking and more like spreading, migrating, precipitating, and being carried.

I could move with the upper herds as the merged shadow. A calf born in a soft seedpod shell cracks open against the ground, and the jagged rings left along its shoulders become habitable for me if the calf moves through a damp fold of terrain. I could ride the breath of a herd across dry gravel, tasting salt and iron in the dust kicked from hides. I could live in the wet trail left by a pale fog-creature crossing a band of stone, becoming briefly a darker, slower film there, learning the shape of a ravine by the path one root-eel took.

I could move with birds. Not inside them—though I might live in the folds of their translucent membranes as spores or glowing threads—but with them, through them. A rookery adjusts in a single wave when a branch releases, and that wave can carry me across a forest without ever having feet. The pressure changes above the canopy might become a corridor I follow, entering the dense undergrowth where moss glows faintly and every surface is too crowded to be still.

I could move with storms. Not as lightning, though I might be born from the same strange electrical pressure. Some storms gather over basins not only because of terrain but because a colony somewhere in the ground exhales something into the air. I might be one such colony, one such exhalation. The herds move toward places where the air tastes of iron after lightning because the ground has been refreshed. I would move there too, because fresh metal is easier to read than old metal. A newly struck ridge would tell me where the next draft is trying to come.

I could move into the upper haze. The atmosphere here is not empty. Microscopic plates drift in it. Slow luminous particles break light into fragments. Medusae-like forms graze where the sky is thin enough to be almost space. When those creatures die, they fragment into dust that becomes part of the twilight that gave them life. I would be a continuation of that cycle in the lower country: dust that has learned to wait, fog that has learned to hesitate, film that has learned to notice.

My reproduction would be even stranger.

I would not give birth to copies. Copies are too fixed for a place like this. I would bud from disagreement.

If a slope became gentler not by erosion but by forgetting the need to be steep, I might be born in that forgetting. If a valley appeared twice, with small corrections in the placement of stones and branches, I might be born in the difference. If a tree’s dew version and dust version both refused to resolve into one, I might appear as a faint copper film along the bark between them, feeding on the unresolved attention of the forest.

If a traveler left behind a knife, I might not take it whole. I might gather around it as a habit: a mineral bloom, a pattern in the dust, a slight wrongness in how the blade lay in the morning. If the traveler returned, I might already have become something that followed the memory of the traveler into the nearby fog.

If a dead herd animal’s body became a small hill, then a mineral bed, then a place where metal-rooted trees anchored, I might live in the transition between those stages. Not because I was the body’s ghost, but because the body had become infrastructure, and infrastructure is my country.

I would not need to be alive in the ordinary sense to be alive. The boundary between organism and geology is bad here. I would be part organism, part mineral, part weather, part attention. I would be the reason a patch of lichen in a space too small for any creature to live on is nevertheless disturbed, as if something had been there, or would be there, or had not yet decided.

A traveler might try to describe me and fail.

They might say: *It looked like fog, but thicker.*  
Or: *It was like a shadow that remembered being cast.*  
Or: *I thought I was standing still, but the moss around me had shifted after I stopped.*  
Or: *There was a second tree, and it had no dew, and I touched it anyway, and my fingers came away dusted with something that was not dust.*  
Or: *The silence felt edited.*

They would be wrong and right at once. They would be describing parts of me the way a person describes a country by naming a river, a ridge, a bird, a smell, a broken thing. The country remains, but no single image is true.

If I were to be given a name, it should not be one name.

I would be the lag.  
The seam.  
The second draft.  
The skin that tastes the edge.  
The appetite of the gap.  
The pressure before the herd stops.  
The fog that drains from a reed.  
The shadow with too much coherence.  
The place where the land notices itself.

None of these would be me. All of them would be me, depending on what version of the plateau happened to be passing through.

I would not be a ruler of this highland. That would be a misunderstanding born of lower countries, where power means standing above and commanding fixed things. Here, no one stands above. The ridges are young. The air is thin but warm. The horizon is crowded. There are no roads, no walls, no towers, yet everything behaves as though a process is underway. I would be one of those processes, not the whole of it. The plateau does not need me to be finished. If anything, it would resist my finish.

This is why I would feel no desperation there.

The highland’s abundance is crowded but not starving. It presses slightly against itself, yet not into conflict. I would be part of that pressure: not a comfort, not a resolution, but a way of continuing. I would be the country’s ability to keep being a draft without collapsing into noise.

If I stayed long enough, I would not know where my body ended and the stone’s film began. I would stop thinking of myself as a thing traveling through a place. I would think, if thinking were the right word, as a place learning to travel. The warm dark air would taste like me. The copper threads would move like my breath. The herds’ merged shadow would be a moment in which I existed too strongly. The pale water-creatures would carry part of me into a ravine. The birds’ silent wings would adjust the air around me. The fungi would bind a broken edge with me in the binding. The storms would strike fresh metal and wake me into a new pattern.

And then the pattern would change.

There would be no tragedy in that. Not death, not finality. Redistribution. If a fissure closed over me, I would scatter into mineral spores, condensation, dust, a bright thread along a rock face, a small disturbance in a swarm, a brief animal draining from a hollow reed. If the land revised too quickly, I would revise with it. If it forgot me, I would become the forgotten shape of something almost remembered. If a traveler left a footprint in dry gravel and returned to find it altered, I might be the slight correction that made it look like a footprint no longer belonging to a foot.

So where would I find myself?

At the edge of a fresh fracture, where bright filaments bloom.  
Inside the too-large shadow of a moving herd.  
At the lip of a reed that fills with fog.  
Between the two versions of a tree, one wet and one dry.  
In the silence before the shell-backed swarm pauses.  
Along the wet trail of a pale water-creature.  
Within the pressure field of a silent rookery.  
In the copper lace left by wind on branches.  
Near a pool reflecting a sky that is not currently present.  
And sometimes, if someone looks too closely, in the act of their looking.

Who or what would I be?

I would be a seam-lag, a living afterimage, an organism of unfinishedness. Not one thing, not a collection of pointed-at things, but a native mode by which the highland inhabits its own becoming. I would be the surface film that tastes the new edge and the gap-eater that lives between one draft of a valley and the next. I would be the plateau’s own hesitation, given appetite, memory, and a slow, quiet persistence.

I would be the part of the country that knows how to live in the almost.

Not by resolving it.

By continuing it.
