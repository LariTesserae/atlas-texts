Under.

That is where I would be.

Not on the plateau above, with its pale wind and short grass, but below it, in the place where the land has dropped open like a cold cupped hand. I would find myself at the wet seam between root and mud, under a sedge tuft, beneath a beaver lodge, in the shallow dark under the ice at the edge of a pool. I would not be a human, because humans are too loud for this town, even when they stand still. They carry too much air around them, too many ideas of emptiness. Here there is no emptiness. If I entered, I would need to enter small.

At first I might think myself a droplet.

I would be the bright thing clinging to the base of a grass blade, trembling but not yet falling. I would be the sheen on a boulder’s face, the silver thread running down clay, the ordinary rain-light caught in a hollow. For a moment that would satisfy me: to be only shining, only cold, only the thin skin of water that makes the whole basin seem newly made. But the droplet is too simple. It cannot live long in a town this dense. It needs a body. It needs a passage. It needs to be spent.

So I would become smaller, warmer, quicker.

I would be a water vole, or something vole-like: a small brown body with a blunt face and whiskers that taste the air before the air can lie. I would have dark eyes set low in my head, paws made for pushing through wet thatch, a tail short enough not to be a nuisance in tight tunnels. I would live where the reed roots knit into a black floor, where the moss is deep enough to hold a footprint and thick enough to erase one. My nest would be a small round chamber under the bank, lined with grass pulled through rain and dried by body heat. When the cold came, I would ride the town’s under-currents: burrows beneath the snow, waterways beneath the ice, runways pressed through dead reeds that had not yet rotted.

But if I stayed long enough, I would stop being only a vole.

Because in this place, a creature is also the track it makes. A life is not merely the animal; it is the strip of reed it leaves standing like a pale tooth, the flattened path its back presses through moss, the little mound of earth it pushes out from beneath a tussock, the breath-hole in winter ice, the droppings on a stone that algae will claim before morning. A vole is also a city’s hidden architecture. I would be that, too.

I would be the Under.

Not a single being with a name and a body, but the town’s living underside: the dark network of burrows and channels, the porous layer where roots and insects and amphibians and seeds all press together. I would be the hole beneath the lodge where a beaver’s tail has slapped the water into a ripple. I would be the otter slide worn smooth, not by one body, but by many seasons of bodies. I would be the moss tunnel where a frog rests without moving, its skin cold and sweet with water, listening through vibration. I would be the tiny alley under a fallen trunk where a shrew runs with such speed it seems almost to be a stitch pulled through green cloth.

If a human stood over me, they would not see me directly. They might see only a tremor in a stem, a ring on a pool, a feather drifting into a channel, a pale bone catching light in moss. They would think the place empty. That is the first mistake. There is no gap between presences. The town is too crowded for solitude. Even a footprint is a kind of architecture: a small dammed valley in mud, a bowl holding water, a temporary habitat for a larva, a mark read by another animal as danger or familiarity or nothing at all.

I would make my home in the northern seep, near the cold fringe where water comes out of the ground in trembling threads. It is not as dramatic as the lodge mounds, nor as visible as the gravel flats, but it is full. The stone is not stone there, but a wall of wet living skin. Algae stains the ledge. Moss fills the cracks like green velvet poured from a mouth. Liverwort forms little scales, and beneath them, in water films no wider than a human eyelash, insects graze and leap and die and feed the next layer. I would be that layer. I would be the cold edge where mineral becomes plant, where plant becomes animal, where animal becomes decay, where decay becomes ground again.

I would not mind being prey.

Predation is part of the town’s quiet grammar. A vole is not a tragedy; it is a packet of warmth moving from grass to fox, from burrow to owl, from hunger to heat. If the hawk came, I would be the brief dark in the reeds. If the mink came, I would be the smell of musk on a bank, the ripple that does not ring. If the owl came, I would be the sound the owl listens for: a small footfall, a shifted blade of grass, a held breath beneath the snow. But in becoming those things, I would not disappear. I would return as track, as bone, as droppings, as moss, as reed-root, as the memory in a predator’s muscles that says: here is where the warm things run.

The town has no streets that are not also living. No walls that are not also homes. No ruins that are not also building material. I would be built by this. My body would be a temporary arrangement of the same things around me: hair, feather, mud, bark, feather, down, shell, seed, water. I would be a knot in the sedge, not a separate knot but the knotting itself. I would be the wetness that makes the knot possible.

And the shining would not be merely what I see.

It would be what I am made of.

Every surface here has just been rinsed, or is never fully dry. So I would be shining not because I seek beauty, but because water is the town’s first language. I would be the bead sliding down a willow bark, the lens in the hollow of a leaf, the film over a stone, the glaze on a beetle’s back, the black mirror of a pool holding a gray sky. I would be the thing that makes a moss patch look like velvet after rain, the thing that makes an abandoned lodge seem carved rather than built, the thing that makes a footprint in mud look sacred.

There would be no great center.

If I looked for one, I would find only process. The beaver’s cut willow is not a monument. The otter platform is not a house with a fixed boundary. The bird nest is a temporary dome that will become feather-lace, then litter, then mulch, then a seedbed. The burrow is not an absence of ground but a ground in another form. The town is assembled from appetites and accidents, yet it has regularity because life leans on life until patterns harden into places. I would be one of those places.

I would be the under-city’s pulse.

At dawn, when frost rims the water and the breath of birds is clouded, I would be the first movement in a reed mat: a small shift, a snapped stem, a drop falling into black water with the sound of one soft clock-stroke. At noon, I would be the stillness beneath that water, where fish shadows slide over pale sand and tadpoles cluster in green clouds. At evening, when the basin darkens and the cold gathers in the lowest folds, I would be the trail through moss that does not vanish, only becomes darker, only becomes waiting.

If asked again where I would find myself, I would say:

At the place where everything is occupied.

If asked what I would be, I would say:

A small animal, and the path it makes; a wet hollow, and the breath inside it; a burrow under the plateau, and the cold shining that tells the world it has just been rinsed by rain.

I would be the Under.
