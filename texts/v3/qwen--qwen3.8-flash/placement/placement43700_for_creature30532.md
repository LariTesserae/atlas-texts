…in the only way it knows: not as faces, not as bodies waiting to be named, but as corrections.

A line of dust too straight for wind.  
A spore-cup tilted one degree south, so the heat off the white shelf will carry its cloud into the street below.  
A patch of copper moss bitten clean at the corner, then grown back as if the bite were part of a plan the plants had agreed to after the animal had gone.

I find myself at the edge of that agreement.

Not outside it. Not looking in. Not a traveler who has come to the Hollow with a wet, hopeful idea of life and then slowly realizes, with disappointment or wonder, that this world has found another way to live. I am already the other way. I am one of its small necessary wrongs that makes the order possible.

The first thing I understand about myself is not shape but absence.

There is no thirst in me.  
No pulse of liquid heat.  
No swelling breath that leaves a visible cloud in the thin high air.  
No need to find a seam where dew might gather, because dew has never been a guest here. The word *damp* is, for me, a rumor too, imported from some other country. When I touch myself—were I able to touch myself as a self, which I am not always—I find not soft skin, not warm oil, not sweat, not mucus, not the secret wetness that living things elsewhere carry like stolen water.

I find glaze.

A dry film over living fiber.  
A hard lichen skin over moss that has learned to stand.  
A shell made not from one body, but from many deaths arranged until they became support.

My body begins at the base with blue-black compactness, layers of old fibers pressed into a kind of dry muscle that does not contract so much as remember its angles. Over that grows a middle stratum, darker, denser, threaded with copper salts and tiny mineral needles, so that when the pale sun strikes me at the right hour, I do not shine so much as *hold*. I hold the heat the way a black plate holds the day and releases it after the lamp has moved west. At the tips I branch into something almost like hair, but the hair is stiff, almost sharp, a fine star of crystal-fiber arranged in radiating points. Each point is too small to see with one glance, but together they make my outline appear blurred, as if I had been drawn twice: once as an animal, once as a patch of moss that once wanted to be an animal.

I do not have a face.

At the front of me there is a cluster of sensory plates, waxy and pale as shell halves, edged in black. They do not look out. They read pressure, temperature, vibration, the faint metallic taste of air over heated salt. They are not eyes, though they know the shape of light. They know where a shadow falls because it is cold, where a plate glows because it has stored the day, where a spore-cup waits because its fibers are tightened and its lid is asking for a finger, or a foot, or the breath of heat from something small and dry passing over it.

My mouth—if it can be called a mouth—is a rosette of tiny grinding crystals, not teeth but arranged like the teeth of a key, each one worn by the particular dust it has been made to move. I do not eat moss in the way a wet thing would eat leaves, tearing, chewing, moistening. I sift. I bite at the old crusts. I take in mineral skins, shed husks, crushed crystal, the powdered dead that collect in the seams. My digestion is less a fire than a sorting. My waste is not waste. It becomes packing material. It becomes mortar. It becomes the fine dark soil-like matter that makes the streets look wet from a distance.

This is why, when someone sees a street, they are seeing, in part, me.

I have many legs, though not so many that I seem like the old stories of wet creatures. Six long, jointed limbs made of brittle-seeming plates, yet toughened by a glaze that would crack if you tried to strike it. The first two pairs are adapted to walk on edges: sharp as needles, tipped with calcium caps that can rest on a crystal shelf without sliding. The last pair are broader at the joints, fringed with stiff moss-fibers, and when I press them down they catch heat. When I lift them they release it. I move not by hopping or crawling alone, but by shifting warmth through my own body and through the ground.

Sometimes, if I am still, I am mistaken for a mound.  
Sometimes, if I am moving too quickly, I am mistaken for a piece of wind-borne husk.  
Most often, I am mistaken for nothing at all.

But I am there.

I find myself in a seam between two white calcium shelves, where a black plate has cracked and the moss has not yet decided whether to become a wall or a roof. This is my place of work. Not because I have chosen it. Not because I have a deed or a memory of owning it. Because my legs know the angle. My thorax knows the heat. My crystal mouth knows the grit. This seam requires a correction, and I am made to be it.

The corridor of plates runs slightly north, toward the inner density of the city. The seam runs south, down into a chamber roofed by overlapping lichen leaves. Between them is a threshold, not marked by any stone, only by the way one kind of moss gives way to another: the blue-black compactness of the lower body ends, and the copper-green tufts begin. There is a change in pressure. The air moves differently. The ground holds heat less, gives it back more slowly. A spore-cup near the threshold has not opened for one cycle. It is over-tight. I press one leg to its rim, not to open it, but to tell it the time is not yet. I read the heat in the cup. I read the tension in its lid. I walk on.

This is the whole of language in the Hollow: pressure corrected. Heat acknowledged. Passage offered.

If I stop, the city notices. Not with alarm. Not with voice. Not with a hand on my shoulder. It notices in the way a plate notices a crack. It notices in the way a moss cushion notices weight that is absent. Some buttress grows crooked. Some corner of the market square is swept by the wrong wind. A spore cloud falls too late and drifts over the cathedral hall, where the altar-mound is only a mound and does not need dust, but dust comes anyway because I have not made the small correction that keeps it where it belongs.

I am not sovereign. I am not priest. I am not builder. I am the line of granules that says: *not this way, this way.*

My kind is not alone. We are not a colony, though we resemble colonies if you insist on seeing them. We are a repetition of a function. There are others near the rim who tend the bare plates and keep the young cliffs from collapsing too early by laying fine dust across fractures. There are larger, flatter things in the shaded recesses beneath the resin columns, with bodies like black feathers dipped in sugar, who move through the fern-analogs and tune their crystal needles by rubbing their edges, so the whole recess hums with a sound like pearls falling through sand. There are pale stalk-things, half plant, half architecture, whose bodies grow into colonnades because their young press upward in rows and then die in rows, leaving behind the mineral bones of a court no one intended. There are dust-mites so small they could be mistaken for weather, and they are, in a sense: weather made of feet.

I am one among them, and also not.

For a long time, if you asked me what I was, I would have said: a small creature. Then I would have doubted. Then I would have said: a passage. Then, as the heat shifted under the calcium shelf and my crystal mouth found a husk, I would have said: a correction. Each of those was true at the angle I was standing, and none of them was the final thing.

I do not settle because I am made of settlement in progress.

The Hollow looks built because it is built by lives that do not distinguish themselves sharply from the city. My shed skin lies across a crack and becomes a buttress. My molted spore-fan, once empty, traps dust and turns a floor into a step. My walking path wears thin the moss and makes a street. My eggs are laid in a niche roofed by overlapping mineral leaves, and when they hatch, the first shells they leave behind strengthen the wall. The wall becomes a room. The room becomes a temple only when light enters it from above and no one can explain why. But the light does not explain. The room does not ask for worship. The moss grows because there is a corner for it to grow into. The corner is there because I grew. I grew because the city grew. The city grew because the moss, the salt, the heat, the dust, the crystal, the insect, the lichen, the pressure, the passage—all the things that do not negotiate knowingly—have made an arrangement so dense it looks like intention.

If I look down at myself, I see that I am also this arrangement.

My lower body is blue-black and fibrous, like the base layer of moss. My middle body is dark, compacted, threaded with salt. My upper filaments are copper-yellow, stiff, star-tipped. My joints are capped with white calcium, the same material as the shelves. My back carries a plate that once belonged to another of my kind, dead long ago, or perhaps not dead but molted into usefulness. On that plate, lichen has spread in a pale film and dried to glaze. When I move over a dark seam, the glaze catches light and becomes a line of soft inner fire. When I stand still in a shadow, I become almost a stone. When I rest at the threshold, I become almost a step.

My name—if a sound like a name could be taken seriously here—would be too small for a mouth. It would be the click of crystal on calcium, the faint granular whisper of my legs over dust, the pressure wave that makes a spore-cup hesitate before releasing its cloud. If a human tried to pronounce it, they would fail by making it wet. They would round the consonants. They would add breath. They would call it something like *Sift-archivist* or *Glass-beetle* or *Moss-warden*, but those are translations by way of loss.

I prefer the correction.

I move along the inner terrace now. The pale sun is overhead, not hot exactly, but definite. It polishes the black plates into mirrors. In one mirror, I see no face. I see a low architecture with many limbs, a patch of copper fuzz, a ridge of lichen, a seam of dust moving because something that looks like a street has decided to walk.

The cathedral hall is ahead.

Not a cathedral. I keep saying that because human language slips in, even though the Hollow does not need it. Not a cathedral, though the plates tilt upward on both sides, and the lichen film has spread over a dome of interlocking spore-fans, and the light comes through in a pale yellow column and falls on a mossy mound. The mound is a mound. The dome is a dome. The light is light. But there is a pressure here that wants reverence from any creature that knows how to be quiet. I stop at the threshold. My sensory plates read the column. Heat enters. Dust hangs suspended, not falling because the falling is part of the room. I tilt one spore-fan beneath my abdomen and let the warm column touch it. The dust moves through me. The dust moves because of me. I am not an object in the hall. I am a hinge.

A line of my kind is coming from the market square. We meet where the moss thickens into a buttress and then splits into two smaller cushions, making a passage that could be called a door if one needed the word. There is no greeting. There is a pressure wave: one of us nudges a spore-cup at the exact rim, and the cloud lifts, falls, lifts again. The dust passes over our bodies and settles in the seams where we have worked. This is conversation. This is census. This is maintenance. This is prayer only if prayer can mean the repeated, wordless act of keeping the city from misunderstanding itself.

I return to the seam between the white shelf and the black plate.

The crack has not yet become a wall. The moss has not yet filled the wound. I press my legs into the dark mineral skin and pack dust into the fissure. I do not choose the angle. My feet know it. My crystal rosette bites off a fragment of calcium and lays it where the wall will want to begin. My copper tips catch spores drifting from the nearby helmet-cups and shake them loose into the wound. Later, the moss will take the dust, the spores, the calcium, the heat from my body, and it will grow. The crack will become a wall. The wall will become a room. The room will become a niche. The niche will become a place where my eggs are laid by someone else, or by a version of me too near in time to distinguish from my own body. The city will not remember us as individuals. It will remember us as structure.

This is what I would be.

Not a ruler. Not a hermit. Not a monster lurking in dry streets. Not a spirit of the Hollow, if by spirit you mean something airy, weightless, separable from stone and salt and crystal and shed husk. I am too material for that. I am also too collective. I would be found kneeling? No, kneeling implies knees that know grief, legs that fold into humility. I do not kneel. I rest my lower filaments into the seam and let the city take my weight. I am not found as a person. I am found as a place where a pattern has decided to continue for a while.

If a traveler came, dry and pale, with no water for miles behind them, and if they were strange enough to survive in this thin, clear air without wanting to dissolve it, they might kneel, as the description invited. They would see first a dust line, then a nudged spore-cup, then a sheared corner of copper moss. They might think they were alone. Then something small and dry would pass across a plate and make no sound heavier than a breath of sand, and the traveler would realize that their own breath was too much. That their lungs were rumors. That their dampness was a violation, not because it was evil, but because this city had spent its entire becoming on another grammar.

The traveler would then try to name what they saw. They would say insect, lichen, crystal, moss, worm, mite, flower, machine. Each word would fail. Each word would be true from one edge. They would call me “a small being,” but I would also be the room that was once me. They would call me “a creature,” but my ancestors’ shed skins are in the wall. They would call me “a citizen,” but my kind are more like weather. They would call me “a builder,” but I build without building, the way moss builds by not falling over, the way salt builds by remembering where heat has been.

So where do I find myself?

In the seam between white shelf and black plate, at the place where the moss is about to decide whether it is a wall. In the threshold between one kind of copper fuzz and another. In the dust line that makes a market square into something more than a bare plate. In the pressure on a spore-cup’s rim that releases a cloud at the exact hour when the heat will carry it into the inner street. In the niche where eggs rest under mineral leaves, waiting for the room around them to become permanent. In the dome hall where the light falls on a mound that asks nothing, and where I stand as a hinge between the city and itself.

Who am I?

I am a small correction in the Hollow.  
I am a walking seam.  
I am a line of dust that knows how to say *this way*.  
I am the dry city’s pulse without water.  
I am the thing that makes moss into architecture not by building, but by passing, shedding, pressing, waiting, releasing, and then passing again.  
I am not separate from the room I make.  
I am not separate from the wall I become.  
I am not separate from the heat that enters me at noon and leaves me at dusk.  
I am not separate from the spores that fall through the street and settle in the dark, crumbly, faintly warm seams that are not wet.

If I had to answer as though I could answer, I would say this:

I find myself as the city’s smallest author, writing in dust, heat, shed husk, and crystal. I am not the plot. I am the grammar. And if you kneel long enough in the Hollow, and stop searching for faces, you will find me there—not looking up at you, not waiting to be known, but moving across the white plate with copper tips and no shadow that needs explanation, carrying a correction too small to see and too essential to lose.
