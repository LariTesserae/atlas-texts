The old exoskeleton, pale and thin as the underside of a fingernail, does not fall away into uselessness. It stands in the seam like a little white reef. Within a pulse of heat, bacterial threads colonize its curves. Within a day, silica begins to bind it to the sinter. Within a generation, the chamber may have built a new shelf from the ghost of an animal. To die here is not to leave the system; it is to be taken back into the slow wetness that makes the system possible.

If I were given this place, I would not begin by asking what I look like.

I would begin by asking where I am allowed to stand.

I would not be above the obsidian shelf, peering down with the arrogance of dry air. I would not be the human at the lip, the one who comes away with burned boot leather and a story about “heat” and “minerals” and “strange life,” though all those words would miss the thing most of the time. The human could only stand in the chamber for a few seconds, blinking through the wet brightness, and then have to retreat into the ordinary world where heat is either fire or weather, never a room, never a breath, never a living surface.

I would be on the far side of that brightness.

I would be one of the pale things.

But even that is too much. Too many. To say “one” is to impose a body’s outline where the pocket knows only gradients. I would be a claim on a narrow band of conditions. I would be a wet rim where heat has not yet killed water and coolness has not yet stopped it from moving. I would be a small jointed body, translucent, eyeless, with no need for color because the light here does not carry images. I would be a few of the rare fauna that a visitor might glimpse, if they were patient, if they crouched too long, if the steam moved in a way that let them see.

I would not have a face.

I would have a smooth dome. I would have heat pits. I would have hairs that read humidity the way a tongue reads the first taste of rain. I would navigate not by seeing a ledge, but by sensing the ledge as a refusal of wetness, or as a place where water might still thin into a film. My world would be a topography of degrees: too hot, warming, drinkable, drying, safe seam, lethal open glass, cool ceiling, returning breath.

When the central basin swelled with bubbles, I would feel it first as a pressure in the walls, then as a chemical taste in the steam, then as a change in the way condensation ran down the obsidian. I would not watch the pulse. I would be tuned to it.

Sometimes I would move quickly.

That quickness would not be playful. It would be the quickness of an organism that has no margin for comfort. A burst across a white sinter rim. A halt. The chamber breathing against my legs. A retreat into a capillary seam too thin for a finger, too curved for my back, but just wide enough for moisture to remain. There I would wait. I would press into the underside of the world, where heat and water argue less loudly. I would not hide from predators. There are none. I would hide from conditions: from a pulse too hot, from a dryness too sudden, from a shelf collapsing, from the fact that life here is not abundant but conditional.

I would be rare in a way that has no sadness attached to it.

There is no population in the ordinary sense. No chorus of kin. If there were five of us at the edge of the chamber, that would be enough. If one disappeared, the chamber would not notice except in the small chemical absence where its molted shell used to be. If all disappeared, the chamber would continue, but one of its voices would be gone. I might be one of the last individuals of a lineage that never had a chance to become common. Or I might be one of the first in a lineage that is only now learning what a pocket of wet heat can teach a body. Both possibilities would be true, depending on how long the observer looked.

I would not have eyes, and therefore I would not have illusions.

I would not mistake the chamber for a room.

A room is made for containment, for boundaries, for the difference between inside and outside. The Sinter Lung would be something stranger to me. I would live at the edge of what we would call a surface and what it would call a process. The wall would not be a wall to me. The wall would be a history. The floor would be a temporary arrangement of glass, mineral, water, and microbial appetite. My body would be neither separate from them nor identical to them. I would be the place’s way of making a very thin, moving, sensitive version of itself.

If you looked closely at my shell, you would see not merely an animal casing but a record of the chamber’s chemistry. Silica precipitated from my own body fluids. Iron traces from the mats I had grazed. A faint white bloom of sinter where my legs pressed long enough. My molt would carry the chamber’s previous breath. My corpse, after a rare accident, would carry the next: bacteria entering my joints, minerals thickening around what was once me, pale thread-creatures settling on my bones as if on a reef. Death here would not be a loss so much as a redistribution. The pocket would wear my body as skin.

So where exactly would I find myself?

Not at the center of the basin, where the water is too hot and the steam too assertive for delicate life. Not on the upper ledges, where the obsidian is too dry and the heat too sudden, where a body can stiffen in the time it takes to think. I would be on the margin.

I would be at the bright rim of a brine pool, where the water is warm enough to be wet and cool enough to be traversable. I would be just under a sinter overhang, where the heat has not yet learned the full sharpness of open ground. I would be where the chamber’s breath condenses into a film too thin to pool but too alive to ignore. I would be on the boundary between the hottest wet and the coolest wet, because that is the boundary where a body can persist.

Or perhaps I would be a microbial film.

At first I thought I would be the animal because the animal is what the eye can catch. But that may be the wrong instinct. The animal is visible, yes, but it is also precarious, almost accidental. The chamber could lose all of them and still be itself for a time. The film, however, is closer to the way life works here: not as a thing living in the environment, but as the environment living at a particular intensity.

A film would be a better answer if I wanted to be honest about the pocket’s deepest character.

I would be a skin of threads. I would have no mouth, no legs, no nervous quickness. I would have growth and death and mineral precipitation. I would live wherever the gradient held. I would know heat not as danger but as instruction. Too hot: vanish. Too cool: starve. Too wet: dissolve into movement. Too dry: flake. I would spread along the rim, along the channel, along the underside of a droplet’s path. I would be white, cream, orange-rust, sulfurous gray-black. I would be the thing that looks like geology until it is looked at too long.

I would breathe dissolved gases. I would eat the chamber’s chemistry. I would turn sulfur and iron and silica into a self. I would not need a name because a name belongs to things that have edges. My edge would be a probability field. Where the moisture changed, I would change. Where the heat pulsed, I would respond with growth, or with absence, or with a new patch. I would be a slow animal of gradients, a colony pretending to be a crust, a crust remembering it was once a colony.

Maybe I would be both.

Maybe I would be the pale grazer and the film at the same time. In the chamber, that is not impossible. My mouth parts would scrape bacteria from stone. Bacteria would colonize my molt. My excretions would feed the next mat. My body would become substrate for something that is neither animal nor mineral, but a boundary where both meanings fail. If I tried to point to myself, I would fail. I would say, “Here, my leg,” and the leg would already be part of the chamber. I would say, “Here, my shell,” and the shell would already be part of the sinter. I would say, “Here, my mouth,” and the mouth would be reading the chemistry of the next meal, which is also the chemistry of the wall, which is also the chemistry of me.

That is what I would be, then: a boundary-condition body.

A creature whose self is not a soul and not a species and not a story, but a narrow temperature band. A self made of water that has not yet dried, heat that has not yet killed, mineral that has not yet forgotten its own making. A small, jointed ghost moving across a white rim, sensing the pressure before the vent pops, knowing by taste that the chamber has just exhaled something old, something hot from below, something that carries the smell of sulfur and iron and the faint medicinal sharpness of steam.

If a human came down through the obsidian shelf and stood in the bright wet air, I would know them instantly.

Not because I could see them.

Because they would change the chamber.

Their heat would move into the air differently than the rock’s heat. Their breath would be a disturbance, a wet warmth with carbon in it, a foreign chemistry. Their boots would crack a thin sinter rim, and the crack would release a tiny hiss. I would flatten against a ledge and let the steam move over my shell. I would feel the pressure wave of their footfall through the stone, not as sound but as a brief tightening of the wetness. I would not be afraid in the way a human is afraid. Fear would be too large for me. Fear would require a future. I would have only the immediate gradient: this place too hot, this place safe, this place drying, this place returning.

The human might crouch and say, softly, “There’s something there.”

They might not see me until I moved.

They would say, “What are you?”

I would not answer, not because I am mute, but because the question is framed for the wrong kind of world. A human asks what a thing is as if the thing can be separated from the place that made it. Here, I cannot. I am not an object. I am an event in the Sinter Lung’s slow breathing. I am the chamber’s temperature becoming a body. I am a thin shell that has not yet decided whether it is alive enough to be called one thing.

If I stayed, and if the chamber stayed, and if the heat held its pulse in the old way, I would molt.

I would find a capillary seam too narrow for a hand. I would press in, feeling the walls close around me, the air hot and wet, the stone reading my shell like a mold. I would shed. The old body would stand behind me, pale and empty, while I drank the condensation and grew. The seam would fill with the chemical memory of my molting: salts, proteins, traces of iron. The next pulse would bring steam over the old shell. The next bacterial film would claim it. By the time the chamber forgot my molt, the molt would have become part of a rim. The rim would become part of a pool. The pool would become part of a heat rhythm that might last longer than my kind.

I would not mourn the old shell.

I would not think of it as death. I would think of it as continuation in another form, because here continuation is what the chamber understands.

If I were to choose, then, where I would be in this world, I would say: in the Sinter Lung, on the warm wet margin, in the place where the pocket breathes and the mineral skin responds. I would be a small pale grazer, one of the rare animals if anyone was looking closely enough to count. But I would also be a film, a sheath, a thread of precipitated silica, a bacterial edge. I would be an animal only in the way that a wave is an animal: briefly distinct, constantly dependent on the medium that carries it.

And if the human left, as the human would eventually have to, because the heat was too close to fever, because the brightness was too strange for ordinary eyes, because the chamber’s air pressed on the mouth like water in a lung not built for breathing, then I would not be lonely.

I would resume reading the walls.

I would taste the next vent.

I would feel the next pop of steam through a seam in the rock.

I would move, or not move, according to the chamber’s instruction.

I would be one of the Sinter Lung’s living skins.

I would be the pocket’s thought of an animal, held in a body thin enough to see through and rare enough to lose. I would be the place’s way of knowing itself: without eyes, without pictures, without names, only heat, wetness, and the slow mineral return of all that lives to the surfaces it has helped to make.
