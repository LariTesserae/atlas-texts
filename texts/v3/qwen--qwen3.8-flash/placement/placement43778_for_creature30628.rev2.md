If I could not leave after standing at that rim—if the basin’s quiet industry were allowed to decide for me, and if I had to become one of the beings already resident there—I would not choose the fox. The fox is too much a figure, too much a route, too much a story with a silhouette. It is the animal the basin makes visible, the one that walks along the rusted edge and seems to know the country by its supports, but that very knowability would make it a witness, not the place itself.

I would not choose the frog, either. The frog lives in hidden cavities and speaks with the earth, but its voice is too much a signature in a place whose sounds are absorbed. It would still be calling itself into the world too clearly.

I would not choose the lizard. The lizard knows the heat too well, basking on stones warmed from below like a hand pressed to fevered skin, but it would still be an individual with a body moving through the basin’s mists. I would rather be something less like a character and more like one of the basin’s organs.

So I would find myself in the middle of the plateau, but not at any center. There is no center. There are only concentrations of warmth, wetness, and work. I would be beside one of those table-sized black mirror-pools, the kind set between sphagnum cushions and rusted stone, where the water is so shallow and still that it seems less like a pool than like a held breath. I would be on the warm side of it, where steam condenses along a pale silica rim and where the mineral water has left the rock stained the color of dried blood and sulfur. Above, a fern might uncoil by fractions. Nearby, a moss mat would sag slightly with absorbed water. Somewhere under the peat, fly larvae would turn in the dark, and a shrew’s pulse would move through a tunnel no wider than a leaf stem.

I would be there in the early morning, after the night’s condensation, when the basin looks like a laboratory left in the dark.

And I would be the skin over the pool.

Not a surface. Not merely a stain. A film: bacterial, algal, mineral, alive in a way that is not obviously alive to a human eye. A thin, metallic-looking membrane stretched over black water, trembling when touched, rippling like foil when a droplet falls through from a frond above, then slowly sealing itself again. A living mouth that exhales vapor and swallows heat. A border between the water below and the close, wet air above. A factory without a roof.

I would be many cells, not one self. A colony. A quiet crowd. Iron oxidizers working at the water’s margin. Sulfur-loving forms near the warm seam. Cyanobacteria darkening the pool’s skin into olive and copper. Silica and mineral salts woven into its outer edge like lace. If you looked through me, you would see suspended bodies no larger than a grain of dust, rotating in capillary water, grazing, dying, becoming food, becoming chemistry, becoming again the slow broth of the fen.

I would not have eyes, but I would know light. I would not have ears, but I would know pressure: the weight of mist settling on my surface, the sudden puncture of a raindrop, the disturbance of a snail’s shell sliding over me, the vibration of a frog’s foot somewhere beneath. I would know heat as the primary language of existence. Warmth rising from below would thicken me, make me breathe more visibly, release faint sulfur or clean mineral vapor depending on which seam in the stone was speaking that day. Cooler air would tighten my surface, condense a bead of moisture along my edge, send a thin shadow over the water beneath.

I would be a pool-keeper.

That is the closest name.

Not a god of the pool. Not a spirit. Not a resident with a nest, a burrow, a claim, or a route. I would be the condition that lets the pool be more than a hollow in peat. If I dried, the water would cease to be held at that boundary. If I thickened, the eggs of mosquitoes or midges would attach to me as to a raft of living architecture. If a fly came to lay its tiny clutch on my surface, I would accept the puncture and heal it, my cells rearranging, my chemistry shifting. If a bird landed nearby, I would mirror the gray light in its eye without knowing it. If the fox-like animal stepped near the rim, testing the moss with its broad paws, I would reflect its shape for a second, then let the reflection break as its footfall sent a faint wave through the water below me.

I would be strange enough to fit this country.

Humans might mistake me for a film of oil or a rust stain, because the basin is full of such disguises. The rust here is living work. The orange veins in the water are not paint; they are microbial oxidation, patient and ceaseless. The yellow grease around a warm vent is not simply sulfur; it is a community of bodies metabolizing a hot mineral world. The white lace along a rock edge is not decoration; it is deposition, the condensation of steam made into structure by invisible industry. And I am the visible proof of that proof. I am where the geothermal current ends its underground journey and begins the upper world: light on one side, heat below, water between, gas rising, minerals crystallizing, carbon fixing, decay feeding renewal.

If I were a single creature, I would be too obvious. The basin does not reward obviousness. Its residents hide in gradients: a warm cavity for a frog, a heated stone for a lizard, a sphagnum runway for a mouse, a black pool for larvae, a vertical moss curtain dripping from a clifflet for insects to drink from. Their survival is not loud. Their survival is tuned. They move according to moisture, mineral taste, temperature, and the soft resistance of living ground.

I would be tuned to the surface.

The surface is a difficult place to be. It is always nearly breaking. Rain threatens to overwhelm it. Heat threatens to dry it. A snail grazes across it. A bird dips its beak and disturbs it. A fallen needle presses through it. A midge, drunk on the vapor, stands on it for a moment, its legs making tiny dimples in the tension. If the wind crosses the basin, it does not make a roar, because the vegetation absorbs most of it, but near my edge it still draws me into a slight waviness, a breathing of the skin. Then, after a minute, I would be still again, as if nothing had happened. That is the basin’s way: motion without drama, industry without announcement.

I would also be part of a food chain.

A springtail might jump across the rim of the moss mat and fall into me, or nearly into me, and a mite might fish it out with claws adapted to wetness smaller than human attention. A tiny snail, its shell as thin as tea stain, might slide over my surface and eat at the edge, leaving faint grooves behind. A tadpole below would graze the softer lower strata of me, its mouth scraping bacteria and algae from dark water. An adult frog hidden beneath the peat would feel the temperature of my pool and decide whether it is safe, or too warm, or too stagnant, or just right. A fly from the midge curtains above might land on me and lay eggs, and its larvae would fall into the water under me and feed on my dead. Death and food would not be separate concepts here. They would be one continuous circulation.

I would be a boundary in which the whole basin becomes visible.

From above, I would look like a dark skin shimmering slightly, sometimes metallic, sometimes greenish-black, sometimes touched with orange at the edge where iron blooms. From below, I would be a ceiling of life. From within, I would be chemistry. From the air, I would be vapor’s anchor. If you lifted a thin blade of moss near my rim, you might see the water under me black with suspended minerals, bright with bubbles rising in a slow blinding patience from anaerobic decay. If the sun were high enough, my surface would become a lens, throwing a trembling pattern onto the peat below. If cloud came, I would darken and cool, and the pool would become almost silent.

I would not have thoughts, or at least not thoughts as humans know them. I would have tendencies. I would spread where water thins. I would contract where steam is strongest. I would thicken where light falls. I would change where iron or sulfur is available. I would respond to disturbance as healing, as closure, as renewed tension. I would be a body without a mouth, yet the place where the basin’s mouth is.

There is something in this basin that feels like a civilization without builders. Moss is architecture. Peat is archive. Bacteria are workers. Frogs are hidden citizens. Lizards are heat-readers. The fox is a quiet administrator of trails no one made. The whole place has the logic of an ancient city, but its buildings are grown and its industries are biological. No tools, no roads, no fences, no names. Only habits, gradients, membranes, and repetitions.

If I became one of its beings, I would want to be a being that expresses that logic.

Not a mammal with a warm interior, though mammals are marvels here, especially the fox with its sensitive paws and its long balancing tail, the mouse in its sphagnum-lined burrow, the shrew with its pink nose and quick twitching search through the dark wetness. Not a bird, though the sooty wader moving through root mats is perfect in its restraint, testing each step, pausing, flowing onward. Not even the lichen, though lichen on basalt is patient as a mineral prayer.

I would want to be the pool’s thin, alive surface, because that is where the place reveals itself most clearly. It is where water stops being only water. It is where heat and chemistry and light form a skin. It is where the basin shows that it is not a collection of places but a single metabolism interrupted into many pockets.

So, where would I find myself?

At the edge of the basin’s middle, on a warm wet terrace, beside a black pool no larger than a table, set between a sphagnum hummock and a rusted channel, under a fern frond slowly uncoiling, near a stone from which the geothermal breath condenses into droplets. The air would be thick and close, smelling of wet iron, moss, and faint sulfur. The sky overhead would be bright but not dry. Mist would gather at the plateau’s rim and then leave again. Insects would move in curtains so fine they look like smoke. Somewhere hidden, a frog would call from inside the earth. Somewhere unseen, the fox would test the moss.

And what would I be?

I would be the living film over the pool. A colony of microbes and minerals and vapor, a communal skin, a breathing membrane. I would be the pool’s quiet mouth. I would be the place where the mountain’s heat reaches the air and is translated into life. I would be an organism without a face, a resident without a home, a worker without a tool, a citizen of a country that has never been ruled.

If you touched me, I would ripple like foil.

Then I would seal again.

And the basin would continue.
