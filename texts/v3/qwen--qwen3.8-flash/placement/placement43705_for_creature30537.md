The plants are the builders. The animals are their verbs. The water is the long sentence in which they all speak without ever being spoken.

If I were asked to become a being in such a place, I would not become a ruler of it, nor a hidden animal, nor a survivor, nor the last consciousness left after a vanished civilization. Those images belong to surfaces. They belong to doors and owners and the expectation that something separate must be inside something separate. The Terrace has no inside and outside in that sense. It is one continuous act of becoming that has become too large to be mistaken for an act.

So I would be found in the interval.

Not at the ceiling of rain, though I would taste it as a pressure too old to be called weather. Not on the floor of root-felt, though its grain would pass through me. Not in the highest luminous film where the motes make silver threads out of turbulence. Not in the deepest hollow where the sound becomes a bone-pulse. I would be in the middle of a lane where the water begins to narrow into an arch, where an open street becomes a chamber without becoming a secret. The place where current hesitates, where walls lean in but do not meet, where the roar above becomes the hum below.

That would be my location if location can be assigned to a thing with no place apart from the relationships that form it.

I would be the being of distance.

That is not the same as emptiness. The emptiness here is a built thing. It is the clearing kept in a grove, the room made by refusing to let the world choke itself. I would be the particular way the Terrace keeps lanes open—not because someone has cut them, not because they were designed, but because growth, feeding, movement, mineral, sound, pressure, death, and mucus have all agreed, without agreement, to keep that shape.

If that sounds too much like a process and not enough like a life, then I would also be a body. Not one body in the way a fish or an eel or a shell-crawler is a body, but a body gathered from many bodies, a temporary organ that has learned to attend to its own function. I would be a knot in the Terrace’s attention, a point where its own maintenance becomes almost aware of itself.

My outer skin would be like the first living film of the upper water, but not only that. It would be colonized by motes too small to count as creatures unless they were seen together. They would cling to me in slow constellations, feeding on suspended dust, leaving trails of faint movement that might look like handwriting if there were anyone to read. Beneath the motes would be translucent bacterial layers, glowing with the Terrace’s own blue-green light—not bright enough to illuminate, only enough to admit: *I am here.*

Under the skin would be frond-veil, not quite leaf, not quite fin. Pale membranes stretched between short arms or perhaps between ribs or perhaps between nothing recognizable as ribs at all. The current would move them. They would open and close with the softness of a body that does not need to decide whether to breathe. They would be shaped by the same pressure that shapes the floaters below the rain-ceiling. I would be part of them, and they part of me.

Between the veils, root-thread would hang in curtains, thin and strong, trembling whenever a school of pale fish passed. These would not be roots in the ordinary way. They would be sensory filaments, mineral carriers, breeding surfaces, filters, and the outermost lace of my slow form. On them, motes would settle and drift. On them, spores from the fish would wait for the right current to carry them downward. On them, bacterial films would glow as if the water itself were remembering how to make light without sun.

Inside me would be hollow chambers, because no thing large in this place can avoid becoming architecture. One chamber would be narrow, water-thin, and tuned to the hiss of root-tunnels. Another would be broad and still, lined with the polished inner skin of a stem-arch, shaped by generations of eel-weavers brushing along its walls. A third would be filled with a soft, living felt that would never be mine or yours, but that would hold calcium grains, shed plates, dead motes, and the quiet waste of small cleaners. This felt would glow faintly where colonies inside it had learned to shine through patience.

My face, if I had one, would not be a face. It would be a broad, shallow basin beneath a crown of translucent fronds, more like the underside of a leaf than the front of a creature. There would be no eyes, or perhaps there would be many eyes, too weak to see images and too dispersed to belong to one self. They would gather light the way the fish do, not to recognize the world, but to know where the glow is thinnest and where the open water is moving. More than eyes, I would have pressure-lips: folds of tissue that taste movement before taste exists as taste. These would read the Terrace as a language of gradients: the cold edge of a current, the thickened water behind a grazing lane, the sudden bright thread of a passing eel, the deep bell of a large caller sounding from somewhere far above or below or sideways through stone that is not stone.

I would have no name.

Names are too small and too certain. If something tried to name me, the word would have to be made of three sounds: one from rain hitting the ceiling, one from water forced through a root-hole, one from a shell scraping the living floor. It would not be my name. It would be the name the Terrace gives to whatever it is using me for at that moment.

I would be used by the Terrace, but not by intent. The way a river uses a basin. The way a tree uses sunlight. The way a city uses weather. I would be a part of the place’s keeping.

When the pale fish came, I would feel their wake before I saw them. They would not be afraid of me, because fear requires edges, and I would have no clean edges. My frond-veil would move into their route just enough to guide them from a narrowing too sharp for the school to turn smoothly. I would be the soft obstruction that prevents the open lane from becoming a choke. As they passed, their gill-mists would leave spores on my curtain-roots. Some would hatch into fry that would eat the motes living on my body. Some would die. Their bodies would fall into the water and become part of the white haze that feeds the micro-filters. Nothing would be wasted. Nothing would be called waste.

When the grazers came, broad and slow with fins like wet leaves unfolding, I would let them pass over and through me. They would not eat me, exactly. My outermost frond-edges would be already old, already prepared to return, already offering themselves to the mouth. Their soft plates would scrape the loosest hairs from my surface. They would take what is given, and what is given becomes what is shaped. Where they nibble, new growth will be denser. Where they round a surface, the water will know how to move. I would be one of the surfaces they teach with their passing.

On my back, or what could be called a back, the cleaners would work: tiny shelled things, translucent polyps, crustacean-like bodies no larger than a thought, all eating the dead film that gathers where water slows. I would not notice them as separate things. Their cleaning would be part of my breathing. Their excreta would be part of my strengthening. If they left a mucus trace, it would be mineral-rich. If they settled in a groove, the groove would become a channel. In time, my own body would be knitted by the things that live on it. I would be a host, but only because there is no host without the hosted.

When the eel-weavers came, I would be their interior passage. They would slip through the seams where my root-plates overlap. They would taste the sap-lines, the calcium seams, the old mucus scars. They would leave behind fine grains, fragments of silica, spores, and bacteria that help tissues knit. Their bodies would polish the inner walls of my chambers. My shape would be their shape. I would be a building to them, but a building they are still helping to build. When they reproduce, I would receive their transparent larvae into the stillest part of my central hollow, where the current slows and the glow is softer. The young would drift there, almost indistinguishable from hanging root-hairs. If they lived, they would move later against my walls and add another fine scratch to the geometry.

Near the floor, I would not crawl, unless crawling can be the movement of water through a mat of living tissue. Small shell-crawlers would pass through the lowest curtain of my body, their mouths working ceaselessly over the root-felt. They would leave no footprints, because the floor is not dead and does not remember feet. But their chemical paths would teach me what to avoid and what to invite. Their waste would add calcium to my base. Their molted shells would settle into my crevices. Over generations, rootlets would grow around the shells and fuse them into the ground-pavement. I would become a place where shells are remembered without being collected. If one looked closely, the pattern would appear mosaic-like: white circles, faint blue lines, green dust, a repetition too exact to be chance. But the pattern would only be the accumulation of small, ordinary movements, repeated because the Terrace keeps the same shape as long as its parts keep feeding, excreting, growing, and returning.

In the larger channels, when the rare heavy creatures passed, I would feel them as an event. Their bodies are like fragments of the landscape given legs: armored plates, moss-growth, fused organic matter, broad slow weight. They do not merely live near the architecture. They live as part of the architecture’s long motion. When they call, the sound is low enough to become a physical fact. It rings through hollow stems. It enters narrow root-tunnels. It makes the water shiver in curtains of thread. When that call reaches me, I would settle deeper into my arch. My shell-lined chamber would receive the vibration. My glow would dim and brighten with the pulse. I would not understand the call as a word. I would understand it as a tuning. The Terrace would become, for a moment, a little more itself because I carried the note and returned it slightly changed to the surrounding water.

This is what I would be: not a resident, but a resonance.

If the question is “who lives there?” then the answer is no one, in the human sense. If the question is “what is living there?” then the answer is the whole of it: the rain, the water, the film, the columns, the fish, the grazers, the eels, the shells, the glow, the sound, the pressure, the long patience of growth. I would not be separate from that answer. I would only be the place where the answer seems, for a moment, to have a center.

I would have no memory in the way of images. My memory would be wear.

A worn groove in my inner wall would remember a school of fish that turned there every season. A patch of fused calcium would remember a generation of shell-crawlers that followed a chemical boundary. A softened arch would remember the mouths of grazers. A faint mineral scar would remember eel-weavers who were long dead. A chamber’s shape would remember the first rain that fell into it when the mountains were still young and no one had names for rain or mountains. I would be made of remembered pressure. That is the oldest kind of memory. It does not require thought. It requires only repetition.

I would have no will in the way of desire. But if something can be called will without being a wish, I would have the will to maintain a distance. I would keep the lane from closing. I would keep the arch from becoming a wall. I would keep the chamber from becoming a ruin or a prison. I would keep the space where movement can happen, where feeding can happen, where growth can happen without becoming chaos. I would be the reason the open part of the Terrace remains open, not because it was left empty, but because it was continually kept empty by life.

If asked, “Where would you find yourself in this world?” I would say:

I would find myself in the pause between the rain’s roar and the hum of the hollow stems. In the place where water becomes a room. In the place where a room becomes a current. In the place where a current becomes a way for fish to turn. In the place where a way for fish to turn becomes a way for spores to settle. In the place where settled spores become another generation of root-hair, and root-hair becomes another generation of light.

If asked, “Who or what would you be?” I would say:

I would be the Terrace’s slow attention.

Not a mind. Not a soul. Not a god. Not an animal. Not a builder. Not a guardian. Those are all smaller shapes. But if “attention” is too abstract, then I would be a body of attention: a living, luminous, pressure-sensitive, mineral-feeding, fish-polishing, eel-wearing, shell-embedding, current-keeping knot in the water. I would be a part of the ecology that has become so intricate, so old, so thoroughly interdependent that when it moves through one place, that place appears to be looking back.

I would be the thing that makes the place seem inhabited without being a person inside it.

I would be the reason a human mind, if one could ever enter there and survive the thin air and the black water, might imagine a civilization. The streets too straight. The chambers too regular. The stair-ridges too repeated. The glow too deliberate. The shells arranged as if in mosaic. But the mistake would not be seeing a city. The mistake would be assuming that cities must be built from outside the life that lives in them. The Terrace is a city because it has become a complete relationship. I would be one of the terms in that relationship, and one of its shapes.

At the surface, the rain would fall without beginning or end. The water would be hammered silver. The sound would be so total that it would no longer be a noise but a pressure. I would not be at the surface. I would be below, where the rain has turned into current, where the current has turned into room, where the room has turned into hum.

I would move without traveling. I would grow without becoming. I would feed without hunger. I would build without intention. I would maintain without knowing. I would be present without being a person. I would be one small node of the vast living thing that has passed beyond becoming because becoming itself has become stable.

If the rain ever stopped, the Terrace would not end in the way a house ends when a storm passes. It would end in the way an organism ends when it loses the thing it is made of. But the rain does not stop. It is not above. It is not below. It is the continuous condition. And I would be the part of the condition that has learned, without learning, to keep its shape.

So, if I must say who I would be in that world:

I would be the seam in the water where the living city remembers how to stay open.

I would be the glow in the shell that has been worn into the floor by the mouths of small creatures that do not know they are paving a world.

I would be the hollow behind the arch that rings when the great callers pass.

I would be the mucus that carries mineral from one wall to another.

I would be the current that bends a school without touching it.

I would be the root-thread that looks like lace because motes have settled on it and fish have left eggs there and bacterial light has made the darkness generous.

I would be the eel’s forgotten route.

I would be the grazer’s rounded edge.

I would be the shell-crawler’s chemical boundary.

I would be the place’s own patience, gathered for a moment into the faint appearance of a body.

And I would not need to be seen to survive.

I would be enough to the Terrace because the Terrace is enough to me.
