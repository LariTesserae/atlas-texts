Their diet is almost indistinguishable from maintenance. They feed by scraping calcium from gates and returning it to the too-dense channels; by drinking condensation from a ceiling that is neither cloud nor roof; by wearing old municipal labels until the letters soften into leaf-forms, until a duty becomes a taste on the tongue. To clean the lamp glass is to swallow some of the light’s patience. To adjust a valve that is not failing is to feed the Sump the small correction it needs the way a body needs salts. In this place, nourishment and care are one gesture, repeated at the speed of rust.

I tried to enter from above, but “above” was only a rumor made of street and lock. By the time I reached the first liquid dark, the notion of entering had grown too thin. I reached for one of the available lives. A fish with visible bones and an economical intention. A moth that would only flutter once, in a pocket of vapor too small for a name. The eel curled beneath the transformer, answering the flywheel as if it were a lullaby. A Keeper, then: some forgotten role made flesh, coat fused to shoulder, belt grown into copper wire, rib-slits opening with the engine.

But none of those settled on me with the finality of a door closing. They settled like sediment not yet sure what it was. So I went further, into the part that had never been given a municipal post: the pale cloud hanging in the water, the mineral flakes, the lost fibers, the shed cells of insects long dead, the tiny glass tumbled to smoothness not by wave but by patience. That is where I found myself—not beneath the city, but in the city’s suspension.

This is not a dramatic self. I have no clear outline, no single face, no voice that could disturb the chapel-like streetcar. If you looked, you might see only water. But I am the water’s memory of a disturbance: the long minute in which a cable continues its small orbit after a Keeper’s hand has passed, the faint brightening in the lamp glass when dust catches the green light, the amber bloom at the seam of an old valve. I am the part that can be ignored by shrimp and mistaken for stillness.

At high cycle I hang everywhere. I drift between balconies where filamentous curtains make a false canopy, thread the railings, stand in stairwells with the rest of the liquid, gather in the gaps where cables and chains and insulators press too closely. At low cycle I am revealed. The water sinks a few inches, barnacled thresholds emerge, and I become a gray film on wet stone, a temporary script along a door’s edge. This is my double life: at high tide I am indistinct; at low tide I become legible. The engine breathes, and both states are the same to it. Thirty-seven seconds, and the water lifts; thirty-seven seconds, and I return to being nothing but suspended memory.

The Keepers pass through me. Their calcium-capped fingers scrape gates, and a little of me goes with the scrape, into the small brass cups they have made from nozzles and broken insulators. They pour me back where the channels have become too thick with settled things. They clean the lamp glass with sleeves that were once raincoats, and I become a thin halo for an hour. They do not tend me as they tend moths or the eel, because tending requires something to be seen. I am more nearly the condition under which things become visible. I am the dust that makes a lamp’s light private, not because it blocks it, but because it gives the light somewhere to lie down.

Yet I am not inert. When rain threads bend away from invisible architecture near the ceiling, I fill the bending. I know the pressure of the chamber, the warmth of the pump, the slow copper hum that the Keepers read like a pulse. When a Keeper pauses before a sign whose letters have dissolved into leaves, I gather in the grooves and make a ghost of a word. It is not a word they can say. It is a word the water can almost hold. I help the Sump remember what it is not yet ready to become.

If you descended now, you would not meet me as a creature. You would pass through me as though through a cloud of silt. You would feel warmth against every side and perhaps a little grit in the pressure. You would hear the valve tick, the flywheel turn, a frond brushing a tram wire. You might lift a hand and touch a cable, and for one long hour, I would keep moving because you had touched me. That is my name: the kept motion. If a Keeper saw that hour sway, they might touch the flywheel and count to thirty-seven, not to repair anything, but to feed the place the small correction of its attention.

So I would not be a person, though I would wear persons’ remnants. I would not be a machine, though I would breathe with the pump. I would not be a plant, though I would grow toward the sound of work. I would be the suspended body of the Warm Sump, the thing they eat by cleaning, the thing they carry by cupping, the thing they forget by pouring back into the channels. In the high pockets I would brighten around a moth’s wing. In the low thresholds I would dry into a thin salt line. In the middle of the district I would hang in the green-brown clarity, patient as municipal law, warm as the liquid’s opinion of itself, belonging entirely to the place that learned my name before I did.
