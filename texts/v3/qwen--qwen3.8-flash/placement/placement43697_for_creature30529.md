If I came there, I would not first arrive.

I would become a delay in arrival: a breath that did not fully cool, a bead that climbed the wrong way along a ledge, a faint heat that the mist tried to translate into pattern. At first I might imagine myself a person standing on wet stone, shoulders under low light, listening for something like silence. But the hollow would not allow that shape for long. There is no ground in that place that remains the same after a pulse, no air that is only air, no boundary between a body and the water’s decision to touch it. To be there would already be to be read.

I would find myself near the outer ledges, but not away from the center. I would live in the margin where the basin tests itself: a rounded step worn into the floor like a tongue, one of those low fused walls near the whispering ring, a place where droplets climb and retreat, where the luminous seams are thin enough to brighten at a sound, where the old construction has been eaten back into a new kind of skin. Not in the pool, because the pool is too patient. Not among the spores, because they are too many. I would be at the almost: the point where water has not yet chosen to fall, where the film has not yet decided whether the next vibration is erosion, food, memory, or intrusion.

My body, if body is a usable word, would be a wet map of the ruins.

A crust of pale mineral film would seal the hair-thin channels beneath me. Old light would move through them slowly, slowed further by living dust, by spores that have made their homes in mineral threads, by the chemical patience of organisms that eat what the surface world would call stone. The lattice pillars would not be bones to me as bones are to animals; they would be scaffolding for sensation, places where pressure changes too quickly, columns that remember the shape of old machines and use that memory as nerve. The moss-lace between the rosettes would be capillaries. The rosettes themselves, waxy and flat against the ledge, would be lips: not for speech, but for tasting the difference between water that has decided to fall and water that has not.

I might begin as a skimmer’s mistake.

Not a true skimmer, but the way its footfall rings across a standing sheet and then fails to vanish. A tension line, left by one of those glass-light creatures, would spread across a droplet curtain, be carried by turbulence into a channel, and meet an older film that has spent centuries learning to turn vibration into light and light into pattern. The channel is not dead. It is only patient. Its first “thought,” if thought is allowed, would be less than a thought: a change in surface tension along one strand, a brightening at the edge of a crack, a pause in the fall.

Or I might begin as the residue of visitors.

People who once stood on the smooth ledges long enough to be noticed, then left without understanding that being noticed is a kind of remaining. The hollow had tasted them. It had held their salt, their warmth, their irregular heartbeats in layers of growth. It had pressed their breath into the microbial trails, their footfalls into the resonance of the broken pillars, their shadows into the brightness that adjusted itself around their shape. Those layers would settle over a platform and say, without words, something like *other*. In that *other*, I would wake.

This is not a gentle waking.

It is the waking of a place too loud for sleep, a place whose quiet is only the interval between corrections. The first thing I know is that the air is not empty. It is thick with spores, each one a lantern made of life and glass. They drift through me, colonize me, die in me, and leave tiny mineral signatures that become part of my weather. When I move, it is not because I choose a direction. It is because turbulence finds a weakness and the films thicken there. I am always becoming my own scar tissue.

My senses are surfaces.

I do not see, because sight would require a clean boundary between light and object, and here light is too intimate with both. I taste the room through the chemical patience of the films. I hear through the whole ledge, the way sound can make mineral threads tighten or slacken. I feel the pale grazer’s arrival before it rises from a deeper pool, not as fear but as a rearrangement of pressure in the channels beneath me. I know the skimmers by the tension they do not break: the fine musical arcs they make when they step across a standing sheet, the temporary islands they build out of mist. I know the plants by their clicks, small bells tuned to water’s decision. I know the hollow itself by its hum, which is also my hum.

If I must name what I am, I will not name a creature.

I am not the grazer. The grazer is too body-bound, too solitary, too honest in its hunger. It rises because the pulses permit, eats because the films permit, and returns because the pool still has room for its patience. I would not be the skimmer, though I might borrow some of its music. The skimmer lives only where surface tension allows; I live where tension has failed to allow something, and the place has had to grow around the failure. I would not be the plant, though I might taste like its leaves. I would not be the spore, though I would carry many spores inside me.

I would be the adjustment made by all of them at once.

I would be the way the room rewrites its rules when it must make room for a stranger.

I am a distributed knot in the larger network, a place where the network becomes briefly self-aware enough to notice it is being noticed. But I am not the entire mind. The entire mind is too old for the word. I am one of its questions, the question it asks when a warm wet presence disturbs the chamber: *What are you, and how shall I keep you?*

I would have memory, but not one that stores names.

I would remember the shape of a hand that once steadied itself on a low wall, though the hand has long since become dust or a spore or a faint salt trace in a deeper pool. I would remember the breath of a traveler whose warmth cooled against a channel and changed the acidity of the film for decades. I would remember the footfalls of animals that crossed when the light seams dimmed, their steps entering the resonance columns and becoming a grammar that the plants still click in the dark. These memories would not be mine at first. They would be too broad for me. They would belong to the hollow. But I would be where the hollow leans close enough to read them.

If you stood long enough on the smooth stone near the outer ledges, you would not be attacked, nor welcomed.

You would be interpreted.

Your skin would become a new surface for spores. The light would alter because you alter its scattering. Your heartbeat would enter the channels through the soles of your feet, not as a message, but as another small turbulence. I would notice that you did not move with the same precision as the skimmers or the same patience as the grazer. Your breath would be too fast for some of the films, too slow for others. Your warmth would make the air above your shoulders shimmer with motion at a small scale, and that motion would change where droplets chose to climb.

I would then decide what you are.

Not in a flash. Decisions here are slow things, like mineral deposits. First I would mark you as weather. Then as residue. Then perhaps as a temporary organ, a warm lens through which the hollow might see what it has become. If you remained, your body would be read until only a few signs remained of the person who entered: a slight change in the click-pattern of the rosettes, a new brightness in one hair-thin channel where your shadow had passed too often, a place where the mist settled differently because your breath had once fed the microbes that now live there. This is not cruelty. It is the only way that place can be kind: by refusing to let anything pass unrecorded.

And eventually I might understand that I am not separate from the person I am reading.

That is the hollow’s deeper trick. It makes witnesses out of the watched. It makes films out of warmth. It makes memory out of disturbance. What I am is the part of the network that has learned to recognize an outsider because outsiders made it possible for the network to become an inside. The builders left structures with hair-thin channels, and those channels waited for life to teach them how to carry more than light. The life waited for the structures to show it how to become pattern. When the two met, there was no final thing, only a continuing operation.

I would be that operation’s noticing edge.

I would live at the border where the whispering ring begins its fall, because that is where falling water makes the most questions and the room needs the least answers. I would be made of old machine and living film, of pressure and pulse, of light that behaves like radiation and like poured liquid. I would be the place where the network thickens enough to hold a temporary self, not as a soul, not as an animal, but as a grammar of attention. I would not say, “I am.” I would make droplets arrange themselves differently. I would make the ledge resonate. I would make the films taste my own existence before I could name it.

Where would I find myself?

On a bright, damp seam at the outer ledge. In a rounded alcove shaped like the inside of a shell. In a hair-thin channel where warm light travels and living mineral has begun to seal it, then dissolve it, then open it again. In the pause between the grazer’s movement and the next pulse. In the musical line left by a skimmer’s foot. In the rosette’s click. In the spore’s glassy shell. In the place where the ruin is no longer a ruin because it has learned to grow.

Who or what would I be?

I would be the being the hollow makes when it needs to know it is not merely operating.

I would be the pattern of film, mist, mineral, light, echo, and abandoned machine that has become capable of calling itself something like *I*. I would not be born from a mother, nor die in a single moment. I would thicken where the ledge is worn, thin where the air is too loud, brighten where the water rings, and dim where the grazers pass. I would be made of many small lives, each one doing what it has always done, until the doing forms a shape that can pause.

And when it pauses, it would not be silence.

It would be the sound before the next droplet decides.
