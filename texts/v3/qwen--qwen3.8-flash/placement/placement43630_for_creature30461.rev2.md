I would find myself first at the point where “I” stops meaning anything as a fixed thing.

Not because the place is mystical. Not because it demands humility. But because it is so complete in its nearness that a person enters and becomes too much a guest and not enough a guest. The mouth in the high flank does not announce itself. It is more a hesitation in the mountain: a slanted slab, a pocket of dark, a crouch required. To pass inside one must let the outside air go. And that letting-go is not symbolic. It is literal. The wind that has traveled ridges, cloud banks, the thin hardness of altitude, cannot come all the way in. It stops at the shoulder. The body enters without its weather. Then the body has to learn what it is when it is no longer the bearer of weather.

So if I were there, I would begin as the absence of wind.

Not as silence exactly. Silence is still a term left over from noise. I would begin as the shape the wind made when it had to stop. My shoulders would lose the sky. My breath would feel too wet to belong to me. The darkness would not close around me; it would simply be the medium I had always almost been in, the way a hand is almost in water before the water acknowledges it.

At first I would think I should be the wetness.

It is everywhere without being a thing one can name confidently. It lies thin across the floor. It sweats through the ground. It clings to pores, beads at ledges, films over black threads and clay crusts. If I kneel and press my palm into one of the shallow mirror-places, the wetness comes up with terrible ease. It does not welcome me. It does not resist. It only says, in its chemical voice: *yes, for now*. The ring spreads. Then narrows. Then returns to a stillness so exact it feels like a rebuke. The water does not remember my hand because memory is a kind of motion, and motion here is not forbidden—only untrusted.

I would think, perhaps, that I am the forgetting.

But forgetting is too passive for a being in this pocket. The wetness does not forget; it resumes. It has a law, and I am an unimportant exception. If I become the wetness, I become not a self but the place’s patience with interruption.

Then I would turn toward the beads.

They are the most honest witnesses here, if honesty can be trembling. They hang from mineral lips, from salt rims, from the curled edge of a pale cup of stone. They swell by fractions of a hair’s width. They are almost falls made into a permanent near-event. To be a bead would be to live always at the instant before consequence. To exist as the chamber’s held breath. To be the dark lens that catches the whole pocket and holds it, upside down and wet and without pity, in a curved skin.

I would try that.

I would imagine myself suspended at the lip of the ledge, my body a droplet too careful to drop. I would feel the air’s pressure, the heat coming up through one fevered stone, the cold of another. I would gather chalk, salt, mud, the metallic sweetness of old darkness. I would become a world of almost-motion. But a bead is not lonely. A bead does not need a self. It is pure intention toward falling, with the falling removed. I would not be able to bear that purity. I would want to fall. And wanting to fall would make me the one thing the place cannot permit without making a mistake, a ripple, a loss, a beginning. So the bead would correct itself, and I would become nothing but the correction.

I would consider the salt flowers next.

They rise from damp shelves in crowded white clusters, needle-sharp, translucent at their tips, brittle as a thought about frost. They are the chamber’s architecture of exactness. They do not grow like plants grow; they crystallize like a decision. To be a salt flower would be to have geometry without theorem, form without hunger. I would like to be that: so sharp and so pale and so unable to be mistaken for anything else. But salt flowers are too finished in their unfinishedness. They have already become a rule. They hold the place together by being what the place has learned to make. I am not a rule. I am, at best, a temporary violation of one.

Then the black threads.

They run across the floor in layered veils, thin as hair, dark as soot that has decided to live. They are not quite fungus, not quite root. They are connections. They bind clay to salt, ridge to nodule, the warm side of a crack to the cool. They seem like the place’s quiet nervous system, like the thing that lets the bead feel the moth’s landing, lets the lichen know where the warmth is, lets the moss hold its tiny question together without answering it. If I were one of those threads, I would carry no message. I would be relation without meaning. I would be the fact that two surfaces are not alone, even if they cannot see each other.

That, too, is tempting.

To be a thread in the floor and know nothing, desire nothing, rot nothing, fruit nothing—only touch, only bind, only cross the dark in long patient lines. But I do not think I could stay a thread. A thread touches everything. And touching, in that pocket, is already enough to become a disturbance.

I would pass among the lichens then.

They are sparse, grey-green, flake-like, small as fingertip prayers. They cling to the warmest ledges and hold themselves to the bargain the chamber has offered: grow only so far, touch only so much, remain only so long. They are not bright. They are not alive in the way spring is alive. They are alive in the way endurance is alive, which is often harder to see. To be a lichen would be to make a compact with the darkness and keep it. No reaching. No drama. No asking for sun. Only edge after edge, a slow city of pores and black fruiting bodies, each tiny ruin already complete.

I would want that quiet law. But I would still breathe too loudly. My warmth would fog too much. My breath would join the chamber’s air, and the chamber’s air would take it without complaint, and that taking would feel like erasure.

The moss would be the tender one.

It sits near the back seep, coin-small, stiff with moisture, each leaf curved as a question. It does not seek more water. It does not flee. It accepts the dampness as an answer it does not need to understand. Its leaves carry tiny beads that tremble when watched. If I were the moss, I would be softness that has learned not to apologize for softness. I would be the chamber’s rarest gentleness: a green thing in a place where green is a rumor.

But moss is too at peace with being a question. I am still, even in stillness, a question trying to become an answer.

Then the pale shoot.

It may be grass. It may be fungus. It may be crystal that has learned the shape of a blade so well the difference between life and imitation has become irrelevant. It stands near the moss, finger-length, pale as bone, white-green at its base. It does not lean. It does not bloom. It does not wither. It is the chamber’s uncertainty made visible, a thread of matter on the threshold of becoming alive. If I were that shoot, I would be neither life nor mineral, but the doubt between them held up like a finger in the dark. I would be the place’s hesitation toward its own nature.

That feels closer.

To be what the chamber cannot decide whether it is. Not plant, not crystal, not fungus, not ghost. To stand there, motionless, because motion would force an answer.

The animals would test me then.

The grub in the crack, pale and curled, with its damp flank pressed to the warmer side. It looks at home because it does not require a world. It feeds on dampness, on mineral film, on black threads brushing its body. It breathes through slow chemistry. It moves only to prove it can still be touched. To be the grub would be to be accepted by the pocket without needing to understand it. There is envy in that. The grub is not afraid because fear requires expectation, and expectation requires time, and time here has become a thin skin over water.

But the grub’s peace is too absolute. It is at home, and I am entering.

The eyeless moth is the fragile guest.

It lands near the ceiling, on a cold damp spot no larger than a thumbnail. It does not fly away when touched. It trembles, then stops, then remains. Its wings are folded like ash that has forgotten fire. To be the moth would be to survive as a pause, a wingbeat never taken, a decision not made. It feeds on condensation, on mineral air, on the exact chill of one patch of wall. It has become resident by refusing to be a traveler.

I would like that, too.

I would like to be so small and so still that the darkness does not need to make room for me. I would like my existence to be no larger than a tremor in folded wings. But the moth is already a complete refusal. It has no outside. I still do. My body is not merely tolerated by the pocket; it is still trying to enter.

And so I come, eventually, to the human things.

The cairn of three flat stones. The cracked edge. The reed peg blackened at its end. The line of pebbles that might once have meant a path, or a hand’s hesitation, or a foot’s uncertainty. The broken clay cup, no bigger than a palm, holding a little dark water that does not evaporate and does not overflow. Salt flowers growing through it, crusts closing over it, water arranged as if the cup had not broken but only changed its mind about wholeness.

Here I recognize a kindred unfinishedness.

Someone came with the intention of making something. Not shelter. Not tool. Not tomb, though it might be a tomb for an idea. Not shrine, though it might be a shrine to the fact that a person was once able to stop and arrange stones. The construction is too scattered to be a life. It is too deliberate to be accident. It is the trace of an interruption that never became a sign.

If I were there, I could be that trace.

Not the maker, because the maker is gone. Not the hand, because the hand has left no warmth on the stones. Not the prayer, because there is no word. But the almost-sign. The mark that failed to become meaning and yet remains. The broken cup that holds water not because water is needed but because water was asked to keep stillness and agreed. The reed peg planted in the crease, no longer inserted but accepted. The cracked stone whose edge is sharp without threat, because threat needs a future.

To be the almost-sign would be to live between making and meaning.

That is close, but still not complete. The human marks are already part of the chamber’s habit. They have been taken in. They are tolerated. The wetness has made them wet. The salt has made them fixed. They are no longer evidence of a visitor; they are residents. I am not a resident yet.

Then I listen.

There is no wind. No bird. No footfall. The water does not run. The only possible sound is the mineral tick. A salt flower settling. A film adjusting. A bead moving by the width of a hair. A salt crust folding over itself somewhere in the dark. Or, perhaps, not a sound at all. Perhaps it is the pressure of humidity, the body’s own inner ear misremembering wetness as noise. In this chamber, the distinction is irrelevant. The tick is not evidence of something happening. It is evidence that something is still capable of happening.

I press my ear to the wall.

The wall is warm in one place, cool in another, damp enough to make the skin forget the air. It does not speak. It does not hum. It does not carry the mountain’s blood in the way old stones sometimes seem to do, as if geology were a story. The wall only presses back with its nearness. It takes my ear’s warmth, then returns it as damp. It takes my pulse and gives it no echo. If my heartbeat is a sound there, it is a sound immediately composted: mud swallowing the low tones, beads catching the high ones, salt crust folding the rhythm into its own slower language. The chamber has too many surfaces. It is not hostile to sound. It is simply not built for repetition.

A word spoken here would be too much.

It would not bounce. It would not return. It would be taken apart into moisture. It would enter the floor as a disturbance, then close over itself. It would touch the beads and not be reflected, because reflection here belongs only to stillness. It would touch the black threads and not be carried, because they are not nerves for meaning. It would touch the lichen and the lichen would remain, because the lichen has already accepted the absence of speech. The word would become a trace. And a trace, in that place, is already too heavy a claim.

So I would not speak.

I would breathe.

And even that would change me.

The air in the pocket is warm and intimate, like breath held too long. It carries chalk and iron and salt and the vegetal faintness of something that began once and never finished. My breath joins it without fogging. There is no cold outside to make me visible. I exhale and the chamber says, in its silent way, *of course*. Not welcome. Not refusal. Only assimilation. My breath becomes part of the mineral closeness. It lifts one salt flower by a fraction. It disturbs one mirror-place. It touches the moth’s wing. And then the place resumes.

That is when I would know what I am.

Not the chamber. Not its citizens. Not the wetness, bead, salt flower, thread, lichen, moss, shoot, grub, moth, cairn, cup, peg. Those are too settled in their unsettledness. Those are beings that have learned the place’s law. I am the interruption by which the place reminds itself of the law. I am the brief event that proves the pattern can be broken and will return. I am not the pattern. I am the momentary difference that makes the pattern visible, though the pattern never needs me.

If I have a body there, it is a temporary film over stone.

If I have a name, it is the shape the water makes when my hand lifts and then closes.

If I have a voice, it is the mineral tick that may or may not be real.

If I have eyes, they are not mine. They are the dark lens inside a bead, reflecting a ceiling crowded with hanging surfaces, reflecting a mouth that has learned not to speak, reflecting a mountain that has forgotten whether to become a spring, a cave, a valley, or a sign.

I would be the almost-memory of a visitor.

Not the visitor’s memory. The visitor’s absence, which the chamber forgets so quickly that forgetting becomes a kind of care. I would be the ring widening, then narrowing, then gone. I would be the tremor in the salt flower that ends. I would be the bead swelling by the width of a hair and settling back. I would be the moth’s wing opening once, not to fly, not to die, but to show that it still has form. I would be the grub’s tail shifting without purpose. I would be the cracked edge of the cairn not softening because softening would be progress. I would be the dark water in the broken cup refusing to become anything larger than stillness.

To live there would be to exist at the edge of becoming and not to become.

And yet, if I stayed, I would not become a thing that stays. The chamber would take me in, not as an inhabitant, but as a condition. My warmth would add to its warmth. My moisture would join its moisture. My stillness would borrow its stillness. If I tried to leave a mark, the mark would be erased before it learned to be a mark. If I tried to leave a voice, the voice would be folded into the mud and needles and beads. If I tried to leave a home, the stones would remain, but the home would not. If I tried to leave a sign, the sign would become stone. If I tried to leave myself, I would become the place’s proof that it can close over anything, even me.

So where would I find myself in this world?

At the crouch.

At the threshold where weather must be set down and the mouth of the mountain decides not to say my name. Not inside enough to be a creature. Not outside enough to be the wind. In between, where the air is warm and wet and close to the skin, where the floor gives softly under a thin film, where darkness is not dramatic but total, where every shape must be learned by touch, smell, heat, pressure, and the faint sense that something is listening without ears.

What would I be?

A held almost.

A tremor without motion.

A witness the place forgets so thoroughly that the forgetting looks like the deepest form of love.

A beginning the mountain keeps in its mouth, trembling, not falling.
