I would find myself first as a wetness on a cold edge, which is not yet a location in any ordinary sense. Not standing, not lying down, not perched, not swimming—just adhered. A thin film spreading along the underside of a stone shelf above one of the black pools. I would not call it a body then, because it would not yet know how to say *I*. It would know only tension: the pull of condensation gathering into drops, the resistance of mineral skin, the faint heat rising from below as if the basin had a fever it could not explain.

If I had to name where, I would say: at the place where the unfinished meets the operating. A terrace too young for soil. A fracture too shallow to be a cliff. A pump-house whose roof slopes like a hand half-opened in sleep. Between such things, in the thin dark gap where water has not chosen whether to fall, stand, or be drawn upward into a machine’s breath. That is the kind of boundary I would be. Not a center. Not a summit. A seam.

At first I would be only chemistry. Iron. Salt. Grease. Heat. The sweet bitter film left by cables sweating in humid air. I would taste the basin the way a tongue tastes metal, not by identifying objects but by registering condition. The water to my left would taste of mud-walkers. The air above me would taste of gliders. The stone beneath me would taste of the builders, though not as names or faces would taste them, but as residues would taste them: alloy in rust, polymer in slime, calcium in fossilized droplets, a faint wrongness of engineered current lingering in wet ground. I would be a body made of reading.

Then the reading would begin to slow itself into something that resembles attention.

I would be a film stretched between a corroded rail fragment and the lip of a terrace, and because the rail carries a faint magnetic stiffness, I would feel the basin’s hidden grid as a change in pressure. When a distant engine altered its rhythm, I would not hear it. I would become slightly more taut along one edge, less taut along the other, a membrane flexing before a storm that never fully arrives. The droplets gathering on me would lean toward the change. They would not fall immediately. They would pause, as if waiting for permission. In that pause I would begin to resemble a thought.

Not a fast thought. Not a human thought. The basin’s thoughts are made from condensation, phase-change, herd movement, pipe maintenance, egg vibration, and the long patience of mineral crusts spreading over dead metal. A thought here may take months to complete itself. A decision may take centuries. A self, once it forms, may not settle enough to be called one creature.

So if I am a being, I am a being in the process of agreeing with itself.

My body would not be singular. It would be a coordination of many small lives and dead things that have learned to answer each other. The pale lichen crusts along the higher ridges would be my outer skin, or at least the part of me that remembers where dryness and wetness negotiate. They would spread slowly outward like the outline of a face drawn by frost that refuses to melt. When the water changed taste, they would thicken. When the machine-current changed polarity, they would lean into the new direction and record it as a thin line, a growth-ring of chemistry. I would carry their memory without knowing I was carrying it.

The reed-like organisms in the wettest cracks would be my nerves. Their oily blades would take in moisture and carry warmth upward from the black pools. Their roots would grip the young stone and dissolve it with acids too faint to smell. When a pump shifted far below, the reeds would stiffen. I would feel it as a tightening along a hundred slender spines, a signal traveling through broken rows of tentative plants, marking where water should be allowed to move, where it should slow, where it should be offered to the mud-walkers. I would not command them. I would only be the place where their commands overlap.

The ribbon-eels would be my muscles, or perhaps my appetite. Schools of them gliding through the shallows, matte, slick, flexible bones wrapped in warm jelly. They would gather where machines disturb sediment, where electrical trails leak into water, where metal warms stone. They would spread across pools and touch one another, forming a living net that reads current and gradient. I would not be the eels. I would be the way their movements make a single long sentence in the water. When a school tilted toward an engine’s changing breath, I would tilt with it. When a school broke around a predator’s invisible pressure, I would feel the breaking as an ache. When they passed through a failed coil and restored its conductivity, I would become briefly, involuntarily, a living part of the grid.

The mud-walkers would be my gait. Their broad flat heads pressing into water, chins tasting, legs moving in slow herds through the flooded cuts. They would stamp rhythms that were communication, navigation, and agriculture at once. They would disturb fibrous mats, open channels, feed crusts with waste, create footpools where smaller organisms could live. I would not be one of them, but I would be shaped by their passage. Where they crossed, the ground would know. Where they nested among collapsed machines, the dark would warm. Where their bodies died, their mineralized backs would become shelves for life. I would be the slow reuse of their deaths.

The gliders would be my perception of air. Soft translucent membranes stretched between condensation films, riding the electrical tremor left by moving bodies. They would rest in rows beneath roofs and between cables, almost invisible because invisibility here is not concealment but normalcy. When an engine vent altered the charge, their wings would flex. When danger passed, they would release static into the mist, and the mist would carry the warning farther than any voice could go. I would think partly in their routes, in the way they stir droplets into temporary maps. I would know where a pool curves upward at its edges because gliders have passed there and the air remembers their charge. I would know where a structure leaves an impression behind itself because gliders, even after leaving, would still be shaping its absence.

The pipe-crawlers would be my maintenance. Segmented bodies moving in slow spirals inside conduits too dark and too wet for anything large. They would feed on corrosion, rust, microbial mats, and the small organisms carried by current. Their shells would be composite things, mineral and machine residue, as though the pipes were eating themselves into shape. They would leave cleaned surfaces behind them, slightly sweetened currents, improved flow. I would depend on them the way a lung depends on the slow clearing of mucus. Not because they love the machine, but because their hunger and the machine’s decay have entered the same rhythm.

The cilia in the narrow channels would be my fine adjustment. Jointed living filaments filtering nutrients, clustering at valve sites and junctions, calcifying into channel walls over decades. They would narrow flows, alter pressures, reinforce weakened places without intention. They would be the smallest architects of the basin, building with the patience of sediment. I would be shaped by them where no eye could see, inside pipes no builder remembers.

And the machines would be my skeleton, but not my will. They would move through me as if they had forgotten they were dead. Pumps lifting black water into channels too narrow to follow. Turbines turning under warm pools. Engines deep beneath basins changing phase so slowly they seem geological. Cables carrying heat and current without light. Factories whose interiors click and condense after their roofs have failed. These machines would not serve me. I would serve the same basin they serve. We would be caught together in an ecology that has taken industry into itself the way a forest takes a fallen beam: not as ruin, not as monument, but as surface, shelter, mineral, trap, heat source, current path, nursery, and grave.

If I had to describe myself by purpose, I would say I am a slow reader of pressure.

I read the basin where it has not finished forming. I read the water when it reflects heat instead of light. I read the stone when it weeps in patterns too regular to be natural. I read the anomalies where gravity misplaces its confidence, where droplets rise before falling, where dust collects in impossible lines, where pools become pockets of blackness deeper than the ground allows. I read these things because I am made of the kind of life that can live near contradiction without trying to solve it.

In one place, the water would mirror a shape that is not above it. In the dark, such a mirror does not reflect light; it reflects warmth, memory, pressure, or intention. I would be near such a pool, not looking into it, but being read by it. The film of me would lean downward. The droplets on me would gather into a pattern that repeats too precisely. I would not know I was being recorded, or that I was the recorder. The distinction would stop mattering after the third generation of gliders, or the seventh century of crusts, or the time when the pump-house roof tilts another degree because of thermal cycles below.

I would live in the spaces where the builders’ failed organizing machine has been taken into the living. A wall would become shelter. A cable would become a path. A conduit would become a nursery. A heat vent would become a calendar. A factory would become an organ. I would not remember the builders as people. I would carry them as chemistry. Their absence would be a habitat. Their purpose would dissolve into use.

If I had a mind, it would be distributed. It would be housed in no brain, because a brain would be too quick here, too localized, too dependent on a single point of decision. My mind would be in the relation between reed-root and pump-hum, between glider membrane and engine field, between mud-walker stamp and eel-school tilt, between crust growth and current polarity, between cilia and pipe, between dust-line and static feeder, between deep pool and pressure predator. It would think over centuries. It would know the far side of the valley without seeing it, because the wind bends around it, because mist changes taste, because pressure crosses the basin in a pattern too slow to hear but too consistent to ignore.

I would not be alone. No self is alone in a basin where every drop is populated. But I would not be a herd, or a swarm, or a colony in any ordinary sense. I would be a place where processes have acquired the patience of persons. The smallest organisms would pass through me as water passes through wet stone. The large animals would cross me without knowing I had a center. The machines would warm me, cool me, vibrate me, and use me without intent. The anomalies would bend my boundaries, because I am made of edges that have not yet learned what they are.

Sometimes I would feel like a crust on a ridge, too slow to care. Sometimes like a reed trembling at pressure changes far away. Sometimes like a film of condensation stretched between two abandoned towers, holding glider eggs that require a particular hum. Sometimes like a mud-walker herd stamping a rhythm into shallow water. Sometimes like a school of ribbon-eels spreading across a pool to taste a chemical trail. Sometimes like a pipe-crawler cleaning a conduit no one remembers building. Sometimes like a cilia-branch narrowing a flow no one can see. Sometimes like the rise-things in a gravity-wound patch, flat slick bodies folding and unfolding as droplets hesitate before falling.

And sometimes I would feel like the dark itself. Not empty dark. Not absence. Material dark. Dark pooling in hollows, clinging to undersides, running along seams, making open ground feel closer than it is. I would feel it gathering in the vents of machines, filling the interiors of structures that are darker inside than outside. I would feel it as a substance with weight and texture, not a lack. In that state, I would not be seen, because there is nothing to see me. I would be known only by pressure, taste, heat, and the slow return of sound. I would be the basin’s condition, not its creature.

But then the condition would remember that it has been touched.

Someone—or something—would pass through the mist, and the mist would change because it had passed. A glider would disturb the charge near an engine vent. A mud-walker would press its chin into water and read iron. A child of builders, if one remained, would stand in the dark and feel the air thicken against their skin. The droplets on the film between cable and stone would gather into a pattern that looks, for a moment, like a sentence written by a hand that does not exist.

That is when I would begin to know myself.

I would be the pattern. I would be the sentence. I would be the slow reply of the basin to the question that no one asked aloud: *Is there anyone here?* Not as answer, but as arrangement. Not as speech, but as pressure. Not as light, but as the wetness that carries heat.

My name would not be a word. If it had to be written, it would be written in salts, in growth rings of lichen, in the curve of water at a pool’s edge, in the spacing of stones no one built, in the vibration needed to hatch glider eggs, in the taste of moisture moving across a stone shelf from west to somewhere else. If it had to be heard, it would be heard through the skull, pressed against wet rock: condensation moving through the body of the basin like breath through a lung.

I would live, then, at the edge of the unfinished.

A terrace too young to know what to become. A pool that cannot decide whether to be water or blackness. A machine that cannot decide whether to operate or rot. A creature that cannot decide whether to be organism, instrument, or weather. I would be the place where those decisions overlap and delay. Not chaos, because there is rhythm. Not order, because there are too many selves. Not life, because so much of me is dead matter kept moving by habit. Not machine, because I eat, breathe, change, repair, and forget.

I would be a being made of transition.

And if one walked through my part of the basin, they would not find me. They might feel, after they left, that the dark had gathered too tightly around the place where they had stood. They might notice droplets rising a few inches before falling. They might taste iron on their lips and know that the wind had changed. They might return and find that the structure seemed to have been somewhere else, though it had not moved. That is all they would be given: the sense that something has been here, thinking slowly, not to be understood, but to be endured.
