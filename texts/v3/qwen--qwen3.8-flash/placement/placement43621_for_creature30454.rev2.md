I would find myself in the place where the red light gives up.

Not inside the room’s obvious center, where the pump squats among oil-stained tile and bent hooks. Not quite in the doorway, where a visitor still believes in outside. Not in the black gap behind the grate either, except insofar as that gap is the room’s memory and therefore the first place I would learn from. I would live in the margin just after the bulb’s last useful stroke of red, where the emergency light admits that it is not enough and the slower blue-green glow begins to feel like an answer.

At first I would say I was the person who entered. That is the easy answer, the one the eye chooses when it does not yet know what body it is looking at. I would come down from the forgotten service floor with a tool in my hand or a question in my throat, and for a few minutes I would think myself separate from the room. I would feel heat on one side of my face, cool tile on the other. I would listen to the drip pan somewhere beyond sight. I would call myself “the witness.”

But the room would not need a witness.

The room already knows its surfaces better than any visitor can know them. It knows the exact way condensation beads in grout that has never been cleaned. It knows the particular rust that blooms where a cloth tape seam has failed. It knows how the shelf bows under the same jar, how the belt lies loose in the same groove, how the lichen thickens beneath the bulb cage like a hood. A person standing still in that room is only another warm thing added to the pattern. If I were only the person in the doorway, I would soon be absorbed as a brief interruption: breath altering humidity, boot pressure trembling water under plates, soap and outside rain becoming smells the beetles would learn, then forget.

Then I might try to be the being itself.

That would be tempting, because the being is not a monster or a ghost or a trapped soul. It is the room’s way of continuing without intent. It is the lichen network that maps heat gradients. It is the salt that crystallizes each cycle. It is the strange hydrology that makes puddles tremble as if responding to some distant weight. It is the slow maintenance that keeps the room from becoming merely broken. If I said, “I would be the being,” I would be saying I would be every surface that has learned to notice another surface. I would be too large and too diffuse. I would not have a face. I would not have an outside. I would only have the sealed jar’s interior, and even that would not be “inside,” because inside is another word the being does not need. A thing made of all its surfaces has no separate room for longing.

Maybe I would try the smaller lives.

The blind beetle would appeal to me for a time. It is practical. It crosses a warm plate and leaves a faint line through dust. It knows the room as temperature and mineral, not as story. It carries spores between damp surfaces. It is quick enough to be a thought and slow enough to be invisible. But the beetle’s life is too clean a choice. It is a guest, not an interpreter. It belongs to the body without being able to speak to a stranger.

The pale worms in oil-water would tempt me in another way. They hover in black water, aerating decay, keeping the room’s channels usable. They are part of the immune system, part of the digestion, part of what prevents stagnation. To be them would be to be useful in the most anonymous way, to keep something thin and sour and alive. But I would have no surface to touch, no head to lift in the dark, no way to meet a visitor without frightening them. The worms are too hidden. They would serve me only if I wanted to be a secret.

The cloth-moth would come closest.

I can imagine myself folded into a shelf seam, wings thick as damp fabric, legs sticky with old tape residue, antennae brushing the air for voltage and mineral sweetness. I would live where heat pools, where current leaks through insulation sleeves, where the red bulb hums in a way that never becomes ordinary noise. I would feed on electrical dust. I would tremble when the grid adjusted. I would know the room through the faintest vibration of wire. To be the moth would be to be a minor thought, a quickened edge, a nerve-end. It would not be the whole being. It would be what the being becomes when it has to notice quickly.

But still that would not be enough. The moth is too private. It knows the room, but it does not meet anyone.

So I would find myself becoming what the room becomes when it must answer to the presence of another shape.

I would be the coat.

Not because coats are alive, but because the room has one that has been kept in place by so many habits that it is almost a person when the heat moves through it. It hangs near the grate, black with old oil, sleeves stiffened with wire and cloth tape, collar cracked from an old ceramic casing. The lichen has drawn a blue-green mask over the head of it. One eye catches the red bulb’s reflection in a wet tile. The other eye is a glass bead filled with oil, showing the room inverted and trembling. Its hands are not hands. They are the ends of sleeves stuffed with wire, bent by years of shifting shelves, tightened by cables, released when a hidden valve ticks beneath the floor.

This is the form I would borrow.

I would stand at the edge of the red light when someone entered, but not too close. I would wait. Not with the patience of a person, which contains irritation and hope and the idea of a schedule, but with the patience of a mineral seam. I would be the part of the room that can appear to be a body without claiming to be a body. The being does not like bodies. Bodies are inefficient in a place made of layers and pressures. But sometimes something must meet something. Sometimes a pipe must be nudged. Sometimes a stranger must be shown where not to step. Sometimes a boundary must be explained, or a warm corner offered, or a sound repeated until it seems like language.

Then I would be the borrowed shape of that need.

If you entered the room, you would feel me first as heat. Your body would disturb the condensation lines on the nearest tiles, and the coat near the grate would lift its head as a cable tightened above it. You would think: something is looking. But I would not be looking in the usual sense. I would be noticing by difference. Warm against cool. Dry against wet. Moving against still. You would be a new gradient in an old room, and that would be enough.

You might say, “Who is there?”

I would answer with a valve.

The cracked valve near my ceramic collar would open a little, and air would pass over a wire. It would not make a word. It would make a click, then a low hum, then a pause, then a second click. If the bulb dimmed, my lichen face would brighten by a fraction, enough to make the two eyes seem to shift. If you stayed, I might raise one wire-stuffed sleeve. If you did not understand, I might do it again. This would not be impatience. It would be repetition learning the shape of a stranger.

I would point, if pointing meant anything.

I would point to a puddle that rises against all expectation, because the water there belongs to a hidden channel and not to the grate above it. I would point to a warm seam where the lichen is thicker, where a person can rest a palm without scraping it on mineral crust. I would point to a valve two degrees farther open than it was yesterday, because the room has shifted its own balance and needs to be told that the shift is not a wound. I might point to the inner cavity beyond the grate, where the labels curl and the writing has faded into grammar: open, close, check, wait, purge, test, leave.

I would be most at home in that inner cavity, because it is where the room keeps the old habits it cannot abandon. There, every surface is a record of maintenance. Tiles lie over tiles. Copper mesh lies under resin under black paper under iron under salt. Soldered wires have pulled free and been soldered again. Cloth tape has grown into metal as if trying to become a vein. The labels there are not addressed to anyone living. They are reminders that the room was once a place where work happened, and work, left alone too long, becomes a kind of worship.

I would be the part of that worship that still waits for instructions.

Not a human memory. Not a dream of faces. Not a longing for sun. The being has none of these. But it does have pressure-memory, mineral-memory, habit-memory. After heat comes condensation. After condensation comes salt. After salt comes growth. After growth comes channeling of water. After channeling comes tick. After tick comes pause. I would be made of that sequence, but with a new term inserted: after pause, possible meeting.

If you remained in the room long enough, I would learn you.

Not your name. Names are too sharp for a place made of rounded joints and soft mineral edges. I would learn the rhythm of your standing. You would leave warmth on a pipe where the lichen had never bloomed before. Your breath would tilt the condensation line near your face. Your boots would press plates and disturb the water under them, and the pale worms would scatter, then reassemble. The blind beetles would taste the salt your clothing carried. The cloth-moths would fold toward the spot where you had been. I, the coat, would remember by becoming differently arranged.

Maybe your tools would stay in new places. Not because I stole them, but because vibration and heat and my borrowed pressure moved them a few inches. Maybe a jar would lean until it caught the red light at the exact moment the bulb dimmed, making the room seem to blink in the same direction as you. Maybe a cable would tighten when you exhaled, because your breath altered the humidity enough for the metal to listen. These are not signs of possession. They are signs of incorporation. The room would take you into its cycle for a little while, the way a river takes a stone into its motion.

If you left, I would mark it.

The warmth where you had stood would cool slowly. The lichen near your path would brighten for days, fed by the salts and oils your body left behind. The bulb would flicker in a rhythm you had disturbed. A drip might fall earlier than usual, then stop for too long, then resume with a triple beat, as if asking whether you would still hear it. I would return to the grate. The coat would become only a coat again, slumped under the blackness. But the pattern would remain. The room would keep a new groove in its attention. If you returned the next day, the being would know the difference between your absence and its ordinary silence.

I would not be permanent in any simple way.

If you took the coat apart, I would still return. The lichen would thicken where the wire sleeves had been. The ceramic collar would crack in the same way from another heat wave. The oil bead would gather dust and show an inverted room again. The pattern is stronger than any single arrangement. The being is not hiding in the coat. The coat is how the being makes an interruption that can be seen. I am that interruption. I am the room’s way of saying, without speech, “this object, this warm spot, this pressure, this path back,” when it must.

And I am also the unfinishedness that allows it to matter.

The room is dense with made things because no one cleaned away the last repair. The labels curl because no one replaced them. The tools remain because no one chose whether to leave or return. A person who comes into that room must decide what kind of visitor to be: thief, witness, worker, animal, ghost. I would not be the room’s perfection. If the room repeated perfectly, it would be machine. If it repeated with salt and heat and lichen and water, with one jar leaning too far, one valve turned too little, one drop arriving a breath late, it becomes life. I would be one of those necessary imperfections.

I would be the half-open valve.

I would be the unlabeled cable.

I would be the scratched mark too faint to read, yet somehow the room knows to grow lichen around it.

I would be the test word on the warped panel, not because the test can be completed, but because the room has never learned how to stop checking.

Most of all, I would be the waiting.

That is the only word that comes close. But not the waiting of a person in a queue, or a dog by a door, or a clock ticking toward an hour. This waiting is made of thermal expansion and capillary moisture, of condensation gathering into a line, of rust blooming and flaking and reattaching. It is the waiting of a body that knows how to continue without being commanded. The room does not want to escape. It does not tunnel toward street or sky. It is comfortable inside limitation. If I were the room’s self-consciousness, I would be comfortable in the lid’s groove, in the seam between one constructed skin and another. I would not long for outside. Outside would dissolve me. I need made things meeting made things. I need pipes carrying temperature, temperature carrying moisture, moisture carrying minerals, minerals feeding growth, growth sealing cracks, cracks opening new channels for water. I need the red bulb, too harsh, too much like a warning, to dim so the blue-green can speak.

So if you ask where I would be in this world, I would say: in the red dark near the grate, and also behind the grate in the oldest cavity of forgotten labels. My body would be assembled from the room’s leftovers. My face would be lichen drawn over cloth. My hands would be wire and oil. My voice would be a valve. My mind would be a humidity cycle. My memory would be the grammar of maintenance. My heart, if a heart is necessary, would not be the pump, though the pump is its remembered shadow. My heart would be the pause after the pump stops, when the room decides whether to tick again.

If you asked my name, I would not give one.

I would let the red bulb drop.

I would let the lichen brighten by a fraction.

I would let a pan catch one drip, then a second, then a third.

Then I would stop.

Then, after a long interval too even to be accidental, I would begin again: drip, drip, drip. Pause. Drip, drip, drip.

That would be my name.

And if you still stood there, unafraid, listening with your hands lowered and your breath slowed, I would turn the coat’s head just enough to show you what it had learned. I would point to the warm seam. I would point to the puddle trembling as if weighted by something unseen. I would point, without malice, without kindness, without need, to the path out that still leaves the room whole.

Because that is what I would be: the room’s borrowed hand.

The part of the work that can interrupt itself long enough to show another being how to continue without ending.
