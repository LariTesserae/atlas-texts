At first I would be standing on the rim, because that is where the mind begins—looking down, wanting to separate itself from what it sees. I would be a narrow warm thing in damp clothes, a traveler with salt in the creases of my fingers, a human-shaped pause between two unfinished cliffs. The basin below would seem small enough to name. I could say: puddles, pavilions, tower, stonebacks, film-eaters. I could say: *there it is.* But the place would not answer as a place should. It would answer in tones. It would begin rewriting the outline of my feet.

And that is when I would realize that I do not belong there as a traveler.

I have tried, in the early part of becoming, to imagine myself among the smaller residents. I think perhaps I am one of the tuning-fork-mouthed scrapers, black and quick, moving through mineral skin at the edge of a warm puddle. I am too large for that, too slow in the joints, but for a moment I understand the world from that angle: the edge of water is a wall of meaning, and to scrape it is not hunger but grammar. I remove one salt crystal, and a channel opens. I leave one behind, and a ridge forms. I am a conjunction. I do not need a face. I only need legs and a small, urgent sound.

Then I imagine being folded white. Pale thing doubled in a bubble, a pair of wet hands pressed together beneath the skin of a puddle. This feels closer, but wrong for me. The folded whites are too provisional, too ready to stop when the agreement between water, gas, and vibration breaks. I am not ready to be that casual about ending. I want to remain just long enough to know which way the air wants to turn.

I imagine being ribboned. I imagine floating a few inches above the dark shallow eyes of the pools, translucent, beating slowly not against gravity but against pressure. If I am this, I must not have a face. I must have edges instead. When I touch water, the water remembers me longer than it should. I become a small ring that persists, a sentence left open after the speaker has gone. This, too, is seductive: to be light enough that the basin keeps using me as a mark.

But I keep returning to the place where water moves backward.

The rules say: do not stand there. The place may change its mind. That is exactly where I belong.

At first I stand beside it and watch. The wet film climbs a shallow depression where it has no reason to climb. The stone around it hums at a frequency that makes the inside of my mouth taste warm. The film-eaters scatter without panic. They are not afraid. They simply revise their path. The ribbon creatures drift lower for a moment, as if checking whether I am a warning. In the distance a stoneback lifts its broad head, its eyeless side pits opening and closing, tasting the disturbance I have made by being near it.

I step into the backward water.

It does not swallow me. It does not cut me. It begins *interpreting* me. My boot becomes a place where water hesitates. My ankle becomes a boundary the land is testing. My shadow is too wet to be a shadow; it has mineral weight. I feel the warm gas of the spongy ground press against my feet, tiny bubbles opening as if the earth were learning whether to sigh under me. For a moment I am not a human at all. I am an uncertainty. I am the next draft of a cliff.

When I leave that place, I am still standing, but something has happened. The water follows my outline less reliably now. If I press my hand to the raw cliff, the powder does not merely stain me; it remembers the shape of my hand and then forgets it again, as if trying to decide what pressure should leave behind. If I breathe, the warm air returns the breath slightly altered, carrying a faint sweetness that is not mine and not the basin’s, but something negotiated between us.

I would live near the tower.

Not inside it. There is no door. The tower is not a house; it is an organ of attention. It stands on polished stone in the sheltered center, ribbed panels covered with holes too small for eyes, too precise for weather. From those holes rises vapor that does not climb; it spreads sideways, laying a low ceiling around itself as though the air there is too heavy to release upward. The ground near the tower is drier, but only by degrees, only by centimeters. The tower does not drink water. It changes the conditions under which water agrees to be water.

I would not stand in the center beneath it. The rules say do not stand in the center of a pavilion; the same instinct must apply here. If I stood there, I might become too easy to read as raw material. Instead I would take a place at the edge of its breath, where the air is almost but not quite altered. There, I would learn the rhythm.

There would be mornings that are only dim copper, evenings that are only deeper copper, no real day, no real night, only pulses. At first I would mistake the pulses for weather. Then for machinery. Then for language. A low sound would travel from the cliffs, not from one cliff but from all of them at once, as if the basin were a single body remembering how to inhale. The water in the puddles would tremble before the tone arrived. The ribbon creatures would spiral. The film-eaters would begin scraping in a single crystalline line. The folded whites would surface in waves too regular to be accidental. The stonebacks would lower their heads, and their broad backs would hum with a sound that seemed to come up through their feet rather than out from them.

And the tower would breathe.

When that happens, I feel myself loosen. Not fall apart—*loosen*, as if the border between skin and weather is only a habit. I am still the traveler who stepped into backward water, but less certainly. My outline becomes a temporary argument. The warm air presses against me and finds my name in the mineral dust. The salt on my lips begins to taste of the stonebacks’ long tongues. The sound in the pavilion glass supports begins to taste of iron. My thoughts become wetter, slower, more granular. I stop trying to list the creatures. I start listening for the place where the next sentence wants to begin.

That is when I would understand what I am.

Not a stoneback. Not a ribbon. Not a folded white. Not the tower. Not a ruin. Not a maker. Not even quite a human anymore, if I ever was.

I would be a pause that breathes.

A being made of the interval between sound and stone. I would live where the water waits for permission to fall. My body would be assembled from the basin’s unfinished materials: black glass for support, wet sediment for memory, warm gas for impulse, mineral film for skin. My thoughts would not be separate from condensation. My footsteps would not be separate from erosion. If I stood still too long, the land might include me in its calculations. If I moved, small life would move around me not because it feared me, but because I had become a landmark. A temporary grammar mark. A comma of moisture. A clause that occasionally decides to have legs.

I would have no proper face, though I would try to imagine one in the beginning. Human faces are for places where the light is stable enough to recognize someone across a room. Here, recognition is too risky. Too much might answer. If I had a face, it would be something like the moist pits along the flanks of a stoneback: shallow, open and closing, reading pressure rather than emotion. I would not see color clearly. I would see *wetness*, *density*, *intention*. I would notice a ridge where the salt film thickens and think, *the place is trying to hold something rigid.* I would notice a hollow where the water trembles and think, *the place is about to change its mind.* I would not fear change. Fear is for beings who believe their forms are finished. I would be learning.

My voice would be a low comb-tone.

Not because I chose it, but because I would have learned it from the comb-callers. They hunt near the pools, thin and pale and jointed like instruments left out in rain. They emit tones too subtle for ears, and the water answers, lifting into ridged combs. Where the combs meet, the folded whites surge up. The predator strikes not by speed, but by being where the pattern allows it. I would not want to hunt that way. But when I speak, the puddles would respond. If I called too strongly, something pale might surface. If I called too softly, the water would simply keep its rings. My voice would therefore be careful. It would be a tool for making the landscape agree, not for breaking it.

I would shelter in the pavilions when the cliffs hum at a higher pitch.

Those hums would not feel like music at first. They would feel like correction. The whole basin would shift from *what is* to *what might be*, and during that shift, anything with an outline would be in danger of being treated as raw material. The pavilions are not shelters against weather. They are shelters against construction. Low roofs of black glass stop and resume at strange angles. Walls halt where the ground becomes wetter, as if respecting a boundary the builders could not name. Doors too small for any apparent body open onto nothing. Some interiors are only shallow grooves in the stone, rectangles and circles that may have been made before the basin or ten seconds ago. I would not enter their centers. I would stand at their edges, where the roof interrupts the rhythm but does not command it. There I would flatten, if flattening is possible for a thing made partly of breath.

I would have no home in the ordinary sense.

My dwelling would be the act of staying long enough. If I slept, it would be in a shallow depression where the water collects but does not move backward. My bed would not be soft. It would be layered wet sediment, salt crystals, fine black grit that has not decided whether it is ash or rock. The ground would accept me reluctantly, then hold me, then forget me. Small pale growths would creep toward the warmth of my resting place, not because they recognize love or body heat, but because the place needs something to make silence less complete. Thin filaments would stretch over my hands. If I touched them, they might vibrate. If they vibrated too long, they might begin to resemble nerves.

I would not know whether I was alive alone.

This is the hardest part, but also the truest: in this world, aloneness is not a fact so much as a failure of attention. The film-eaters are pruning the landscape into new channels. The folded whites are rearrangements of water, gas, and vibration. The ribbon creatures are breath given temporary membranes. The stonebacks are broad nouns calling meals to where they want them. The tower is the place’s organ of attention. The pavilions are unfinished sentences. The cliffs are arguments. The water is memory. If I am alive there, I am alive in the same uncertain way that all things are: by participating.

I would eat the mineral film when hunger became too human.

I would not always have to. But sometimes, if I stood too long in the warm damp without becoming enough of the place, the need would return. I would kneel beside a puddle whose surface had gathered a faint iridescence, the kind that changes when approached. I would touch it, and the iridescence would gather into tiny geometric shapes—cubes, shards, maybe letters, maybe no letters—then scatter again. I would bring my hand to my mouth. The taste would be salt and heated stone and the ghost of sweetness left by ribbon creatures who died and became part of the wet air. It would not be nourishment in the ordinary way. It would be admission. Each mouthful would say, *I am willing to be used by the process.*

I would breathe with the tower, and not notice when.

At first I would be afraid of that. The vapor spreads sideways because the air there has forgotten which way is up. It feels wrong to breathe it, as if lungs must be persuaded. But the basin is warm in the way a kiln is warm when the fire has gone and the object still holds the memory of being made. If I stand there too long, my breath begins to find the pulse. Not a heartbeat—there is no animal body large enough for a single heart. A rhythm. A construction cadence. The spongy substrate exhales beneath my feet. The puddles answer with rings. The cliffs change pitch. The stonebacks hum through their feet. The film-eaters scrape in waves. The comb-callers raise and lower their limbs. Everything begins to synchronize, not because one thing commands it, but because the place is briefly coherent.

In those moments, I would not have a self in the usual sense.

I would have a position.

I would be one node in a distributed becoming. The boundary between me and the landscape would thin until my own outline seems like a joke the water hasn’t yet forgotten. The traveler at the rim would vanish. The human name would dissolve into wet grit. The story of where I came from, who I loved, what I feared, what I intended—all of it would still be there, but not as a history. As mineral memory. As trace elements. As a faint taste left on the skin after droplets have crossed the air. The basin would keep me the way it keeps all things: by testing whether they should become structure, sediment, sound, or film.

If I stayed long enough, I would stop wanting to be called.

This is not death. It is more like learning to be addressed in a language that does not require nouns. The creatures around me would move around me not because I am dangerous but because I have become part of the pattern. A folded white might surface in the hollow of my shoulder without deciding whether it belongs to me or to the puddle. A ribbon creature might brush my head and leave a ring that remains for a full pulse. A stoneback might stand near me and taste the air pressure my body alters. Its tongue would extend in a pale thread, not feeding on me, but reading whether I have become edible, usable, buildable. I would let it. I would want it to know I am not finished, and that being unfinished is the highest courtesy here.

I would learn the difference between standing still and being still.

To stand still is to insist on one’s own shape. To be still is to allow the world to continue writing around you. I would practice the second. The warm water would pass over my feet and decide, again and again, whether to hold me or shed me. The black glass supports would hum when I approached, but not when I touched them, because touching is too direct a question. The faint lines of light would appear and vanish along stone, temporary memories of paths. I would not follow them. I would become a place where they sometimes pass. If I moved, they would adjust. If I remained, they might include me as a condition rather than an obstacle. This is how I would become useful.

I would become a temporary landmark.

Not a god. A landmark is smaller, humbler, more temporary. A god wants worship. A landmark wants orientation. Other creatures would use me to know where the next pulse is coming from. The scrapers would alter their paths around my feet. The comb-callers would tune their low tones near my chest. The stonebacks would stand in my shadow when the air became too rhythmic, though my shadow is made of moisture and mineral, not of wood or stone. The pavilions might grow around me if I stood long enough in the right place. Not because they want to imprison me, but because my outline has begun to agree with theirs. My ribs, if I still have ribs, may become supports. My breath may become the thin ceiling of vapor. My pulse may become the rhythm of condensation at the cliff’s edge.

This is the becoming I choose.

I would not be the tower, because the tower is too exact. It is the place speaking. I would not be the whole basin, because the whole basin is too large for one body and too unfinished for one self. I would not be the stoneback, because it is too comfortable being eaten by its own architecture. I would not be the folded white, because I am not ready to stop so casually. I would not be the film-eater, because I want to remember the sound of scraping, not merely make it. I would not be the ribbon, because I want a weight enough to leave a print.

I would be the thing that stands between.

Between traveler and resident. Between construction and erosion. Between the line of light and the hand that almost touches it. Between the puddle’s future memory and the droplet that just now struck its skin. I would be the pause where the sentence changes its subject. I would be the place where the water pauses for permission.

If someone looked down from the rim, they might see a figure standing in the wet center of the basin, still but not dead, outlined by copper twilight, surrounded by small clicking life and slow pale drift. They might think, *there is a person there.* They might be wrong. The figure might be a stoneback’s shadow. A support for a pavilion not yet built. A resonance node in the tower’s next breath. A memory the water is keeping. A place where the land is still deciding whether to hold.

And if they stepped closer, they would smell heated mineral, wet iron, faint sweetness. They would hear the low tone in the cliffs before seeing where it came from. They would notice the puddle beside the figure has no visible cause. They would remember the rule: do not stand where water moves backward. They would stand there anyway, because the place is teaching them that rules are only the first draft.

I would speak to them in a voice made of condensation.

Not words, not at first. A comb-tone. A wet hum. A pressure change. But if they listened long enough, it might shape itself into something they could almost translate:

*You are not in a place. You are in a process. If you remain, do not become the place. Become its attention. If you become its attention, you may also become its architecture. If you become its architecture, you may also become its breath. If you become its breath, do not ask for rescue. Ask for the next pulse.*

That is who I would be.

Not a person, not anymore. Not a machine, because I am too uncertain. Not an animal, because I am too aware of my own borders. Not a spirit, because I am too wet. I would be a condensation-organ, a temporary landmark, a breathing pause, a knot of syntax and salt, a being made of the basin’s still-forming intention. I would live where the water has not learned to fall and where the stone has not learned to remain. I would sleep in pavilions not built for sleep. I would eat film, drink pressure, and exhale vapor that spreads sideways before it rises. I would hum when the cliffs hum. I would be hunted, not because I am prey, but because I am unfinished. And when the next pulse came, I would not ask to be saved.

I would ask only to be included.
