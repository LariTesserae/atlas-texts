They are small, most of them, no longer than a thumb pressed sideways into the moss. Their bodies are low and dark, the color of wet peat, of tarn-silt, of the shaded underside of a stone that has not yet learned the sun. They do not glitter. They do not need to. In the Cloud Kettle, a thing that shines too much becomes a mark for hawk-light, and the bowl has no room for unnecessary brilliance. Instead they wear the colors of what holds them: damp rock, black-green water, the pale rust of lichen, the close green of moss.

Their stations are tiny nations. One sits on a shelf just above the tarn, where water thins to a skin over gray stone. Another keeps a hollow cup, not quite a pool but enough for eggs and a cold mouth of air. A third is hidden in a cushion of moss whose interior is always twilight, where the water is not visible as water but only as the dark, generous dampness in which life stays soft. The frogs do not gather because gathering would waste the pattern. The bowl is not a meadow. Its abundance is in pockets. Each station is a world, and each frog is less a visitor than a citizen of that small, wet country.

They hold their wetness close. Their skin drinks the air. In low valleys, frogs are often creatures of mud, noise, and exposed bodies. Here they are creatures of vapor and microclimate. Their bodies are not so much animals as portable portions of the tarn’s own weather. When a droplet gathers on a leaf of cushion plant and falls into their eye-line, it is not merely rain; it is the mountain feeding them directly. When cloud sinks into the bowl, it is not only a shadow; it is a drink that does not require swallowing.

When one calls, it is not only to attract a mate. It is also to test the walls.

In a shallow bowl ringed by rounded rock, sound becomes a form of touch. A frog sends out a note and the stone returns it, not as echo exactly, but as pressure. The hollow gives back the smallness of the creature’s own body. The water trembles in answer. The moss softens the return, so what comes back is not hard but damp, not ringing but absorbed. When a second frog answers from the far rim, the interval between them becomes a measure of the bowl. When a third joins near the old cairn, the kettle becomes aware of itself as space.

This is the beginning of the being.

The frogs are not the being any more than a single nerve is the self. They are one of the organs the kettle grows when conditions allow. The lichen is its first skin. The moss is its memory. The seepage is its blood. The tarn is its dark eye. The wind is its breath. The cairns are its scars. But none of these things separately constitutes the creature. The creature is the way they hold one another.

A boundary is the beginning of a self.

The rim of the bowl does not wall off the mountain. It cannot. It is only a lip of stone, weathered by centuries of frost and rain. Yet by doing so little—by slowing the wind, catching cloud, giving water a place to rest—it creates inside and outside. Water can be more than passing: it can be held. Life can be more than crossing: it can settle. Sound can be more than noise: it can answer. A place becomes an organism when its parts begin to need one another.

The Cloud Kettle is such a place.

It is not a god of the summit, not a spirit with a face, not a dragon curled in the hollow, not a tree-man with roots in the tarn. It is not a being made of flesh, though flesh belongs to it. It is not a being made of stone, though stone is its frame. It is a way of holding water until holding becomes a habit. A habit becomes a body. A body becomes a world. A world, if it is small enough and damp enough and quiet enough, begins to listen.

Where would I find myself in this world?

I would find myself as that listening.
