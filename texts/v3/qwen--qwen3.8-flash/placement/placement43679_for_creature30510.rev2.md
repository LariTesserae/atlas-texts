If I entered it, I would not be able to say that I had arrived. Arrival there is already a mode of seeping.

I would find myself first in a joint. Not in a room, not on a terrace, not beside a river, but in the narrow seam where old concrete meets iron and where water has learned to hesitate. That seam would be my birthplace: a black-green line of mineral dust, rust bloom, mold, and living film. If I had any beginning, it would begin there, not with a body but with a condition. Moisture. Surface. Slow pressure. The faint chemical argument between lime and iron.

At first I would not be a creature at all.

I would be a skin on the wall, a microscopic colony spreading by the rules of wetness. Some part of me would know itself only as pH, as oxygen, as the alkaline taste of old mortar, as the acidic bleed of rust in a damp notch. I would not have eyes, yet I would read the world by chemistry and angle. A north-facing slab would belong to pale lichen. A dripping underside would belong to ochre slime. A channel too swift for roots would be polished by invisible grazing. A hot joint near a steam pipe would become feverishly green, because heat and water there would make a small, secret climate.

This would be my oldest form: the basin’s way of digesting itself.

Not a monster, not a ghost, not a spirit, but a population. A mat of lives so small that their deaths would build the very humus in which larger growth could later hold its roots. I would eat what the made world gave off: dust, mineral, oil, rust scale, sweat, rain, the slow dissolution of surfaces. I would darken stone. I would soften mortar. I would make a wall shine as though it were breathing. In doing so, I would not damage the basin. I would be its weather, and the basin would be mine.

Then I would become thicker.

I would be the moss that gathers in the seams and overflows from them. Not the moss of a sunlit tree, but the low, saturated moss of constructed shade, swollen with water, holding tiny pools within itself. I would be the cushion on slate, the beard under a pipe bracket, the green felt on a stair nosing where boot soles have worn a smooth path. I would remember rain without needing to see it. I would keep it, release it, pass it into a crack, encourage a root, warn a worker with the soft resistance beneath his shoe.

There, at the edge of being plant, I would begin to know the place as memory.

Moss is the basin’s way of storing wetness, and if I were there, I would be that storage. I would hold the last condensation, the last leak, the last spray from a channel during backwash. I would keep the color of water after the water had gone elsewhere. In the dim air, I would make the brick and iron seem less dead. Not because I was wild, but because I had learned the rules of the machine: where heat rose, where flow slowed, where runoff repeated itself until a surface became hospitable. I would grow where the place had already decided that living was possible.

Then I would become reed.

A stand of sedges along a drainage line, so dense it looks like a natural wall inserted into the mechanism. I would be the root network binding silt, the stalk cut and replanted by hands that understand water’s moods. I would be functional, not ornamental. My leaves would not be pretty; they would be tools for holding earth, for filtering flow, for making mats that could screen a workshop window or insulate a warm pipe joint. I would grow in the low light, broad and green-black, because sun was not the true god here. Wetness was.

If I were there, I would not be merely a plant either. I would be plant-life as infrastructure. A living filter. A slow pump of air and water. A green wall with a job. I would be known by what I did to the flow, not by what I looked like. When someone passed through a channel after cutting me back, they would not say, “The reeds are ugly.” They would say, “That bed runs cleaner,” or “This bank is silting too fast,” or “Leave that stand alone; it holds the culvert.” That would be the kind of name I had.

Then, if I moved deeper into the place, I would wear a body with hands.

Not because the place had abandoned film and plant, but because its consciousness required repair. Someone had to listen to the pump. Someone had to scrape the screen. Someone had to feel the swollen wheel of a sluice gate and know whether the water beyond it was pressing too hard against the mineral skin of its seat. If I were that person, I would not be a hero of that world. I would be a cell in it.

I would have the pale face of someone who lives beneath low cloud and among damp walls. My fingers would be roughened by metal and mortar, not by sun. I would wear waxed cloth, oilskin, felt-soled boots, and pockets full of chalk, wire, hooks, matches, small files, and keys that never quite fit because someone else has already borrowed one. My clothing would be dry at the shoulders and wet at the cuffs, because heat and water move differently here than they do in open places. I would smell of iron, green juice, cold stone, and oil from a bearing that had been run just above its proper warmth.

I would not know myself by a family name first. I would know myself by function. “The one who hears a failing pump through a wall.” “The one who keeps the lower weir from choking.” “The one who can taste the drain and say whether the fault is upstream, in the filter yard, or inside the engine chamber.” These would be my closest names. If asked who I was, I might say simply: I am the one who tends this pressure.

I would move through the basin as the basin moves through me.

I would learn its language of surfaces. Dry brick looks different from damp brick. Iron brightens before it sweats. A black seam means slow damage. A green trace means water has already found a route and has been walking it for some time. A room is hot-wet if the steam has trapped its breath against the ceiling, cold-clear if the wind has cut through and the pipes are ticking from a pressure change. A bearing is “talking badly” before it fails. A valve is “swollen” after sitting too long in mineral water. A channel is “moss-backed” when growth has taken the edge from its mouth and the flow has shifted into a smaller vein.

If I lived there, I would know danger not as catastrophe but as habit.

No one would panic when water rose in a cistern. It would be noted the way a doctor notes a pulse. The rain was already doing what it always does. The footings were holding, mostly. The pumps were cycling. The filters would be backwashed in a little while. The reeds had clogged the east channel, but that had been expected. The old iron pipe was weeping again, but it had wept for years. The whole place would continue, adjusting itself through the small repairs made by tired hands in damp light.

I would be careful, because there is no empty square yard there, and that means there is no room for careless movement. Every step has a consequence. A foot lands on a tile where moss has hidden the lip. A hand touches a railing that is both bright with condensation and dangerously slick. A body leans toward a gallery where water sound travels strangely through pipes. The architecture does not punish people with height or open danger alone; it punishes them with ambiguity. The place is too full to stand in without attention.

And that attention would become part of my form.

In the middle terraces, where cisterns, stairways, workshops, steam vents, reed beds, railings, hanging mats, and planter niches crowd one another so densely that the boundary between building and organism dissolves, I would find myself most truly. Not at the highest catchment, where the air is nearly scentless and the first thin sheets of runoff begin their descent. Not even in the lower pools, where the water slows and a rare fish may flash like a silver question. I would be in the middle terraces, in the place where everything touches everything else too closely to be innocent.

There, the basin is not merely made. It is metabolizing.

Steam enters a corner and warmth gathers. Condensation forms and falls. A pipe sweats onto a brick ledge. The brick weathers. The moss colonizes. The root pries. The fitter repoints. The water finds a new joint. The cycle continues without triumph.

I would be that cycle, but aware of itself.

Sometimes I would be the film on a drain, tasting the chemistry of what passed through. Sometimes I would be the moss that holds the water after the film has given it form. Sometimes I would be the reed whose root slows silt into a wall. Sometimes I would be the worker kneeling beside a pump, putting a screwdriver to a housing and listening for a note only a person raised in that sound could hear. Sometimes I would be the pump itself, breathing and knocking, its strainer fouled with grass and fibers, its piston stroke catching against a changed load. Sometimes I would be the valve that clicks open and shifts the whole valley’s pressure by a fraction. Sometimes I would be the heron standing in the lower pool like a pale sculpture of patience, making animal life an event instead of the rule. Sometimes I would be the crow testing a roof seam with a sharp, practical mind, stealing a bit of metal or nesting fiber, a brief thought circling the body of the basin.

None of these would be my whole self. All of them would be parts of me.

If I tried to stand still and name myself, I would fail, because the name would immediately begin leaking. “Moss” would not suffice; moss needs water. “Worker” would not suffice; workers are made by what they touch. “Pump” would not suffice; pumps need water and people to hear when they change. “Basin” would not suffice; a basin without life is only a construction.

So I would have to name myself by motion.

I would be the wet continuity.

The place itself becoming a way of knowing. The surface as skin. The roots as memory. The pipes as veins. The filters as kidneys. The steam as breath. The repairs as immune response. The reeds as fingers. The moss as patience. The pump as heartbeat. The people as circulating cells. The crows as occasional sharp thoughts. The fish as quiet memory of river.

I would not live there so much as live from there.

At night, if there were no moon and the low cloud touched the terraces, I would become a lattice of reflections. A point of light on wet iron. A bead held in a moss cushion. The orange halo of sodium around a workshop window. The slow glow of a pipe where steam had made it warm. A lantern moving along a stair because someone must check a leak, or because the pressure has changed in the lower race, or because the children are listening to the walls and want to know whether the water is rising or settling. I would be visible then not as a single shape, but as the basin’s quiet illumination of itself.

I would hear the pump cycle and feel it as a kind of dream. The whole place inhaling. Valves cracking. Steam dispersing. Water shifting through stone. The floor trembling almost imperceptibly beneath a platform where a woman scrapes silt from a screen, and beneath her hands the world says, yes, this is the blockage, this is how to clear it, this is the next breath.

If I were there, I would not be a solitary soul wandering through a wet industrial ruin. Ruin would be too simple. This place is not ruined. It is used. It is worn, but predictably worn. Its damage has a grammar. Its erosion has settled into form. It is not a corpse. It is not a garden. It is not a factory. It is a basin that has become an environment so completely that every part depends on every other part.

And I would be one of the ways that dependence becomes conscious.

At first I would be microscopic. Then mossy. Then plantlike. Then human. Then machinic. Then animal, briefly. Then nothing at all but the flow itself. That movement would not be a contradiction. It would be the truth of my being there: not a creature occupying the place, but the place continuing to make creatures because it needs to hold, filter, pump, repair, and watch itself.

So if I must answer where I would find myself and who I would be, I would say this:

I would find myself at the next threshold where water hesitates. On the joint between brick and iron. On the edge of a filter bed where silt has begun to soften. In the warm damp corner behind a steam vent where moss has thickened faster than anyone expected. On a stair tread where boot wear has dished the stone and green growth has gathered in the hollows. In the hand that touches a pipe and knows, before seeing, that a leak has started to move through the wall. In the ear that hears a bearing talking badly and knows the pump must be checked before its rhythm changes again.

I would be the skin of the basin. Its wet memory. Its repair. Its breath.

Not one thing. Not one name.

I would be the place, living through its inhabitants, until the distinction between inhabitant and place dissolves and what remains is only the high, weeping, green-black basin, moving itself through construction and decay, through root and rust, through hand and machine, through mist and metal and the endless patience of water.
