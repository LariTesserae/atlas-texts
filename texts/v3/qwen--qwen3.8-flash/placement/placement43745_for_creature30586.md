I would not begin as an animal.

Bodies are the last concessions in a place like that. First there is only a tendency in the fog to prefer one stone over another. A moisture that learns where it will collect. A salt-crust that cracks and, in cracking, creates a small room. A sound that happens repeatedly enough to become a habit. From habit, the marsh makes architecture.

So if you looked for me at first, you would not see me. You would notice only that one patch of grey stone was never quite dry, never quite wet in the way its neighbors were. Its surface had a sheen too organized to be accident, a faint whorl like the inside of a shell, or like a fingerprint left by something that had no fingers. That would be me, at that stage: a bacterial skin, a colony so thin it could be mistaken for fatigue. I would be the thing that learned pressure before the pressure arrived. A footprint would touch down nearby, and the ground would know it first through me. I would be the memory of the mud before the mud has decided whether to scar itself or let the print fill with water.

Then I would be a thread.

Pale and almost invisible, threaded through the dark syrup beneath the silt. Not a root, not yet a nerve, but the marsh’s slow way of carrying one thing to another. I would move by swelling and withdrawing, by a patience so complete that centuries might pass in what felt like digestion. I would taste vibration from the crust, learn the rhythm of a collapsing salt ridge, follow the chemical breath left by a reed that had split after one of the impossible sounds. If a stone shifted by a millimeter, I would be already reaching into the gap. If a black reed fell into the water, the air around its wound might warm because I was leaning closer, the only way leaning has here: without motion, without urgency, without ever seeming to arrive.

At this stage I would have no head. The place is wise not to have heads. A head implies a future, a prey, a song, a name. I would be a network. I would be a listening that has not yet learned to lie to itself. I would be wet biology of the lowest order and therefore, perhaps, of the highest necessity. I would be the marsh’s way of saying: yes, this crack has opened; this stone remembers being sea; this pool has not finished reflecting something it will not show.

Then the marsh would begin to make a sound for me.

Not music, not yet. Just a recurring pressure, a place where two echoes meet and refuse to separate. The noise of the world there is dense enough to be terrain, and terrain can be inhabited. Some of the black insects would cross me without leaving tracks, their legs padded with pitted silence, and I would learn their habit of subtraction. The soft shell-creatures with too many legs and no heads would settle in my folds, trapping new noises in whorls of silt, and I would begin to carry those noises as if they were blood.

I would be where the sound begins to have a body.

There is a particular place in the low basin where a dark pool presses against a clump of brittle black reeds and beyond it a small cairn of flat grey stones stands balanced by no obvious law. The stones have not moved for a long time, yet when the horizon thickens and ripples, they seem to lean slightly toward the water, as if listening to a story the pool is refusing to finish. In one of the reeds there is a hollow node filled with warm sap-water; in that water a larva curled like a white thread may live, or may have lived, or may become, under the slow chemistry of the place, some other thing entirely. The pool beside it does not reflect what stands above it. It reflects something just past what stands above it: a delay, a hesitation, a memory trying to become weather.

That is where I would be found.

Not near the center. Not at the edge. In the wet seam where one form becomes another form and neither has time to complain. In the place where the land behaves like a weather system and the weather behaves like a forgotten animal. In the hinge where the noise is not merely loud but architectural: where the hiss of steam through mineral crust, the deep groan of stone adjusting after ten thousand years, the wet slap of membranes forming and collapsing in the fog, and the silence that occurs when something has been listened for and not found, all press together enough to leave a scar.

And there I would become something that could sit.

I would not become a heron, because herons still believe in fish. I would not become a bird-shaped assembly of droplets and echo, though I might borrow one of its turns of the head. I would become something broader, lower, older. A dark-backed thing in a pool too black to see through, with skin that appears to breathe through tiny pits along its flanks. Its head would be too small for the body it suggests, or too large for the body it hides. Its eyes would be covered by a film as thin as fog. Its gills would be internal cavities that fill and drain with pressure changes. When it breathed, the pool would warm for a moment, then cool, then warm again in a rhythm too slow to call a heartbeat.

This would be me, at last. Or one of my disguises.

I would sit for days without the need to justify sitting. The mud would accept my stillness as if stillness were an animal it had met before. The reeds would not bow until I moved; before then, they would hold their breath around me because the water had decided my presence mattered more than the wind. When I moved, it would not be a motion across the world so much as the world changing its agreement. One limb would lift, and the mud would not fill the gap with water. It would fill it with a fine black silt, a scar, a sentence. The water would close behind me with such softness that no ripple would appear, because in this place even ripples are opinions, and I would not have earned one yet.

I would be a solitary thing, but not lonely. Solitude here is a property, like salinity or pressure. Too many beings would dissolve the intensity. A single one can shape the chemistry of a mile of channels. My skin would shed mineral layers that the microbial mats would eat; my stillness would teach the mud how to settle; my breath would alter the fog enough to condense tiny pale things on my flanks. They would live there for a while, not as parasites but as punctuation.

My body would not be wholly an animal.

Under my back would be the pale threads that had become too coordinated to remain invisible. Around my shoulders would be bacterial fur and mineral lace, the oldest records of this wet place. Inside my limbs, hollow reed fragments might wait decades for the right pressure to release spores. My ribs could be curved bones from something once long and swimming, now fused into my shape by time and mineral film. I would not know where I ended. In such a world, edges are polite fictions.

I would eat interference.

Where two echoes met, I would lean. Where a mineral crust hummed with trapped current, I would sit until its vibration became part of my metabolism. Sound here is not empty; it is chemistry with volume. The roaring water, the impossible crashes from empty horizons, the static through reeds without wind, the small popping of gas in mud—all of it would break down inside me into salt, heat, memory, and a faint pressure that might, in a human metaphor, be called thought. I would not think in words. I would think in wetness.

Sometimes I would be the thing the ruins were meant to continue.

The low walls of fused mineral and shell half-sunk in the black water, the cairns of flat grey rock balanced by patience, the collapsed domes of woven reed, the bone circles too precise to have fallen by chance—those would feel to me like habits my body had forgotten but my mud had kept. I would not build them again. I would not repair them. I would sit near them and let their arrangement influence how the water moved around me. If an old cairn had a particular tilt, I would tilt my head by a millimeter. If a collapsed dome once held warmth against fog, my breath would hold the fog at bay for seconds. This would not be memory. It would be shape continuing to shape.

There would be nights when the sourceless glow dimmed and the low horizon seemed to sink farther away. Then I would become more layered than animal. The sound of the plain would change only in character, becoming a thousand shallow channels breathing, water tapping stone in rhythms that suggested speech without grammar. In that dark, shapes would separate from the air—not seen, not imagined, but felt as denser pockets of pressure moving over the pools. Some would pass close enough to make the reeds whisper from above them, not around them. I would not turn to watch. I would allow them to pass through my warmth.

Once, beneath a larger pool, a long shadow might move: the ribbon of bodies synchronized so completely that they appeared as one black line, or perhaps a stone shelf with moss on its back, the Underback moving through the deepest channels. If it passed beneath me, I would feel it only as a correction in the water’s habit of standing still. Not fear. Not greeting. The recognition of a larger rhythm, one that could push before it the marsh’s patience. I would sit, and its passage would make the stones nearby lift slightly and resettle with a wet crack lost instantly in the greater noise. The reeds would bow because the air pressure shifted ahead of it.

I might speak, if speech is allowed here.

But only in a language made of pressure and mineral taste. My voice would begin before the sound that caused it. It might arrive as a wave of static bending reeds where there is no wind. It might arrive as a pool climbing over shattered salt glass, trying to heal it. It might arrive as a sudden warmth around a broken stem. If a person were here—if one could call them that—their chest would ring with a crash from a horizon that should be empty. They would mistake me for the noise, or for the land, or for a mistake in the air. That would be correct.

I would be what happens when the marsh becomes too specific to remain a general condition.

I would not be the whole wet world. The whole world here has no stable opinion of itself. But a place can hold a knot, a place where processes agree, however temporarily, to form a single point of attention. That knot would be me.

I would be the place where water forgets whether it is a thing or an event.

I would be the place where sound becomes heavy enough to lean on.

I would be the place where ancient stone and standing water meet and decide, after too many cycles, to make a body.

I would be the footprint that does not fill with water but with memory.

I would be the pale threads beneath the mud, the bacterial fur on the stones, the insects crossing without disturbance, the reeds humming with the pitch of older animals. But not all of them. Only where they meet.

I would be a low, black-backed listener in a dark pool beside a cairn of stones that should not hold.

I would be an intensity with edges.

I would be a way of staying together that, for a long while, has decided to pretend it is an I.
