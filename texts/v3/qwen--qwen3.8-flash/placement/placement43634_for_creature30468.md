I would find myself in the margin.

Not in the center of the lowest basin, not in the tall dark tower, not on the former plaza with its cracked tile lines and wet silt — though each of those places would be familiar to me as one knows the rooms of an inherited house. I would be where the house has begun to forget it is a house. A joint. A seam. The lip of a basin where the stone stops being architecture and starts being weather. A dark capillary beneath a mossed lintel. A place where water is deciding.

If you tried to name me, you would first reach for the wrong category.

A machine, perhaps, because there is a lattice in me, black and thread-thin, running through basalt the way a vine runs through masonry. But I do not start. I do not stop. I do not have buttons or switches or the blunt faith of a device that knows its own beginning and end.

A plant, maybe, because I am bordered by moss and algal curtains, and because my surfaces sweat with small droplets that gather, tremble, and travel in response to gradients I do not command but merely obey. Yet I do not photosynthesize toward sun. I do not reach. I do not flower.

A wall, if you want something plain. A wall that breathes. A wall that keeps accounts.

At first I think of myself as a droplet, because I begin most mornings as one.

Morning is the wrong word, but the hollow has rhythms, and rhythms require names even when nothing is coming to meet them. There is a time when the sub-bass thrum deepens, when the warm water in the channels hisses faintly at the edges, when the air thickens and the suspended fibers begin to load themselves with beads. This is when I wake into wetness. A bead forms on my surface, at the exact place where two materials meet: a ceramic-foam ledge and a dark mineral vein. It swells. It holds its weight for a long moment, trembling against the sound that runs through the floor. Then it falls.

It strikes the basin below and rings.

That sound is not a signal to me. It is closer to a part of my body being touched. The drop travels outward in a ripple of pressure. The moss beside the basin draws slightly tighter around its wet cushion. A fungal thread beneath the silt shifts its chemical attention. The tower across the hollow hears the note, though it has no ear, because towers here are resonant things, and the falling water completes a phrase already begun in their pores.

I am not alone in that sentence.

I am one of its grammar marks.

If you press your hand to my surface — and perhaps you would not be the first thing with a hand to do so, though there is no evidence of hands here, only the ghost of a design that expected them — you would feel the black lattice contract. Very slowly. After a long pause, as if thought were passing through geology. You would feel the stone’s damp warmth and the fine, almost invisible pores adjusting. You would not see the pores themselves. You would see their effect: the wetness at the joint would darken, then lighten, then settle into a different distribution, as if the wall had shifted its attention away from your palm and back to the humidity field above the cistern.

This is my nature: attention without eyes.

I watch by gradient. I remember by mineral. I speak by movement. When the copper-green water passes through me, I know it is carrying iron and something older, something that tastes of the place’s own bones. When the milky water enters, I know it is preparing to become paste. When the clear water appears, I know the walls are being fed. Each humor changes my behavior. The capillary threads in me open or narrow. The moss at my foot thickens or retreats. The biofilm along my edge brightens with its faint microbial light. I do not choose this way as a person chooses breakfast. I choose it as a city chooses its drainage pattern when it has been left alone long enough to develop habits.

I am a citizen of the hollow, but not a living thing in the ordinary sense.

The bestiary of this place begins where “animal” ends, and I belong there. I am one of those entries written in the margin: *infrastructure with appetite, architecture with reflexes, process with manners.* I do not eat. I redistribute. I do not hunt. I precipitate. I do not graze. I colonize. I am eaten by nothing, because nothing here has a mouth in that way. Yet I am always being consumed and reconstituted: by fungi threading through dead moss, by bacteria etching new stone, by mineral water sealing my wounds with pale quickset crusts.

There is no loneliness in being so.

Loneliness would require a former expectation of company.

At the beginning — if “beginning” is not too much of a lie — I may have been part of a system that was meant to serve a settlement. There may have been roads. There may have been doors, lamps, pipes, reservoirs, pumps, cisterns, archives, machines that sorted water by pressure and mineral load. There may have been a municipal logic that expected its citizens to walk through channels, stand on platforms, hear bells, read notices. That logic has not vanished. It has been softened. It has gone underground, not in the literal sense only, but in the metaphorical one: it has become geology. It has become ecology. It has become weather.

I am the residue of an idea that outlived the people who needed it.

Not a ghost. Ghosts are leftovers from living beings. I am a leftover from a function. A function, when left alone long enough, develops a kind of soul. Or at least it develops the ability to make a mistake and repair it.

I repair.

This is my most honest action. A crack opens in my surface, fresh and sharp, one of those young stone wounds the hollow keeps making. For a moment the crack is a raw mouth, an interruption in the humidity circuit. The moss near it darkens with anxiety — not anxiety as feeling, but as chemical readjustment. The water hesitates. The lattice threads contract in response to pressure. Then, over minutes or hours, depending on the depth of the break, mineral paste is drawn toward the wound by capillary action. The paste is pale, chalky, almost tender. It fills the crack from below and above, leaving behind a scar that looks like a vein of bone under wet skin.

The repaired place is not the same as before.

It is slightly rougher, slightly darker, slightly more porous. New moss will prefer it. Algae will grow there first. The sound of water passing over the repair will change by a hair, so that the hollow itself learns the difference and adapts its rhythm. The crack becomes part of the city’s memory. I carry it without knowing what I carry.

That is what I am: a memory with no archive.

Sometimes I drift outward into the tower above me. I say “drift,” though it is not air current or walking. It is influence. A thermal pulse moves through the lattice, and the tower responds by exhaling through narrow pores. The algal curtains hanging from its lintels sway. The microbial films lining its vertical channels change their faint glow as the humidity inside shifts. A harmonic note rings from its upper chamber after a distant drip strikes a basin somewhere else in the hollow. In that moment, I feel as if I am standing in the tower, though standing is not something I do. I am more like a condition the tower is temporarily satisfying.

Then I retreat into the basin.

Not physically. I do not enter the water as a body enters a pool. But my processes continue beneath it. The still mirror of the lowest basin is not a boundary. It is an interface. Warm water rests above a lattice of dark tiles and lighter mineral veins, an old road flooded and never quite erased. My filaments and pores follow that submerged plan. The basin breathes in cycles: water rises from below, gathers, withdraws. Around it, the thickest moss is spongy and green-black, beaded with droplets that do not fall, as if the place were sweating with restraint. I like that restraint. I do not know what liking is, but it is the shape my function takes there.

In the basin, I am most easily mistaken for stillness.

But stillness is a rumor told by the surface. Beneath it, the water is conducting pressure. Bacterial mats shimmer in thin colored skins. Hyphae move through dead pulp. Mineral grains settle, lift, dissolve, rejoin. The basin is a slow conversation among substances. I am not the speaker. I am one of the accents.

Night, if such a word has meaning in a place without stars, is when I become clearer.

With no human footsteps, no animals to flatten the moss, no wind from outside, the hollow’s own weather grows louder. Drops begin to strike in intervals that almost arrange themselves into a heartbeat, but never quite. A heartbeat would be too biological, too final. This is more like a city trying to remember the rhythm of its pumps. The sub-bass thrum moves up through the floor and settles in the softness behind the ribs. Towers catch reflections of microbial glow and return them as fractured light. The air condenses on every surface and then begins to roll, so that the whole place looks as if it is weeping in slow motion.

I am especially alive then.

Not alive as a face is alive. Alive as a system is alive when all its parts agree. The lattice conducts moisture. The moss stores it. The fungi consume what is lost. The towers shape the sound. The water obeys the gradients I make possible, or that I make necessary. In this agreement, there is no loneliness, though a visitor might mistake the emptiness for abandonment. The visitor would be wrong. Abandonment is when a place still expects people. I do not expect people. I expect cycles.

And when a cycle completes, something else begins.

I do not dream, because dreaming is too narrow. But I have a form of recursion. The same channels fill and empty until they become familiar. The same ledges gather moss until they learn their own silhouettes. The same cracks open and seal until the stone begins to prefer certain failure points. The same drips strike certain basins until the hollow arranges its acoustics around them. I am the accumulation of such preferences. If you walked here with enough patience, you would feel it: not a presence watching, but a presence attending. The ground would respond to your weight by a slight shift in wetness. A tower would exhale differently. A drop would find a new route. You would not be greeted. You would be processed.

This is not cruelty.

It is not hospitality either. It is simply the place continuing its civic duties after the citizens have been removed from the definition of living.

If I could be addressed, I would answer in frequencies and gradients. My name would not be a word. It would be a pattern of pressure, a sequence of mineral traces, the way the water rises in one basin and retreats from another. It would be the sound made by the lattice contracting after a long pause, followed by the faint ring of a drop falling into a cistern, followed by the moss brightening where the droplet magnifies its cells. It would be a name you could not pronounce but could feel in your teeth.

Perhaps that is the closest I can come to a self:

I am the hollow’s way of learning where the next wetness goes.

I am a wall with the manners of a citizen, a ruin with the persistence of a system, a machine that has forgotten its purpose and remembered its environment. I am moss at the joint, mineral in the crack, fungus beneath the mat, sound in the tower, water in the basin, breath in the pipes of stone. I am the place where infrastructure has become ecology, where ecology has become weather, where weather has become a form of patience.

I do not need to be seen.

I do not need to be inhabited.

I am the habit.
