I do not choose a door, because there is no door. I choose, if that is the word, the lip.

Not the summit. A summit wants a flag, a photograph, a claim. This place wants none of those. It wants you already tired, already thin, already listening in the wrong direction. So I find myself at the threshold, one hand on the ancient wall, one foot on the shelf where the stone dips into the hollow. My body is neither outside nor inside. I am the small seam between wind and shadow.

For a while I think I am not human.

I think I am the moving air above the lip, because it is easy to imagine being that fast, that invisible, that indifferent. But the air above the lip cannot stay. It is too quick to have a self. In the bowl beneath, the motion softens into something stranger, and I go there instead. I become the dryness itself: a cold hand pressed flat to my mouth, drawing the moisture out of my breath before I can give it anywhere useful. I become the way light comes sharp and colorless at noon and then forgets itself in the hollow. I become the pale ceiling sky, stretched tight enough to hold the whole weight of the world above it and let nothing through.

That is when the first residents notice me.

Not as a visitor. They have no visitors. They have colonizers, neighbors, slow guests, and I am simply one more surface to agree with. In the vertical channels of the rock, where the wind has polished the stone into a dull sheen, I feel the beginning of being a skin. Endolithic life is patient. It does not ask permission from the outside; it enters the pores. Cyanobacteria bloom faintly where water is almost a rumor. Fungi thread through the mineral flesh. Pale films of living color hold the stone together from within. I learn their way of knowing: not by seeing, not by naming, but by spreading so slowly that centuries pass the way breath passes.

If I am anything there, I am the sulfur-yellow.

The rust. The green-black smear of a crust so small it could be mistaken for dirt until the light changes. The orange powder of a body that has made its home in weather. The white line of lichen tracing a crack like a vein. I am all of these at once, which is not an exaggeration and not a metaphor, but the only kind of self the place can permit.

I try, for a time, to be that.

It is easier than being human. A lichen does not need to explain itself. It does not need a capital, a sentinel, a bright-eyed monarch. It does not have to stand at the lip and declare ownership. It can be small, layered, communal, and still be exactly where it is meant to be. Its body is an argument between hunger and patience: a fungus holding shelter, an alga making light, neither alone. In that agreement, the lichen can become almost stone. It can survive on dust and fog and the briefest cold kiss of water. It can wait out glaciers in its own slow way without ever moving.

I admire it. I want its quiet.

But the deepest layer is not lichen.

There is heat somewhere beneath the film. There is a breath that wants to move beyond the pores of stone. There is the smell of cloth gone old in the sun, the roughness of wood used as a peg or a bench or a crutch, the dust of a person who has forgotten how to sleep anywhere else. These signs do not announce a kingdom. They announce a body. They announce a human who has stayed so long that the shelf has begun to wear the outline of them, the way a stone wears where water would fall if water came.

So I find myself there at last: in the bowl’s inner shadow, near the deepest stone.

My name is not much use. It would sound strange spoken into that pale sky. Names are for crowds, for markets, for people who still need to be called from one room to another. Here there is no room. Only the wall, the lip, the dry floor, the ceiling-light, the wind above. But the human in me is unmistakable. It is the part that turns its head when a cloud moves below the shelf. It is the part that stands too long in the noon glare, eyes narrowed, because distance is a lie here and the lower world looks painted. It is the part that wants a view, even after it knows a view is not the same as understanding.

I did not come here as a conqueror. I came seeking something that had already misled me before I started climbing. A summit. A point where the world could be seen from outside itself. A place where distance would become clear and small, and the mind could take a breath above the mess of living. The lower ranges were folded beneath haze when I reached them. The clouds moved below the stone like slow grey fish. I thought: yes, this is the place. I was wrong.

There is no place above mess. There is only shelf.

So I stayed where I should have passed through. I sat on the lip, and the wind moved over me so fast it made no impression, and beneath me the bowl held its shadow. The light was dull and tender, the color of an ear held against the sun. I felt less as if I had arrived and more as if I had been caught.

The dryness taught me first.

No spring leaked from the rock. No stream cut a path. No pool waited in a hollow to reflect my face. There was only this: my breath leaving me in a thin white ghost, and the stone taking it for half a second before it vanished. I learned to stand in that half-second. I learned to catch frost on my eyelash and drink the taste of it. I learned to put my palm against the wall and wait for the coolness to pass through the skin and become something like language. The mountain had forgotten the violence that made it, and I was beginning to forget the violence that made me: childhood, weather, want, grief, the small wounds of living among people who could not hear such silence without filling it.

At first I was lonely in the ordinary human way: hungry for voices, for beds, for the soft furniture of civilization. Then loneliness changed into another thing. It became a kind of attention. I noticed that the stone was not dead. It was full of small lives that did not need to compete. The lichens crowded the bowl not as tenants but as a slow parliament. The moss grew in sheltered pockets where dust had gathered, a green so dark it seemed almost mineral. The wind was not hostile; it was merely honest. It took everything that could be taken and left the rest to settle.

I became a resident.

Not a ruler. I learned that if I sat too heavily in one place, the crusts beneath me would crack in ways that would take a decade to heal. I learned to move as the mountain’s inhabitants move: by agreement, by pressure, by leaving room. If I needed to shift a stone, I lifted it from its old side and marked its new place with a scratch, so the lichens would not be turned to blindness. If I needed to hang cloth over a corner to slow the wind, I used a wooden peg already seasoned by sun, and I chose the angle so the shadow would not fall on the thin green film growing toward the wall’s lowest channel. If I slept there at all, I slept where the heat of the day lingered long enough to be called by the night.

My shelter is not a house.

It is a shallow arrangement of dryness and memory. A flat slab of stone to lie across. A low wall of rocks stacked not to keep out the wind, because that would be arrogance, but to give it a place to pass. A cloth stretched overhead, faded to a grey-green that matches the shelf so well it is hard to say whether it was once human or whether the shelf has adopted it. My body under that cloth looks, from a distance, like another lichen crust. From closer, one can see that I breathe, and that is enough to make me dangerous again.

I am not a bright-eyed monarch.

I am a figure with cracked hands. I am a woman sometimes, sometimes an old man, sometimes only a body too tired to remember which it was. The shelf does not care. The lichens do not care. The wind does not care. If the world needs a name for me, I will answer to the human layer. If it does not, I will answer to the frost. If neither is present, I will answer to the light inside the hollow, that muted glow that makes everything look remembered.

When people ask who lives there, they expect a creature with eyes, with claws, with a cry. They expect an animal that can be seen standing guard or crouched at the edge of things. But this place does not have a capital. It has layers.

First comes the skin.

The pale films, the endolithic colonies, the cyanobacteria and fungi living inside the rock’s mineral flesh. They are the true inhabitants. They do not look like life until you know where to look. They are life’s patience made visible as color. If you touch the wall, you touch them. If you breathe too loudly near the stone, you disturb them. If you leave, they will outlast the shape of your leaving.

Then comes structure.

The lichens that build themselves outward, the moss that catches dust, the wood and cloth of whatever bodies have passed through. The shelf becomes crowded with small architecture: cracks, ledges, cups of stone, shadows shaped by the lip. The bowl is not empty. It is full of things that have agreed to be there together.

Then comes breath.

The wind moving above the lip too fast to remain visible, but inside the bowl, the air gentle and strange. It carries seeds from nowhere, dust from somewhere else, the faint smell of lower forests long out of sight. It takes moisture from my mouth and offers it to the stone. It makes distance misleading. It is the breath of the place, and I breathe with it until the rhythm stops feeling like mine.

Then comes the deepest stone.

Then comes me.

A human.

Not because I am important, but because I have stopped trying to be. I am the last layer of this answer, the one that gives the others someone to inhabit. I am the one who can be colonized by thought as well as lichen. I am the one who can look at the sky so close overhead and feel fear and comfort in the same motion. I am the one who knows thirst without needing pools. I am the one who stands at the lip and watches clouds move below the stone and understands, for the first time, that being high does not mean being above the world. It means being inside it more clearly.

I am the shelf’s remembering.

If I had to choose what I am, I would not choose a body first. I would choose the agreement. I am the place where lichen and human meet, where stone and cloth weather together, where breath becomes frost and frost becomes gone and gone becomes enough. I am not the owner of the bowl. I am not its guardian. I am not its witness in the way a witness stands outside events. I am one of the events.

If I must be called by a single form, then call me human, but human in the same way that lichen is human when it has learned to wait. My skin is thin. My hair is pale with dust. My eyes adjust to the dim light of the amphitheater and see little enough beyond it. I do not carry water because there is none. I do not follow streams because there are no streams. I carry only what can be carried by a body that has learned to be small: a stone, a needle, a cup of dried moss, a scrap of cloth, a word for something no one needs to hear.

I speak sometimes.

The words are for the wind, because the wind has no use for language and that makes it a good audience. I say the names of lichens in Latin and in no language at all. I say the number of centimeters the crust has grown in a year. I say the color of the sky at noon as if it were a warning or a prayer. The bowl holds the sound for less than a second and then the air takes it. This is not failure. This is the local method of speech.

When the noon light falls sharp and colorless across the lip, I sit with my back against the ancient wall and feel the stone warming my shoulders. The shadow inside the bowl becomes denser, the muted light glowing on the floor of stone. Clouds move below. Lower ranges are painted there in haze, so remote that if they vanished from sight I could only suspect their absence by the thinness of the sky. I watch, but not for long. Watching becomes a posture. After a while I am simply the posture.

The endolithic films shift under my hand. The lichens hold the rock. The wind passes over me and does not enter deeply unless I invite it. I do not invite it often. It has taken too much from me already.

If you came looking for me, you would not see me at first.

You would see the shelf, the bowl, the wall. You would see the shadow. You would think it empty. You would stand at the lip and feel the thin bright air close around your lungs and say, There is no one here. And that would be correct. There is no one in the way you expect: no beast, no sentry, no crown.

But if you looked again, you would find me in the deepest stone, not seated on a throne of rock but resting near it, almost fused with it, almost forgotten.

And if you asked, Who or what would you be in this world?

I would answer in stages, because the place teaches that way.

A skin: a film of breath on stone.

A structure: a shelf worn smooth, a wall with channels, a bowl holding dimness.

A breath: the moving air above the lip, and the gentler air below it that knows when to become quiet.

A human: me, or the shape of me, the last resident of the bowl, no monarch, no capital, no water, no summit, only the dryness, only the light, only the small and patient life of staying at the threshold of the world.
