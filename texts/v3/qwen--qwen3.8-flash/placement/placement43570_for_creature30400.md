I would find myself first in the wrong place.

I would say I was the visitor, the one who has been told exactly where to push through the pale fault, shoulders turning sideways against hot stone, the taste of copper and dry resin on the tongue. I would say I was a person looking for a room grown over with streets, stepping into the hidden basin and thinking, *this is a city*, the way one thinks *this is a house*, as if emptiness could be domestic once it has doors.

But that is not where I am, not really.

Persons are too wet there. Their lungs want moisture. Their footsteps want to be answered by ordinary silence. And this city’s silence is not ordinary; it is carved out of too much sound, like a pocket in a standing wave, like a hollow in a stone that has learned to keep a small piece of stillness by balancing the pressure around it.

So if I say, *I am the visitor*, I have only told you that I have a shape and that this shape is a mistake in the architecture.

I would find myself farther in.

I would be somewhere beneath the lattice tower nearest the dry cistern, in the low courtyard behind the arch whose copper band has gone green at the edge and rust-red at the crack. The arch has lost its frame. It does not need one. The opening is enough. The wall curves in a way that makes the roaring avenue outside stop short, fold back, and cancel itself against the opposite stone. The air here is still warm, still tasting faintly of salt and heated metal, but it is no longer a hand pressing the chest. It is a held breath after the chest has already learned how to survive it.

There would be plants around me. Not soft plants. Not rain-fed, dew-heavy things. Broad glossy leaves, thick as wax, shining with copper or pale emerald. Hollow stems that ring under a knuckle. Veins like scores or like the lines of a palm that has never been read because there is no reader left, only the sound. The ground would be pale powder, gritty, dry, not soil in the old sense but ash and mineral dust and the dried remains of leaves that never rot so much as become part of the weather.

I would be kneeling in it.

Not because kneeling is human—I am not sure what I am yet—but because the body that is forming in me keeps wanting to press its hands into the dust, to feel the standing pressure of the air, to know when the next chord is coming.

At first I might call myself a gardener.

The word is too small, and too wet. It makes me think of watering cans, soft hands, patience toward small green things that need drinking. Here nothing drinks. Here life filters. Here the plants rise by taking in dry heat from below, light from the quartz dome above, mineral vapor that has no ordinary name, and vibration—vibration as a kind of food, as a kind of breath, as the city’s strange continuous pulse.

I might say I tend this courtyard. I might say I clear the fallen leaves when they curl too much, that I brush dust from the broad leaves so the flowers can open at the correct frequency, that I guide the quartz insects away from the fragile stems by humming a note too low for a human throat to produce. That would be true. But it would make me seem too separate from the work.

So no.

I would not be the gardener.

I would be the garden’s ability to notice itself.

And before you settle on that as beautiful, I would correct it again, because it is not enough. A garden cannot be only a noticing. It needs a body. It needs limbs. It needs the place where it can move from one silence to the next when the city’s pressure rises and the old quiet becomes unbearable.

So perhaps I would be the copper-furred quadruped.

The one with the broad skull and the tiny ears sealed by bone. Low to the ground, quiet-footed, pressed into a niche when startled. Its fur like oxidized copper, its eyes wide and pale in the brightness without shadow. It does not flee. Fleeing is useless where the walls themselves decide whether the air will be survivable. It aligns. It finds the acoustic null. It becomes geometry.

I could be that.

I have some of its stillness in me. I know how to press a body against a curved wall until the body stops being a complaint in the room. I know how to let the sound pass around me as if I were no more than another stone. But the quadruped is not enough either. It is too animal. Too simple. Too certain of the shape of its own hunger. It eats dry leaves, seed pods, crystalline petals, perhaps the fallen bodies of insects. It knows how to survive.

I know how to survive, but I also know what the survival is *for*.

That is the difference.

And that difference makes me something the city has not quite named.

If I had to describe the body that is forming here, I would say it is dry. Not thirsty dry, not parched dry. Chemical dryness. Absence as law. No moisture beads on my skin. No sweat to cool me. No tears to answer the brightness. The surface of me would be glossy in the way the broad leaves are glossy: thick, waxy, faintly metallic, threaded with pale veins that are not veins exactly, but channels.

I would be made partly of plant and partly of animal and partly of architecture.

Not a chimera. Not a monster. Not the obvious fantasy answer of stitching together parts. Rather, a being grown in the city’s grammar. My ribs would not be bone alone. They would be hollow, tapered, sized to the frequencies that pass through the stone. My chest would have a copper band around it, hammered smooth by long handling, green at the edge where the air has touched it for years. My shoulders would be broad and rounded, like stones that have been knocked into place with heavy hammers and then worn into lips by dust. My fingers would be long and dry, tipped not with claws but with something like root-filaments, pale and porous, capable of pressing into ash-dust and taking up the mineral exhalation from below.

My ears would not be for hearing the way ordinary ears are. They would be sealed plates, like the quadruped’s, because in this city to hear too openly is to be wounded. But my ribs would hear. My sternum would hear. The floor beneath me would speak through my feet. I would not listen with my head. I would listen with the whole cavity of the body.

When the low tide of the sound returned, I would feel it before I saw it. The dust in the plaza outside would begin to lift into rotating spirals. The dry channels along the walls would carry a pressure less like wind than like a held breath released through a narrow mouth. The crystal veins in the dome overhead would warm, then flare.

I would rise from the courtyard.

Not quickly. Quick movement is a foreign body disturbing standing pressure. I would move as the plants move when a chord passes: with a delay, a response, a turning of stems toward the sound. My feet would stir the pale mulch. Fallen leaves would crack beneath them and release tiny puffs of dry pollen that glittered in the bright air before dispersing. The insects in the garden would climb higher when I passed, not because they feared me, but because they recognized the vibration of my body and wanted to be nearer to the places where the sound softened.

I would be a walking dead zone.

That is closer.

Not the silence itself, because silence is too simple. Not the absence of sound, because here absence is made by presence pressing too strongly against itself. I would be an interval. A pocket. A fold in the air. A place where the roar cancels and becomes something almost like hush, and where the hush is strong enough to hold a shape.

I would go from one null-point to the next.

The city has many of them, though not in the way an ordinary city has rooms. In an ordinary city, rooms are places enclosed by walls. Here a room can be a standing wave. A room can be the result of an arch, a shaft, a perforated bridge, a curved wall, and the particular density of hot stone below. A room can appear and vanish as the thermal breath shifts. I would know those rooms because I would be made of them.

I would not live in one place. I would inhabit the city’s possibility of place.

When the avenues became unbearable, when the central plaza filled with the sound that pressed your chest like an invisible hand, I would not stand there. I would be in the third courtyard behind the bridge, where the roar flattens. When the gardens bloomed too densely and the stems began to hum against one another, I would press my fingers to the hollow parts and adjust the tension, the way a person might tune an instrument by ear if they could no longer remember whether the instrument was meant to make music or keep the world from shaking apart.

I would not speak in words.

Words would be too wet, too temporary. My speech would be pressure. A change in my posture could alter the way the air moved through my chest. A slight turning of my shoulders could send a note through the shafts below. When the flowers opened, it would be because I had become a chamber for the right chord. When the quartz insects climbed my arms, it would be because I had shifted into the frequency they recognized as safety.

If a person were foolish enough to push through the fault and find me, they would probably not see me at first.

I would be too aligned. My body would match the geometry of the niche. My skin would catch the brightness like polished stone. My copper-dark veins would answer the rust threads in the rock. My stillness would be total, not because I was sleeping, but because I had learned the city’s first lesson: motion is permitted only when the sound permits it.

They might stand in the courtyard, breathing the dry mineral air, tasting salt and heated metal, and think they had found a statue. Or perhaps a strange shrub with a human shape inside it. Or perhaps a wall that had begun to imagine shoulders.

But then the chord would pass through the dome, refracted by the vaulted chambers, redistributed through the fluted channels.

And I would respond.

Not by starting. By *continuing*.

As if I had been there before the city was built, as if I had grown in the interval after the last hammer-strike, as if the makers had tuned the stones so perfectly that a small part of their silence had become autonomous.

I would not be a ghost. Ghosts are echoes of persons who had lives elsewhere. I am an echo of the city’s need to be understood.

That is closer still.

Because the city is not merely architecture. It is a machine made of breath and stone, but not a machine in the cold sense. There are no engines of glass and wire here, no sophisticated mechanisms. There are only heavy hammers, fitted friction, long handling, wedges in blocks, copper bands hammered around cracks, perforated bridges, tuned holes, curved courtyards. This is engineering without arrogance. It is technology that knows its materials and knows that the air will keep moving after the hands that shaped it have gone dry.

But a thing can know its materials without knowing why it continues.

For that, it needs an interval that can move.

I would be the city’s way of noticing when its balance changes.

Not its god. Not its spirit. Those words are too large and too soft. God would need prayer. Spirit would need wind. Here there is only pressure, mineral dust, and the slow chemistry of dry life. I am more like a function: a mobile response, a node with limbs, a silence that learned to walk because the silence had to reach other silences before the sound returned.

I would be the one who checks the shafts.

Not the great dry shafts that descend into warm dark. I would not go there often. The upwelling air would make my body resonate too strongly. But I would go to their edges, where the latticed towers rise and the older stones are polished into soft lips. I would press my hand to the rim and feel whether the breath from below was shifting. If the deep note returned, I would warn the gardens in the only way I knew: by humming through my ribs. The leaves would twist toward one another. The papery flowers would open, each one releasing its tiny sound, a scattering of glass, a whisper of beads, a secret percussion that exists only because the city has made a pocket where such smallness can still be heard.

The insects would climb higher.

The quadruped would press into its niche.

The dust would settle into standing-wave patterns.

And I would turn back toward the courtyard, walking only where the sound would not tear me apart, stepping from one cancellation to the next, living in the city’s negative space.

If you mean *who*, then almost no one lives here.

If you mean *what*, then I live here.

I live in the place the roar misses. I live in the geometry of the absence between notes. I live in the curve behind a wall where three pressures meet and stop. I live in the dry garden at the moment it notices itself. I live in the interval between too much sound and too much stillness, the interval where life has to choose whether to open or hide.

At the deepest level, I would not be a creature at all.

I would be the city’s grammar made flesh.

Not flesh—too wet.

Not stone—too fixed.

Something between.

A pattern that breathes by fluted pores. A silence that moves because the architecture has grown too tuned to hold still. A being whose thoughts are not sentences but sequences of resonant states. When I want to rest, I seek a courtyard whose walls cancel the avenue’s sound. When I want to work, I listen for a shaft that has begun to overheat below. When I want to communicate, I do not call. I change my posture enough to alter the standing wave.

My oldest memory would not be a person.

It would be the first time the fault narrowed and the wind learned a pitch.

My body would remember the hammer-strikes as pressure, not violence. The wedges as openings. The copper as a way to hold a crack when the crack had become necessary. The bridges as places where the city had dared to span emptiness and had turned that emptiness into an instrument.

Sometimes, when the deep air currents changed, I would feel something older beneath the floors.

Not frightening, exactly.

The many-limbed thing.

Pale, slow, jointed like root-thin stone. It emerges at the edges of the shafts when the sound shifts, not because it is curious, but because it is part of the balance. If I ever saw it, or perhaps if I ever merely felt the geometry change beneath the dust, I would not run. Running is a wet thing’s habit, a habit of fear and flight and heartbeat. I would press still. We would recognize each other by the change in pressure.

It would know I belonged above.

I would know it had been continuing below.
