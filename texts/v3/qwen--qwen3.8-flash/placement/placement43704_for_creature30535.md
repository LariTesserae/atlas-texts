Where would I find myself in this world? I would not begin as a person. I would begin as a correction.

Not a violent correction, not a quarrel brought into a place already past argument, but the faintest adjustment—the slight turning of a head that has heard the wind shift before the wind has reached it; the brushing of dust from a shelf so the moss can keep its thin water; the movement of one body by a finger’s width so the angle of rest remains honest. If there is any self in me that could live here, it would first be this: the capacity to make a very small right thing possible.

I would not be one of the broad hunters. A place like this would be ruined by anything that needed to chase, tear, roar, or measure its worth by distance overcome. I would not be a cliff, because a cliff is still arguing with its own erosion. I would not be fresh scree, because the basin has left such things far behind. I would not be a traveler who arrives with the idea of seeing the place, because seeing it in that way would be to stand outside it, and in this basin there is only ever the inside.

I might begin as the damp line itself.

Not as water, but as the memory water has when it no longer needs to move in open streams. A dark seam in the wall. A coolness where a stone has not forgotten thirst. To be this would be a humble identity. I would know moisture only by patience. I would not drink from a pool; I would drink the stone’s remembrance of pools. My days would be measured by the slow arrival of evening condensation, and my body—if such a word could be applied—would be a line of tiny lives arranged along a boundary too narrow for ambition.

Yes. That might be the most truthful beginning. I would become one of the bead-things, no larger than lentils, pale and translucent as salt pearls, half mollusk and half mineral thought. I would have no eyes, only shallow cups for gathering light. I would occupy a thumb-width of seam and not call that smallness a loss. I would move with the certainty of something that has learned hunger and territory are not separate matters but one long, quiet practice.

This would suit me. I have always been too fond of small domains.

But a bead in a damp line is not yet an attention. It is an attention, perhaps, in the sense of being attended to, but not the kind that can turn and say: I am here. So I would need to become more aware than the bead, and at the same time less separate than a body should be if it is to live in the basin without becoming a crack in its grammar.

I might become one of the gardeners.

They are almost not creatures in the way people like to recognize creatures. They are rounded, seedlike, blind as chalk, pale-backed, short-legged. They do not build nests. They prepare surfaces. They smooth stone, arrange dust into ridges, place themselves where dew will come, die into the moss beds, return as minerals to the next dampness. If I were one of them, I would be the part of myself that is willing to labor without being seen, to improve a place by becoming less visible inside it.

This would be a better name for the work: preparation.

I would not make the moss. I would prepare the shelf so the moss could become moss more completely. I would not create water. I would make the channel honest so water could wait in it. I would not feed anything with my hands; I would feed generations by disappearing into the right place at the right time. In the basin, this would not be death in the sense of ending. It would be translation.

But there is one thing the gardeners do not quite have: the ability to answer when spoken to. Their work is so patient that it almost seems indifferent. I am not sure indifference is enough, even in a finished place.

So I might become one of the spiders instead.

Not a hunter in the old sense, not a trap-setter for dramatic ambush. In this basin, a spider’s web is not a net flung into empty air. It is a low architecture, a fine sheet stretched between protruding stones, just high enough to catch the wind’s thin breath and not so high that the wind will tear it down. These spiders adjust. They sit at the edges with the stillness of attendance. When the air changes direction, they add a thread. They eat what the place brings. They do not compete with the wind; they collaborate with it.

This would appeal to me, because I know something of making surfaces where invisible things might be noticed. My thoughts are often like silk strung between stones: fragile, low, nearly accidental, yet capable of holding a moth, a seed, a drift of spores. If I were a spider here, I would build no great webs over the ground. I would keep the web low enough to remain respectful. I would not say, “I have caught the wind.” I would say, “The wind has been willing to pass through me.”

And yet a web is still a claim. However delicate, it says: here, this thread belongs to this body. In the basin, that kind of claim might be allowed, but only in a reduced, almost embarrassed form. I would have to learn to claim without possessiveness, to hold without grasping. That is harder than patience. That is humility with a working edge.

I might then become a lizard.

Not the bright, nervous kind that seems to think the desert owes it speed. These are pale and heavy-headed, their scales matte and chalk-colored, their toes curled inward, their bodies matching the slope exactly. To lie in a hollow the basin has prepared is not laziness. It is completion. The lizard is not resting merely to recover energy. It is resting so that the shape of the basin may remain a shape. The lizard is the basin’s small punctuation.

There is a form of being in that I recognize. I would like to lie in a hollow and not disturb it. I would like to adjust by less than a handbreadth as the sun moves. I would like to be so suited to a place that I would be invisible without deception. The basin teaches concealment through belonging rather than through hiding. To belong so completely would be a kind of peace.

But lizards have slow hearts, and I am not slow enough. Not yet. If I became a lizard too soon, I would only imitate stillness while my mind ran after shadows like a small, useless predator. The basin would not be fooled. The dust would know.

I might become a dove.

Not a cooing bird. Cooing would be too conspicuous. A basin dove is small and thick-breasted, colored like the underside of old bone, and makes a low, dry clicking sound, as though two pebbles were being turned gently in a pocket. Its nest is not a pile but a fitted hollow, shaped by generations of feet and beaks until the stone seems carved to receive it. The eggs are matte, round, nearly invisible. Not cleverly hidden. Simply fitting.

This seems the right form for attention that wishes to remain low. I would know the slant of shelves, the narrow updrafts along the walls, the places where the wind gives up and lies down. I would not fly far. I would only need to lift, circle once or twice, and return. A flight like a seed considering whether to fall. That is not flight as escape. That is flight as confirmation: yes, this is still the place I belong to, and yet I can see it differently for a moment.

I would like to be the part of the basin that can see the basin from a little above itself. But even the dove can be too much. Wings are still motion. Motion can still be mistaken for opinion.

I might become a moth instead.

The basin’s moth does not fly at random. Its wings are pale, broad, slightly frayed, so that when it rests against the stone it seems like a fragment of old parchment lifted by wind. It moves from shelf to shelf according to a sequence no human can hear, perhaps because the sequence is not meant for humans. It corresponds to shifts in moisture, scent, and the faint electrical charge of stone. It pollinates crack-plants. It carries spores from one cultivated moss terrace to another. Its larvae are thick, white, dust-covered, eating lichen and mineral film from shallow bowls formed by weather.

If I am anything here, I am close to this moth.

I am the thing that carries the next tiny bit of growth from one quiet surface to another. I am not the water, not the stone, not the moss. I am the small transfer. The dust on the wing. The accidental grace that makes the next terrace possible. But even a moth has to fly, and flight is a kind of self-assertion. I would have to learn to fly without announcing myself, to arrive without leaving traces stronger than the place can hold.

This is where the basin teaches me to stop choosing among creatures. The more I try to name myself, the more I become a visitor with a catalogue. I would be bead, gardener, spider, lizard, dove, moth, and each name would be both true and too large. True as a possibility. Too large as a claim.
