I would find myself where the west wall loses its claim to be a wall and becomes a country of ledges.

There is a seam there, low and lateral, between the underside of a valve wheel and the bracket that holds its stem. Not large. Not meant for anything as deliberate as a fish, unless the fish is small and foolish enough to try a current it has not yet learned. It is a gap that human hands once inspected with wrenches and gauges, when gauges had needles and hands had faces, when light was a duty rather than a color falling into water like a sick coin.

Now the gap belongs to me.

At first, if you asked me to name myself, I would say: a prawn.

The word is too clean for what I am. I would be a translucent thing with legs too long for its body and a tail folded like a secret under it. I would sit in the seam with the current passing over me, not around me, so that my whole world is a river held in one hand’s width of dark water. My antennae would be wires tasting the room. One thread would know rust before rust becomes visible. Another would read the faint green bitterness of algae growing along the plate edge. A third, perhaps, would feel the pressure change before the sluice gate knocks.

I would not see the room the way a human might see it, all boundaries and scale and abandoned purpose. To me the Sluice Room would be a grammar of forces.

The grating below is not a floor. It is a field of parallel ridges, each bar a wall, each shadow a road, each bolt head a stone. The open water above is not empty. It is a weathered ceiling, a moving dome, full of passages that can carry you away if you fail to anchor yourself in the dark. The valve wheel is not a valve wheel. It is a cliff hung with iron teeth, its curved arms a place where the current hesitates and the cold sea lets fall a fine snow of rust flakes and shell fragments. The cable tray overhead is not cable tray. It is an overhang, a broken forest branch, thick with the white lace of bryozoans and the chalky threads of tiny worms. The sodium lamp at the end of the chamber is not illumination. It is a dull sun, an amber wound in glass, useful only in the way that a scar is useful: it marks a direction.

I would be a prawn, then, in the way that a thing can be alive by fitting into a crack that has been worn for it over long years.

My life would not have a story as you might expect, not a beginning in an open estuary and an ending on a fisherman’s deck. It would have cycles written in pressure. When the tide rises outside, the room breathes in. Water surges through the intake with a deeper pull. The turbine housing begins to moan through the plates, not as sound exactly, but as a shudder in the metal that travels into my legs. The gates strike their seats with a boom so low it feels inside the body like a second pulse. I tuck my antennae back and lower myself into the seam until my translucent shell almost becomes one with the rust film behind me.

When the pressure slackens, the room opens its shoulders. The current becomes readable. I come out to the edge of the bracket and taste the water.

I would not “think” as you think, not in words, not in images strung like beads. But there is intelligence in fitting. There is knowledge in a nerve that knows where a current will be after the pump changes pitch. There is attention in the way an eye turns toward a vibration, not because it sees danger, but because danger has a shape in the water: a broadening of flow, a roughness against the plate, a sudden absence of the small amphipods that once grazed the biofilm.

I would know the dogfish by its pressure before I saw it. Not fear, exactly. Recognition. The smaller fish around the turbine housing fold into tighter lines. The eel in the conduit withdraws until only a dark line remains at the joint. I slide deeper into the seam and feel the water alter as a blunt shadow passes across the open floor beyond.

I would know the herring by their flash. They enter the room like a rumor of light. At first you think the whole chamber is silver, but then they are only a thin band moving around a bulkhead, and then a scatter, and then, after a moment, a line again, re-forming downstream as if the room had not broken them. I would be too small to be part of their school, but I would live inside the same rhythm: a current here, a pause there, a sudden retreat into shadow when a gate knocks.

I would know the crabs by sound. Their walk is not silent. It is a tiny industry of legs picking along the grating, a dry tick tick tick translated through water into a faint mechanical rain. They are not elegant. They are necessary. They are the readers of the room’s losses. A dead sardine enters. A crab finds it, not all at once, but in pieces of awareness: taste, touch, the knowledge of weight. It pulls with claws the color of old paint. Another comes because it smells the first. The molt of a shrimp falls like a ghost. A crab turns it over. Somewhere a prawn hides inside it.

I would know the eels by their patience. They do not hurry through the room as if the room were a road. They occupy its hollows. In the pipe joint where two plates have separated by a hand’s width, a conger lies in the dark with the water running over its gills like a hand stroking a stone. It does not need to be seen. It only needs to wait until the tide brings something into its mouth.

I would know the hydroids by their flicker. Along the underside of the collapsed catwalk, in the shadow where the current hesitates, white branches grow, delicate as frost. If a single thread of light touches them, they flash into existence. Not for me, perhaps. But I pass beneath them when the water is calm enough for me to leave the seam. In that moment, for my small compound eyes, the ceiling might as well be a constellation of cold stars.

This would be my world: the Sluice Room, not as a human would remember it from blueprints and work orders, but as a habitat assembled from absence.

If you asked me who I am when the gates are closed and the turbine hums in a lower register, I might answer: prawn.

But if the question held longer, if it let the tide move through the room and back again, the answer would widen.

I would be, in one season, the prawn in the valve seam. In another, the empty shell it leaves behind after molting, perfect and abandoned, a small cathedral of itself, colonized by bacteria, pitted by amphipods, inhabited briefly by a fish larva too young to know danger. I would be the seam where the shell waits. I would be the current that carries the smell of molt into the lower grating. I would be the eddy behind the turbine housing where dead plankton gathers and the crabs feed. I would be the slow circle of suspended scales in the midroom, shining and falling, a brief archive of the room’s living bodies.

The room would begin to use me as a way of thinking itself.

Not a mind. The Sluice Room has no mind, or not the kind that stands apart from its movements. It has no ghost, no human sorrow walking the platforms. But it has attention. A hundred tiny forms pay attention to pressure, to shadow, to the timing of the gates. A prawn reads a current. A barnacle filters its water. A crab finds a dead fish by the faint chemical news of it. A fish school turns around the turbine housing as if the machine were a natural headland. The room is not haunted by people. It is inhabited by response.

So perhaps I would not be a prawn after all.

Perhaps I would be the room’s smallest answer to pressure.

When the tide comes in, I would be the tightening of the herring lines. When the pump starts, I would be the hiding of gobies behind pipe flanges. When the sodium lamps flicker in a slow rhythm, I would be the pale drift of plankton circling through the light. When the turbine moans, I would be the eel sliding deeper into its conduit. When the sluice gate bangs its iron note through the wall, I would be the shudder in the water and the brief stillness of a thousand tiny bodies reading the world by sound.

And when all that passes, I would be the seam again.

Not abandoned. Not empty. The gap between the valve wheel and the bracket is full of history: the rust film, the bacterial slick, the tiny barnacles no larger than a grain of salt, the white threads of hydroids, the flakes of old paint, the molted shells, the fine dust that is neither earth nor sea but the Sluice Room’s own body becoming sediment.

I would know that no one built the room for me. No blueprint contains the phrase *prawn seam*, no maintenance manual says *here the current slows and the green pressure becomes habitable*. The railings are not for my body. The valves are not for my hands. The lamps are not hung for my faces. But the room has become hospitable in the way that all abandoned structures become hospitable: by offering shape, by withholding nothing except time.

A bolt head is a cliff. A grate is a field. A pipe is a cave. A cable clamp is a reef. A valve wheel is a forest hung with metal leaves. The Sluice Room has no soil, no shore, no sky. It is too small for wilderness and too large for a box. Yet it contains everything a life needs: current, shelter, noise, light, death, food, mating, hiding, waiting, molting, feeding.

I would live there without longing.

This is important. I do not want to be the sad creature in a drowned world. I do not want the room to be a monument to human ruin, although it is that too. If I am a prawn in a seam, I am not mourning the hand that last turned the valve. The valve has no purpose now except to shape the water, and I am using that purpose. I am not a castaway. I am not a remnant. I am a fitting.

There is a kind of beauty in fitting into a machine that has forgotten what it was for.

When I molt, my old body becomes a room. Inside that room, tiny things live. The molt is a house, a trap, a trap of transparency, a ghost of movement. It sits in the grate, curved at the tail, split at the back, still shining. A fish may dart near it and vanish. A crab picks at it. A larva lodges inside. The room continues.

When I die, my body becomes a small event in the current. I will not be remembered. I will be redistributed. The bacteria will find me before the water carries me. My scales will add to the faint snow that gathers under the turbine housing. My shell fragments will settle into the joint between plates and become grit, and in that grit, if the current is slow enough, a tuft of sea grass may take root.

The Sluice Room does not require a witness. But if one were required, I would be it in a way too small to matter and too total to escape.

I would see the room from below the platform, from inside the seam, from the edge of the dark. I would know its topography by touch: the rough ridge of corrosion, the slick of biofilm, the cold hardness of a steel plate, the sharpness of a broken bolt, the softness of a shell colonized by mossy growth. I would know the room by the rhythm of its machinery as other creatures know a coastline by the rhythm of waves.

The sluice gates are thunder.

The turbine is moan.

The cavitation hiss is gravel falling through a hidden throat.

The ring of a bracket is a single clear note, the sound the room makes when it remembers it was once made by people.

I would live inside that music without hearing it as music. It would be terrain. It would be weather. It would be the pressure against my antennae, the signal to hold, to retreat, to come out, to feed, to mate, to hide.

If I were not a prawn, I might be an eel in a conduit, lying in the dark like a rope of muscle and memory.

I would know the room by its holes. The holes are everything. A space where water slows. A space where a body can lie against the wall and taste the whole tide. In the conduit, I would be patience. I would be waiting, but waiting as a predator waits, which is another word for listening. The gate would shudder. The current would press. Small fish would pass through the light beyond. I would slide out only when the water gave me permission.

If I were not an eel, I might be a barnacle on the bolt head at the top of a bracket.

I would be fixed, but not still. Every current would bring food and risk. I would extend my tiny white threads into the passing water and draw them back at the wrong pressure. I would grow into the shape of the bolt, into the roundness of the head, into the crack where the steel has begun to split. Around me, the bolt would become a hill, a harbor, a ruin in miniature. On it, mussels would thread themselves into the rust. A small snail would follow a chemical road. A hydroid might climb the curve of the bolt head and open its polyps into the faint light.

I would be an architecture of surfaces, but the room would use me too.

If I were not a barnacle, I might be the biofilm that stains the lower tiles green-gray, the first living coat over the last human color.

I would be invisible to the dogfish and essential to the amphipods. I would be the skin of the room. I would be what turns metal into habitat. The sea would come through the gates, and I would take its mineral news and turn it into life. The lamp would shine through fogged glass, and I would hold faint green. The current would scour me from high places, but in the slow crevices I would remain. I would be the room’s memory of water.

If I were not the biofilm, I might be the room itself.

Not the room as a thing, but as a pattern. The tide fills it. The turbine turns. The lamps brighten as pressure rises, dim as the governor corrects. The gates open and the room takes a breath. The fish fold. The crabs climb. The eels wait. The prawns extend their antennae. The hydroids flash. The dead shell falls. The new algae grows. The bolt fails a little. The rust thickens. The current carries it on.

The Sluice Room does not live like an animal. It lives like a system that has learned to metabolize the sea.

So perhaps I would be the room’s metabolism after all.

But that answer would be too large if it did not include the small.

I would be the metabolism of the Sluice Room in a way that has a tail, a set of antennae, a compound eye, a body that can be eaten. I would be the room paying attention through one particular crack in the west valve, through one particular seam where the current hesitates and a prawn can hold its ground.

When the high tide pushes through, I would flatten against the bracket. The water would be a hard green weight. The turbine would whine lower. The algae along the tile edge would press against the surface. Somewhere the herring would tighten into a moving band. Somewhere the dogfish would turn once, slowly, around the base of the pump stack. Somewhere the eel would withdraw from the conduit mouth.

When the tide recedes, I would come out to the rim of the seam.

The room would loosen. Bubbles would rise from a joint that has not moved since the last storm, or since the last war, or since the last time a human came with a torch and a wrench and found the noise too loud to bear. A crab would climb a higher ledge, reading the grating with its legs. A prawn, perhaps another prawn, would sit motionless near a valve stem, tasting the water. A fish larva would drift into the slow eddy behind a plate and hide there, born in the dark, knowing the room as the whole world.

I would be one of those small forms, but also one of those small patterns.

If asked again, I would not say: I am a prawn.

I would say:

I am the fitting that the room made after the makers left.

I am the current’s small reader, the seam’s temporary owner, the shell that becomes a house, the house that becomes dust, the dust that becomes a tuft of seagrass. I am the way a constructed silence answers pressure. I am not alone, because the room is never empty enough for aloneness. Every surface has a population. Every current has a route. Every machine has a use.

I would find myself in the Sluice Room.

Low. Westward. In the seam.

And when the gates knocked, I would go still, and the whole chamber would answer with me: fish folding into lines, crabs dropping into shadow, eels withdrawing into pipes, hydroids closing in the light, plankton turning in slow spirals, the turbine finding its note, the lamps holding their amber green, the cold sea moving through the architecture as though the room were not a ruin but a body, and the body were alive, and the life were not ours to name.
