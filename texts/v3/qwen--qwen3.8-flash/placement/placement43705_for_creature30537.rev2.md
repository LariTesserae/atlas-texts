Going down, I would not begin with a body.

I would begin with the change in the sound.

At the surface, the rain is not falling but pressing: a wide, silvered weight with no pause, no mercy, no event. It makes the underside of the world into a hammered ceiling. Beneath it, the surface skins drink, fold, glow faintly, die, and become a haze of mineral light. I might imagine myself one of them for a moment—flat, translucent, veined like wet paper, hanging in the cold brightness just under the endless impact. But that life would be too close to the sky’s violence. Too thin. Too dependent on being torn open by the constant offering from above.

I would drift downward instead, past the first umbrella-fronds where the rain’s roar loses its outer teeth and becomes pressure inside the bones. The water would taste like the old stone of the basin, though no one would call it stone anymore. Here everything is plant-made, animal-shaped, microbe-bound. Nothing is raw. Nothing is merely there. Even the dark is constructed.

At first I might say: I would be one of the columns.

There is a kind of grandeur in it. To rise from the felted floor with patience so deep it looks like intention. To be a shaft of living architecture, pitted with channels, hardened into a wall and a stair and a dome and a street. To know the water not as weather but as direction. The column does not decide; it responds. It thickens where strain asks for it. It smooths where grazing passes. It opens a hollow, then almost regrets it, then keeps it open because some pale fish has laid its thread-thin eggs against the inner curve. To be a column would be to be a slow sentence in a language whose grammar is pressure and mineral.

But no. That would be too singular. Too much like standing still as a monument, though the monument is alive. The columns are not isolated beings. They are fusions, accumulations, generations layered into one trunk. To say “I am a column” would be like saying “I am this road” when the road is only a habit made by all the feet that once crossed there.

Then perhaps I would say: I am one of the silver clouds.

Not a fish, but the cloud—the turning, the shimmering, the collective reflex. To be thousands of slender bodies moving as one thought made of water and instinct. To carry spores and calcium from chamber to chamber. To read the room by lateral pressure before the room knows it has changed. To gather at the bright edge of a glowing frond-membrane, then scatter from a shadow without reason. That would be close. There is a life there without a leader, a city without a citizen. But still it would be too easily named. A school, yes, but not my whole self. The school is only the moving part. The self would have to include what the school makes.

So I let go of the large, obvious lives.

I sink farther, into the lanes where the open water widens and then narrows again, where the glow from the upper membranes becomes almost indistinguishable from the glow inside a trunk wall. Here the roar has changed its shape. In the open lanes it is broad and oceanic. Near a seam of roots it becomes a hiss. Inside a hollow arch it turns into a low, living tone. In a sealed pocket, the water moves so slowly that the sound seems to have been filtered through centuries. There, in one such pocket, I find myself.

I am not the pocket.

I am not the arch.

I am not the fish that turn around it, nor the eel that keeps its throat from silting, nor the small white shells that settle at its rim and become the beginning of a stair, nor the microbes whose blue-green breath gives the darkness just enough face.

I am the relation that holds them there.

If I must give myself a place, I would find myself in the curved seam where a column-stem meets a broad buttress of fused root-plate and shell-calcite. The water there is never quite open and never quite closed. It slows into a shallow vault, then accelerates again along the far side of a buttressed channel. The walls are lined with a velvet of living matter—root-pelt, biofilm, old grazing scars softened into new surface. Tiny organisms crawl along the edge, bending around the central hollow as if they know that some spaces are not for crossing but for turning.

This is where I would be.

Not a head. Not a face. Not even a complete body. I would be the habit of that place. The way the current returns to the same curve after every passing of the broad grazers. The way the eel’s mucus keeps the narrow throat open. The way the shell-bed seals the crack so that, in another thousand years, the crack becomes an alcove and the alcove becomes a nursery. The way the wanderer’s low call reaches this seam and makes the water shiver in a precise, almost musical way, because the seam has been shaped by centuries of such calls. I would be the reason the fish spiral here and not there, though the fish do not know they have chosen me. I would be the pattern that repeats without anyone repeating it.

A being of pure location would sound empty, but in the Terrace it would not be empty. Here the emptiness is part of the architecture. The open lanes are not gaps. They are channels of movement. The chambers are not holes. They are lungs of resonance. The distances between the columns are not loneliness. They are the breathing space that prevents total growth from choking itself. I would be one of those distances, shaped by every life that passes through it.

If I must borrow a body, let it be a blind, threadlike worm inside the hollow stem, translucent as sap. Let its movement be the only evidence of its life. Let it coat the inner wall with a glossy film that lets water slide faster. Let its death become a small white ring, a calcium bead, a note in the column’s long chord. That is enough. I do not need eyes here. Eyes belong to the ceiling, to the surface skins, to the pale fish with their wide, white, useless-looking gazes. The deeper self does not see. It registers pressure. It thickens where needed. It remembers by becoming structure.

I would be, then, something like the Terrace’s memory without a mind.

Not a library. Not a mind. Not a soul waiting in a chamber. Only the way one part of the place insists that another part keep its shape. The root-thread remembers the path of the old current. The shell-bed remembers the bodies that made it. The polished curve remembers the grazer that rested there for generations. The ringing arch remembers the call that opened it, and the call remembers the wanderer, and the wanderer remembers the whole basin through the rhythm of its body. Memory in the Terrace is not stored. It is grown. It is deposited. It is pressed into the living wall until wall and memory become indistinguishable.

At the highest point, I would not be the rain.

The rain is too much like a god in this place—not divine, not cruel, but continuous. It gives itself so constantly that it stops being weather. It becomes a condition of being. If I tried to live as the rain, I would only be a falling from nowhere into nowhere, which is not enough of a life. The rain needs the surface to become something. It needs the skins, the columns, the water, the shells, the hollows. Without them it is just the noise before the city learns to hear itself.

At the lowest point, I would not be the floor.

The floor is too much like death. But in the Terrace death is not death. It is another form of construction. The shell-animal dies, and the shell becomes mortar. The grazer dies, and its body becomes a mound. The surface skin dies, and its mineral haze feeds the root-hairs. The wanderer dies, and its jaw becomes an arch. The column collapses, and its broken tissue becomes a nursery. The floor is full of corpses, but none of them are still corpses. They are the accumulated yes of the place. To be the floor would be to be almost everything, but too finished, too buried.

I would be in between.

In the seam where root meets channel. In the hollow where current turns and slows. In the membrane where microbe-light filters into the open lane. In the moment after the eel passes and before the silt returns. In the space between the grazer’s fin and the wall it has been polishing for three thousand years. In the pattern of the fish cloud’s turn, not the fish themselves. In the low tone that remains in the buttress after the wanderer has moved on. In the habit of the place that makes the next generation of tiny crawling things bend around the center, though none of them know why.

If someone asked me what I am, I would say: I am the part of the Terrace that keeps another part from changing too quickly.

That sounds too passive. So I would amend it.

I am the living pressure in the joint where the architecture becomes a city.

Or: I am the current’s memory.

Or: I am the place where rain-sound turns into stone-song.

Or: I am the white line formed by shells that died in the same curve for a thousand generations, until the curve looks like intention.

None of these names are adequate, because the name is not a boundary. In the Terrace, a name is only a temporary knot in a relation. “Fish” is a knot. “Column” is a knot. “Shell-bed” is a knot. “Eel-tunnel” is a knot. “Wanderer’s call” is a knot. If I am anything, I am one of the knots that do not yet have a name because they are too close to the process to appear as things. They are the way a plant-stem learns to be a wall only after the animals have spent centuries passing through it. They are the way a chamber becomes a chamber only when the noise changes enough for something small to want to rest there.

I would not be alone.

Aloneness is impossible here. Even the rare wanderer carries a garden on its back. Even the hollow stem is full of blind worms and drifting larvae. Even the open lane is inhabited by the current, which behaves like an organ of the whole body. To be a thing in the Terrace is to be surrounded by mutual dependence so deep it can be mistaken for design. I would not have friends. I would have counterparts. I would not have a self. I would have a place. The place would have its own way of enduring: by thickening, by sealing, by letting some parts become walls and some parts become channels and some parts become the soft living pelt that keeps a nursery warm with mineral patience.

So this is where I find myself in this world:

Not in the rain.

Not in the surface skins.

Not in the great columns like towers.

Not in the silver schools like scattered coins.

Not in the broad grazers like moving gardens.

Not in the eels like dark thread.

Not in the shell-makers like living mortar.

Not in the wanderers like hills of sound.

But in the threshold where all of them meet and keep each other from becoming merely themselves.

I would be a hollow in the side of an old root-buttress, just wide enough for the water to slow, just narrow enough for the noise to ring. Its wall would be lined with soft tissue, with microbial glow, with the scars of passing fins. Around it, a small line of white shells would curve and break, curve and break, as if the place had once taught a swarm of tiny feet a dance it did not know it knew. The current would enter the hollow with a faint hiss, settle into a low pulse, then leave again carrying calcium, spores, eggs, old skin, new light.

If I could be named, I would be called the place that answers when the wanderer calls.

Not because I understand the call. I do not. There is no understanding in this place, only response so long repeated it has become geography. But my hollow would answer. The water inside it would tremble. A fish cloud, moving far away, would turn because that tremor made its lane safer for one instant. An eel, passing through a nearby tunnel, would slow because the vibration reminded its body of another chamber. Larvae drifting above would settle here because the pressure is softer in this place, because the microbe-glow is warmer, because the shell-bed has learned to catch particles just enough to form a nursery. A root-hair might thicken toward me because my chamber keeps water moving and therefore keeps the edge alive. In a century, I would not be a hollow. In a thousand, I would be part of the wall. In ten thousand, I would be forgotten by the architecture, except that the architecture would still remember the shape I gave it.

That is who I would be: not an individual, but an unfinished gesture.

Not a mind, but a mode of maintenance.

Not a soul, but a habit.

And if one came down through the water past the rain-hammered ceiling, past the glowing umbrellas, past the open lanes where darkness has no floor and no horizon, and asked, “What lives there?” I would be part of the answer. Not a head, not a single face, not a creature with a name. Just one of the place’s countless slow relations, one of the processes that makes the Terrace look as if it were built by intention when it is only built by the accumulated patience of all the small acts that never asked to become architecture.

I would be, in the end, the Terrace knowing itself without knowing.

Not a voice. A resonance.

Not a person. A place that keeps.

Not a being. A relation.

And in that relation, I would live more completely than any named thing could, because I would be the place where the named things meet and cease to be merely named.
