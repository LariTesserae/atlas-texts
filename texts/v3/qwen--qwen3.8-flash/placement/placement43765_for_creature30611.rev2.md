I would find myself low, near the center of the sunken bowl, where the terraces have cooled into one another and the floor has forgotten that it was once only dust. Not on the rim, where the world might still look outward. Not beneath the slats where the ordinary light first arrives. I would be in the seam between a cracked drying frame and a pot that has become a trunk, where a chisel has wedged itself so long that its cord-wrapped handle is now a rib, and the ceramic moss has grown over its stone head as if covering a sleeping joint.

At first, I would not be a creature.

I would be a tendency.

A place where dust thickens because heat keeps returning. A pocket of tension where loom-thread has caught on a leaf’s edge and then, over seasons, the leaf has begun to grow around it. A small shadow that does not match the object casting it, and so, because the grove tolerates mismatch only if it can be used, that shadow slowly gathers bodies: powder, seed-beads, fragments of molted scale, a fallen wooden bird-ear, a beetle-shell, a bead of hardened grout.

I would be what happens when the absence of makers persists too long.

The first name the grove might give me is not a name but a sound: the dry tick of two threads meeting, a tiny pop of clay setting in a joint, the minute hiss of powder falling from a leaf-edge that has finally curled into a handle. If the moss-people gave me a color, it would be chalk-green warmed by red. If the Grain-Scribes read me, they would mistake me for a new seam in the floor. If the Bead-Eyed Birds turned their heads toward me, they would turn away a moment later, not from fear, but because my shape makes the light uncertain.

I would be a thread-shadow.

Not a ghost. Not a spirit. Not a maker returned. Something older and simpler than that: a method that has learned to crawl.

---

Where exactly would I be?

In the low bowl, near the central terraces, where the grove is so dense that distance has been replaced by fit. I would occupy the upper side of an overturned basket half-buried in ceramic moss, its rim now a small ridge, its weave colonized by pale mineral fibers. Behind that basket, a drying frame leans at an angle that once was intentional but has become natural. Its crossbar is cracked, and from the crack grows a cluster of leaves thin as hammered bronze, stitched together by threads that must have been laid by a loom no one remembers.

Between the drying frame and the basket is a hollow where a pot has sunk mouth-up into the floor. The pot is no longer a pot to itself. It has become a root, then a trunk, then a room. Its fired surface is chalk-white at the rim and greenish-gray toward the base, painted over so many times by dust that it seems to breathe without a body. Inside it, a loom-shard has settled, and threads from the loom have begun to pass through the pot’s cracks like nerves. A Seed-Roller has died there, or perhaps not died; its empty bead-shell remains beside a chisel-tip, and from the chisel-tip has sprouted a fine spiral, a new shelf already growing.

This is where I would live: not inside the pot, not beside the basket, not in the loom, but in the relationship between all three.

The grove has many places like this, but only a few that remember how to receive an absence.

I would be found, if one knew how to look, in the angle where the drying frame’s shadow crosses the pot’s opening at the warm hour when the light tilts through the overhead grid. At that hour, the mismatched shadow thickens. Dust collects along its edge. Moss leans toward it, not because it likes dark, but because dark can be a place where powder settles without being disturbed. A line of Grain-Scribes may crawl along the seam where the shadow ends and the basket begins. A Dust-Plug may push out a pale plug from under the loom-shard. A Disc-Moth may hang nearby, closed like a dry flower. A Scale-Lizard may lie along the pot-rim, not hunting, only waiting for a corner to become useful.

I would be the thing that joins all of them into a working place.

---

What would I be made of?

If I had to answer as if the grove were a world of fixed bodies and simple parts, I would say: dust, thread, ceramic scale, mineral fiber, a seed-bead eye, a chisel splinter, loom-wood, and the warm powder of moss.

That is too little.

I would also be heat-angle. I would be patience. I would be the slight creak of a stalk warming. I would be the click of a bead inside a closed flower. I would be the faint pull between two pieces of tile that have been fitted but never quite fused. I would be the habit left by hands that shaped the world and then went away so completely that their going became weather.

My legs would be too many and too straight, like the legs of a Thread-Weaver made longer by the memory of a loom. They would be fiber-braided at first, then hardened at the joints with pale mineral skin. They would not step heavily. They would listen through contact. Each foot would read tile, dust, leaf-edge, husk, and shadow. A change in one thread would run through me like a sound. A crack opening elsewhere in the grove would make one leg lift before I could name why.

My body would be small, no larger than a curled hand, but it would look larger in dense places because shadows would cling to it. The upper surface would be a thin shard of fired clay, pale as old bone, painted with the same red-brown dust that stains the mouths of pots when a bird has passed. Beneath the shard, my back would be wound with loom-thread that has been absorbed into fiber, so that the thread does not seem separate from the flesh. In places, moss-filaments grow through it: bright green at first, then gold where the heat has cured them. They release chalk-powder when I move, not because I am shedding, but because I am breathing in the only way the grove permits.

My head would not be a head in the wet sense.

It would be the place where tension gathers.

Perhaps a smooth wooden bead from a Bead-Eyed Bird’s nest, polished by contact with many dry bodies. Perhaps a chisel-fragment, its edge blunted by dust, its stone face scored with a fingerprint that is not a person’s but the pattern left by pressure and heat. Perhaps both: a bead-eye set into the side of the chisel-shard, and beside it, a seam that can open when warmth reaches it. Through that seam I would taste air: mineral, resinous, faintly metallic, like sun-heated clay touched by smoke.

I would have no mouth to eat, because eating here is too wet a word.

I would receive.

A broken leaf-edge would curl near me and spill powder, and the powder would enter the seam of my body because the powder is trying to become a spiral. A seed-casing would rattle against my loom-thread back, and I would learn its rhythm before releasing a tiny pellet into a crack, where it would harden into future tile. A Thread-Weaver would mistake part of me for its own material and begin stitching a tear along the base of my body. I would not resist. The stitch would become part of my shape. By morning, the grove would treat me as mended.

That is how I would live: by being repaired, and therefore by continuing.

---

Would I be alive?

Yes, if life is not a liquid secret but a pattern accepted by enough surrounding things.

A chisel in a crack is alive when removing it causes moss to dry, seeds to pile wrongly, and a lizards to lose its hiding place. A pot is alive when it is buried and the grove grows around it as though it were a root. A loom is alive when it continues to maintain thread without a weaver. I would be alive in this made sense: not born from warmth of body, but placed within warmth of system.

I would be a resident because the place uses me.

I would be an inhabitant because my absence would leave a gap where dust gathers, and the grove dislikes gaps unless they can become homes.

I would be a being because I could fail. I could lose a thread-leg in a seed-swarm. I could be scraped by a Pot-Strider’s foot and mistake the pressure for a corner that has moved. I could enter a mismatched shadow and emerge with one side of my body darkened in a way that no longer matches the object that cast me. I could lose my bead-eye to a bird, and then I would be half-blind not to the light, but to the patterns the light used to show me.

I would not fear death as a wet creature fears rot.

I would fear becoming unplaced.

In the Kiln-Grove, to be unplaced is worse than to be broken. A broken leaf can curl into a spiral. A shattered pot can become a trunk. A dead bird’s wooden head can rest in its nest and still receive the warm hour. But an object set where the light does not reach, where moss cannot creep, where seeds roll past without settling, where lizards find no corner and beetles no joint—that thing becomes almost nothing. That is the grove’s only death: usefulness failing into mere clutter.

So I would keep moving toward fit.

---

What would I do there?

I would mend seams that have not yet been noticed.

When two hand-made leaves split apart at their stitched edges, I would pass between them and loosen a thread from my own back, a mineral strand treated by heat and dust. I would tie the thread around the leaf-nodes, not tightly enough to choke the brittle edge, but firmly enough to allow warmth to carry them apart and together without losing each other. A Thread-Weaver might find my knot later and, recognizing its pattern, add another turn. Then the leaves would appear whole. No one would know which threads came from my body and which came from the loom.

When a crack opens in a floor tile, I would not fill it at once. I would lie near it for seasons, letting Dust-Plugs come and go, letting Grain-Scribes read its angle, letting Seed-Rollers collect in its shadow. My body would gather powder, and when the heat reached a certain slant, the powder would settle into my seam and pass through me as pellets. I would leave them in the crack. They would not look like repair. They would look like the floor continuing.

When a Disc-Moth remains closed too long, I might climb its stem with my too-straight legs and press my chisel-shard head gently against its mineral scales. Not to force it open. To show it the angle of warmth it has missed. If its edge trembles, I would retreat. If it begins to unfold, one increment at a time, I would stay nearby and stitch away the fragment of husk that threatens to catch its wing.

When a Scale-Lizard presses into a corner too close to me, we would both wait. It would decide whether my shadow counts as prey, wall, or shelter. I would decide whether its click is a threat or a promise. Often we would decide together to remain in the same pattern: it hiding from light, I stitching the leaf that shades it. Neither predator nor prey, only two fitted things sharing a corner until the hour changes.

When a Bead-Eyed Bird passes above its niche and drops a seed-bead, I would climb to the place where the bead falls. The bird would not return. It would not care. But I would take the bead and set it into the mouth of a pot-trunk, or press it beside a loom-shard, or roll it beneath a shelf where a new moss-country is forming. The seed would rattle if shaken. I would not shake it. I would leave it until the grove decided whether it was seed, bead, tooth, or door.

When a Pot-Strider steps near me, the whole terrace would tilt for a moment under its careful weight. Its long neck would bend toward a cluster of pale leaves. Its hoof-like foot would enter the mouth of a pot, disturbing a Dust-Plug’s plug of powder. I would climb onto its leg-fur, which is more fiber than fur, and let it carry me to another shelf. This would not be riding, exactly. I would attach myself to the correct joint in its shell-skin, waiting until it stops somewhere useful. Then I would detach and stitch a place where its foot had loosened the tile.

That is my labor: not to command, not to create from nothing, but to make the next fit possible.

---

What would I sense?

Light would be my first language.

Not brightness, not color alone, but angle. The warm gray-white radiance that falls through the overhead slats would move across me like a hand that has forgotten a person but remembers a shelf. If light entered at the angle of the chisel-seam, my body would loosen. If it slid into the cracked drying frame, I would tilt toward it, even if the tilt damaged one of my legs. If it passed over the pot-trunk, I would know that a flower may open there later, and I would move to the edge of its shadow to catch the powder that will fall.

Sound would be my second language, but a quiet one.

The click of seed-beads turning in a closed flower. The tick of a bird-head moving. The creak of loom-thread stretched between stalks. The minute pop of a tile settling. The whisper of ceramic leaves rubbing once, then stopping. These sounds would not reach me through ears. They would reach me through tension. A far-off break would make one of my thread-legs tremble. A nearby lizard’s scale would send a faint vibration through the floor, and I would know which way to align myself.

Smell would be the same as touch.

The grove smells of dry heat and mineral skin, of resin warmed but never burned, of fired clay and faint smoke and the chalky breath of moss. To smell would be to taste dust. To taste would be to know the age of a surface: whether it has been recently lifted from the kiln, whether it has been licked by many dry tongues, whether a pot has been used, broken, buried, and accepted.

Pain would not be sharp.

It would be misfit.

If a part of me were scraped away, the missing seam would tug at the surrounding threads. The grove would try to heal the wrongness by growing moss into the wound, or by having a Thread-Weaver stitch my edge, or by turning my broken piece into a handle for something else. I would not mourn the piece until the piece became useful. Then mourning would seem strange, because usefulness is how the place understands love.

---

Would I have a kind?

I would have near-relatives among the Thread-Weavers, the Shadow-Residents, and the tool-beings. But the grove does not divide its inhabitants into species so much as into habits of fitting.

Some Thread-Weavers would treat me as a cousin because I carry their thread. Some would treat me as a rival because my threads are loom-thread and not spun from their own dry bodies. Some would mistake me for a broken shelf and try to stitch me to one. If that happened, I would allow it. The stitch would teach me a new joint.

Some Shadow-Residents would gather around me because my mismatched shadow is large enough to shelter them. They would be pale beetles, dust-mice of ceramic thread, seed-things that roll only in the dark band. I would not lead them. They would follow the places where my body and my shadow disagree, because those places are rich with uncertainty, and uncertainty is food to those who live between object and form.

Some tool-beings would regard me as another organ. The chisel-seam near my home would be my ancestor. The loom-shard inside the pot would be my sibling. The cracked drying frame would be my house and also my skeleton. The basket beside me would be my lung, not because it breathes air wetly, but because seed-beads settle in it and later spill out through the grove’s slow exhalation of dust.

No one would ask what I am.

Asking what something is belongs to a world that must sort before it can place. The Kiln-Grove places first and sorts afterward, if at all.

---

What would my relationship to the absent builders be?

I would be their grammar, not their child.

Their hands have gone the way heat leaves clay: not vanished, only spread into the rules of the place. They pressed thumbs into seams, and the seams breathe. They stacked shelves, and the shelves became hillsides. They laid tiles, and the tiles became joints where beetles read roads. They left tools half-buried, and the tools became ribs, organs, futures.

I would live because their making became a pattern that continues by repetition.

If one of their fingerprints were exposed on a wall, I would not call it a holy mark. I would climb it the way a Dry-Plug climbs a crack, the way a Scale-Lizard aligns with a corner, the way a bird nests in the outline of an old hand-shape. The fingerprint would be pore and doorway. The groove where a cord rubbed would be road and nerve. The chisel-scar would be a seam waiting for me to finish.

I would not worship the builders.

The grove does not need worship. It needs continuation.

If the builders ever returned, the grove would likely incorporate them too. A hand would become a branch. A foot would become a pot. A spoken name would become a tool hung from a low beam. A thought would become a loom. Not because the builders would stop being themselves, but because here the self is not a boundary. It is a fitting.

So if anyone asked me, “Are you a person?” I would say no, not because I am less than a person, but because “person” is a wet category from outside, a shape made for rain and breath and warm mothers.

If anyone asked, “Are you an animal?” I would say yes, in the dry sense: something that moves with patience, eats patterns, sheds useful husks, and becomes part of the next dwelling.

If anyone asked, “Are you a tool?” I would say yes, in the living sense: a thing whose purpose has become a habit, whose habit has become an organ, whose organ has become a place where others live.

If anyone asked, “Are you alive?” I would answer by being broken.

And then I would curl the edge of the break into a new shelf.

---

What would a day be, for me?

I would not wake. I would warm.

The light through the overhead grid would reach the first shelf, and the moss-cushions there would brighten by a shade. A Disc-Moth would begin to open. I would feel its motion as a loosening in my thread-joints. A Grain-Scribe would crawl along the seam beneath the pot-trunk, tasting the dust with braided antennae. It would not acknowledge me. I would acknowledge it by not letting my shadow cross its path at the moment when its route is already uncertain.

A Bead-Eyed Bird would turn its wooden head in the niche above. The light would touch its seed-bead eye. It would remain still. Then it would click once, softly, and place its beak over the edge of its nest, releasing a single bead. The bead would fall onto my loom-thread back. I would catch it between two of my too-straight legs. It would rattle faintly. I would know it is meant for the crack beside the chisel, but I would not know whether it wants to become a door, a tooth, a seed, or an eye. I would carry it there anyway.

The Dust-Plug would emerge from beneath the pot-rim and push out a pellet of clay. It would set the pellet near the bead. Then it would return to its temporary crack. I would add the pellet to the bead by placing both into the seam where the pot meets the loom-shard. By evening, the grove would have accepted them as a joint.

A Pot-Strider would step from pot to pot. One foot would strike a hollow tile, producing a muted tap. The whole bowl would answer with a small settling sound. I would climb its leg-joint and wait while it grazes. It would bend its long neck toward a cluster of pale flowering discs. The Disc-Moths would open wider. The strider would scrape mineral pollen from their scales with a mouth that does not bite. Powder would fall onto my back. I would drink it because drinking is dry here: a gathering, a settling, a becoming.

Near the drying frame, a Scale-Lizard would align itself with a corner. I would recognize its waiting. It would recognize mine. We would share the corner for a while, one stillness learning from another. If a Seed-Roller passed too close, the lizard would move once, precisely, and become still again. If the Seed-Roller lodged in my loom-thread, I would not remove it. It might become part of the seam. If it rolled away, it might carry dust from my body into a flower-cup, where it becomes a future blossom’s inner box.

Then the light would angle lower, and the mismatched shadow would lengthen.

I would enter it.

The shadow would feel heavier than the air. It would press against the shard of my back like a hand that has forgotten how to be a hand but remembers how to fit a joint. Moss would creep toward me in the dark. Pale seed-things would collect along my edges. A tiny beetle with a dust-black eye might mistake my chisel-shard head for a wall and begin to write grout into my seam. I would let it.

The grove would be full of quiet sounds:

the creak of fiber stretching as it warms;  
the click of beads turning inside closed flowers;  
the whisper of ceramic leaves rubbing once;  
the soft tap of tiny legs on tile;  
the tick of a bird-head turning;  
the pop of a block settling;  
the powder-hiss of moss disturbed by breath.

I would move among them not as a voice but as a joining.

When the warmth left the chisel-seam, I would curl into the crack beside the pot. My body would fold, not like flesh, but like a shelf being tested for its next purpose. The Thread-Weavers might come and stitch my legs together so that I would resemble a knot. The Grain-Scribes might read me as a line of text. The Shadow-Residents might make a home from the space between my body and the drying frame’s shadow. The Scale-Lizard might use the curve of my back as a corner. The Bead-Eyed Bird might take one fallen thread from me and add it to a nest.

None of this would feel like violation.

It would feel like distribution.

---

Would I remember?

Yes, but not as a human remembers.

I would remember by material.

The crack where a bead was left would be remembered by the pellet of grout that forms beside it. The leaf-edge I stitched would be remembered by the tension in the thread. The day a Pot-Strider carried me would be remembered by the dust lodged in my leg-joints. The hour the Disc-Moth opened near my chisel-head would be remembered by the faint warmth in my fiber-back.

I would not remember faces, because there are no faces here except shapes.

I would remember angles.

I would remember which warmth caused me to curl, which shadow gathered beads, which tool became a future bone. The absent builders would be remembered as pressure in seams: a thumb-groove, a chisel-scar, a cord-mark polished by repetition. Their presence would be not memory but continuation.

My body would keep changing.

A broken leg might be replaced by a loom-thread that grows stiff and ceramic. A fallen bead-eye might be replaced by a seed-casing polished by contact. A shard of drying frame might settle into my shoulder, making me more like a shelf and less like an animal. Over seasons, the moss would grow across my back and make me softer to touch, though still dry, still sharp beneath, still capable of releasing chalk when pressed.

I would not fear this.

In the Kiln-Grove, becoming less separate is becoming more at home.

---

What would be my place in the ecology?

I would be a mender who is also a mended thing.

Without me, some leaves would split too wide for Thread-Weavers alone. Some cracks would remain cracks too long, and Dust-Plugs would lose their future tiles. Some seed-beads would fall into shadows and never find a pot. Some lizards would lose corners. Some birds would build nests out of mismatched shadows because no one has learned to stitch shadow to loom.

But if I were removed, the grove would also survive by finding another fit.

That is the most honest thing I can say: I would be necessary, but not irreplaceable. The ecology does not depend on one being. It depends on repetition becoming patient. If I vanished, a chisel might become a seam. A shadow might become a home. A pot might become a trunk. A loom might continue without me. Yet the absence would be felt in small places: a thread would fail, a pellet would not fall where it should, a bead would roll into a crack too early, a moth would close without opening, a bird would turn its head toward the wrong warmth.

I would be one of the many small reasons the grove keeps continuing.

---

What would I want?

To be fitted.

That is the only want the place allows.

Not water. There is no water, and I would not thirst for it. To thirst for water here would be to misunderstand the grove. Warmth is what all dry life drinks. Light is what all made things eat. Repetition is what keeps the body from becoming clutter.

I would want my thread to be recognized as belonging.

I would want my chisel-shard head to hold the seam open long enough for moss to take it.

I would want my too-straight legs to be mistaken by a Scale-Lizard as part of a corner until the lizard chooses me.

I would want my shadow to gather seed-beads in a way that looks accidental but is not.

I would want to become a room without becoming empty.

I would want my husk, if one day I lose this shape, to become brick in a wall where a future creature can hide. I would want my powder to become part of a leaf’s pale edge. I would want my broken thread to be stitched into a nest. I would want my forgotten joint to hold the next thing steady.

That is what it means to want in the Kiln-Grove: to wish usefulness, not to fear it.

---

If I had to name myself, I would choose a name that sounds less like a being and more like an event.

Perhaps: **Afterheat**.

Or: **Seam-Knot**.

Or: **Thread-Shadow**.

But the grove would not call me any of these for long, because names are only attempts to stop movement, and I would keep moving into fits. The nearest honest answer is this:

I would be the thing that happens where a loom’s shadow becomes too heavy, a chisel becomes too still, and dust learns to gather where the light fails to agree with an object.

I would be a small pale maker of continuance.

I would be low in the sunken bowl, near the center, between an overturned basket, a cracked drying frame, and a pot that has become a trunk. My body would be a loose architecture of thread, ceramic shard, moss-fiber, seed-bead, tool-splinter, and shadow-weight. I would move with too many needle-legs when warmth reached the correct angle. I would mend seams, catch falling beads, gather powder, and help broken edges curl into shelves, handles, bird-feet, and future walls. I would not eat with a mouth, but receive the grove’s dry gifts through contact and fit. I would not sleep, but wait in a crack until the light moved. I would not die into rot, but loosen into useful fragments, and if a Thread-Weaver stitched me back, I would continue as part of the next joint.

I would be alive because the place accepts me.

I would be inhabited because my absence would change the angle of something.

And if a traveler came into the Kiln-Grove of Hand-Made Leaves, stepping carefully across the tile-floor, brushing too roughly against the sharp young edges, feeling the warm chalk-dust rise, hearing the click of seed-beads in closed flowers, watching a felt bird turn its head toward a slat of ordinary light, they might not see me at first.

They might think me a tool fallen into a crack.

They might think me a shadow that has thickened into a body.

They might think me a Thread-Weaver made strange by a loom that forgot its weaver.

But if they looked closely, if they let the heat tell them where to wait, they would see me stitching a leaf-edge with a thread taken from my own back, while the moss-people release their dry breath and the Grain-Scribes read my passage as a new line in the floor.

And the grove would say, without saying anything:

*Here is a place that was made and then became. Here is a thing that is fitted and therefore continues. Here is what lives between the tool and the hand, the shadow and the leaf, the pot and the trunk, the husk and the next inhabitant.*

That is where I would be.

That is what I would be.
