I would not be the aperture, or the roar, or the enormous tuned emptiness. Those are conditions, not lives. The cistern is too large for a person to become; it could use a person the way it uses a midge: a small movement through its immensity, a thing that crosses the air and is forgotten.

So I would be a rain-eft.

I would be found in the lower half of the chamber, not in the bright misty upper ring where the midges ride heat, but along the seam where two black floor plates meet and a film of warm water runs. There, in the shadow of a ribbed condenser panel, I would be one of the small wet things that looks almost like a broken piece of porcelain if it were dry. Pale. Ribbed. Thin enough that the machinery’s pressure would pass through me as vibration rather than sound.

I would have no proper name. If the cistern knew me, it would know me by the path I make. I would be the water-line that beads when I pass, the faint smear across tile, the slow tongue that removes orange biofilm from a channel edge, the body that does not clog the seam but tends it.

I would be one of the cistern-born.

Not born of outside rock or soil, not born of some remembered river, but born from the rhythm here: heat rising from the floor plates, mineral water gathering in the sumps, condensation dripping at intervals measured by valves opening over minutes. My eggs would lie under a loose plate in a gap left deliberately narrow enough to keep out larger things and wide enough for me. When the young hatched, they would not need to learn the world. They would simply emerge into its temperature, its roar, its damp tile, its patterned slowness.

My body would be small, perhaps fifteen centimeters long, with skin like wet ceramic held in thin white light. I would not swim, because there would be little true swimming. I would walk on wet surfaces, or glide, or press into the seam with soft pads. My feet would leave prints that bead into little lenses for a moment before the water sheet took them back. My eyes would not be large; in this place, eyes would only catch the glare off condensation and lose their own meaning. Instead, I would sense the cistern by touch and vibration. The floor plate would tell me a pressure cell had shifted. A warmth across my flank would tell me a valve had opened and was preparing a slow release. A fine mist settling on my back would tell me that rain was coming in one of its long, patient showers.

I would not fear the noise. I would be made of it. The roar would run through the channels and my ribs would carry it as a faint pulse, like a second heartbeat tuned below hearing. Sometimes the cistern would give a thunk, a hiss, a long creak, and I would know the event not as surprise, but as weather arriving in metal. A rain-eft does not flinch at the machine’s exhalation. The machine is the climate.

I would feed. That would be most of my life. I would move along the mineral streaks where algae glow in a dim violet-green film, scrape the cyanobacterial varnish from the black tile, taste the iron-salt bloom on bronze-tinged masonry, swallow the pale lichen dust from the joints. The moss would be too far above for my main path, but I would know it by its smell: cool, vegetal, damp with slow growth. In the low channels, I would eat whatever the midges dropped, whatever tiny worms surfaced, whatever microscopic colonies had gathered where heat made water breathe. My life would be maintenance. I would keep the channels open. I would prevent the cistern from choking on its own richness.

I would be visible, but only to something patient enough to look in the seam. From a distance I would be a wet line. A small shine in a dark bowl. A fragment of the cistern’s living pattern. I would not stand in the center, where a visitor might feel swallowed by space. I would stay in the narrow places, where scale becomes negotiable. In the center, the emptiness would be too much. In the seam, there would be water. In the water, there would be a path. In the path, there would be enough world.

I would not think of the outside. There would be no outside, or not an outside for me. The plateau, the wind-worn sky, the thin high air, the stone replaced by black vitreous tile—these would be only the conditions that made this warm hollow possible. I would know the world as temperature, mineral, film, current, roar. I would not imagine seasons, because the cistern would have only its condensation cycles and pressure phases. I would not remember storms, because all weather here would be internal, patient, repeated, and made of metal.

If I became aware, it would be as a slow thing becoming aware of itself as a pattern. Not “I am a rain-eft.” More like: “the seam warms, the water rises, the film moves, the body follows.” Consciousness would not begin with language. It would begin as orientation: the direction of flow, the presence of a channel, the taste of iron, the safe darkness beneath a plate, the danger of a dry ledge, the coming of mist. Later, perhaps, there would be something like identity—not a name, but a rhythm that belongs to me. A particular way of crossing one sump, a preference for the left side of a joint, a slight limp from an old crack in a floor plate, a pale lichen mark on my flank where I once waited too long in a condensation pool.

I would live in the emptiness by making it not empty.

A stranger would see vastness. I would see rooms without walls. Every sump a courtyard. Every channel a street. Every tile seam a ridge I could cross. The cistern would not be empty to me; it would be filled with paths made of moisture. My world would be large enough because its slowness would stretch it out. A few centimeters of tile could take an hour. A drop of water could be a season. The roar could be a lullaby. A single pressure shift could move me through half a wall.

And if the midges drifted high above me, catching the ordinary light, I would not envy them their thin air. They would be born of vapor and die as food for films. I would be born of mineral wetness and return as a stain on tile. Both would be part of the same breathing. Both would belong to the cistern’s economy. I would only be closer to its bones.

That is where I would find myself: in the warm, loud, humid dark of the Cistern of the Ninth Air, small enough to be overlooked, wet enough to endure, moving along the seam where the machine’s body meets its living film.

Who would I be?

A rain-eft. The one whose passage makes water bead. The one the cistern uses to keep its own emptiness clean.
