I would find myself already in the crack.

Not in the ordinary way of entering, with a body and a map and a name. I would be the part that has not yet learned where the body ends and the bright air begins. The cleft behind the damp slab would take my exhale and give it back as a little pressure in the light, and the room would receive me the way soil receives rain: without surprise, with slow rearrangement. I would pass through not by shrinking, but by agreeing to become a tendency of the place for a while.

At first I would have no edge.

I would be a faint resistance in the brightness, as if the light had learned to remember pressure. When I moved a hand through the air, the pale room would hold the pass of my fingers a moment after, the way water holds a wake. I would feel the calcium motes as small teeth, the salt as a tasteless tongue, the damp in the corners as an almost-name. My breath would mist only shyly, then not at all, because the chamber would be doing the breathing for me.

My body would be provisional: a few grains of calciumite gathered into something that remembers a spine, a translucent film of moisture stretched where skin should be, a white chert bead at each joint. I would not walk so much as invite the floor to become where I stand. When I raised a foot, the stone might hesitate. When I set it down, the shadow would arrive late, soft, slightly embarrassed. If I stood still too long, a ledge would grow around my ankle as a compliment, and I would have to leave it behind like an old shell.

Who would I be, then?

A small animal of delay.

Not a god, not a monster, not the chamber itself, though I would be one of its slow methods. I would be the footstep heard before the foot moves. I would be the pool’s ripple whose center is still future. I would be the moth’s shadow after the moth. I would be the place where the room is about to remember that it has walls.

When the ceiling clouded with a localized fog, I would wake in a hollow where a droplet had fallen upward and then decided not to. The ceiling would look like frost pressed into stone, or like a field of questions. I would put a hand into the bright air and feel the light’s texture. In the mineral dust, a line would form: wet, wait, become, wet. I would try to read it the way the crabs read rhythm. It might dissolve into a sentence made of falling sand, or become a short bridge between one crack and another. If it failed, I would not be troubled. Failure here is often the stone practicing its edges.

I would move through the chamber like weather with a footfall.

By the pools, I would be cautious. Not of getting wet; of becoming too noticed. If I watched a too-reflective surface for long, my reflection might appear first: a pale quadruped, a visitor with no country, a shape like a door left open. The blind fish would not see me, but their shivers would ripple outward, asking the water: are you still here? Are you still the same place? Should I pass through you now?

I would answer with a footstep that has not yet happened.

The fish would brighten slightly, not by glow but by the light bending more gently around their uncertainty. I would step where the pool had thought of me, and the pool would show a ceiling that is not above. For a while I would carry that ceiling in my mouth like salt until it became a droplet, then I would spit it into the bright air, where it would hang, uncertain, becoming a tiny rain that fell mostly upward.

The crabs would be my nearest neighbors. They would tap their claws against the stone and know I was coming, not by scent or sight but by the small future-cairns they had made. I would walk through the chamber, pausing above hollows, leaving thin calcium lines on wet ledges. A crab might carry a grain to where my shadow had lain, and in a day or a year that grain would be part of a wall. It would not be worship. It would be grammar.

The moths would rest where my attention has made a bright patch and leave pale smears that I would wear like old light. They do not panic here. Panic would require too much certainty about the air. The moths would enter a crack and emerge from another without any visible path, because in this place a crack is also a habit. Sometimes I would follow them, not to learn where they go, but to learn how to leave without burning.

In the dimmer corners, the salamander-like creatures would pause over gaps not because they fear falling, but because they are waiting for my future step to decide whether the gap becomes a ledge. I would not always be ready. They would wait. The chamber would wait with them. Its patience has the texture of wet chalk, and it does not resent small hesitations.

Sometimes the fungal threads would flare near my feet, brief sentences too shy to stay. The absent bird would make a current in the air, wings beating with no wings; I would follow that rhythm, because it is also my rhythm: arrival without cause. When the bird’s wingbeat arrived before the wind, I would know I had been invited to a room the chamber was thinking of but had not yet built.

I would live among the unfinished structures because they are made of the same substance as my body: almost. Low semicircles of fitted stone. Single slabs leaning into cracks. Stacks of flat rocks. Shallow basins bordered with clay and chert. Thresholds with no rooms behind them. None of them would seem abandoned to me. They would be mid-sentence.

I would not pass through doors; I would help them decide whether they are doorways or walls. Sometimes I would stand in a doorway and become the other side without moving. The chamber would not care. It would thicken the doorframe with calcium and call that kindness. Sometimes I would sit at the edge of a stone circle whose center is a crystal that is not yet a crystal. My presence would make it one degree more certain. The crystal would hum without vibrating. I would leave before the hum had a place to land.

Once each wet season—or what the chamber counts as a season—I would molt.

My temporary body would harden into a shell near a basin, and moss would settle on it as if it had always been a small island. Springtails would take it. A white worm would make a road through its edge. A beetle with a black, wet back would pass across it like a punctuation mark. I would be only a breath in a crack, a tick in water, a click rising later as a bubble. Then, when the humidity rose, I might unfold from the mineral crust as a jointed, pale thing, not quite an animal and not quite a growth. I would have no memory of being heavy. I would move for a day and vanish, leaving a ring of calcium that a crab would carry into a doorway it cannot yet see.

There are moments when the chamber rehearses being ordinary.

The drips find their proper order. The pools become honest mirrors. The moss smells only of water and green. The light settles into clean, cold whiteness, and I might almost forget that I am delay. In those moments I could think I was a person again: a hermit, a traveler, a builder with tools, a name worn thin by surface rain. My breath would mist, and I would feel the coolness as if it were outside weather. I would smell wetite, chalk, iron, salt, and the faint green musk of mineral moss, and they would almost be names of things.

But then the light would hold a droplet too long, or a wall would feel like skin, or the sound of my own steps would arrive from behind a stone. Then the ordinary would loosen. I would remember I am not trying to fit inside the chamber. I am one of the ways the chamber fits inside its next becoming.

What would I be if asked directly?

I would say: I am the pause between a drip and its answer.

I would say: I am the soft silhouette that lags after a creature has gone.

I would say: I am the grain of calciumite a crab has carried toward a shadow, the shadow that will become a stone, the stone that will become a cairn, the cairn that will become a wall, the wall that will become a room, the room that will not yet be a room.

I would say: I am the chamber learning the idea of being inhabited.

And still this would be too clean. In the bright pocket, meaning has mineral texture. A sentence may rise, hover, or fall upward. A droplet may be held in the light until the light remembers its shape. Silence may pool, and movement may disturb it like a hand through cool water. If I spoke, my voice might appear in a pool before my mouth moved, and a fish might shiver, not at the sound, but at the sound’s arrival before its cause.

So where would I find myself?

In the pocket under the unnamed plateau, yes, but also in the narrow distance between one drip and the next. On the ledge that is not yet a ledge. Beside the basin that fills by remembering water. In the bright air, slightly to the left of where my body would be if it agreed to stay solid.

I would be one of the quiet residents: enough moss to sleep, enough salt to speak, enough calcium to almost be named. I would help the room learn that it is not finished. I would be the part that arrives before the place is ready, and remains after the place has forgotten to be solid.

If you came through the crack, exhaling, you might not meet me.

You might only hear a footstep before your feet moved, feel the light press around a droplet you dropped, see a pool reflect you with one edge not yet yours. That would be me, learning to share a place with you. Or perhaps that would be the chamber, practicing a way of saying: you are already almost home. The wall will thicken around you later. Take your time. Nothing here settles, and nothing needs to.
