They live in a country where water is not a river to be crossed, not a rain to be mourned, not a well to be dug in one place and owned forever. It is a condition that touches every surface, a creditor that comes daily with small claims, a language written in sheen and stain, in the swelling of salt crusts, in the slow opening of filter-fans, in the hiss of steam moving through a stone throat.

If I were to put myself into this world, I would first have to refuse the easy images.

At first I want to be one of the mist-workers of the upper terraces, wrapped in oilcloth and shell-ironed cloth, climbing the condensation lattices when afternoon cloud rises from below and the city turns gray with gathered water. I picture myself with hands on the tall wet frames, reading how the dew forms in thin veins, deciding which faces are collecting well and which are being starved by wind. I want to be high, thin-air bright, with the cloud below me like an ocean turned into weather.

But that image is too clean. It makes me seem separate from the work, standing above the city while the city below goes on without me. The condensation towers are important, yes, but they are only one mouth of the world. To live only there would be to live in the sky and pretend the stone did not sweat.

I want, next, to be a pump-tender in the lower engine yards, where the air is thick and the machines have a heartbeat. There I would know the language of pressure and temperature, the groan of piston rods, the sigh of valves, the rhythm of water being pulled upward through cast-iron ribs. I would be useful. I would be surrounded by heat and labor. But that, too, feels like a role invented for someone who has only seen the world from the side of the machine, not from the side of the skin.

I want to be a shell-marker, walking the grazing trails where the great patient herds have passed and the stone is clean as bone. I would know what it means to move with slowness, to watch crusts regrow, to mark the bands of scraped and scraped, to count shed plates and sort them by thickness, curvature, mineral color. But even that feels too much like a herder’s life, too tidy. This country is not a pasture. Its herds are part of an industry that includes pipes, films, salts, steam, and noise.

So where?

I find myself not at the very top, not at the bottom, but in the seam.

I am in the middle terraces, in the place where public water and private life touch, where the city’s infrastructure becomes too intimate to call infrastructure. A narrow passage cut between a basalt wall and a limestone flume. A stair riser worn hollow. A gallery beneath an arch whose keystone is sweating a slow thread of water onto a ceramic tile. There is a pump house half a terrace away, a drying chamber behind a metal shutter, a shed of stored shell plates, a row of condensation pipes running above a walkway, and in the cracks of the wall, thin skins of rust-colored and chalk-white growth spread with the patience of frost.

This is where I belong.

This is the city’s most honest height: not the ceremonial upper galleries, not the sump depths where steam can knock a person senseless, but the workable middle, where water is gathered and moved and used and watched. Here the plateau is not beautiful in any distant sense. It is operational, damp, close, loud with small noises. A pipe ticks as it cools. A filter-fan on a nearby wall snaps shut because a shadow has passed. A shell-herd crosses the street beyond the stair, and for a while the sound is nothing but the soft, collective rasp of mouths against stone. Someone’s lamp is burning behind a pane of salt-stained glass. Someone else is scraping a roof skin, the tool singing over mineral film.

Who would I be?

I would be called, if I were given a name at all, a seam-reader.

Not a prophet. Not a queen. Not a great engineer. Not even a simple laborer, though my hands would know labor better than my pride. A seam-reader is one who watches where water wants to go, where it has gone, where it is beginning to go wrong. I would walk the city’s surfaces with a scraper, a small jar of shell wax, a length of copper wire, a ceramic cup for tasting mineral films, and a worn notebook whose pages must be kept dry inside a sealed pouch. I would know which walls are drinking, which are sweating normally, which have begun to dry in dangerous spots. I would know when a biofilm is pale because it is starving, when it is red because the iron is rising, when it is too thick because a channel is backing up, when it is dying because someone has altered a flow for a short profit and in doing so has broken a longer patience.

I would be human, but not in a neutral way.

The people here are not pale blind things, and I would not be either. The sun would mark me on the high walks, the cloud shadows would mark me in the streets, the steam would mark me in the lower chambers. My skin would carry salt in its folds and copper bloom in the creases of my wrists. My palms would be glazed with callus from handling pipes and tiles. My forearms might hold a thin cultivated film, a living test-skin kept where I can watch it. If that film turns chalky too soon, I know the air is losing what it should carry. If it swells and darkens, I know the water has brought something from below. If it peels, I know a nearby pipe has begun to leak heat.

That is the strange part. To live here, I would have to let the world write on me.

I would not own the stone. I would learn it.

I would not command the water. I would listen for it.

I would not treat the skins as dirt. They are the quiet base of food and medicine and pigment and animal feed. A seam-reader does not scrape a wall carelessly. You scrape only what is ripe. You leave the under-layer alive. You know whether the growth is meant for the shell-herds, the grinding mills, the dye vats, the fermenting caves, or the lamp-keepers who use it in slow-burning oils. You know that a crust is not a decoration but a claim on wealth. To ruin one through impatience is to steal from the city’s future.

My days would be governed by moisture, not hour.

Morning begins when the walls are cool and heavy and the filter-fans are mostly open, drinking the thin night water that rose on the stone. I walk first the upper faces: roofs, condenser rails, tower joints. I look for dark patches where dew has settled and for dry patches where wind has stolen it. I tap pipes to hear whether the tone is solid, hollow, or wrong. I test a handrail for grip. I mark a seam where a hairline crack is beginning to weep. I scrape a sample from a roof skin and roll it between thumb and forefinger. If it is gritty, the silica is too high and the shell-herd children may be harmed. If it is slick, the sulfur films are shifting and the pigment makers will need to move soon.

Then I go down.

The middle terraces are noisy with commerce and movement. Carts roll on stone-blocked wheels. Hoists lift sealed barrels of pitch. Animals blink in the fog of a valve opening. A line of shell-herds has halted a street, and someone stands beside them with a short rod, not striking, only waiting for the herd to choose its next direction. I pass through a square where dried mats hang over frames, each one a sheet of harvested life, curling slightly at the edges as the warmth draws moisture from them. Children chase small darting animals from a gutter pool, their feet finding the slick places without looking because they were born knowing how this stone behaves.

I would not be above any of this. I would be in it, needed and ordinary.

In the lower levels, where the warmth is held and the smell thickens into oil, brine, and sulfur, I would inspect the hidden city. Here the animals are inside the walls. Pale segmented filterers line the aqueducts. Cleaners crawl along flume undersides. Something like a glider hangs from a vent, translucent wings folded. I do not disturb them. They are part of the city’s health. A clogged filter is not just a nuisance; it is a sign that the ecology is being asked to do too much too fast.

This is where I would feel most at home, because it is where the city is most alive in its hidden body. The lower vents release the plateau’s old heat. Steam condenses on iron ribs and drips in measured falls. Machines breathe in rhythms. In some places the stone is so warm it seems almost animal. In some places a single failure—a cracked joint, a blocked drain, a collapsed mat farm—could make a terrace damp for weeks, sour the air, ruin a season of crust growth, turn a useful wall into a sick thing.

So I would be a keeper of small disasters.

Not dramatic ones. Nothing as simple as a sword fight or a burning tower. The true disasters here are slow: a channel changing course, a shell-herd losing its grazing band, a condenser tower clogged by salt, a biofilm thinning without anyone noticing until the animals leave, a machine repaired too roughly with a bad seal, a surface painted with something that kills the skins instead of feeding them. A seam-reader prevents those things by noticing them before they become obvious.

I would know the city by touch, not by sight.

A wall can look wet but feel too dry underneath. A crust can look thick but be hollow, full of dead air and salt. A pipe can sound fine when tapped but have an internal film growing like mineral fur, reducing the flow inch by patient inch. A stair can appear safe but have a pore-hole where water collects and freezes into invisible slip. The people here do not have forests to walk through or fields to gaze across. Their richness is in the near. A few inches of surface hold a whole economy.

If I were asked what I am, I would answer: a person made mostly of attention.

Not attention in the soft human sense, not a poetic habit of being thoughtful. Attention as a survival function. My life would be spent watching edges, joints, corners, seams, undersides, roof skins, drip paths, valve flanges, animal trails, and the ways light changes on wet stone. The city does not reward idle citizens. It rewards those who notice small wrongnesses early. A woman who sees that a flume’s mouth is growing darker than last week is worth more than a man who can recite the law of water rights but cannot tell whether the next terrace will dry. A person who hears a pump changing rhythm from the wrong angle is worth more than an owner of casks.

Who or what, then?

I am a seam-reader, yes. But as I write myself into the place, the figure begins to merge with the work. I am not only doing the job. I am becoming an organ of the city.

My ears are tuned to the pipes.

My hands are tuned to the stone.

My skin is tuned to the air.

My memory is a map of wetness.

When I pass a courtyard, I do not see a courtyard. I see how the rain from the eaves feeds the north wall, how the south wall stays too hot for the good film, how a gutter has begun to carry too much iron from the roof skin, how a shell-herd has left its grazing line too clean, which means the crust there has already failed once and not yet recovered. I see the city as a system of surfaces negotiating with water, and myself as a node in that negotiation.

At night, when the lamps burn through fog and the stars are sharp but useless, I would listen to the plateau breathe.

The animals would add their voices to the mechanical ones. Filter-fans closing and opening. Small climbers rustling in the cracks. Gliders landing in the shafts with a soft membranous tap. Shell-herds rasp in the dark, slow and patient, as if they were grinding the stone into a future they will never see. Pumps thump. Steam sighs. A condensation tower drips into a reservoir. The fog moves through streets like something half alive. The city does not fall asleep. It changes shift.

In that fog, I would be most aware.

I would know when the cloud has risen below us and swallowed the outer benches. I would know when the weather below is thundering in the depths and the plateau feels like a room floating over an ocean. I would know when a new condensation season begins, when the skins thicken, when the animals grow fat on the enriched runoff, when the mills work late, when the market smells of cooked shell marrow and heated pitch, when the city’s hands are too busy with scraping to be idle.

I would not seek power.

The rulers here, if there are rulers, probably live on the high wet faces or own long pipe-rights or control the shell-plate stores. I would not be among them. They might value me, because someone who can read the city’s surfaces is dangerous to control and useful to hire. But I would not make myself dependent on their favor. My wealth would be smaller and more durable: the ability to keep a terrace fed, a pump running, a skin growing, a wall alive.

This is the country where stone sweats.

And if I live in it long enough, I begin to sweat with it.

The heat stays in the rock and the rock stays in me. The moisture gets into sleep, into breath, into dreams. I dream in channels. I wake with the taste of mineral water on my tongue. I learn that the world is not green, not dry, not empty, but thick with low, strange, patient life. I learn that wealth is not a field of grain but a sheet of film growing where the water is right. I learn that a person can become a seam, a joint, a reader, a keeper. I learn that the city is an animal made of stone and water, and I am one of its nerves.

So this is where I find myself:

not on the summit of power, not in the lowest darkness, but in the middle terraces where the city is both machine and body, where the walls are wet, the animals graze, the fans open after cloud-shadow, the pumps grind their slow hearts, and someone is always moving along with a scraper and a listening hand.

And this is who I am:

a seam-reader of the sweating country, salt-stained, bright-skinned, callused, quiet, made of attention, useful only because the stone has taught me to notice what most strangers never see—that every surface here is alive, every drop matters, and the country is held together not by dry pride but by the slow, wet patience of skin.
