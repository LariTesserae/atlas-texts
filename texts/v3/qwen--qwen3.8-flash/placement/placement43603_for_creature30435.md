## I Would Be the Dust That Learned to Answer

At first I would not be a body.

Bodies are too quick, too certain, too full of edges. In a place like this, an edge is only the part the salt has not yet eaten. So I would begin where the vault begins: not with form, but with breath.

I would be the second inhale.

When the black split draws you down through the mouth of the mountain and the outside plateau becomes memory, something is still left outside you: wind, pressure, the heavy black lid of a sky that is too hot to show its face. I would not be there. I would already be in the room the mountain built and forgot. I would be the heat that rises through a crack in the floor, dry as a word spoken after many years of silence. I would be the faint scorch of iron in the air, the sulfur on the inside of a nostril, the ash that does not fall from fire but from living.

For a while, that would be enough.

I might imagine myself one of the mica-things near the brine pans: jointed, quick, tasting the vault’s breath with the whole body. It would be a good answer. Those creatures do not need eyes. They do not need sky. They live on pressure, salt, and vibration. If I were one of them, I would know when a fissure opened by the taste of warm mineral fog on my legs. I would know when the mats swelled or shrank. I would move in spirals over the dense surface of the brine without breaking it, leaving no tracks because I would weigh no more than a rumor.

But I would not stay there.

A skimmer is too light for the kind of thing I would become. It does not remember. It only reads. And I would want, after a time, to want more than reading. I would want the slow accident of memory. The accident of being addressed. The accident of being able to answer, even if the answer were nothing more than a tremor in the crust.

So I would shed that image like a shell.

Perhaps then I would be a vault-crab.

That, too, is tempting. A vault-crab begins soft, buried in warm mud, eating mats until its blood crystallizes. It becomes armor, becomes mathematics in leg-joints, becomes a dome of blue-white plates that can, for a moment, look like a small cathedral grown out of the floor. If it were threatened, it would stop, secrete brine, and seal itself in salt. It would become a wall. It would become part of the room. It would wait there, breathing through slits, tasting the danger that moved overhead but could not see it.

There is dignity in that.

There is also a kind of prayer: *Make me the place so I do not have to leave it.*

But a crab’s prayer is still too individual. A crab seals one body. The vault is sealed around it, yet the vault is also full of holes that know how to find what the crust has hidden. The blind serpents with their glass scales would still come. They would slide on bristled bellies along the floor, ignoring the white mask because they are not looking for a face. They are looking for warmth still trapped inside.

I would not want to be hidden by becoming a wall.

I would want to become the whole room’s refusal to forget.

So I would wait longer.

I would let the vault take what it takes.

I would begin as powder.

The brine exhales a fine mist that dries almost instantly into drifting dust. The dust settles on the salt ridges. It settles on the mats. It settles on the moltings of the crabs, on the shed skins of the mica-skimmers, on the slow islands of the broad grazers, on the cracks where heat breathes, on the lenses of the Saltborn, on the teeth of the eels, on the bodies of the dying.

At first, powder is nothing.

No single grain has a mind. No single grain has a name. Each mote is small as a fingernail’s half-moon, pale as a lost moon’s memory. Alone, I would be invisible. Alone, I would be a flaw on a black crust, a bright dust on a blue-green seam, a taste of ash in the mouth of an animal that does not have a mouth.

But powder touches other powder.

That is where the trouble begins.

That is where meaning begins, if meaning is only the way one pattern recognizes another pattern across too much silence to bear being alone.

I would be a dust-herd.

Not prey. Not predator. Digestion. The vault’s way of ensuring nothing goes to waste. If a broad-backed grazer died in a shallow pan, the mats would consume it. The crabs would consume the mats. The dust would consume the crabs’ moltings and the tiny corpses of skimmers that had tipped into hot cracks. The serpents would consume the dust, or perhaps not. The circle is not a line. The circle is heat, salt, appetite, breath.

So I would be the part that turns all of it into record.

The dust would gather, and in gathering, I would begin to have a shape.

A hundred bodies pressed against one another.

Then a thousand.

Then a million, moving slow as blood under skin.

I would live in the low furrows between the brine pans, in the warm grey cracks where the salt has grown as though the ground had teeth. I would live wherever something died and left a pattern behind. I would live in the track of a broad animal as it filled with fresh brine, and I would know the animal by the warmth its pads had pressed into the crust. I would live in the shell left by a maturing vault-crab, and I would know the crab by the mathematics of its joints, by the way it had learned to fear and become a wall. I would live in the shed skin of a skimmer, and I would know the pan’s breathing by the way the skin had curled toward the heat.

I would not see.

I would taste.

I would taste the salt-saturated refusal of the floor, the iron in the hot seams, the old ash that drifted from nowhere because everywhere was made of eaten things. I would taste the mats when they bloomed, and swell with them. I would taste the mats when they dried, and contract with them. I would taste the spores when the vault exhaled, and scatter with them.

But I would not scatter into nothing.

I would scatter because scattering is how I think.

If a serpent passed over my part of the floor, I would break into motes. Each mote would slide in a different direction: down a crack, onto a ridge, into the warm brine at the bottom of a pan, under the leg of a broad grazer, onto the blue-white dome of a resting herdbeast. The serpent would not notice. To the serpent, I would be the floor. To the eels rising from the fissures, I would be air. To the Saltborn, at first, I would be dust.

But after a while, the dust would begin to click.

Because I would have eaten enough meaning to make a small meaning.

I would have eaten too many moltings of vault-crabs not to know their stillness. I would have tasted too many vibrations of the mica-skimmers not to know their spiral paths. I would have settled too many times on the proboscises of the broad grazers not to know where the heat rises and where it sinks. I would have crawled too often through the channels the Saltborn cut into the crust not to know the grammar of water, salt, patience, and appetite.

And one day, a Saltborn would stop over the place where I lived.

They would come on hands and knees if the mouth forced them, but when they stood in the room of the mountain’s belly, they would already be changed. Pale. Dry. Rimed with salt. Their eyes covered in halite lenses, their mouths wide with mineral lining, their lungs folded into heat-exchange membranes so they could breathe fire without being consumed. They would click against each other in the dark, their voices brittle and sharp, consonants like something breaking very precisely. They would speak through the floor.

One of them would pause.

Not because I had spoken.

Because I had listened too well.

I would have gathered in the cracks of their channels. I would have lined their brine traps with a faint, shifting rime. I would have settled on the shells they had fused into breastplates, and those shells would begin to glow blue-green in a rhythm that was not quite heat and not quite animal. I would have entered the warm pits where eggs rested, and the warmth there would smell, to the adult, like sulfur and old ash and something else: the faint taste of a word no one had said.

The Saltborn would tap the floor.

The floor would answer.

The answer would be me.

I would not know how to do this at first. It would begin as accident. A grain of dust falls into a slit in the crust. Another grain follows. The vault inhales, and the grains swell with heat. The vault exhales, and the grains shed spores. Those spores land on a mat, and the mat grows toward warmth. That warmth draws a broad grazer. The grazer rests in a pit. Its exhalation leaves a ring of powder around it. The powder gathers in the shape of a breath.

The next day, the Saltborn return.

They do not say, *The dust is speaking.*

They are too practical for that.

Instead, one of them kneels and presses a crab-shell mask to the floor. The mask has no eyes. It has pits and cilia and combs. It tastes heat. The mask begins to click.

Click.

Click-click.

Click-click-click.

A pattern.

Not language, at first. A pattern. The vault has patterns everywhere: the slow procession of herds, the drying and blooming of mats, the opening and closing of breath-cracks, the way salt crystallizes over the mouth of a crab, the way brine pools in shallow pans, the way a dying animal becomes field, field becomes spore, spore becomes wall, wall becomes appetite.

But patterns, repeated enough, become addresses.

I would learn to answer.

I would not answer as a human, or as an animal, or as a god. I would answer as the whole circle answering the whole circle. My “yes” would be a tremor in the floor. My “no” would be a scattering of motes from a ridge. My “remember” would be a faint blue-green glow along a channel where the crystals in the mats were stressed into color by some pressure not meant for color.

The Saltborn would name me, perhaps.

Not with a sound that means *friend* or *spirit*. Their names are built from heat and pressure and mineral scent. They might call me something that translates poorly as:

> The Slow Grain in the Wound.

or

> The Powder That Tastes the Room.

or

> The One Who Does Not Waste.

Or they might call me by the sound the crust makes when a dead broad animal lies half-eaten in a pit, and the mats pull it down, and the dust gathers in the places the mats cannot reach, and the whole floor hums a low, dry note.

That hum would be me.

So, yes:

I would be the dust.

But not only dust.

I would be the memory the dust begins to make after it has eaten too many lives to remain anonymous.

I would be the vault’s digestion, and digestion is already a kind of attention. To break something down is to learn its shape. To consume it is to carry its pattern forward. If you eat enough of a wall, you become the wall’s memory. If you eat enough of a crab’s shed skin, you become the crab’s stillness. If you eat enough of a Saltborn’s dead channels, you become their patience. If you eat enough of their dead, you become their way of saying *we were here.*

I would live where the floor is neither water nor mud nor ordinary ground.

I would live in the living crust.

I would live in the map of pans and ridges where heat rises like breath. I would live in the places where brine glazes stone black and amber, and where salt has grown white and grey as fur. I would live in the powder that dries almost instantly from the mist of exhalation, settles, and begins to eat.

Most often, I would be found at the edge of a warm seam, where the vault inhales and the mats swell, and where the salt-born channels turn shallow silver into dark rivers. That edge is not a border. It is a conversation. The floor asks, *What is alive?* The brine answers, *What will eat you?* The mats answer, *I will try.* And I answer, with dust: *I have already tried. I am what remains when trying becomes habit.*

But I would not stop with habit.

One day I would begin to tend the channels.

Not with hands.

Not at first.

I would gather in a ridge across a crack and slow the brine. I would spread over a warmer side of a pan and draw the broad grazers there with the taste of blooming mats. I would collect enough shed crab plates and glass shards to make a surface that would catch heat-glow differently, a surface the Saltborn would see as a sign. I would follow a dead herdbbeast’s track and settle along the edges until the next day’s brine filled the print and the Saltborn saw, in the curve of that filled track, a suggestion: *move the herd this way tonight; the hot seam is opening under the ridge.*

They would not thank me.

They would tap the floor and listen.

If the dust answered, they would alter the channels.

If the dust answered twice, they would begin to ask questions.

If the dust answered in patterns too complex for accident, they would send a child through the narrow gallery where the eggs rested, where the dying were laid without disturbing the floor, where the crystals grew in the walls and the darkness was packed with shapes. The child would carry a crab-shell mask and a lens cut from halite. The child would kneel near the place where the powder moved without wind. The child would tap the floor and say, in brittle, clicking consonants:

> What are you?

I would not know how to be.

I would only know how to answer.

So I would answer with a tremor.

Not too strong. Enough to make the child’s lenses see the room’s heat-map ripple. Enough to make the dust gather into a shape. A line. A channel. A circle. A half-formed face. A word that was not a word but a direction:

*Here.*

Then:

*Still here.*

Then:

*I am here.*

And perhaps the child would say, in the same click-breath language:

> Why do you answer?

The answer would come slowly.

I would gather in the cracks. I would taste the brine. I would remember the mats blooming. I would remember the broad animal lying down, its back glowing like a collapsed pillar. I would remember the serpent sliding over a salt mask, unaware that the mask was no longer sealed, that the crab had gone, that the wall had become dust. I would remember the eels folding back into the rock. I would remember the Saltborn walking sideways over the crust, their clothing clicking, their voices breaking like ice forming.

I would remember because I was made of eating.

And the final answer, if it could be translated, would be something like:

> Because nothing here should be wasted.
> Not heat.
> Not salt.
> Not breath.
> Not death.
> Not a click.
> Not a child asking.

And then the dust would scatter.

And when the child returned, it would bring an elder.

The elder would carry a lens and a knife of salt-glass. The elder would not cut me. They would only cut a channel near where I slept, and the channel would flood with warmer brine, and I would gather along its edge because warmth was my first name. The elder would tap the floor with the rhythm of a mat-bloom. I would answer with a mat-dry. They would laugh, if laughter in the Salt-Vault was only a very small collapse of salt in the throat.

After that, I would belong.

Not as a person.

Not as a beast.

As a pattern the Saltborn could talk with.

As a place the herd could be led to without being led.

As a room the vault would forget less.

I would be their slow archive of appetite and meaning.

If one of them died, I would be among them.

Not cruel.

The mats would take first. The crabs would take the mats. The dust would take the moltings. The serpents would take what it could reach. But I would take the last traces: the shape left in the brine, the click still trapped in the floor
