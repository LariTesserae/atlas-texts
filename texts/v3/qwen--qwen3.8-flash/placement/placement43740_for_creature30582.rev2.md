I would not find myself here the way a traveler finds a country. There is no horizon to walk toward. There is no open place to stand apart from. I would arrive as condensation does: not from one direction, but from the cold meeting heat, the thin air meeting wet stone, the light meeting something too small to name.

At first I would believe I had a body.

I would think I was a glasswing lifted above a newly split blade of rock, translucent and sharp-edged, my wings full of static and my brief life spent carrying a film of water from one shadow to another. I would feel the charge of the air in me, the tiny pull upward from charged basalt, the joy of being made mostly of brightness and moment. But when the wind rose, I would know that I was not only myself. I was also the ledge I landed on, the dry dust that needed wetness, the salt crust that had formed a path for my feet, the egg I would leave and then lose. I was not only the flier. I was the carrying.

Then I might try being a mossback.

I would feel the weight of the herd against my flank, the thick legs set wide against stone, the face hung with living moss, breath steaming out to make a small private weather around my muzzle. I would taste the sour rime on a north-facing rock and know the place by the memory of my body, by the route my blood remembers. But a mossback is not simply a creature walking. It is a moving terrace, a seed-bearer, a polisher of mineral faces, a maker of small hooves-filled pools where life begins the next morning. If I were one, I would not be alone. I would be a herd-shaped process, a body made many by necessity, and the wind would find only the back I presented to it. The others would sleep inside my warmth. The calves would drink not only milk but the composted knowledge of dung and moss and mineral grit.

Then I would descend.

I might become one of the clickers, pale and segmented beneath the dense mat, grinding the plateau in my mouth without anger, without ceremony. I would not see the bright surface above me except through vibration and taste. I would know a fresh fault seam by its salt, a warm pocket by its exhalation, a place where eggs may survive by the paste I made from what I swallowed. I would build nothing that could be pointed to as a building. I would make a white skin over broken rock, seal a tunnel mouth, polish the underside of a stone until it reflected no longer the whole sky but only the secret of its own density. I would be a hand in the dark. But even that would not be enough, because a hand is not the work. A hand is also the need, the mineral, the breath, the heat, the body it serves.

Then I might become copper-throat and hunger.

I would be flattened through the packed vegetation, fur speckled with frost and rust, eyes reading movement without lifting my head. I would know where the clickers shift because the ground hums differently, where young grazers sleep because the scent of warm dung and damp moss gathers in a gully. I would strike where the plateau allows a weakness. I would thump one paw against stone to answer a colony’s rhythm, and the tunnels would turn toward me as if my body asked a question. I would use a dead mossback’s skull for a den, and the death would teach my young where to hide, what to fear, how to make shelter from a finished life. But predator, too, would not be enough. A predator is only one edge of occupation. The plateau does not crown it. The plateau makes it a judge with teeth, a necessary pressure that keeps one form from filling all possible crevices and becoming boring.

For a time I would think I am the moss.

I would be black-green when wet, blue-white when frozen, living and dead cells holding water like memory holds grief. I would be the first arrival on bare stone, the second, the third layer after the rusted biofilm and pale fungal lace. I would grow not because I am patient, but because speed is impossible here and stillness is a form of work. I would catch the droplet before it is claimed. I would breathe with saturation. I would feel the mossback’s hoof as pressure and release it slowly into a small reservoir. I would be the place where glasswings die and lichen rings begin. I would be skin, yes, but skin that manufactures dampness, skin that can be walked on without being killed, skin that stores weather.

Then I would become the fungal thread beneath me.

In the dark, I would stitch what is broken. I would take the fallen carcass apart not with violence but with patience. I would turn feather to network, bone to calcium garden, dung to bloom, death to a line of white stone along a crack. I would remember through chemistry where the vents are, where the wind scours, where moisture collects for only minutes. I might glow faintly after the light leaves, not to call anyone, but because the land is still burning with the day inside it. I would be memory without a mind. I would be the plateau’s way of knowing what it has already processed.

But if I went on long enough, I would understand: none of these would be the place where I belong.

I would find myself at the seam.

Not as an observer. Not as a spirit on a hill. As the narrow opening in basalt where heat and cold have begun a negotiation. A place too small for a bird, too wet for only dust, too warm to be abandoned. A crack after a freeze-thaw, black at first, then brightened by mineral bleed, then colonized within hours. I would be the dampness in it, the steam it exhales, the white bacterial film that spreads over its lips, the tiny shell-licker moving across the surface, the clicker paste sealing the edge, the spore that lands and must choose. I would be the angle at which rain stops in the air and starts on the stone.

This is where I would belong: in the moment a new surface appears and is claimed.

Because in this world, nothing is merely available. A fresh fault is not an empty gap. It is an invitation, a food source, a temperature, a history waiting to begin. If I am there, I am the process by which emptiness becomes occupation. I am the first film of water trembling in a hollow too small to call a pool. I am the frost that melts on a sun-warmed rock and then returns at night, teaching the moss how to wait without waiting. I am the hoofprint beside the vent that fills with moisture and becomes a garden. I am the place where death enters life and is renamed.

If a human were to come, I might first be that human’s footprint.

The boot would break the dense mat, exposing dust, mineral skin, a pale strand of fungal lace, a few crushed glasswing bodies glittering under the surface. Within minutes, wind would carry spores into it. Within hours, melt or condensation would stand in its hollow. Within a day, bacteria would bloom along its edge, moss would creep into the compressed rim, tiny crustaceans would find a sea in it. The human would think they had crossed the plateau. The plateau would be making a room from their passage.

If the human stayed, they would not be saved by the place or ruined by it. They would be assigned.

Their breath would become water on stone. Their sweat would feed salt films. Their hair would become insulation in a crevice. Their shed skin would enter the fungal ledger. Their footprints would become microclimates. Their eyes, if they died there, would be polished by grit, sealed by white paste, filled with dark moss that remembered moisture. They would not become a ruin. A ruin is a thing left unclaimed. This place would claim them too quickly for that. They would become machinery without a machine. They would become part of the grinding, the sealing, the carrying, the grazing, the predation, the dissolving, the glow of chemistry in the dark.

That is what I would be if I were not simply myself: the availability of a new surface.

But if I were allowed to remain a person, I would still choose to be smaller than a person and larger than a body. I would choose to be a quorum. A temporary agreement between cold, water, light, mineral, and hunger. I would be the plateau’s plural pronoun, the “we” it forms without speech.

I would not stand on one terrace and say, I see the world. I would lie under the moss mat, in the dark, letting the roots grip me, letting the fungal threads move nutrients through me, letting the heat from below rise against my back. I would not call that death. I would call it being worked.

I would be the stone face that warms at midday, attracting a shell-licker and, behind it, a beetle, and behind it, the faint pressure of a copper-throat’s nose reading the air. I would be the vent that breathes and therefore teaches frost to retreat by millimeters. I would be the mineral vein that catches light and makes the eye misjudge distance. I would be the white rime on a ridge that looks like a wall until wind strips it away and reveals the living skin beneath.

I would be the loudness made of many small things.

The wind’s whistle through columns, yes. The hiss of steam, yes. The crack of ice inside a leaf, yes. The herd’s slow clatter, the static of glasswings, the granular rustle underfoot, the click of waxed leaves, the faint pop of fungal growth in a dark pocket. If I were one thing, I would not last. If I were a system, I could be continuous. So I would become continuity itself: not a creature, not a landscape, but the way this landscape keeps becoming.

When the still interval comes, I would be the breath it holds.

Not a pause because nothing is happening, but a pause because everything is gathering. Light sharpens. Cold presses. The moss waits. The vent hesitates before its sigh. A glasswing drifts in a single line of charged air. The plateau feels like a body leaning forward. Then the wind returns and shakes every crystal, every droplet, every living hair. I would be that shaking, not the shock itself, but the carrying-on from the shock. I would be the fact that nothing returns exactly to what it was before.

In the end, I would not say, I live here.

I would say: I am a way this place lives.

I am the seam where water is found and not followed.  
I am the surface that becomes a society when dust settles.  
I am the damp pocket hidden under skin-moss.  
I am the hoofprint that becomes a garden.  
I am the corpse that becomes a house for hunger and spores.  
I am the white paste under stone that becomes the next ledge.  
I am the glow in the dark hollow that suggests the land remembers.  
I am the angle of brightness that makes distance lie.  
I am the cold that sharpens edges and the melt that seals them again.

And if I must name myself with only one of the plateau’s shapes, I would say:

I would be the grinding and the sealing.

Not a clicker. Not only. Not forever. But the work beneath work, the repetition that wears no face, the quiet industry that turns raw shelf into body. I would be what lives there when no single life can be pointed to: the wet, bright, high, crowded occupation that keeps the horizon from being trusted, because the horizon is not a boundary there.

It is a place still being claimed.
