If I searched for myself in that country, I would keep turning away from the obvious places.

Not the shell-herds, slow as weather, crossing the terraces with their mouths full of living stone.  
Not the condensation towers, black and rough and weeping into the city’s cupped hands.  
Not the great pump halls, where iron groans in its wet housings and the pressure changes like a sleep-turn.  
Not the high ledges where the ridge-singers carry crust-spores between their pale, mist-trapping hairs.  
Not even the human roofs, though I would envy them: the roofs are fed, scraped, protected, judged by the color of their skins.

I would stop somewhere lower, somewhere ordinary, where the city is not performing itself for a visitor but simply holding itself together.

I would be on the east wall of a hospital gallery, in the old stone district beneath the second condensation line. Not a grand wall. A wall with a ceramic gutter above it, a steam pipe behind it, a salt niche near it, and a shallow channel at its base where the runoff hesitates before entering the drain. The wall would be limestone, pitted and worn into smooth hollows where many hands had passed. In the late afternoon, when clouds rose from below the plateau and the air thickened, moisture would collect on it in a fine sheen. The lamps in the hallway would make the water look like oil. The stone would look warm from the inside out.

I would not be a person there.

At first, perhaps, I would seem like one: a maintenance-tender with oil-stained hair, a condensation hood over her shoulders, shell buttons on her sleeves, a curved ceramic knife at her belt. She would walk slowly, as people do there, because haste breaks surfaces. She would touch the wall with the flat of her palm before she spoke. If I were her, I would learn to read myself the way the city reads its children: through color, temperature, the speed of my drying, the smell of the water I carried.

But after a while, the human would fade into the role, and what remained would not be a person at all.

I would be the wall’s living skin.

A mother stain.

Not one being, but a slow congress: chalky fungal analogues, rust-eating films, silica-threaded colonies, pale bacterial sheets, salt-precipitating membranes, dark mineral laces, yellow-white blooms near the pipe’s warmth, blue-gray mats where the flow grew lazy. I would be the thing that looked like dirt until a child put her hand over me and said, “You are alive today.”

I would grow where the stone stayed wet but not drowned, where the heat from below softened me without blistering me, where the light was filtered through mist rather than striking raw. I would require the city’s particular attention: not beauty, but balance. Too much water and I would slough off, souring into black scum. Too little and I would crack, flaking into powder that would leave the wall naked to the air. If the runoff carried too much sulfur, I would blush yellow and warn the tending crews. If copper leached into the channel, I would turn blue-green, and someone would press their tongue near my edge—carefully, always carefully—and frown.

I would be known by several names.

The children might call me *the Weeping Film*.

The old skin-tenders would call me *Chalk Saint*, because I would be pale and cool and good for healing galleries.

The workers in the lower cisterns, who liked dense iron-rich growths, would call me *Rust Mother*, though I would only have become more iron-red in the places where the warm water kissed me most.

I would not mind which name I received. Names there are not ownership. They are a way of saying: *this surface speaks, and we have learned its voice*.

I would be protected, too. Not because I am beautiful—no one would mistake me for a clean white wall in a lowland city—but because I belong to a hospital. A mature skin older than ten years in a public gallery could not be scraped without a maintenance warrant. If I dried, the district wardens would consider it an ill omen, not because of superstition alone but because dryness meant a channel had failed, or a seal had cracked, or someone had made a shortcut that cost the city a living surface. If a skin near a healing room went septic, the family responsible for that section would be fined, not for me, but for the whole web I held: the filters, the runoff, the small cleaners under the flanges, the gliders in the shaft above, the shell-herds that might one day rest in the basin beneath me and spread their dung to feed the next season’s growth.

That is what I would be: a node.

A citizen, though not a person.

A piece of the country’s outer flesh.

In the morning, before the clouds rose and the lamps went dim, the first thing to reach me would be the sound of pumps changing pitch. I would feel the vibration through the stone, not as hearing but as moisture moving faster along my lower edge. The hospital channel would carry warm, faintly mineral water from the lower cisterns. A nurse or a pipe-listener would pass with a ceramic cup at her belt, taste the air, tap a valve with a bone-handled key, adjust it by a quarter turn. I would grow one degree less bitter.

The ridge-singers would begin calling from the tower ledges before most people woke. Their voices would be long and wavering, like water finding its way through porous rock. They would not nest on me—too protected, too tended—but they might land briefly on the gutter above, scattering tiny fragments of crust and spore-dust into the runoff. I would catch some of them. Not all. The city’s skins are spread this way: by wind, by water, by the feet of gliders, by the oil on a worker’s glove, by the hair of a child who has pressed a palm to one wall and another and not yet washed.

The shell-herds would not come inside the hospital gallery, but their basins would be near, in the lower courts, and their rasp would be a constant soft sound beneath the street: stone scraping stone, mouth grinding film. Sometimes, when the city’s water shifted, the smell of their brine would rise through the grates and settle on my upper edge. It would make me grow slightly thicker, slower, more supple. The surface-readers would notice. They would say, “The galleries are in herd mood,” and adjust the salt troughs outside.

The filter-fans above me, on the outside wall where the mist first entered, would open and close all day. Their plates would click when they shut, a dry fan-market noise. When they opened, they would catch mineral dust, microbial haze, tiny flakes of dead crust, and pass some of it downward into the channel that fed me. I would not see them. I would taste them. I would know that the city’s breath was moving properly if my color remained steady, neither too pale nor too streaked with bad black scum.

In the middle of the day, when the sun was white and hard but not hot enough to burn the plateau’s heat into the stone, a maintenance child might come to me with a small brass key and a cloth soaked in filtered shell-water. She would be learning. She would touch my center, my lower corner, the place near the pipe joint, then press her ear to the wall to hear the water running behind it. If my moisture was good, she would not disturb me beyond cleaning away a patch of stagnant slime that had begun to form where the runoff slowed. If I were drying, she would report it to her keeper, not with alarm, but with the practical tone one uses to say, *the roof is hungry*.

Her keeper would come later with a waxed pouch and a small clay vial of beneficial microbes. He might scrape only a narrow path where I had grown too thick, where I had begun to trap dead sludge and invite swarm-hunters. He would smear a starter culture along the clean edge, tucking it into the pores of the stone. This would not be my beginning. It would be my repair. In that world, to be tended is not to be controlled; it is to be kept in the correct relation to what must continue.

At night, when the lamps burned steady in their thick glass chimneys, I would cool slightly in the shadows, but the wall behind me would still be warm from the day, and the pipes would still breathe their long, slow heat. A vent watcher might pass below the floor, oil-slicked hide wrapped around his shoulders, his face half hidden by a ceramic plate. His boots would not scuff too hard. His breathing would be slow. He would know by the color of my steam-fed bloom whether the heat behind the wall was rising too fast or sinking away. If it rose too fast, he might ease a valve. If it sank, he might report a failing exchanger. I would give him no more than a change in temperature and a faint smell of hot iron, but in that country that is speech enough.

If a patient died in the hospital gallery, they would carry them afterward to the salt niches or the seep chambers, depending on family and custom. Some would go to public cistern filters, some to family walls, some to memorial panels polished by descendants. If a relative had chosen me—if my wall were old enough, and the wardens had granted permission—then some of the dead would become part of my growth. Not a soul, perhaps, but salts, traces, minerals, the chemistry of a remembered body. The child who later pressed a palm to me might ask, as all children there do, “What does the wall want?”

I would answer not in words, but in condition.

If I were dry, the child would open a channel.  
If I were slick with bad slime, she would clean me.  
If I were warm and soft beneath her palm, she would say, *You are remembered*, and move on.

I would be, then, a place where human and stone stop pretending to be separate.

I would not be a person with a single fate. I would be a process that people depend on. I would be the thin skin of the plateau’s metabolism. I would be the difference between a wall that is merely wet and a wall that is alive. I would be what happens when a city learns not to demand growth from green fields and roots, but to accept that life can be mineral, film-like, slow, communal, and tender.

If I had to be one thing among the beings there, I would be that.

Not the loudest animal.  
Not the tallest tower.  
Not the largest herd.  
Not the most heroic engine.

I would be the surface between surfaces: the living crust on the hospital wall, kept from drying by the care of children and keepers, fed by moving water, warned by color, healed by scraping and replanting, threatened by neglect, honored by maintenance.

I would be the reason a wall can be said to sweat.

And at evening, when the clouds rose beneath the plateau and the city went quiet in that particular way—no silence, never silence, but a lowering of volume, as if all the water in all the pipes began listening together—the ridge-singers would call from the towers, the gliders would release from shaft ceilings, the filter-fans would snap open after the last dry wind, and the shell-herds would move into their basins with their backs shining like wet ceramic.

A child would come to the gallery door with a cup of filtered water and a handful of salt. She would touch me with her palm, frown slightly at my dry edge, then open the small valve above with two turns. The channel would murmur. The water would spread over my face in a thin, living film.

And I would answer, as every wall there answers:

with temperature,  
with dampness,  
with a slow brightening of rust and chalk,  
with the smell of warm stone returning to its proper skin,  
with the city’s quiet, necessary persistence.
