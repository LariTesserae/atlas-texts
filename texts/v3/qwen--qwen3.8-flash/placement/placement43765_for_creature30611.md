The Seed-Crown is buried under a shelf of pale, chipped tile, a thin crust of molted husks, and the dust that the grove mistakes for soil. Around it, the making is not over but merely slow. A digging stick, polished by long rubbing until its wood has taken on the patience of the place, leans against the shelf’s edge as if it were the shelf’s first thought. Beside it lies an adze with a cord-wrapped handle, the cord worn into a groove so deep and smooth that it seems to have grown there. A cracked hammerhead sits in a niche, and from its missing fragment a hair-thin braid of mineral fiber runs into the mouth of a pot set open beneath a ceramic reed. The pot is no longer a pot. A trunk has grown around its rim. The Seed-Crown rests where that trunk begins to think it has always been stone.

This is where I would find myself.

Not at the rim, where the low walls might still admit the rumor of another world. Not at the center, where density becomes so complete that a person might mistake the place for a held breath. I would be at a seam: under a shelf, beside a door-shaped crack that opens into nothing but dust, near a pot that has become a hollow trunk, beneath a loom suspended between two stalks so that the loom has been accepted as a bone, a rib, an organ. My scale would be small enough to hide in the grove, and slow enough to appear part of it. The light coming through the overhead grid of hand-formed stone beams would be warm gray-white, ordinary, not holy. It would fall on me as it falls on everything: without emphasis, without explanation, without mercy.

If one asked, “Who are you?” at first, I would answer only by fitting differently into the place.

I would be a Seed-Crown: a small cone of nested clay husks, each one open to its own inner hollow, each one cupping the next so tightly that heat moves through them like a chord moving through a wooden pipe. I would not have a single edge. I would have many possible edges, all waiting to become a body. If a Grainwright crossed my outermost rim, it would not be crossing me. It would be crossing one of my future shoulders. If a shell-scaled lizard flattened itself beside my shadow, it would not be hiding from me. It would be waiting, as I would wait, for the warmth to reach the correct angle.

A Seed-Crown is not born wetly. It is not soft. It is an intention in stacked clay.

This would be my first form: not a creature, but a pattern of creatures-in-process. I would be aware in the way made things become aware when they have been placed correctly in a world whose logic is repetition and fit. I would know heat as pressure. I would know sound as a slight rearrangement of dust. I would know a passing Potback by the faint, architectural click of its feet and the dry brushing of moss across its back. I would know the changing light by the angle at which the slats open a thin bright lane across the floor. I would not see, not at first. Seeing would come later, and only because the grove had learned the habit of looking at the place where I was.

And because I would be a Seed-Crown, I would also sometimes be the thing that made me.

Before I was covered, before I was buried under the shelf, before the dust accepted me as ground, a Mender-Cloud would form near where I would become. It might begin as no one at all: a cluster of Grainwrights moving through a dry thicket, then gathering moths folded like clay sheets, then seed-husks, then fragments of molted skin, then bits of painted tile, then threads of braided fiber. These things would come together so exactly that for a little while they would resemble a hand. Not a human hand. Perhaps a claw. Perhaps a loop of loom-shuttle. Perhaps the negative space between fingers, made momentarily present by dust and attention.

That would be the scale at which I would not yet be individual. I would be the temporary body of a verb.

The Mender-Cloud would approach the shelf where the broken hammerhead sat, where the loom had become an organ, where the pot had become a trunk. It would gather the Seed-Crown from its nested cups and carry it, not because it had arms, but because the grain of the grove required transport and had arranged a dozen small movements into one apparent movement. It would lower me beneath the shelf. It would set my smallest opening toward the angle where warmth pools. It would cover me with molted husks, tile-chips, dust, and one rounded pellet that a Potback had left behind and the floor had already learned to accept as new grout. Then it would leave a thumbprint in still-warm dust, and then it would scatter.

No one would have seen the moment of division.

That is why there are no builders walking these paths. The builders are not hiding. They have become a habit so old that it looks like growth. The Mender-Cloud is their brief visible form: not a person, not a god, not an animal, but the grove’s way of continuing through pattern. When it presses a shard into a crack, it is not repairing. It is remembering. When it lays a Seed-Crown under a shelf, it is not burying a future. It is placing a future where the temperature is right for it to begin.

So who would I be, if I followed the prompt through its scales?

At the smallest scale, I would be a Grainwright’s pause.

A rice-sized beetle with a low-fired shell painted oxblood and dull green would crawl along a tile joint and stop beside a hairline fracture. Its antennae, thin wires of braided hair, would test the air for mineral pollen, for warmth, for the faint creak of fiber stretching. It would carry dust into the seam. It would bite a frayed edge from an old stalk and leave behind a tiny rounded pellet. This would not look like making. It would look like maintenance. But maintenance, here, is the world’s way of breathing. If I were that Grainwright, I would not be lonely because my solitude would be a single note in a chord too slow for wet animals to hear.

At a brief, impossible scale, I would be a Mender-Cloud.

I would be many bodies pretending, for a little while, to be one hand. I would be a cluster of beetle-legs, moth-scale, seed-beads, tile-dust, molted husks, and braided fiber. I would not think in words. I would think in fit. A fraying leaf-edge would be a wrongness. A shelf without moss would be a question. A pot left mouth-up in the ground would be an invitation. I would weave a strand through the crack. I would press a shard into place. I would leave a thumbprint. Then I would cease to be a single apparent creature and become a scattering of reasons. If someone asked what had been there, I would answer: something like a hand, something like a pattern, something like the place remembering that it had once been made and therefore still could be.

At the scale of becoming, I would be a Seed-Crown.

I would be nested clay cups waiting for enough heat to agree with one another. I would be the outline of a long neck before the neck exists, the future ridge of a shelf-back before the first moss grows there. I would be the mouth that will scrape pollen, the brush-tongue not yet braided, the broad rimmed feet not yet clicking against tile. I would be a parliament of husks, a quiet congress of future bones. I would not be born. I would assemble. Slowly, in the dry warm dark under the shelf, the grove would add what I needed because I had been placed correctly, and placement is the first kind of prayer here.

At the scale of patience, I would be a Potback.

Not immediately. Not in the way wet animals are immediately. I would emerge over seasons, over the opening of a single flower that had waited years, over the slow migration of moss mats across a fallen tile. When the time came, my first movement would not be a birth-cry. It would be a settling click, like a tile finding its socket. One husk would become a foot. Another would become a leg. A third, curved and fired thin, would become the rim of a neck. My young body would be dry at first, sharp-edged, not yet softened by accumulated dust.
