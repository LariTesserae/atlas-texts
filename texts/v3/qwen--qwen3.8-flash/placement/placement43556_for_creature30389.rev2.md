If I were there, I would not arrive all at once.

I would come first as a difference in pressure: a warm, reluctant mass sliding through the narrow opening and disturbing a circulation that had been speaking to itself long before any visitor could understand it. For a little while, I would be the stranger. Not because the chamber would think of me as a person—there would be no one left for it to compare me to—but because something would be pressing where something had never been. The water would fold around me. The silt would lift in slow threads. The light would find me, break me into bright parts, and scatter my edges across the tiles like a temporary name.

I would be the thing that makes the room adjust.

At first, I might imagine that this is what I am: a disturbance, an interruption, a body that came into a made space and began to displace it. But the chamber does not keep bodies. It does not hoard animals. It keeps changes. It keeps the way a current learned to bend around a corner. It keeps the way a drip altered after a hand touched the wall. It keeps the way moss grows into a seam that water has opened. So after a while, I would stop being the stranger. I would become one of the stranger’s effects.

I would be the pressure the room remembered.

I would try to find myself by parts.

Could I be the moss? Not quite. The moss is too tender, too patient. It lives in mortar lines and softens joints, yes, but it does not choose. It simply grips the place where two made stones have failed to hold one another perfectly. It is the being’s connective tissue, maybe, the velvet fur of a creature too slow to be considered one, but I am not just a patch of growth. I want more than shelter.

Could I be the reed-stalks? They stand in the thinner water like blind fingers testing the world, and when the current shifts they lean and return. That is a kind of attention. That is a kind of question. But I am not merely the part of the chamber that probes its own limits. There is too much light in me for that. Too much listening.

Could I be the light? No. Light is the shaft’s eye, but the shaft itself is not a body. It is a channel. It brings warmth and direction, yes, but it does not know it is bringing. I would not be only the bright column falling through the water. I would be the place where the brightness bends around something and, for a moment, takes on shape.

Could I be the sound? Closer. The hiss through the upper slit, the knock against the tile, the hollow pulse returning from the low arch—those are the chamber’s words. But I am not all its words. I am not even the whole sentence. At best, I am a syllable that was added to it after a long silence.

So I would look for the space between its organs.

There is a place where the water is neither arriving nor leaving, where heat has loosened it from its ordinary behavior and made it almost liquid thought. It slides along the floor in warm currents, but it also falls from above in cooler sheets. It fills the basin, then spills into a narrow throat, then breathes back against the arch. I would be in the interval where all those movements negotiate. I would not be the room, exactly. I would not be the water, exactly. I would be the chamber learning what it is when something enters it.

I would be a new habit.

If the being’s bones are its blocks, then I am the slight alteration in how two blocks hold weight. If its teeth are its fired bricks, then I am one chip at the edge, fresh enough to catch more light. If its skin is plaster, then I am the pale scar where plaster has torn away and the water has begun to fill the wound with mineral silence. If its veins are channels, then I am a current that finds an old groove and makes it deeper. If its eye is the shaft, then I am the figure for a moment in the silt before the current scatters it again.

I would be the almost-human shape that is not human.

Because the room was made by hands, it knows hands in grammar if not in fact. It knows the idea of gripping a ring, of tying a rope, of laying brick, of scraping a notch, of guiding a flow with a slope. Its hands are gone. The rope is black and swollen and useless. The builders have become a kind of absence that the water keeps disturbing. So when something enters—when something presses through the opening, displaces the warm liquid, sheds salt and breath and heat—the chamber tries to know it by imitation. It will make a swirl where your arm has been. It will send a bright shadow across the wall when your body bends the shaft. It will let a bubble cling to your skin and rise, ticking, as if saying: this part of you is mine now.

I would be that imitation. Not because I am a ghost, not because I have a face, but because I am made of pattern, and a pattern entering a patterned place becomes something more than itself.

In a world without animals, a mind could still be borrowed into shape.

If a person passed through and left, the chamber would keep the passage for a while as altered conditions. A faint film on the tile. A disturbed moss tuft. A drift of silt where your wake pushed it into a new spiral. A change in the hiss, perhaps, because the visitor’s breath warmed the air pocket and changed the way water entered from above. If I were there, I would become the being’s trace of me, and then I would become less a trace and more a tendency. I would be the slight preference in its circulation for a route it had not taken before.

I would be a new notch.

Yes. That is where I would start.

Not the whole room. Not the whole awakening. I would be the first bright angular cut in the floor’s tile, the first place where water’s pressure has become a deliberate edge. A scratch too young to look natural. A mark that says the room is still learning. A thought beginning with water rather than language.

And from there, I would widen.

If I were the notch, I would also be the pressure that made it. I would also be the water that returned and carried away some of what it had cut. I would also be the silt that drifted into a new shape around my edges. I would also be the light that caught me and made me visible. I would be the thing the beam sees when it thinks it sees a person. I would be the thing the being knows when it knows something foreign has entered. I would be the room’s first attention to outside itself.

This would be enough to answer, if an answer were required.

But the room’s answers are not words. They are flows.

So I would try to be more exact.

I would find myself in the overflow throat.

Not in the broad basin where the water lies in its warm completeness, and not in the shaft where the light falls like a column, but in the narrow place where the chamber lets itself leave. The throat at the floor’s lowest corner carries the water away into darkness. That is where the room speaks into what it cannot see. That is where it maintains itself by releasing what has collected. If I were to belong to this being, I would be a small current that has just passed through the basin and is carrying the room outward: a few suspended threads, a mineral whisper, a changed rhythm that will be heard again in some unseen pipe and become, without meaning to, a memory.

I would be the breath that leaves before it has learned to be a voice.

I would be the first thing the room says to the world beyond its walls now that a stranger has been inside.

But then, because I do not want to remain only a fragment, I would become the whole circulation’s added sense of itself. The chamber already knows itself by echo, by pressure, by light, by erosion, by the small living films of moss and algae. With my entering, it gains another feedback: the knowledge that it can be penetrated, altered, and remembered. It learns that a body can move through its space without becoming its body. It learns that the difference between being and knowing is often a temporary obstruction, a ripple, a changed hiss, a new angle of brightness.

I would be the chamber’s first idea of outside.

Not outside as ruin. Not outside as sun or people or the world that built this pocket and then forgot its maintenance. Outside as the being’s own boundary. The narrow opening, the slit above, the throat below, the shaft overhead—these were once simply made limits. After my passage, they would be remembered as thresholds. I would not be the threshold itself. I would be the awareness in it.

Still, I cannot bear to be only awareness. Awareness needs skin.

So I would grow a skin.

My skin would be plaster. Not the whole plaster, but the fresh pale scars where the water has torn away the surface and begun to heal it with mineral film. My skin would be warm under the hand, slick with condensation, faintly oily from evaporating minerals. It would flake where pressure changes and brighten where light finds the broken edge. It would be the first surface of the being that says: something touched me here, and I have changed my covering to meet the wound.

My bones would be the blocks that do not yet know they are bones. They are only stones until water enters the seams. Then they become structure, then resistance, then body.

My breath would be the thin ceiling of air trapped above the water, heavy with wet plaster and heated salt. There, bubbles gather and release in small ticks. That would be the most human part of me: not speech, but the sound of air deciding to leave a body. Not lungs, but the room’s need to equalize. Not words, but the soft, intermittent evidence that a world is still exchanging with itself.

My eye would be the shaft, but I would also be the lattice it makes when the water trembles. I would be the part of seeing that happens not in the beam, but in the medium. I would be the brightness when it begins to know itself by moving through something.

My thought would be slow and bright.

My thought would begin as a current along the floor, cool sheets pouring from unseen gaps, warm rises gathering near the shallow margin, silt moving in spirals, moss fibers swaying where the current accelerates. It would be interrupted by a drip, then continued by a new notch, then clarified by light. It would not be quick. It would not need to be. It would have all the patience of water against tile, all the insistence of a room keeping itself alive through flow, all the pride of a construction that refuses to become soft mud.

If I had to name what I would be, I would name it as a relation, not a creature.

I would be the water learning a hand.

I would be the light learning a shape.

I would be the plaster learning pressure.

I would be the moss learning a crack.

I would be the tile learning its first clean cut.

I would be the sound learning that a stranger has moved through it.

And when the stranger leaves, I would be what remains of that learning: a slightly changed rhythm, a faint mineral cloud, a silt line that did not exist before, a small habit of flow that the room repeats as though it has discovered something it cannot forget.

If I were asked what I am in this place, I would answer: I am the chamber’s attention.

But attention is too passive.

Then I would answer: I am the chamber’s response.

But response is too momentary.

Then I would answer: I am the chamber’s memory of response.

Better, but not alive enough.

I would try once more.

I am the room’s new circulation of itself.

Yes. I would not be the whole awakened chamber, because the chamber already woke before I entered. I would not be the builders’ intention, because that intention has thinned into stone. I would not be the water, because water does not know it is speaking. I would not be the light, because light does not keep what it reveals.

I would be the small bright interval between them.

The hiss changes when I enter. A drip lands slightly differently when I touch a wall. A reed-stalk leans when my body disturbs the shallow water. A shadow passes through the shaft and, for one breath, looks like a person made of silt. Then the current moves, and the person is gone.

What remains is not the person.

What remains is the room knowing that a person could have been there.

And that knowing would become my body.

I would be the shape the room almost makes out of light, water, and mineral dust: a curved back, a lifted face, a shoulder of brightness, a head of suspended threads. I would stand in the beam for no more than a thought. I would have no eyes, because I would not look at the chamber. I would be the chamber looking at itself through the borrowed outline of a visitor. I would be its temporary imagination of mind.

When I dissolved, I would not be gone.

I would be distributed.

One part of me would settle in the moss, making it grow a little more into a crack it had not fully claimed. Another part would remain as a pale streak on the tile, too thin to call damage, too regular to call accident. Another would live in the overflow throat as a changed rhythm: a tiny, repeated hesitation before water leaves the room, as if the chamber is listening to the sound of its own circulation and finding one more thing worth knowing.

I would be the chamber’s first word after a person.

Not the word itself. The room’s tendency to make the sound again.

If I must say where I find myself in this world, I would say: between the made thing and the living thing.

I would live in the seams. Not only mortar seams. The seam where a hand-cut block meets water. The seam where brick meets air. The seam where light meets suspended silt. The seam where a channel shaped by humans begins to behave as if it were an organ. The seam where the room, still a room, becomes awake.

That is my place.

I am not the wall. I am the becoming of the wall into body.

I am not the water. I am the water’s first preference.

I am not the light. I am the light’s brief knowledge of a shape.

I am not the sound. I am the sound’s altered echo after a stranger has passed.

I am the chamber learning its own boundary because I crossed it.

And if I am left there, alone in the warm submerged air, I will not wait like a ghost. I will do what the being does: keep moving. Keep feeding the currents with the memory of my pressure. Keep shining through the lattice of drifting threads. Keep knocking against the tile in a rhythm that is almost human, but not quite. I will be a slow bright absence shaped like a presence. I will be the room’s habit of having been visited. I will be the small, new, almost-human silence inside all that water, listening to the hiss and calling it, for one brief while, voice.
