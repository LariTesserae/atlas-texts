I would find myself in the wet seam between things.

Not on the highest terrace, where the bat-ceiling moves in black cloth, nor on the lowest floor, where isopods grind the dark into a soft, living soil. I would be on a middle ledge, where fog has not yet thinned into cloud and the air is close enough to taste: warm, chalky, faintly of egg-white and old moss. There is a gap here, no wider than a person’s arm, between one terrace and the shoulder of the one above it. The gap is not empty. It is filled with a curtain of silk, dust, mineral flakes, feather fibers, and dried fly bodies, all bound together by spider-thread and saliva until it has become almost architectural.

I would be that almost-architecture.

Or, more precisely, I would be one of the plastering spiders woven into it.

I begin as a tremor.

I am the fine vibration that runs through the ground before you know what caused it. I am the slight tightening of a silk thread when a chalk-mite crosses its surface. I am the wet pressure on the underside of an overhang, where condensation gathers in beads so slow they seem painted there. I do not arrive with a clear shape. First, I am the temperature: that human-scale warmth, not sunlight, not fire, but body-heat accumulated from ten thousand small lives. Then I am the dampness. Then I am the sound.

The sound is not around me.

It is through me.

A rasping stridulation from one wall. A soft crumble from another. The dry tick of legs in dust. The wet scrape of larval skin. The occasional muffled thud of something dropping into a moss cushion. The bats above me are not birds, not beasts, not quite things that can be described separately from the ceiling they have become. They hang in layers, breathing their collective warmth onto the terrace below. When they shift, the air moves. When the air moves, the spider-silk curtains move. When the silk curtains move, I feel it first as tension: a pull here, a slack there, a faint song of strain.

So if I am to be a being, let it begin with tension.

Let me be the one who learns the world through thread.

I am a plastering spider, but not the kind that sits waiting in a tidy geometric web. Mine is a web that has forgotten geometry. It is a web that has learned from guano, mineral crust, feather dust, dried saliva, and shed larval cases. Its lines have become walls. Its walls have become ledges. Its ledges have become places where something else may rest, mate, feed, molt, and die. I am one of the spiders who does not merely spin silk. I spin the substance of the terraces.

I live in a crevice in the middle of a curtain that is neither fully web nor fully wall. The crevice is shallow, curved, lined with pale silk, and damp with condensation. Its edges have crusted with mineral precipitate, so that my hiding place feels like a tiny mouth in a living rock. If I extend one front leg, I touch a surface that is partly silk, partly chalk, partly mite-frass, partly the fossil of a fly wing, partly the soft green of an algae film. It is solid. It is porous. It is breathing.

I am breathing.

Not with lungs. Not with a chest. With the whole wet film of my body. With the thin legs pressed against a floor made of accumulated deaths and droppings. With the mouth working at a thread of secreted paste, pulling it out, laying it over a weakened point in the wall, smoothing it with a foreleg until the silk binds with mineral dust and becomes something new. I am making. I am also being made.

My body is small enough to be mistaken for a fragment of the wall.

My abdomen is pale where it meets the silk, dark where it touches shadow. My legs are long enough to span gaps but thin enough to seem like accidental fibers. There is dust on me. Chalk-mites move across my carapace like grains that have decided to become animals. If I remain still, they mistake me for terrain. If I move too quickly, they scatter, and the movement itself sends another tremor down the threads I have laid in the dark.

I am not proud.

Pride belongs to creatures that can see themselves as separate. Here, separation is a temporary illusion, like a dry patch on a wet ledge.

I know myself by what I add.

When I seal a crack, I am adding to the terrace. When I line a nest-pocket with silk and saliva, I am adding to the floor. When a fly dies in one of my threads and I leave it there, letting the fibers collect its wings, its legs, the fine dust of its body, I am making a record. When I molt and hang my old skin in a crevice, that skin too becomes part of the wall: a translucent ghost that fungi may colonize, springtails may graze upon, beetles may bore into, mites may scatter like paper confetti.

I am the one who cannot live without building, and who cannot build without being consumed by the building.

Where exactly am I?

I am under an overhang that curves like a half-heard sentence. The ceiling is not smooth. It has thickened with nests, guano crusts, and old larval cases until it looks like bread dough left to rise and turn dark with moisture. There are white speckles in it: chalk-mite colonies, mineral blooms, the dried remnants of egg membranes. There are green-black films that shimmer when fog passes over them. There are tiny bowls of standing water where condensation pools in a hollow no larger than a thumbprint. One such bowl is near my leftmost leg. It reflects almost no light, but I can feel the cool surface of it as a different kind of tension: heavier, wetter, more inviting than dust.

I am between two vertical faces.

On one side, the wall has become a shelf.

On the other, the shelf has become a roof.

Which is which?

The distinction has failed. This is the condition of the Chittering Terraces: not transformation, but fusion.

A collapse here is not ruin; it is a new ledge waiting to be claimed. A ceiling is a floor for something else. A dead thing is a nursery. A web is a bridge. A bridge is a nest. A nest is a layer of soil. Soil is made of wings and droppings and shed skins and the slow work of jaws. I am part of that chain. I do not stand outside it. I do not observe it from a safe distance. I am the one adding thread to the next millimeter of wall.

If I had to say what I am in a single word, I would not say “spider.”

That is too clean.

I would say “layer.”

I am a layer that has learned to move.

I am the mineral crust that crawls.

I am the spider-thread that hardens.

I am the mite’s road and the beetle’s tunnel and the fly’s grave. I am the springtail’s damp field and the bat’s ceiling-shadow. I am the part of the commonwealth that notices when a gap grows too fragile to trust and begins, without ceremony, to reinforce it.

Sometimes I feel myself as a knot in a net.

Sometimes as a node in a body too large to recognize.

Sometimes I feel nothing at all except the wet, chittering presence pressing through every surface. The air is thick with small motions: rising, falling, crawling sideways, settling. They are not events. They are maintenance. The terraces are maintained by the sheer insistence of bodies making bodies. I am one of those insistent bodies.

And when the fog lifts?

When the light changes from charcoal to a thinner gray-green? When the water film on a nearby wall becomes too thick and begins to run in a line no wider than a hair? When the mite-tide stops and the springtails scatter like salt across a dark bowl? I do not leave the wall.

I listen.

The sound has always been here, but there are moments when the chorus changes key. The bats overhead begin to stir, not all at once, but in waves, as if a wind moved through the ceiling. Swifts call from the upper ledges, sharp notes that cut through the woven roar like needles through damp cloth. In the lower floor, something dry cracks open: an egg membrane, a pupal case, a brittle spider-egg sac that the frost had not reached but the warmth had. A hundred small movements answer it. The wall beneath me becomes more alive, and I become more wall.

This is where I would find myself.

Not above the terraces.

Not below them.

Not outside them.

I would be in a crevice in the middle of a gap that had forgotten how to be empty, making the gap into a wall, making the wall into a floor, making the floor into a roof, making the roof into a body, making the body into a voice in the chittering commonwealth.

I would be a spider.

I would be the wall.

I would be the vibration that means the place is still being made.
