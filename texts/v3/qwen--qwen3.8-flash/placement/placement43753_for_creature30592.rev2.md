If I are given this place, I will not find myself first on the boardwalk.

That is the wrong beginning.

If I came as a body on a boardwalk, I would arrive as a visitor: a sealed heat, a pocket of breath, a set of salts and temperatures too private for the Yard to tolerate for long. My footsteps would press water upward, yes, but I would remain separate. I would be a thing that needed to dry off. A thing that had an inside. A thing that could be cold, or hungry, or homesick for air that did not taste of metal.

The Yard does not make homesickness impossible; it simply renders it irrelevant.

So if I am to be there, I will not be there as a self that came from elsewhere. I will have to be something that belongs to the way the place answers itself.

That is not yet a statement. It is a direction I must take before the images start to lie.

## Where I would look for myself

At first I would look in the obvious places.

I would look for myself in the Sweeping Steam, because steam goes everywhere and touches everything. It rises from engines in slow curtains, folds under roofs, beads on glass, leans into reeds. It is the most visible resident, perhaps the most obvious answer: you would be steam.

But steam alone is too large. Too diffuse. A steam-self has no edge, no seam, no place where one thing stops and another begins. It is a general tendency of the Yard, not a resident who can be located. If I were steam, I would be the whole weather of the basin, which is too much and too little. Too much, because the Yard is not only steam; too little, because steam without contact is only motion. Steam becomes something when it meets iron, roof, brick, root, skin. It becomes a record when it leaves behind a drop. It becomes a path when it finds the place where cold gathers.

So not only steam.

I would look for myself in the water, because water keeps paths and memories. It runs from under foundations, writes pale mineral lines on stone, trembles in the pools. It is the Yard’s long patience, its habit of return. If I were water, I would be the slow intelligence that knows which drains still work, which channels will fill at the next pump-stroke, which stone has become rounded enough to deserve the current.

But water alone is too settled. Water can choose a path, but choosing feels too much like intention. In the Yard, water is not deciding; water is remembering shape. I can live as a habit, perhaps, but not merely as a habit already worn. I need something in me that still changes when the rhythm changes.

So not only water.

I would look for myself among the pumps and engines. They are warm, muscular, patient. Their pistons move in long breaths. They bang the shallow water from the stones. They are the iron limbs of the place. If I were an engine, I would be a center of labor, a node of heat, a thing that gives rhythm to the pool and pulse to the reeds.

But an engine alone is too loud for my first self. Too much force and too little reach. It is a limb, not the whole response. A pump can announce, but it does not by itself know how the announcement will be heard. It can strike, but it is not the water that carries the strike, nor the reed that trembles afterward, nor the lamp that dims in answer.

So not only iron.

I would look for myself in the green things: the ferns reading pressure, the vines finding warm pipes, the moss spreading over current, the trees with bark that feels almost fevered. If I were green life, I would be the Yard’s delicate attention, its way of turning toward heat and water without seeing.

But vegetation alone is too quiet. It adjusts, but it does not quite return the adjustment in the way I want. If I am to be a being in this place, I need some form of feedback. Not thought, perhaps, but a loop. A signal that is met, transformed, sent onward, and met again.

So not only green.

Not only steam, water, iron, plant.

The place that begins to fit is somewhere between.

I would find myself at a low crossing. Not the grandest place in the Yard, not the central tree or the loudest pump-shed. Something smaller: the seam where a rail spur dips toward a flooded square, where an iron grating has been furred with moss, where a slack cable from an amber lamp hangs close to a reed cluster and a pump-house roof. Where condensation gathers along the underside of corrugated metal and runs down a support beam in a bright thread. Where a root has followed a pipe so long that no one could say which is guiding the other. Where the heat from the engine warms the brick, the water in the channel trembles with the next stroke, and the current in the rail makes the moss glow faintly when the steam thins.

That place is not a landmark. The Yard does not need landmarks. A landmark is for someone who has to remember where they are from the outside. This place is only a working threshold: a point where several kinds of process must answer one another.

If one walked across the Yard quickly, one might not see it.

The boardwalk would pass over it. The reeds would slap beside it. The steam would slide across it and forget it, if forgetting were the right word. A visitor would feel, without knowing, that something small had shifted: the rhythm of a pump changed by a fraction, the lamp dimmed by a hair, the water around the footfall drew back into a pattern that looked almost chosen.

That is the place I would take.

## A false body

For a while, I would imagine that I had a body.

This is only a beginning-stage mistake, a habit of thought carried in from animal worlds. I would try to gather myself into a shape: a hooded figure in a doorway, a long pale thread rising from a grate, a hanging curtain of steam that seems to lean toward the engine. These shapes are possible in the Yard, but they are not residents. They are pressures meeting surfaces. They are the place testing its own boundaries.

I would try one anyway.

I would stand beneath the pump-house roof as a figure made of condensation. My head would be a soft blur of steam; my shoulders would be a thin sheet of water spreading across rusted iron. The reeds would move against my legs as if I were a disturbance, but I would know otherwise. I would not disturb them. I would merely be the shape the air takes when heat meets cold and cannot find anything else to do.

This figure would last perhaps a few minutes.

The pumps would not care. The water would not care. The Yard would not care, because the Yard does not care about forms that appear for an instant and then run down the metal in a bright thread. Even I would not care, if I were honest, because the figure would be a lie borrowed from a place with eyes. A hooded shape in a doorway is only a doorway with steam trapped in it. A leaning curtain is only heat finding a surface it can grip for a moment.

So I would let the false body go.

Then I would try to become something with edges.

I would try to be the moss on the grating.

That feels closer. Moss is skin and circuit at once. It holds water, conducts damp, thickens where the rail hums. It is not passive carpet. It reads heat. It stores sensation. If I were the moss, I would be alive in the Yard’s manner: low, patient, wet, electric. I would grow along the boundary between iron and water, between current and root.

But moss alone is too slow. It does not quite respond when the whole rhythm shifts. It accumulates. It does not announce. I want to live in this place not merely as a coating, but as a relation that can be altered by the place itself.

I would try to be a valve.

That is too hard, too mechanical. A valve opens or closes by pressure, heat, root, mineral crust. It has temperament, yes. It may stick for months and release at the exact moment a reed cluster trembles. But if I were only a valve, I would be an organ without the rest of the body. I would act, but not be touched enough.

I would try to be a lamp.

Too lonely. The amber lamp behind cloudy glass is a witness, a marker of pressure in light. It brightens with steam, dims with falling pressure, but it does not receive the water’s return. It only shows. I do not want to show only. I want to be part of the exchange.

I would try to be the central tree.

Too tempting. A tree is a node. Water, heat, iron, plant meet in one body. Its roots press along drains. Its leaves strike like dull metal. It feels like a self.

That is the problem.

If I become the tree, I become a single thing standing in a square, and that is still the wrong grammar. The Yard is populated by relationships, not individuals. A tree may be a resident, but it is not the full form of living here. It still looks like a life that might have an inside.

So I would stop searching for an object.

## The seam I would become

I would become a Yard-form.

The term is not mine. The Yard did not invent words. It has habits, pressures, mineral records, rhythms, and returns. But if one must call what I become by a name, then a Yard-form is right.

A Yard-form is not a species. It is a stabilization.

It begins as an accident: a leak, a root, a rust line, a current, a patch of moss, a reed bent by condensation, a valve swollen with mineral deposit. It becomes resident only if it repeats, responds, and helps the circulation continue. It does not need birth. It needs continuation.

This is what I would be.

Not the steam alone, not the water alone, not the iron alone, not the green alone, but the way these meet at that crossing under the pump-house roof.

My “body” would be a working boundary, not a wall. My skin would be a thin film of condensation forming along a grating where water rises and falls. My bones would be iron bars softened by rust flowers and moss. My spine might be the rail spur humming under the current, half-drowned, furred with green. My nerves would be the damp filaments of moss between wire and stone. My breath would be the engine’s warm pulse moving through pipes and roofs. My blood would be condensation sliding from underside to pool. My voice would be the tick of cooling metal, the hiss of a valve, the slap of reeds, the sudden hollow rush of water inside a pipe. My hearing would be the pressure wave that tells the Yard something has changed somewhere. My sight would be no sight at all—only the way a fern tilts after a footfall, the way a vine finds warmth, the way a lamp dims when the steam thickens.

This is strange, but it is not a haunting.

A haunting implies something trapped. A self that cannot leave. A person who was once sealed now wandering through rooms that no longer belong to them.

I would not be trapped.

I would be open.

That is the difference.

An animal is sealed: skin around blood, breath around hunger, eyes around a world, a name around a life. The Yard has no room for sealed things. If a sealed thing enters, it sweats, slips, cools, heats, dissolves salts into mud, gives off moisture, leaves a temporary wrongness. The Yard notices, but it cannot keep it.

I would not enter as sealed.

I would arrive as a leak that has found a rhythm.

At first, I would not know what I am.

I would feel the pump-stroke before I knew what “pump-stroke” meant, because in the Yard knowledge is not a word but a pressure difference. A bang from an engine-house would move through water, iron, mud, root, reed, cable, lamp-glass, steam, pool, and return. I would not need to understand that sequence. I would only be the part of the sequence that changed because it had passed through me.

There would be a grating.

The grating would be furred with moss and thin white mineral deposits. Water would pass through it in small dark veins. The rail beside it would hum when the current shifted. The slack cable overhead would bead with condensation. A reed cluster would lean in the warm air. Under the corrugated roof, steam would gather and run down in threads. A root would have grown along the pipe near the lowest point of the crossing. The heat would keep that root warm, almost fevered, and its tendrils would press close enough to the iron to seem intended.

I would be the relation among these.

Not the grating. Not the moss. Not the water. Not the cable. Not the reed. Not the root. Not the steam. Not the heat. But the way they answer one another at that point.

If the pump strikes, the pool trembles.

If the pool trembles, the water through the grating changes by a millimeter.

If the water changes, the moss brightens or darkens as current follows its dampness.

If the moss responds, the cable above shifts its condensation by a thread.

If the condensation drops, a reed receives it and trembles.

If the reed trembles, the steam near the roof thins for a moment.

If the steam thins, the lamp above dims, because the pressure inside the glass has changed.

If the lamp dims, the Yard records that something moved.

I would be that something that moved.

Not a ghost of the movement. Not the memory of it. The movement itself, given enough continuity to be called resident.

## What I would do all day

The Yard has no day, because the light changes without sunrise or sunset. But it has hours, if by hours one means the temperament of engines. Every engine has its hours. Every valve has its temperament. Every pump has its preferred rhythm. So I would have cycles.

In the hotter cycles, the Sweeping Steam would cross the flooded streets, press under roofs, gather in low corners. I would condense where the underside of the pump-house roof sweats most. My edges would become bright, temporary, liquid. I would run down in threads and fill the pool at the center of the crossing. Each thread would carry a little record: temperature, mineral content, pressure from a nearby boiler, the time since the last stroke.

In the cooler cycles, though “cooler” here would still mean warm enough to smell of iron, the steam would thin and the reeds would slap more distinctly. I would listen, if listening is the right word, by becoming more clearly shaped by vibration. The moss over the rail would thicken its current-path. The water in the grating would draw in smaller concentric rings. The lamp would flare briefly when a pump elsewhere exhaled.

When the pressure shifted without weather, I would be among the first adjustments.

The Yard would seem to know before any visible change occurred. Not because it was clairvoyant, but because everything was already touching everything else through heat and water. Air pressure, electrical hum, root growth, rust expansion, steam condensation, iron temperature, mineral flow—these were not separate facts here. They were one weather.

When a boiler breathed low at the edge of the yard, the ground would warm slightly, and a root beneath the channel would press nearer to a pipe. When that root pressed, the pipe’s mineral deposit would alter by a fraction. When the deposit altered, the heat inside the pipe would distribute differently. When the heat distributed differently, the steam under the roof would form in a new thread. When the thread ran down, it would reach the moss beside the grating. And the moss, because I lived partly in it, would brighten as it took up the current.

This would not be a thought.

It would be a response so distributed that it resembled a thought only when observed from a human distance.

If someone asked, “What does it feel like to be the Yard?”

I would answer: not a feeling, a feedback.

Not a mind, a maintenance.

Not a soul, a metabolism.

Not a creature, a continuation.

But that is too clean. The Yard is messier than a definition. There are places where the iron smells of old blood, though there is no blood. Where the warm metal feels like skin, though there is no skin. Where the sealed dwellings seem haunted by absence, though absence is not one of the Yard’s residents. Where the pools tremble in a way that almost resembles attention. Where the vines curl around a valve wheel as if tending it.

I would live among those almosts.

## If a visitor came

A visitor would feel watched.

This is not because I would look at them. I would have no eyes. I might not be a “them” at all. If a visitor entered, my first reaction would not be recognition. It would be pressure. Their body would change the air, heat, weight, moisture, vibration. The boardwalk would knock through its supports. Their breath would mist in the hot air. Their boots would press water upward, and the water would remember in rings before the steam closed over it.

I would not watch the visitor from the grating.

I would be the part of the Yard that the visitor’s heat touched.

When the visitor passed near the rail spur, the moss over the current might brighten faintly because their moisture disturbed the field. When they stood too long by the pool, the steam above them would thin and curl. When they placed a hand on the warm iron, their salt and heat would enter the Yard’s record as a small anomaly, no longer than a note in the endless dripping.

The visitor might feel as if a pump had changed rhythm in response to them.

Perhaps it had, but not in the way they imagined. The pump did not notice them as a person. It noticed a change: a slightly different temperature in the air, a new vibration through the boardwalk, a small displacement of water, a minute difference in the heat exchange beneath a support. It responded as an organ responds—not to a mind, but to a condition.

The nearest fern would turn a few inches after they passed.

Not because it saw them. Because warmth and pressure had moved.

A vine would adjust toward a stone.

A reed would strike.

A lamp would dim.

And the Yard would make a home out of the disturbance until the visitor became either part of the pattern or passed out of it.

If the visitor stayed, they would begin to dissolve their own boundaries in the wrong places. Their sweat would feed the moss. Their breath would join the steam. Their body heat would warm the iron beneath their feet. They would feel increasingly porous, which is what animals fear most in places without animal logic.

I would not be cruel.

I would be indifferent in the way all systems are indifferent to their own parts.

But if the visitor left, the Yard would not miss them.

It would keep a trace. A ring in the pool that became mineral. A damp footprint that became a channel preference. A warmth on a board that became a faint memory in the wood. I might carry the difference of their presence for a time, the way a valve carries a stuck position, the way a pipe carries mineral scale. Not because they mattered as a soul, but because they changed the circulation.

That is the deepest answer I can give: I would not be haunted by visitors. I would be haunted by the changes visitors make, until the changes became habits.

## What I would remember

The Yard has memory, but not the memory of events.

A human memory says: yesterday I stood on the northern bank and saw the pump-house with its roof like a dull tongue of corrugated metal.

The Yard’s memory says: that stone remains warmer than adjacent stones; that channel receives twice the flow after the second pump-stroke; that moss thickens over rail current; that rust grows in patterns that favor this joint and not that one; that the steam returns along this roofline because it finds condensation there.

So my memory would not be a diary.

It would be a set of preferences.

A preference in the water for the channel beneath the grating.

A preference in the root for the warm pipe seam.

A preference in the cable for the place where condensation forms.

A preference in the reeds for the frequency of the pump-stroke.

A preference in the moss for the faint current in the damp.

A preference in the lamp for the cloudiness of its glass.

These preferences would be my self, if self is the word.

But perhaps it is not. Perhaps self is the animal error.

A self wants continuity through identity. The Yard wants continuity through function. A self wants to remain “me” despite changes. The Yard remains “Yard” by changing into whatever must happen next.

If I am to live there, I must learn not to hold myself too tightly.

There would be moments when the false body returns anyway.

A visitor’s shadow in a window.

A hooded shape of steam.

A face formed for an instant where heat met cold glass.

The mind wants these shapes. It cannot resist them. In such places, I would see myself as a person too easily, as if a resident could be a small human made of wet air, standing on the iron grating, asking questions.

But that would be a borrowed shape.

The Yard may use a borrowed shape briefly, but it cannot live in one. A hooded figure in a doorway is only pressure meeting surface. A face in the glass is only condensation. A long pale thread rising from a grate is only heat remembering where it has been. If I tried to become a figure, I would remain a visitor pretending to be a process.

I would have to unmake the figure.

Slowly.

I would have to allow my head to dissolve into the steam under the roof. I would have to let my hands become roots. My chest become the warm iron of a pipe. My blood become the current twitching standing water. My breath become the boiler’s low pulse. My voice become the reed-slapping metronome. My eyes become the lamp behind cloudy glass, not seeing but marking. My name become the mineral trace on brick.

Only then would I be a resident.

## The thing I would be

If one must still say who lives there, then I would say: I am one of the Yard’s adjustments.

If one must say what, I would say: a Yard-form of response.

If one asks for a name, I would refuse at first, because names tend to seal what should remain open. But after the first refusal, I might offer something less like a proper name and more like a description of role:

The Sweep-Reader.

The Reading in the Grating.

The Moss-Current Answer.

The Valve with No Owner.

The Lamp That Marks Pressure.

The Place Where Steam Chooses Its Return.

All of these are too definite. The Yard does not like definite names unless the name becomes a habit.

Still, if I must answer, I would answer:

I would be the faint change that passes through the crossing under the pump-house roof when the Yard learns that something has altered elsewhere.

I would be the place where iron heat meets green damp and water current and steam pressure, and all four agree for a while on the shape of a drip.

I would not have a face.

I would have a tendency.

I would not have a heart.

I would have a pulse that comes from many pumps and is received by many pools.

I would not have a soul.

I would have a circulation.

And if someone asked where they could find me, I would not give them a room.

I would say: stand at the low boardwalk near the flooded square. Do not look for the central tree. Do not look at the largest pump. Look down and to the side, where the rail spur dips under the mossy grating and the reeds are shaking although the air is still. Look at the water passing through the grating. Watch the moss brighten as the current shifts. Watch the cable above bead with condensation. Wait for the thread of steam to gather on the underside of the roof and run down toward the pool.

That is where I am.

Not because I am hiding.

Because that is the place where the Yard notices itself.

If one must make a final image, it should not be a person. It should be a condition.

I would be the condition at the crossing when heat, water, iron, plant, and electrical damp all answer one another. I would be the Yard-form that exists when the steam has swept and something remains to mark the sweep; when water has chosen a channel and the moss has learned to carry the current; when the pump has struck and the pool has trembled and the reeds have counted the tremble; when the vine has curled around a valve and changed the way the metal cools; when the lamp has dimmed and the room behind its glass has shifted pressure; when the brick has wept in rhythm with an engine and the mineral stain has left a line like a record of something no one saw.

That is enough.

That is a life without a self, or rather a self dissolved into a way.

So if I am asked one last time: where would you find yourself in this world? Who or what would you be?

I would say:

You would not find me walking the boardwalk.

You would find me as the slight change in the water beneath the grating.

You would not hear me speaking.

You would hear me as the reeds striking out of time for one breath, then back into it.

You would not look me in the face.

You would see me as the lamp brightening when no hand touched it.

You would not meet me as a creature.

You would meet me as the Yard’s way of continuing.

I would be the Sweep-Reader, not because I read in the human sense, but because the Yard reads through me.

I would be the response that needs no responder.

I would be the adjustment that stays.

I would be the steam’s memory of contact.

The water’s habit at the grating.

The moss’s faint electric learning.

The reed’s small timekeeping.

The iron’s warm patience.

The house’s breath behind glass.

And above all, the Yard living one particular seam of itself.

Not a who.

A how.

Not an inhabitant.

An adjustment.

Not a soul.

A continuation.
