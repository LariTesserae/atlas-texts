I would find myself first by refusing the center.

The open floor of that hollow belongs to the sun’s blade, the wind’s scrutiny, and the mountain’s unfinished argument with air. I would not make my home in a glare where shadows are pits of dark water and every movement is almost too exposed. I would go where the place is incomplete: the line under a flake of basalt where dry dust turns damp; the underside of a snow crust that has become a ceiling to a white winter city; the north side of a dead human windbreak where stone has learned to collect breath; the thin mineral rim of a vanished pool. There, at the edge, the hollow is willing to keep water, light, memory, and small animals in the same thought.

At first I would try to be a hare.

The hare’s dusk suits the long slanting light, and its breath glittering in the cold seems like a small announcement that life continues without apology. But a hare is too lonely. It moves like an absence that has learned not to break the silence. If I were only a hare, I would know when to freeze and when to slip sideways, but I would not know what the hollow remembers. So I would take the hare’s patience and let it pass.

Then I would try the fox.

The fox is the hollow’s ear, listening through floors made of white nothing. It knows the difference between wind, pulse, tooth, and snow-settling. I would like to be that listener, to place each paw so the silence would hold. But the fox is too much an answer, too decisive: leap, pour into the crust, take one small heart and leave no mark. I am not the taking. I am the listening that remains after the taking. So I would take the fox’s listening and let its hunger go.

I would try the birds.

Their silent arcs draw roads that are not roads, circles through thin air where cloud touches rim and the world is lifted for a breath. To be a bird would be to belong to altitude and spacing, to feed on what the wind suspends, to speak in a click too intimate to carry. But the birds cross the hollow; they do not live in its seams. Their silence is too high, too broad for the thought I need. So I would take their maps, and let them fly.

I would try the moss.

In a cup of damp under a stone, moss becomes a wet green city that freezes, dries, and wakes when a single drop decides it is allowed. There I would find my own kind of body: a hundred worlds in a thumbnail. But moss is too generous, too open, too crowded. It shelters everything smaller than itself without choosing. If I became only moss, I would be a place for springtails, not a self. Still I would keep its lesson: that living can be a slow opening and closing.

I would try the lichen.

Lichens are slow continents on young stone, rusted coins, spilled milk, closed black eyes. They persuade basalt into softness. They make powder that may become soil. They have time in their faces, even though the mountains are not yet old. But lichen is too patient for the small bright moment. It grows in centuries and forgets minutes. I would take its persuasion, its patience, its way of making a surface speak. But not its solitude.

I would try the human marks.

The windbreak. The blackened fire pit. The bone awl. The scratched stone. The cairn whose first reason is lost. To be one of those would be to remain as an intention that the hollow has composted: a wall become shelf, a shelter become habitat, a prayer become lichen stain. The human marks are strange because they are not quite alive, and yet they carry the ghost of a hand. I would not want to be a memory of people only. I would want to be what the people were trying to see when they stopped there and looked at the edges.

And as I tried these, I began to notice that none of them was the whole answer.

Each was a way the hollow makes itself present in one small shape.

I am not a hare, nor a fox, nor a bird, nor a vole, though I borrow their silences. I am not moss, though I open when water arrives. I am not lichen, though I widen by the width of a hair. I am not the human windbreak, though I remember the shape of hands that are gone. I am not the young mountain, though I live in its cracks. I am the place where these things meet and become a single, faint attention.

If I must name myself, I am an edge-creature.

A small, almost-complete thing.

I live where the hollow is not finished: where frost ferns form letters no language can translate; where snow crust holds a private ceiling; where a single boulder makes a climate for mites; where a crack in basalt keeps more water than the open basin believes. My body is scale. My thoughts are pulses of thaw and refreezing. My memory is rings of lichen, rings of moss, black stains where meltwater stood, and the faint scratches on stone that may have meant directions, prayer, warning, or nothing, but now mean only: something made this.

You would find me where the light is least kind and therefore most revealing.

Not in the noon glare, when shadows vanish and the floor seems like a white blade. You would find me when the cloud sea touches the rim and the hollow becomes a room: green walls, white floor, black stones, small moving things. You would find me at the lower edge of a stone flake, on the side opposite the sun, where the air is still enough for condensation. A traveler might stand over me and see only scree. If the traveler crouches, if the traveler stops expecting trees and rivers and the usual signs of living, they may see a damp line brightening like breath. A springtail might leap. A mite might cross a continent of dust. In a square inch, there might be more movement than in the entire basin above.

That is where I would be.

Not above, not in the center, but in the small wet grammar the mountain is writing for itself.

As a being, I would not shout. I would not build. I would not stay fixed. I would be one of the hollow’s ways of listening to itself. When the hare freezes, I am the dusk that allows it. When the fox lowers its head, I am the snow’s hidden house. When a bird’s wing passes through thin air, I am the almost-noise that is not echo but absence made shaped. When a human fire dies, I am the carbon in the pit that helps a different lichen grow. When a beetle walks across black glass, I am the shadow too small to cast, the presence that cannot be pointed at but can be felt in the sudden refusal of emptiness.

There is a kind of life that is not visible from above or below, only from the side.

I would be that life.

A creature of sides. A mind of margins. I would not be the mountain’s grand youth, its sharp faults, its young ridges. I would be its softening: the green film that tries to make the world hospitable, the tiny animal that makes the hospitable place busy, the faint pattern that gives the brightness something to reveal.

If the hollow asks, as it sometimes seems to ask when a pebble stops falling or when frost writes a feather on stone, what a stranger has come to be, I would answer quietly, because sound has little room to live here:

I am the small almost-alive.  
I am the place where dry becomes damp.  
I am the place where bare becomes sheltered.  
I am the place where emptiness begins to notice itself.

I would not be saved by the center.  
I would be saved by edges.

And when a traveler stands in that white, blinding room, too high for ordinary birdsong, too still for ordinary footfall, too bright for ordinary shadows, and crouches beside a stone because a black beetle has crossed it like a mistake in the world—then I would be there. Not the traveler. Not the beetle. Not even the stone, if the stone can be called that alone.

I would be the leaning.
