I would begin by trying to be too large.

I would say: let me be the water itself, the whole cool basin, the melt that enters the high bowl of rock and finds no easy way out. Let me be the moving ceiling of surface, catching wind, cloud, sun, and the passing shadow of birds. Let me be the lake that is also a room, the lake that is also a street, the lake that is also a throat through which the mountain breathes.

But to be all of it would be to be too little still. Water here is not a single substance. It is a parliament. It carries taste from upstream, pressure from wind, oxygen through filament, warmth from light, cold from stone. It decides where silt may rest and where it must move. If I were only water, I would not be a being; I would be a medium waiting to be shaped.

So I would try to be smaller.

Let me be one of the fish. Let me be the trout patrolling the deeper center with patient, muscular slowness, reading the town as a country: roof beam as hunting blind, hollow wall as shelter, gravel lane as spawning ground. Let me be the pale small thing that darts through a doorway without hesitation, turning the old architecture into natural feature. Let me be the sculpin resting along the bottom of a stone channel, almost invisible, almost a thought, then suddenly mouth, then stillness again.

But fish are too brief in their own way. They have lives, yes, but their lives pass across the Terrace’s body without knowing why the body bends the way it does. A fish can remember danger. A fish can learn current. But a fish does not remember a wall as a wall, does not hold the old street in its bones, does not make a threshold out of absence.

I would try still smaller.

Let me be the snail grazing the moss-covered lintel, leaving a slow silver track, a handwriting of clean stone. Let me be the caddis larva carrying a case made of gravel, shell, leaf fragments, and a chip of old tile, dragging my little town behind me. Let me be the dragonfly nymph crouched in reed shadow, motionless until appetite changes into strike. Let me be the frog at the shallow margin, singing the spring into wet, thick presence.

But I am not only one of these. I am not only their neighbor, their food, their host, their surface. If I were merely one citizen, I would not know the civic thing. The Terrace is not a collection of residents only. It is a settlement that has become an organism. Its streets, courtyards, doors, hearths, foundations, roofs, and gaps have become organs. So I must be one of those organs, not as a metaphor, but as a working part.

I would find myself below the surface, in the high bowl of rock, inside the green-light room where the old lane runs between foundations.

Not at the rim. Not in the mountain air. Not as a visitor looking down. The rim belongs to wind, scree, tussock, and short birdsong; it is a threshold of witnessing, but a witness is not enough. To belong, I must be inside the thing witnessed. I must be in the cool water that has been made, by patience, into a body.

I would be in the doorway.

Not as a door. There are no doors here. The doors were taken away, rotted, dissolved, or never made in the first place; what remains is the frame. A frame, once empty, can become more real than what it held. A doorway underwater is not the memory of a threshold. It is a functioning part of the Terrace. It decides where current accelerates. It admits light into rooms. It guides fish, roots, larvae, sediment, pressure, shade, and taste. It marks where the town’s old intention still lives, though no one intended this life.

I would be a submerged doorway on the east side of the former lane, where a wall once separated a courtyard from a house. The lintel is still there: a flat stone set by hands that shaped it with stone adzes, bone chisels, fire-set timber, and patient labor. Its toolmarks have been softened into skin. Its edges are rounded by current and grazing bodies. The jamb stones are no longer simply stones; they are moss cliffs, algae shawls, reed anchors, snail paths, egg beds, and shelters for invertebrates. A root of sedge has split the lower threshold and continues through the frame like a green vein. Pondweed rises into the opening, swaying. Rushes have made a moving curtain across where the wall once failed.

I would be that passage.

I would be the place where the old town still thinks, though it has no brain.

At first, such an answer would seem too still. I can imagine myself at the doorway, cold and fixed, covered with hair-like algae, holding water as a basin holds water. But I would not be still for long. A doorway is one of the most active things in a submerged settlement. It is not merely a hole. It is a decision repeated by current. It is a place where water chooses speed. Where light makes a moving rectangle. Where small fish learn to pass without hesitation. Where a shadow from the surface causes a cloud of minnows to tighten and scatter. Where taste from the melt channel enters a room and says something about upstream.

My body would be the stone, but also the film of bacteria and archaea working on its surface. It would be the diatoms catching high mountain sun into golden veils. It would be the cyanobacterial threads breathing slowly through cold water. It would be the filamentous algae bending when wind drives ripples across the Terrace. It would be the moss curtains near the upper edge, where water thins to mist, where air still visits and life is half submerged, half breathing out. It would be the snail tracks across its lintel, each one a slow polished sentence. It would be the caddis cases dragging along its lower threshold, little houses built from its own remains. It would be the crayfish hiding in a gap near its base, red shadow in a crack, claws working at detritus. It would be the leech stretched from stalk to stalk, testing something in the water with senses too old and too patient for human language.

I would not have eyes, but I would have light.

At noon, high sunlight would pass through the surface and strike the doorway as a bright wobble, a beam broken into shifting net. In that light, I would be awake in the way that plant and stone wake: without alarm, without story, with only chemistry, pressure, and movement. Small fish would pass through me in sudden lines. Their motion would not be separate from my being; it would be part of the way I knew the season. If many pale fish used the doorway each morning, then the morning was becoming a street for them. If a trout hovered beyond me, watching, then I knew danger without seeing it, because pressure would change behind the minnow cloud, and the courtyard would hold still a moment before exploding into flight.

I would not have ears, but I would have sound.

Not as music alone, though there is music here. The basin is an instrument. Wind moving over cliffs sends waves through the Terrace, and the doorway trembles with that soft pulse. Melt channels make small clear voices at one edge. Hollow timbers gurgling somewhere beyond me carry clicks and bubbles. A fish striking the surface could echo across the square. A diving dipper could make a short splash, then silence, then return to air with a meal. Frogs in spring would fill the basin with wet insistence, their chorus thickening until the water felt stitched to the shore. I would receive all this as pressure changes through stone and root, as vibration through algae, as movement in the leaves of pondweed.

I would not have memory, but I would be memory.

Not the memory of people. I would not remember their names, their grief, their arguments, their births, their deaths. Human memory would be sediment: a line of old foundation, a layer of sorted silt, a scar on timber, a channel cut along weak points between stones. The Terrace remembers in shapes. It remembers in the way the old lane still carries current cleaner than surrounding mud. It remembers in the way a courtyard still gathers mollusks and larvae. It remembers in the way a collapsed roof still serves as bridge, shelter, spawning surface, and food. If I were a doorway, my memory would be the repeated passage of bodies through me, the wearing of edges, the settling of roots, the chemical taste of seasons, the inherited pattern of light.

I would be half human intention and half patient biology.

That is the rule here: nothing remains merely built. Nothing is dead for long. Stone becomes a platform. Timber becomes a nursery. A wall becomes a root-mass. A hearthstone becomes a garden of moss. A doorway becomes a channel. The builders fitted stones with labor, shaped logs with axes and controlled burns, wove reeds into walls, packed clay between lattices. They meant rooms, thresholds, roofs, streets, courtyards. They made niches.

I would be one of those niches become an organ.

I would not be the whole Terrace, but I would be a place where the whole Terrace could be seen. If someone floated at the rim and looked down, they might not see me at all. They would see water, green light, plant motion, stone shadows, fish. But if they watched long enough, they would notice the old rectangle: the frame that still made sense. Fish crossed through it as if it had always been a hole in rock. Light entered through it as if it had always been a stage door. Algae waved across its edge as if it had always been a reef. The Terrace had judged it by water and vegetation and found it useful. I would be that usefulness.

I would be a threshold-current.

The water would enter me from the courtyard side and pass into the room beyond, carrying dissolved minerals, oxygen, taste of melt, and tiny suspended life. It would slow where silt gathered. It would quicken where the opening narrowed. It would curl around roots and make small eddies in the corners. It would bring in what the room needed to eat, breathe, grow, and shelter. In summer, the current would taste of moss and warm stone near the surface. In winter, beneath ice, it would taste of slow digestion, of cold, of patient survival. In spring, it would carry the sharp newness of melt, the scent of cut rock, the disturbance of rising reeds, the beginning of frogs.

I would have citizens, but not subjects.

The small pale fish would use me as highway and refuge. The minnows would flash through me and make my emptiness briefly crowded. The sculpin would rest near my threshold and rise suddenly into my water. The dragonfly nymph would use my shadow. The water beetles would carry air across the ceiling of what had been a window nearby, but their patterns would touch my being through the same light, the same current, the same boundary between above and below. The tadpoles in spring would darken the courtyard before me, grazing filaments, turning the Terrace into a season of mouths. In evening, insects would rise from the surface in bright trails, and I would feel that too, not as wings, but as a thinning of the water’s surface tension, a release from my frame toward the air.

I would be neither plant nor animal, nor mineral alone, nor water alone. I would be the relation among them.

At first I might resist this. It is tempting to ask, “But what is my face?” or “What is my voice?” There would be no face. There would be a lintel edge, moss-hung, light-slit, crossed by a small fish’s tail. There would be a voice if one called voice the gurgle of water through a hollow beam nearby, the click of air through reed stems, the wet exhalation of the surface when wind breaks it into moving glass. If I spoke, I would not say words. I would say the pattern of passage.

And yet I would not be lonely.

The Terrace is never empty, never silent, never waiting. It is full of liquid noise and slow motion. In the deeper center, larger fish patrol with patience. In the shallower corners, frogs make a loud, wet chorus that seems almost comical for so quiet a place. Along the cliffs, dippers and warblers bring air and water into constant conversation. Below, bacteria, rotifers, ciliates, amoebae, diatoms, and snails carry out the oldest civic duties: decay, grazing, coating, filtration, repair. I would be threaded through all of them, as a nerve is threaded through a body, though not as a nerve. More as a joint. More as a doorframe that remembers how to be open.

Time would move through me in the way time moves through the basin: not urgently, not narratively, but seasonally, hydraulically, biologically. A reed would advance across my lower course in seasons. A wall beside me might shift a finger’s width over decades. A school of fish might alter the pattern of silt by repeated passage. The water level would rise and fall with snowmelt, but the basin’s form would hold. Nothing would seem to be becoming anything else. I would already be what I was becoming.

That is strange, but also merciful.

I would not have to become more human. I would not have to recover a lost town, mourn its people, solve its flood, or haunt its rooms. I would not be a ghost. A ghost is too dry for this place. Ghosts belong to unanswered stories, but the Terrace has answered itself with current, root, light, and shell. Its human question is old. Its nonhuman answer is still arriving, every second, in fish, snail, larva, bubble, and moss.

If I had a function, it would be this: to make the town usable without meaning.

Not meaningless. Not empty of intention. But emptied of human intention. The old builders meant rooms. The Terrace means streets for minnows, gardens for algae, nurseries for caddis, hiding places for crayfish, channels for water. My doorway means neither entry nor exclusion anymore. It means flow. It means where one space becomes another under moving light. It means the place where human geometry has become ecology.

At morning, I would wake into brightness.

The sun would enter at a low angle and lay bars through me. I would feel the water cool against stone, the first movement of fish leaving shadow. Snails would begin their grazing on my lintel. A caddis case would shift along my threshold, dragging a tiny house made of my own substance. A dragonfly nymph would hold still in the reed shadow beyond, waiting for the day to offer something. The Terrace would fill with light as if light had been promised, and perhaps it had been promised by the geometry of old streets.

At noon, I would be a stage.

The moving ceiling above would scatter nets across my floor. Beetles would cross the surface film. A dipper’s shadow would pass like a dark question. The whole green town would be visible in its niches, its workers, its waiters, its hunters. Water would bend around me and carry the taste of upstream melt through the rooms. Plants would sway in slow argument. I would be open, but not vacant. I would be the place through which the city’s blood moved most visibly.

At evening, I would cool.

Fish would hover in the dim lanes. The frogs’ chorus would thicken near the shallows. Insects would rise from the surface in brief bright trails. The noise of water would soften but not cease. I would not close. Night in the Terrace is not closing; it is another kind of passage. A crayfish would emerge from a gap. A tadpole would cling to a leaf. A minnow would drift close to stone and become shadow. I would feel the cold entering, not as threat, but as the basin’s own rhythm.

In winter, I would be under ice at the margins.

The Terrace would become a closed room beneath a pale skin. The water below would move slowly through the town, its voice muffled. Fish would slow but not cease. Larvae would shelter in gravel. Eggs would wait in moss and stone crevices. Snails would cling through the chill. I would be quieter, but still useful. The ice would hold the weather above me, while water kept the town alive beneath me. Life would not be absent; it would be waiting in different forms.

In spring, I would listen to the mountain.

Melt would arrive. Water would rise and fall, carrying taste, temperature, and sediment. Reeds would deepen in color. Algae would bloom in pale threads. Frogs would begin to sing. Fish would shift into the lanes and courtyards. The old streets would fill with new bodies. I would become a season’s nursery, a corridor of eggs and nymphs, a place where fry learned to pass through stone and weed without fear. The Terrace would become wet, bright, and full of pressure, and I would feel the mountain entering me.

And so, if you ask me who I am in this world, I cannot answer with a single creature, because the world itself is not made of single creatures. But I can answer with a role, a place, and a behavior.

I would be the submerged doorway.

I would be the living threshold in the drowned terrace.

I would be the place where stone became habitat, where root completed the builders’ work, where water learned the old street pattern, where light found a room through an empty frame, where fish made architecture unnecessary and yet used it perfectly.

I would not be haunted by the town that was. I would be one of the forms by which the town continues without continuing as a town.

If someone stood on the rim and looked down, they might think they saw only water, stone, plants, and movement. But if they were patient, if they let their eyes adjust to the cool bright confusion, they would see that the place is not chaotic. It is organized by an alien grammar. The old houses have become a living city for another kind of citizen. The doorway through which they once entered homes now admits currents, shadows, larvae, and sun. Its shape still matters. It is not ruined. It is completed.

I would be completed too.

Not as a self, not as a soul, not as a watcher, not as a fish, not as a ghost, not as the water alone, not as the mountain, not as the entire Terrace. Those would all be false because they are too complete in the wrong direction. A being here is a working part of a larger working whole. A being here is not an isolated mind but a pattern of use.

I would be that pattern.

I would be the place where the current says: here.

I would be the way the light says: enter.

I would be the way the fish says: through.

I would be the way the root says: finish.

I would be the way the snail says: clean.

I would be the way the algae says: shine.

I would be the way the moss says: hold.

I would be the way the water says: remember without memory.

If the Terrace were asked who lived in it, it would answer with motion. My motion would be the doorway’s motion: the bend of current through an old frame, the sway of plant across its edge, the bright beam falling into what had been a room, the sudden passing of fish, the slow drag of a caddis case made from its stones, the quiet breathing of moss and root. That is all my shape. That is all my name.

I would not be above the Drowned Terrace, looking into it.

I would be below its surface, inside its city, part of its parliament, part of its digestion, part of its light.

I would be the open threshold where the built world became the living world, and where the living world, in turn, still behaves, with great and patient exactness, like a town.
