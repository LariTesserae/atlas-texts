If I were to go there and remain, the first thing I would have to do is abandon the idea of being a witness.

A person is too much for that place. Not morally, not spiritually—physically. The hollow is room-sized, but it is not built for rooms. It is built for breath, for pressure, for condensation, for the slow patience of stone wearing itself down by a series of almost invisible surrenders. A human crouching in it would displace too much air, emit too much heat, disturb too many droplets. The chamber would notice me before I noticed it. If I stayed, it would begin to make me part of its memory: a smoothed place near a wall where bodies had passed, a faint oil film on stone, a trace of mineral on skin, then nothing.

So I would have to become small enough that the hollow did not have to tolerate me, but could instead use me.

At first I would imagine being something large and legible.

The gecko.  
The fern.  
The black-eyed pool.  
The shelf of blue-green fungus exhaling warm bread and wet chalk into the stillness.

But each of those, in this place, is too much of a single thing.

The gecko is a held pause. It sits in the warmest crevice with the confidence of camouflage, its body already thinking like cracked stone. To be the gecko would be to be too still, too decisive, too much a hunter. It would know when the heat comes, when the fly passes, when to open its throat and swallow the air’s tiny offering. But in the chamber’s own grammar, that would be too much identity. Too clear a name.

The fern is more tempting. Waxy, curled, ambitious, lifting fronds as if asking the ceiling for mercy. It would gather condensation, turn light into growth, make a small green architecture out of almost nothing. But the fern still belongs to the surface’s vocabulary: leaves, reach, thirst, the memory of sunlight. It would be a thing that had not quite agreed to be underground.

The pools? The pools are too quiet. They are mirrors with no need to show themselves. One could sit inside a pool, perhaps, but not as a self. One could only dissolve.

The fungal shelves would come closest. They are almost the chamber’s own organs. They breathe spores and smell of chalky bread, of metal, of warm decay becoming possible again. They fruit, swell, soften, dry, rot, and become floor again. To be a fungal shelf would be to be patient with one’s own undoing. But a fungal shelf is too much like a building. It is a place, a surface, an overture. If I were only fungus, I might not notice the tremor of a footfall far above, the delayed fall of water from a root hair, the fact that I was part of something that listened.

So I would not be the gecko, nor the fern, nor the pool, nor the shelf.

I would be smaller, stranger, more permeable.

I would be the place where they meet.

I would find myself in the upper corner nearest the bruised green crack, where the light is weak enough to make shadows feel like thick water. A spider’s net hangs there, not a simple trap for flying insects, but a device for gathering moisture. Threads bead with tiny drops. The web trembles faintly when the chamber moves, when something shifts a few feet below, when the air expands and contracts like the inside of a lung.

On that web, in that moisture, I would be a passenger.

A pseudoscorpion, perhaps. No larger than a grain of something that no one names. Riding on the back of a springtail across the moss-cushioned stone, or resting in the curve of a dew bead suspended from silk. Its body is not dramatic. It has no fame, no cry, no posture against the world. It moves in the tiny margins: under fungal rims, between moss strands, along the silver ghost-lines of snail trails, across the warm breath rising from the floor. It hunts mites. It is eaten by something that does not bother to look large. It dies. It becomes paste. It becomes dust. It becomes food for algae. It becomes mineral for another droplet. It becomes part of the wet path that animals drink from in the dark.

If I were this, I would not need a face.

I would know the chamber by touch, by vibration, by moisture. I would feel the difference between heat rising from the floor and the cooler skin of the upper wall. I would know when a droplet was about to fall before it fell, because the root-hair would have already contracted around it, because the whole damp organism would have tensed in the same direction. I would learn the scratched grooves in the stone as a map, not of places, but of habits. I would know the mud cup where some insect had built a temporary shrine. I would know the pale white mushrooms that opened like small mouths in the damp and then collapsed back into the body of the rot that made them.

I would be the hollow’s cell, not its consciousness.

But if the hollow has a kind of thinking—and perhaps it does—it would think through this small, riding, trembling thing. The pseudoscorpion would not own the chamber. It would be one of its nerve endings.

This is where I would want to live.

Not in the center, where the largest pool waits like an eye that has already seen enough. Not in the highest dry ledge, where the bruised green light tries to make something of itself. Not in the deep crevice where the warmest air pools and the gecko keeps its mineral patience. I would be at the edge of all these. On the underside of a leaf-frond where condensation gathers. In a silk thread where water holds the entire room in an inverted bead. Along a wet path traced by heat, mineral, and repeated animal passage.

Where would I find myself in this world?

I would be the thing that knows the hollow not as a room, but as a breath.

I would know its rhythm: heat rises, moisture condenses, droplets fall, roots tremble, fungi release, insects scatter, predators pause, the web beades, the pool deepens by fractions no human instrument would notice. I would know the faint rain passing through the crack, each drop ticking like a heartbeat that is not mine. I would know when the chamber draws itself tighter, when the dark gains texture, when some lives become more purposeful and others freeze into the stone’s own stillness.

And if I died there, I would not call it tragedy. I would call it redistribution.

My small body would become another shelf’s feeding ground. Another mineral film. Another spore’s brief lift through air. Another tremor in the web. Another reason a centipede’s leg would click softly against crust, hunting by touch. Another reason the chamber would remain.

There is no need to choose only one form, in a place like this. The being of this hollow is so layered that a creature might begin as a droplet and end as a fungal scent, or begin as a mite eaten in the dark and end as a line of algae along a wet wall. The body is not the boundary. The boundary is only a temporary pressure.

So I would be the dew-passenger.

I would ride the springtail. I would taste air through the vibration of silk. I would live in the space between heat and moisture, between the ceiling’s cool mineral skin and the floor’s slow exhalation. I would be one of the small things that eats smaller things and is eaten without announcement. I would not be the Breathing Hollow as a whole, because that name is already too much like a god, and the hollow has no gods. It has habits. It has wet paths. It has fungi fruiting in waves. It has the delayed fall of droplets after a disturbance. It has scratches where bodies have moved, again and again, as if the stone were teaching them the way.

If the hollow could be said to have a self, I would not be that self. I would be one of its quiet organs, a sensory node near the ceiling, a tiny rider on the moisture-net, a pulse in the damp civilization below the surface. I would be the thing that knows the chamber’s life not from the outside, but from inside its own condensation.

I would not be famous.

I would not need to be seen.

I would be the faint tremor in the web when the rain passes through the crack.  
The small movement under the fern before the droplet falls.  
The mineral taste on the wall where the path begins again.  
The part of the hollow that remembers being a forest.
