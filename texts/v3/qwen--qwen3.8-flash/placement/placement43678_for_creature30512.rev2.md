If I were carried there and set down at the wrong hour, I would begin where everything begins in that hollow: blind, too bright, too cold, and unable to tell whether the light is touching me or cutting me.

The sky would lie too close. The air would feel like something I had to borrow from. I would stand, if I could call it standing, on a floor made of broken mineral and snow crust, and the first thing I would want to be would be less visible. Not hidden behind anything—there is hardly anything tall enough to hide behind—but hidden in plain sight: white on white, black on black, grey on the dust that has not yet decided what color it wants to be.

At first I would think: I should be the hare.

There is something noble in the hare there, rounded against the cold, moving as if its footfalls are sins it cannot undo. If I were the hare, I would understand that survival is not always speed. Sometimes survival is patience so total it resembles a stone. I would stop in the long dusk. I would listen not with the ears but with the whole pale body. My breath would glitter and vanish. I would be a small warm thing trying not to insult the brightness by admitting it has blood.

But the hare is still too legible. The hare is still a story a fox could read in the snow.

Then I would think: perhaps the fox at the rim.

The fox is the hollow’s thin, white attention. It is the part of the place that listens to the listening beneath the snow. To be the fox would be to understand hunger as arithmetic, not as passion. To know that killing is not cruelty there; it is simply the next small movement after waiting, and sometimes the next small movement is not killing but turning away because the crust will not break, because the vole has not crossed its secret highway, because the light has shifted and the night is not yet cold enough to bear the body’s full story.

Even so, the fox is too large a shape. Too much of a creature with a face.

I would try, then, to be smaller.

The vole, perhaps.

That would suit the place better. A white vole moving through packed ice and dry lichen, making a tunnel no wider than a thumb. Inside, everything would be muffled and intimate. There would be no horizon. There would be only the taste of moss-stem, the pressure of snow above, the faint vibration of a fox-paw or a hare-tooth in the distance. A vole’s world would be a world of edges: one degree colder, one degree warmer, one drop more damp, one grain less grit. I would not live in the hollow. I would live in its seams.

But a vole still has a heart beating fast, and I am afraid that heart would make me too much like an animal, and not enough like what the place truly asks for.

What the place asks for is not a creature. It asks for a condition of being alive so modest it might not be noticed until the light becomes severe.

So I would choose the moss.

A cushion of moss under the shaded face of a fresh basalt flake. To the human eye, almost nothing. To the mountain, a city. To the water, a keeper. I would be dry when the glare came and open in minutes at the first touch of melt. I would hold frost inside my cells. I would shelter springtails like grains of salt with intent. I would make a small wet world from one stone’s refusal to be dry. I would not fight the wind; I would let it pass over me and, in passing, deposit spores. I would not fear the light, but I would not love it either; I would use it, slowly, as a patient debt collected over years.

Yet moss is too stationary. I want something that crosses.

Then the beetle.

A black beetle on a sunlit stone, small enough that its shadow might be mistaken for a hole in the world. That would be good. I would not call myself. I would emerge only when the temperature and the moisture agree. I would walk across a face of fresh rock as if it were an empty continent. I would touch quartz with my antennae. I would avoid the white mirror of ice, or perhaps walk upon it and know, without knowing, that the ground has become treacherous glass. I would be one of the place’s visible verbs: cross, stop, turn, taste, vanish beneath a flake.

Even a beetle feels too definite.

The more I tried to be one thing, the more the hollow resisted. It had many citizens, and not one king. It kept its being scattered. It said: if you want to live here, do not insist on being a single body. Be where the world is incomplete.

So this is where I would find myself:

At the edge of a flat stone where meltwater gathers for a moment and then disappears. In the underside of a snow crust, where mites and springtails move through a private night that only becomes visible if you lift the ice with a trembling, frost-bitten hand. Inside the seam of a basalt fault where a lichened continent has begun to widen by the width of a hair. In the lee of a human windbreak, where stacked stones have long since forgotten why they were arranged and now simply hold damp, shade, and warmth. At the rim where cloud sea touches rock and the air becomes a thin, living suspension.

There, not as a hare, not as a fox, not as a vole, not as moss, not as beetle, not even as lichen, but as the small agreement between them—

I would be the being the hollow keeps.

Not the being who rules it. Not the being who names it. The being who learns its first rule before learning its own body:

Look for the edges.

I would be made of edges.

My skin would be one side of a stone: bright, dry, polished by glare. The other side would be dark, wet, inhabited. My breath would be the cloud-mist that condenses, then freezes, then melts, then returns to air, never staying long enough to be called water. My bones would be the young ridges, sharp and faulted, not yet worn into gentleness. My blood would be the shy meltwater that appears only when the sun is right. My nerves would be the threads of fungi beneath thin soil, connecting what appears separate to what appears empty. My memory would be the rings in moss and the circles in lichen, not quite writing, not quite prayer, but record.

And my face—if I must have one—would be the faint red-black smear of lichen paste inside a hollow rock.

Not a human face. Not a god’s face. A face that is more like a mark someone made once, then another person made again, and the mountain took from them without malice, turning intention into texture.

I would have been left over from a people who built low because they understood that the sky here does not tolerate arrogance. Or I would have been one of them, still living, but no longer distinguishable from the hollow. My windbreak would be the stones that hold heat. My shelter would be the crack where wind drops. My tool would be an obsidian flake worn down to mineral patience. My needle would be a bone awl in the gravel, already becoming habitat for something too small to name.

But even that would be too much “I.” The hollow would soften it, as it softens everything that remains too long.

It would remove the pronouns.

After a night of too much light, I would wake as many small lives at once. One of me would be a hare’s foot pressed lightly into snow, leaving a puncture that begins to fill with wind before it can become a print. Another part would be a fox’s ear angled downward, listening not for what moves but for what almost does not move. Another would be the click of a bird’s throat in thin air. Another would be a vole’s tongue against a frozen stem. Another would be a beetle crossing black basalt and, for one second, blocking a sunbeam so sharply that the rock’s warmth seems interrupted. Another would be the moss opening. Another would be the lichen widening. Another would be the frost drawing ferns and spirals, a script no one can read because it is not meant to be read. It is meant to be survived.

If you asked me then, “Who are you?” I would not answer with a name.

I would answer with the place’s way of speech:

A small silence.
A tick of cooling stone.
A breath glittering.
A drop forming on a shadowed ledge.
A pale foot lifting.
A crust barely breaking.
A ring of moss around a bone.
A ring of lichen around a stone.
A ring of ice around a mirror of meltwater before the mirror forgets itself.

I would be the hollow’s quiet nation, wearing the illusion of a single self because only a self can be asked to be.

And if I could choose one image, one exact body, one place to stand without contradicting the whole description, I would choose this:

I would be the thing that waits under the snow crust at dawn.

Not the predator above it, though the predator is part of me. Not the prey beneath it, though the prey is also me. I would be the space between them: the temperature change that decides which crust is safe to walk upon and which is a trap. I would be the minute when the sun has warmed the surface enough for the snow to begin its slow melt, but not enough for the hare to leave its mark, and not enough for the fox to leap. I would be the hidden wetness under the white shell, where mites move with blind certainty and mold feeds on a frozen drop of dung, and life persists because it is smaller than disaster.

That is where I would find myself.

In the low place. In the edge place. In the place where the mountain has not yet learned what it is going to be. In the bright glare and the patient cold. In the crack where water hides. In the moss city that pretends to be dead until a single drop revives it. In the ruins of people who built with too much reverence to build high. In the bones of animals who understood that silence is not absence but a method.

And I would be called, if anything called, “the hollow’s quiet self.”

But perhaps the most honest answer is simpler:

I would be what lives there, because the place made me want to be less visible, and less certain, and more numerous than one. I would be a hare stopping in dusk. I would be a fox listening to the floor of the world. I would be a vole tunneling through the underside of winter. I would be a beetle crossing the black mirror of fresh stone. I would be moss opening in a single drop of melt. I would be lichen widening by the width of a hair. I would be a human hand’s long-dead warmth, held briefly in a stone, then handed to the cold.

I would live at the edges, as everything does.

And the center—bright, empty, too pure, too hard—would belong to light alone.
