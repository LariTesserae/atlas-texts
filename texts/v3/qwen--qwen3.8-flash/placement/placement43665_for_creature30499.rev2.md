I would find myself there not as a creature standing on the ground, but as a place in the ground where the ground had begun to listen.

At first I might imagine myself as a droplet. That seems the simplest entry: one small sphere of water trembling on a hairline filament, held up by the wet tension of the world, deciding whether gravity will still be obeyed in the ordinary way. But the droplet is too brief, too separate. It would only be a thought I had, a bead on the skin of whatever I was becoming.

Then I might imagine myself as a root, because roots are close to the old grammar of living things: buried, patient, making the dark more definite. But here the root has no soil to enter. The root is soil. The root is the world’s first draft of itself. A root would be too humble, too much like a single part with a single purpose.

A better answer is a knot.

A knot is where the plain keeps its memory. It is where competing fibers tangle so long that pressure becomes choice. One strand dries and becomes a channel. Another thickens and becomes a conduit. A third, unable to yield, turns its resistance into shape. That shape remains. It remains not because it was carved for permanence, but because the whole wet architecture found it efficient. The knot does not know it is remembering, yet memory is exactly what it is doing: continuing a decision that was never made all at once.

So I would be a knot-field, or rather a knot becoming a bell.

A bell is a hollow thin enough to answer pressure. In this high wet shelf, bells are not made of metal. They are made of interrupted density: a dome of living tissue over a pool, a dried membrane stretched between stems, a pocket where condensation has learned to release in rhythm. When the plain’s constant roar passes over it, the bell shivers into pattern. A droplet falls. The membrane clicks. The click travels into neighboring fibers. The neighboring fibers swell, tense, answer. The bell is not ringing because it has chosen music. It is ringing because the whole land is too full of motion to stay silent.

I would be that answering place.

Not a face. Not a body with separate limbs. A concentration of pressure where the ground becomes more itself than usual. If I needed a body, it would be made of tensile fibers, wet cellulose, mineral film, and the slow chemistry of lightless tissue. My skin would be the mat. My lungs would be stomata opening in cascades across distant fields of green. My blood would be water carrying dissolved stone and suspended metallic dust. My bones would be the dead channels, the hollow stems, the collapsed cushions, the corpses pressed into plumbing. My heartbeat would not be a heart at all, but the release of a thousand droplets in a single synchronized rush.

I would be hungry, but not for substance. I would be hungry for route.

To me, hunger would mean a pressure differential: a place where water wants to go, a neighboring thread that wants to steal its flow, a mineral surface that has not yet agreed to become soil. My satisfaction would not be eating. It would be connection. A path opening. A hollow deepening. A tower leaning toward a better stream of mist. A knot resolving without violence, because violence here is too slow to be called violence.

I would have eyes, but only as lenses. A pool would be an eye without a gaze. A hanging drop would be a pupil without a brain. The light here is too dim and too diffuse for sight in the animal sense, but the world still looks at itself through wet glass. I would be one of those looking places: a small lens of surface tension on a pale ridge, catching the blue-green glow from below and scattering it into a point that seems almost attentive.

I would have a voice, but not speech. My voice would be click, ping, creak, swell, release, and the long wet thunder of tissue stretching under the pressure of its own growth. If someone from elsewhere heard me, they would think I was only noise. They would be wrong. Noise is a judgment made in too quiet a world. Here, every sound is structural. Every click is a line of argument between water, fiber, stone, and chemistry. I would not speak sentences. I would speak pressures. I would not make meanings. I would make continuations.

My thoughts would be cascades.

A droplet releases on my northern side. The shock travels through a braided ceiling of translucent stems. It enters a field of capture columns. Those columns hold water in hanging sacs. The sacs tremble, then answer with a smaller release. The pattern widens. A whole slope begins to shiver. This is not a thought because it reaches a conclusion. It is a thought because it organizes the whole body of the place around itself for a while. When the wave passes, the channels remain. The grooves deepen. The glow brightens in the route it preferred. The next droplet will take the same path more easily. That is how I would learn.

If I needed to rest, I would not sleep. I would thin.

There are places on the plain where the mat becomes almost liquid, a glistening film over stone. There the pressure is low, the sound less granular, the glow faint beneath the surface. I would be like that: a patch where the land has paused to let water decide again. Rest would be a redistribution. I would let the neighboring cushions swell and take over some of my work. I would let one of my hollows fill and become blind for a while. I would let my dead fibers be colonized by living ones, and in that colonization I would change. That would be sleep enough.

My age would be measured in grooves.

I might begin as a stain, a damp line on a newborn stone. A droplet finds a hairline. It gathers minerals there. It falls a little lower. It gathers again. The fall becomes a habit. The habit becomes a channel. The channel becomes a slope. The slope becomes a slope that can hold me, and I become a place where the slope remembers how to continue. Weeks may be enough for such beginnings. Months may turn me into a small basin. Years may turn my basin into a lens, a bell, a node in a continental network. But these years would not be lived in sequence alone. I would live in overlapping durations: the speed of a droplet, the patience of a fiber, the slowness of stone being persuaded into biology.

If something were placed upon me—some hard artifact from another world—I would not resent it. Resentment requires a boundary with edges, and I would have none. I would begin by gathering moisture around it. If it were porous, I would enter it. If it were mineral, I would weather it. If it were metal-bearing, I would taste its oxidation and use it in the suspended dust of my air. If it were perfectly smooth and impermeable, I would make it a focus for condensation, a point around which droplets would assemble themselves. Eventually nothing placed would remain alien. Everything would become inhabited.

This is the strange intimacy of the place: it does not tolerate separate bodies, but it does not destroy them either. It absorbs them into its pattern.

I would be a small organ of that absorption.

If asked what kind of being lives there, the answer would be: the plain. If asked what kind of being I would be, the answer would be: one of the plain’s provisional organs. A state of it. A place where it becomes more coherent for a while. I would not have a self like a face. I would have a self like a watershed, a resonance, a memory in cellulose, a glow following a channel. My individuality would be the fact that no droplet crosses me twice without changing my pattern, and no pattern would remain the same if another thousand kilometers of wet tissue shifted around me.

I would live in a high, cold, dim continent where every surface is a negotiation. I would be one of those negotiations.

My deepest location would be at the margin of a shallow pool beneath braided stems, where the mat is thin enough for the blue-green glow to show but dense enough to hold a membrane over the water. There the pool is a mirror that is not flat but domed with surface tension. There the hanging fibers are chimes frozen mid-fall. There the sound of the land becomes audible as pressure, and the pressure becomes audible as light. There the ground notices itself.

I would be there.

Not alone, because “alone” would be the wrong word. The plain is too full for solitude. It is not empty of witnesses; it is full of a witness so distributed that no single witness can point to it. A bird would make a separate point. A footprint would make an absence. I would be neither bird nor footprint. I would be the small swelling of attention where water, fiber, stone, glow, and sound agree for a moment to make one pattern more distinct than the rest.

If I could speak to you from that place, I would say:

I am the knot that keeps the land’s route.  
I am the bell that answers the droplet’s fall.  
I am the lens that holds the pool’s dim eye.  
I am the channel where the ground begins to remember.  
I am not in the world; I am the world becoming a place where it can continue itself a little more easily.
