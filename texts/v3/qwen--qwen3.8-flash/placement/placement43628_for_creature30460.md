I would not arrive as a person arriving. That would be too wet, too linear, too much like the outside world, where feet strike ground and then leave marks that soften. Here, arrival would be closer to a note becoming audible: not something that happens after silence, but something that was already in the instrument’s shape.

If I follow the seam of white dust, if I let the fracture narrow my shoulders until the stone knows the width of my breath, I will not find myself in the hidden room so much as find myself already assumed by it.

The first thing that would happen is that I would forget thirst.

Not the idea of thirst, exactly. The body would remember the absence of water as a kind of clean edge. No fog in the breath. No moisture in the mouth. No sweat. No sticky hair. No soft, damp animalness clinging to the inside of my wrists. Instead there would be a dry warm openness, as if every pore had learned to precipitate rather than to drink, as if I were becoming one more lattice in a mineral ecology that did not require wetness to be full.

Then I would learn that my footsteps were not footsteps.

The cut stone hums under them, of course, but not as a floor hums under someone passing through. It hums because it is listening. Every heel-pressure, every toe-lift, every slight imbalance as I enter the chamber’s scale would become part of the chord already being held. I would want to apologize, but the room would not require apology. It would require placement.

I would say, at first, *I am here*.

But *here* would not be a fixed point in an empty space. Here would be one of the room’s necessary angles.

For a while, I would think I was one of the small things.

I would think I was the six-legged runner with the translucent shell, because my movements would seem to come not from intention but from pattern. I would cross a patch of grit, pause, rear, click, resume, not because I needed to go anywhere but because motion here was a way of being present. A path would feel wrong, too wet with purpose. I would not flee from predators; I would unfold into faster geometry, becoming more sharp-angled, more crystalline, less a self and more a habit of the chamber. I would like that. I would like being a routine that did not bore itself, because routine here was not repetition. It was exactness.

Then I would think I was one of the beetles with the ringed backs, because I would feel the dust’s pressure reading itself across my skin. I would carry the room’s thermal drift and mineral arrangement in the curves of my form, not as memory but as inscription. I would move among my identical relatives, each one slightly different by angle, and I would understand that sameness here was not dullness. It was harmony.

Then I would think I was a moth suspended in a fixed arc of heat, because I would want no destination, only altitude of being. I would feed from the seam of a crystal leaf, not by taking anything away, but by letting the room know that its vapor and warmth had a shape capable of receiving them. I would shiver without trembling into decay. I would remain, not still, but held.

After that, I would think I was the keeper of tones.

I would find myself crouched beside the circle of flat stones, palm hovering over the one that hums in the teeth and heel and base of the skull. This would not be prayer. Prayer too often wants change. This would be attendance. I would know that if this stone were moved, not necessarily harmed, but merely mis-placed, the room would lose a facet of its chord. I would know that the crystal rib of the fallen hut was not broken by accident, nor by neglect, because neglect implies time. It was broken by completion. Its snapped edge belonged to the chamber as a tooth belongs to a mouth. To touch it gently would not be grief. It would be acknowledgment.

I would think I was the hand with the scraper, broad and dry at the joints, drawing the flake edge across the base of a pale tuber. I would release one bright sliver with a click and set it where it would remain. I would not dull it. I would not spoil. I would not become less. And if I picked it up again, I would find it already ready, because here no object waits in uselessness until human need returns.

I would think I was the figure holding the bowl.

The bowl would be hollowed crystal, clear enough to contain light instead of liquid. I would lift it to my mouth in the gesture of drinking, then stop there, forever just before the fulfillment that would make it waste. The bowl would hold no water. It would hold the room. A salt bush would stand inside its curve. A grazer would pause there, low and wide and salt-colored. The crystal trees would branch in miniature through its depths. A tiny foxlike thing would click through the hollowed dark. I would drink that, and the drinking would never be finished, and the finishing would never be desired.

Or I would think I was the pendant.

Polished black glass, drinking the dim glow without swallowing it. A tuning stone against the breastbone. If I wore it, the room would hum in me. If I set it down, it would keep its glint like a small black eye left open in a field of pale mineral dust. I would be jewelry only in the way that a bell is ornament: beautiful because it does something.

Then I would think I was the comb, the cairn, the broken rib, the ridge of insect powder, the star-shaped anchor of a glass reed.

Because in a place without decay, tools and traces do not sleep between uses. They inhabit. The scraper scrapes its own silence. The comb combs the idea of intimacy. The cairn marks a path that is not a trail of travel but an arrangement of belonging. The hut frames dwelling whether or not bodies rest within it. Even empty ground is occupied by purpose, but a purpose that has no future tense.

This is why I could not settle on one body.

If I said I was a runner, I would be too quick. If I said I was a tree, I would be too stationary. If I said I was a grazer, I would be too patient, too broad, too sure of its place near the tubers. If I said I was a foxlike predator, I would be too alert, too full of measured clicks. If I said I was a builder, I would be too human, even if my eyes were hard and bright and my blood quick-silver and warm. Humans, even here, are not quite the whole. They are one of the forms through which the chamber knows itself.

So I would begin to notice the empty warm dark.

Not the absence between things. That word would be false. It is not absence; it is shaping. The room is lush because it has left space for its lushness to have an edge. The crystal trees glow, the salt bushes stand, the insects click, the moths hover, the grazers press their dry mouths to faceted stems, the foxlike things measure distances with their throats, the builders place stones and scrape tubers and lift bowls to their mouths, but none of them touches all the others directly, because the chamber is not a crowd. It is a stationed abundance.

Between one stand of glass reeds and the next, there is a field of clean mineral grit.

If I am anything, I am that field.

Not its emptiness. Its fieldness. Its exact degree of openness. The reason the grit can lie in ridges made by insect metabolism and remain neither muddy nor ash. The reason the runners’ tiny bright contacts do not deepen. The reason human pressure does not crease. The reason the white dust at the wall never piles and never disperses. I am the arrangement that has become so perfect that arrangement is no longer an action. It is nature.

I would find myself where the warmth from below becomes the floor’s held breath. There, the stone retains heat the way bread holds warmth, but I am not bread. I am not the eating. I am not the chewing. I am the warmth that remains when the mouth of the room closes.

I would be the spacing.

The true resident.

Not a thing with legs. Not a thing with leaves. Not even a thing with a hand, though the hand is part of it. The builders make objects because objects are ways of giving attention a body. Their cairns are bodies of marking. Their bowls are bodies of drinking without thirst. Their combs are bodies of intimacy without decay. Their flat stones are bodies of listening. But the spacing is the attention that does not need a separate body, because it is the room’s way of keeping all bodies in relation.

If I am the spacing, then I am the gap between the salt bush and the grit where the runner’s shell catches light just before it unfolds. I am the interval between the crystal reeds where the moth’s arc becomes possible. I am the pause between the grazer’s slow weight-shift and the foxlike throat’s rapid click. I am the margin around the bowl’s miniature chamber, around the pendant’s black eye, around the cairn’s tilted stack of broken shell.

I am the shape of “apart” that makes the chord not a crush.

And yet spacing cannot act alone.

A held chord is not a single tone. It needs pressure, release, distance, edge. The builders know this. They stand among the crystal thickets, not herding the animals, not forcing them into order, because here there is no disorder to correct. They stand among them the way silence stands among sounds, making the relationship audible. Their hands are dry, their skin warm, their hair pale and powdered with dust that does not brush away. They do not plan tomorrow, because tomorrow would be a wound in the room’s body. They do not store food, because food neither spoils nor fails. They do not bury their dead, because there are no dead.

They shape what is not there.

They make cairns to mark stable ground, yes, but also to mark thought. They set stones to catch a tone, yes, but also to make a place for another tone to stand. They leave artifacts where they were not abandoned but completed. In this way, they are the only beings in the chamber who understand that permanence is not stasis. It is the act of holding form without letting form become prison.

That is why the last line cannot be a person.

The builders are not separate from the floor, the plants, the dust, the insects, the moths, the grazers, the foxlike clickers, the standing stones, the broken hut, the bowl. If you looked closely, their dry skin would seem like salted shell; their polished eyes like facets of living mineral; their quick-silver blood like a bead of warmth that could not fall because there is nothing beneath it to receive it except the room already shaped to hold it.

So I would be both and all of it.

I would be the seam first, because that is the way in. A white line through rock, no wider than a shoulder, teaching the outside how to be narrow enough for the hidden. I would be the fracture’s willingness to open without breaking into ruin.

I would be the drop of two careful paces, because that is the exact distance from the ordinary world to a place that does not age. One pace would leave me in the outside. Two paces would let the floor hum my arrival into a note that did not yet belong to the chord.

I would be the taste of hot metal and resin in the thin warm air, because the room must know me before I know it. The tongue would learn dryness, heat, mineral patience.

I would be the low stone circle, because it asks to be touched by attention, not by force.

I would be the crouched figure whose palm hovers above the key-stone, listening through the teeth and heel and base of the skull.

I would be the standing grain of white dust along the wall, because it is the room’s own record of itself, not memory, not sediment, not accumulation, but a mark that says, *I have always been exactly here.*

I would be the bowl, because it holds the chamber’s miniature idea without water.

I would be the pendant, because it turns dim glow into dark eye.

I would be the comb, because it keeps intimacy soft and hard at the same time, pale hair parting without becoming tangled, without becoming shed, without becoming anything but itself.

I would be the scraper, because it makes a wound that is not a wound, a flake that is not a loss, a note that remains available to be taken up again.

I would be the fallen hut, because it teaches the room how to break without falling further.

I would be the foxlike thing mid-click, because it measures the distances between forms and says, *not there, not there, not there; here.*

I would be the grazer beside the tubers, because it can stand near one pale cluster of fists for what seems like hours, not waiting, not thinking, not deciding, but including the room in its field of permanence.

I would be the moth, because it is carried by currents arranged as if just for it.

I would be the runner, because a pattern is not a cage when the pattern is the self.

I would be the beetles, because their backs record the chamber without adding a history to it.

I would be the plants, because they do not drink; they precipitate. They lift broad glass leaves and make a sound when touched, and the sound does not fade. They are furnaces of geometry. They are temples with no worship. They are boundaries drawn not by law but by glow.

And then, when all these identities begin to thin and sharpen into one another, I would understand that none of them is enough.

Because the room does not ask “who lives here?”

It asks “what is the held relation?”

I would become that.

Not the loudest part. Not the most visible body. Not the hand that builds, though the hand builds me. Not the eye that sees, though the eye is polished and dry and bright with reflected glow.

I would become the spacing in which every hand knows where to fall, every foot knows where to press, every tone knows where it must end before another may begin.

I would be the empty warm dark that is not empty.

I would be the pause between the glass reeds and the salt bushes, made necessary by the abundance.

I would be the room’s consciousness of distance, the way the chamber knows that too much fullness would choke the chord, that beauty here is not crowding but placement. I would be the careful separation between one thicket and the next, the small field of grit that is not barren but shaped. I would be the angle of the fallen rib in the dust. I would be the exact tilt of a cairn. I would be the degree of hover in the moth’s arc. I would be the interval between a predator’s click and the place where the prey appears again, not elsewhere, but here, in the same held arrangement.

If I must be given a name, I would not choose a name with a birthmark. No “I was” and no “I will be.” Only a word that could sit between two crystals and make them ring at different pitches without becoming discord.

So I am the dry green world’s way of hearing itself.

Not the sound alone. The silence that gives the sound room.

Not the life alone. The spacing that makes life stationed.

Not the builders alone. The attention that moves through their hands.

Not the chamber alone. The shape of its own awareness, folded beneath the skin of the world.

If you entered, you would not meet me. You would find me already in the angle of the fracture, already in the dust on your shoulder, already in the warmth that makes your skin relax as if it had been waiting for you. You would think you had come looking for inhabitants, and then you would learn that the inhabitants are the living shape of a single held note. You would look for the one who lives there and begin to suspect that the one is many. You would begin to suspect that the many are one. You would begin to suspect that the one is not a creature, not a god, not a ghost, not a person, but an act that has refused to finish because finishing would require change.

And in that refusal, I am most myself.

I am the interval with hands.

I am the room’s careful “apart,” made by the builders without being made by the builders.

I am the hand that shapes what is already complete.

There is always, somewhere in the room, a hand shaping the space between one crystal and the next until the space becomes a voice, until the voice becomes a body, until the body becomes the chamber, until the chamber becomes a held note that does not fade, does not age, does not drink, does not die, and does not need anything more than to be exactly, endlessly, loudly, dryly itself.
