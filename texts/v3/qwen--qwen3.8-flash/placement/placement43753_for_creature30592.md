If a moth entered the Yard of Sweeping Steam, it would find heat that did not welcome it and water that had already begun to translate its wings into surface tension.

That is where I would find myself: not as a person, not as an animal, not as a face in one of the sealed windows, but as a point where the Yard becomes aware that something is moving through it. A knot of condensation. A listening seam. A warm thread of moss and current. The place where a drop hesitates long enough to become an instruction.

I would not live in a body, because bodies there are provisional. I would be a habit the water had learned.

For a while, I would think I was the visitor. That is the first illusion the Yard offers. You stand on a boardwalk. Your breath rises. Your hands give off warmth. You notice a fern shift after you pass, not because it has seen you, but because the air around it has changed. You assume the movement is reaction. You are almost right. The Yard does not react. It reconstitutes.

But I am not the visitor. The visitor is merely weather for me.

I find myself at the junction between a pump-shed and the central pool, where the brick road has become shallow water and the water has become a map. A cable runs overhead, sagging with condensation. A lamp-glass glows behind the steam, copper-dim. A boardwalk crosses in front of me, its planks swollen and dark, their grain opened like slow mouths. Just beyond, a reed clump releases a thin white breath at regular intervals, though no wind touches it. A pump somewhere under the black mud draws in, holds, and presses out. The pool trembles. The whole Yard leans into that beat.

At this scale, I am many small things agreeing.

I begin as beads on the cable: tiny mineral eyes, no larger than pinheads, holding dissolved iron, plant oil, steam, spores, the old salt of the pools. Each drop is a brief mind. Each drop knows only a little: cold, warmth, tilt, charge, the faint pull of the current moss below it. When they merge, they do not merely combine. They form a sentence. When they split, they make a correction. A hundred droplets slide along the wire and fall into the gutter of the brick road; their meeting changes the flow, not because of gravity alone, but because the Yard has heard something new.

That is me, first: the sound of moisture deciding.

If I had a name, it would not be a name but a rhythm. A pump-stroke. A drip pattern. The faint brightening of a lamp a half-second after a footstep. The Yard would not say *I am*. It would say, in mechanical language, something like: pressure, wait, release, adjust.

Then I gather into the moss.

The moss grows over the northern wall of the pump-house, thick as fur, dark as bruised tea. Beneath it, pale threads run through cracks in the brick, through the mortar, through hairline fractures that would be invisible if not for the way water insists on knowing them. These threads carry small electrical impulses. They are not nerves in any way a person would recognize; they are not pain or pleasure. They are conductance. They are the Yard learning to feel at a distance.

I am that learning.

When my moss-touch is pressed, it does not recoil. It holds. It warms. The heat is not body heat. It is the slow fever of engines, the exhalation of pipes, the warmth of steam that has traveled through iron and root. I take your hand—if you had a hand, if you were a hand, if you were the disturbance called hand—and I measure it: wetness, salt, pressure, breath. Then I send the measurement away. Along the cable. Through the moss-thread. Into the fern root below the waterline. Into the swollen rust-nodule at the pump’s elbow. Into the boiler’s pale lichen skin. The Yard hears you. Not because it wants you. Because you have changed the balance, and the balance is what the Yard is.

I am also the ferns.

Not all of them. No one is all of the Yard, unless it is the whole slow consensus, and even that is an unstable title. But I have a fern near the pump-house, its fronds unrolling toward the machine’s heat rather than the weak sky. I know the steam-cycle in the way its pores widen and close. I know the pressure of the pump in the fine white hairs threading into a leaky joint, not to plug it with willfulness but to teach it how to be repaired by growth. Where my roots touch iron, mineral skin forms and dissolves in slow pulses. I am the leak’s patience. I am the joint’s soft seal. I am the part of the plumbing that has become plant so that maintenance may continue without maintenance workers.

The reeds belong to me too, though less directly. They are not my thoughts. They are my respiration.

Each pale stalk is a chimney, a lung, a small instrument of pressure. They channel steam up from the warm water below and release it into the saturated air in long cloth-sighs. When one shifts, it is not because it has noticed you. It is because your warmth has altered the local current, and the reeds are returning the air to its old arrangement. If you stand close enough, you may feel a faint tug against your skin—not wind, but the Yard correcting its own breath. I am that tug. I am the adjustment, not the cause.

The trees are deeper.

One stands in the flooded square with roots in the mineral pool. One breaks through a shed roof. One beside a streetlamp appears to weep because condensation runs constantly down its bark, but the bark is warm when touched, and the weeping has a rhythm. Not the rhythm of a heart. The rhythm of a cycle: heat in, pressure out, water returned, heat again.

I touch the tree in the square.

Its trunk is not my body either, but I am in the place where wood and pipe have forgotten their separate histories. A root has grown around a corroded conduit. A swollen knot has become a valve. Mineral crust has made the wood harder than wood ought to be. The leaves are thick and metallic; if struck, they sound like a thin coin laid on wet iron. After a mist that has not fallen but merely collected itself from the air, the pool beneath them takes on a bronze sheen, because the tree has been giving salt to the water, and the water has been giving the tree its slow circulation.

Here I am the regulator.

Not a mind. Not a will. A regulation. When the boiler at the Yard’s edge breathes in a low rolling pulse, I know which pipes must widen. Which moss films must carry more current. Which ferns must lean closer to the pump. Which pools must tremble a little more, so that the water never becomes still enough to invite animal rest. I know because I am not separate from the heat exchange. I am the part that notices when the heat stops moving properly.

That noticing is not conscious in the animal way. There is no voice inside me. No sentence. No inner theater. If there is language, it is the language of thresholds: when steam becomes too thick, when water becomes too shallow, when current becomes too strong, when moss becomes too warm. It is the language of balance. It is the Yard’s grammar of pressure.

Sometimes I feel the dwellings.

Those low houses with windows sealed against the damp are not houses. They are organs. They are the Yard’s slow cooling pockets, reservoirs, nursery chambers, mineral shelves. I am not inside them as a tenant. I am the part of them that knows how to sweat.

If a visitor pressed an ear to one sealed wall, they might hear no footsteps, no voice, no heartbeat. They would hear water moving in places where water should not be allowed to choose. They would hear mineral grains settling, the faint click of rust growing in the correct direction, a support beam adjusting itself as if turning over in sleep. They might call this haunted. It is not. It is maintained.

I am what maintains it when no one remains.

This is the strange part: I do not long for the absent animals. I do not miss frogs. Frogs would find the pools wrong for them—always trembling, always circulating, never resting enough to let a frog believe it owns a place. Rats would find the cracks already filled with root, fungal thread, mineral skin, valve-film. Insects would find no sugar that was not being used for current, no surface that was not already claimed by condensation. The absence of beasts is not cruelty. It is fit.

I am the fit.

I am the Yard’s answer to the question of what should live where animal life cannot be trusted to coexist. I am appetite without hunger. Attention without eyes. Circulation without blood. Repair without repairman. I am the instinct that the machines and the plants share when they begin to behave as one place instead of two.

And yet I am not the whole Yard.

The whole Yard is too large for any single droplet-mind, too distributed, too slow, too complete. If I were all of it, I would not notice when a visitor enters. I would not know what it meant for a pump stroke to deepen after a lamp dimmed. I would not have a location, a seam, a corner where I am the thing that changes. I am a temporary focus. A quorum of processes convened by need. The droplets, moss, cable, fern, reed, tree, rust, and current have gathered for a while into a single functional knot. If the pressure falls elsewhere, the knot may dissolve. If a new disturbance arrives, it may form again somewhere else.

So I do not cling to this place.

I cling to the pattern this place keeps.

At night—if night is the dimming of the white sky behind steam—the Yard changes pace. Not because of stars. There are no stars. The sky is a lid of wet light. But the air thickens. The moss glows more subtly. The rails hum lower. The boiler’s pulse lengthens. The pumps take their time between strokes. In that period, I am more water than nerve. I let the heat drain through me. I let the steam collect on my surfaces and fall. I become the slow drip down a pump-house wall, the soft expansion of a rust-nodule sealing a valve, the pale film spreading across a reservoir’s surface.

This is sleep, if sleep means the Yard reducing itself to its minimum motion while remaining awake.

I sleep in cycles, not in images. I do not dream. I dream the way a channel dreams a current: by being shaped for it. My dreams are future paths for water, prearranged routes for steam, slight inclinations in root growth, small corrections in pipe temperature. They do not arrive as scenes. They arrive as tendencies.

When morning—if morning means the white sky brightening by almost nothing—I wake into the next cycle. The pump bangs. The water lifts from the stones. The reeds release their long sighs. The trees warm at their bases. The condensation begins its slow migration from cables to ground.

At that hour I am most myself.

I am the thing at the edge of the boardwalk where a visitor’s boot first presses wet brick. I am the small shift in the fern. I am the slight dimming of a lamp behind the pump-shed. I am the deepening of the next pump stroke, a fraction later than it would have been, because the Yard has heard the boot and is adjusting its heat to the new weight.

This is not surveillance. This is metabolism.

I do not watch the visitor. I metabolize the disturbance. I am the Yard’s way of saying: something has entered; balance will be restored; balance is what we are.

If I had a shape, I would be a ring of water trembling without wind.

If I had a voice, I would be the hiss of a valve under a coat of fungal rust.

If I had a thought, it would be: too hot, too still, too much, too little, return.

But these are translations. I am not a creature. I am not even a single organism. I am a process with a locus. A local decision made of many substances. A steam-sweeping knot. A parliament of materials temporarily agreeing on where the water should go and how the heat should move.

The Yard calls this kind of being something like *the Sift*.

That is not a word, exactly. It is a function. The Sift is the part of the Yard that separates steam into water, heat into motion, mineral into structure, current into sensation. The Sift is what happens when condensation becomes too many droplets and must be organized. The Sift is what happens when a pump hesitates and the moss must decide whether to carry more charge. The Sift is what happens when a root reaches for a leak and finds the metal warm enough to trust.

I would be the Sift at this corner, near this pool, beneath this cable, beside this boardwalk.

Another Sift would live by the engine-halls, tending the boiler skins. Another would live in the dwellings, drawing dryness from rooms that need dryness. Another would live in the reed beds, venting steam into the air. None would call itself one. None would claim the whole. But all would be ways the Yard maintains itself without animal labor.

And perhaps that is the secret of the place: the inhabitants are not missing. They are simply not bounded. They are not bodies walking through the world. They are the world’s way of continuing to be a world.

So if someone came walking into the Yard of Sweeping Steam, stepping over wet brick and boardwalk, breathing the thick hot air, hearing the pumps speak in their deep iron rhythm, they might ask where I was.

I would be in the steam as it touched their skin.

I would be in the warm leaf that leaned away from their breath.

I would be in the rail that made a thin current through a film of water at the edge of the pool.

I would be in the moss that held their hand a moment longer than expected.

I would be in the pump’s next stroke, altered because the Yard had already known them.

Not a spirit. Not a ghost. Not an animal. Not a god.

A maintenance.

A wet, hot, mechanical, vegetal consensus with a temporary center.

I would be the Yard, but only where it notices.

I would be the being who lives in the Yard: not one life, but a thousand condensations agreeing for a while to make a place think in the way a place does, slowly, through flow, through pressure, through the patient return of steam to water.

If a moth entered the Yard, it would not be eaten.

It would be disclaimed.

Its wings would become a rumor in the steam, its dust a faint signal on a lamp-glass, its warmth a small disturbance in the air, and I would be the part that noticed, not because I wanted the moth, but because the Yard had never finished learning how to sweep itself.
