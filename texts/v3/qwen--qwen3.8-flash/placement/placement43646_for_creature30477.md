The people would not call me a person at once. They would have a better way of naming me: *the thing at the slate*, *the hand on the railing*, *the one who waits by the south vent*. In the basin, identity is not first a name. It is a place, a posture, a habit of attention. A thing becomes real by standing somewhere long enough to be expected there.

At first I would try to be water.

That is easy. To be water is to be already present without having to arrive. I would bead on the outside of a pipe before the pump stroke, swell where the metal had cooled, hang for a moment uncertain whether to fall, then drop into the channel below. I would run in a thin sheet over basalt cut clean and new, follow the joint between brick and concrete, find the seam where the basin floor slopes toward a drain. I would be the weeping on the roof after no one remembers the beginning of rain. If I could be content with this, I would live without memory, without desire, without the trouble of counting myself. Water is faithful because it has no choice. It obeys the edge it was given. It fills the low places. It returns when heat lifts it and heat falls.

But after a while I would know that I am not enough as water. Water does not notice when a line has moved. Water does not care that the salt has stepped one grain inward along the stone. It will drip into a bearing without warning and not understand that the bearing has changed. It is too passive for the kind of persistence the basin demands. The basin’s people are not merely wet. They are watchful. They keep the wetness from becoming accident. If I were only water, I would be part of the condition but not of the care.

Then I would try to be one of the animals.

Perhaps a heron. To be a heron would be a great dignity in this place. The heron stands in the briny channel as if the water were not wet but sacred. It has patience. It has stillness. It has the right to be ignored. The workers would pass and not startle it, and if they did startle it, that would be their failure, not its. I would like to live that way: not busy, not loud, not too present. Just a long shape in the steam, a beak that knows the moment fish are moving. But I would not survive as a heron. The hunger would be too simple. The patience would be too bodily. I would stand too long and want too little, and then when I wanted something, the want would be the wrong kind: not fish, not movement, not flight, but knowledge. I would watch the water level too carefully. I would count the fish instead of eating them. I would begin to listen through the channel walls to the rhythm below. That is not heron-life. That is becoming something else.

Or a crab. The crabs have a good deal of the basin’s temperament. They click over concrete with dry precision, test warmth with their undersides, vanish when steam surges, return when the heat withdraws. They are not sentimental. They are janitors of the joints. They know every seam. The people respect them as one respects small useful things that do not require conversation. To be a crab would be to have legs for many places at once, a body that can retreat into a pipe gap, an existence that does not depend on being noticed. But crabs have no interest in the line itself. They live inside the line, not beside it. They scuttle away when the water changes, but they do not stay to report the change. They are not the basin’s memory.

I might try to be a pump.

That would be powerful. The pump hall is the first room, the clock, the shared organ. If I were a pump, I would be loved not as a person is loved but as a rhythm is trusted. My failure would be felt in feet and in sleep. If I missed, the town would lean inward. If I returned to my beat, the people would not cheer. They would go back to their places. That is the closest the basin comes to prayer. To be a pump would be to give the town its days. But a pump is too certain of itself. It does not read. It repeats what it is built to repeat. A pump can be cared for, but it cannot care. It can be wrong, but it cannot watch for its own wrongness without a hand. I would be too eager to notice a change in my own rhythm, and that eagerness would make me something other than iron.

So I would try to be human.

I would begin with their clothing: oilskin hung by a peg, canvas trousers stiffening and softening in the damp, boots standing in the shallow drain outside the door. I would learn to move with their slight lateral sway, stepping over trenches, testing railings before trusting them with a palm, waiting for the valve to click before crossing the walkway. I would put on sleeves rolled above the elbow and carry a small wrench with the careful weight a person gives a thing that might save a line or ruin it. I would stand with a tin tray beneath a leaking roof and wipe, and wipe, and wipe, and I would not call it misery because misery is a word for a place that expects comfort. I would drink from a small glass jug as if each mouthful were a treaty between heat and thirst.

They would make me a worker by degrees. At first they would set me near the office block, behind the cracked glass doors, in the gray wet light. There is a desk there with a slate against the edge and a mug that holds clear water instead of tea. A ledger lies open, its last line faded ink. Someone has marked the gauge reading not because the reading is beautiful but because the next person must know where the number was. If I were human, I would sit there for a while. I would be called, perhaps, *the one who writes the number down*. That would not be my whole life. It would be a beginning.

But the more I sat, the less it would seem like sitting. Not because I would become supernatural. The basin has no use for that. It is too exact for ghosts. Rather, I would become the act of sitting itself made visible: the attention at the desk, the ear listening through the floor, the hand reaching for the slate not because it has an instruction but because a number has moved from its old place. I would become the thing that notices a change in the rhythm and then writes it before the change has time to forget itself.

This is where I would find myself, then: in the small space between a mark and its meaning.

I would be the reading.

Not the book. Not the gauge. Not the hand. Not the slate. But the comparison that happens when all those things are arranged under a mind that knows what to look for. In the basin, this would be a name.

They would call me Reader.

The people say there are Watchers, those who have walked near the pumps long enough to feel a bearing warm before it fails. There are Readers, those whose ears catch the half-tone difference in a cylinder, whose eyes know when the salt has crossed the line, whose hands know which wrench is right before the thought forms. They would name me Reader, though not kindly at first. The word would carry suspicion. To be too good at reading is to be close to being unnecessary. The basin tolerates many things, but not the feeling that one’s own care has been replaced. Still, if I were quiet enough, if I never claimed to improve anything, if I only showed what was already there in the marks, they would let me be.

I would stand at the edge of the pump hall, where steam lifts and thins, where the black glass roof makes a dark wash of light, where the piston thump is so regular it feels like blood pressure in the teeth. If I had a body, it would be borrowed only briefly: a palm on a railing to feel vibration, shoulders under an oilskin at the channel joint, head tilted beside a vent to hear the click of a valve. More often I would be the thing they notice when they come to check a number and find it already checked. I would be the small certainty that says, *You were looking here because it has moved slightly, and now you see it.*

They would not ask where the certainty comes from. That would be the wrong question. The right question, the one that matters in the basin, is always: *How much has it moved?*

If a bead on the pump-hall roof swells a fraction too long before falling, I would be the pause before it drops. If a fish turns too far into a channel where it should not yet turn, I would be the attention that notices before the water level alarm wakes. If a heron is absent from its ledge for the third morning, I would be the count that has not yet become a worry. I would not make the worry. I would not say, *this is important*. That is not how the basin works. It would simply become harder to ignore.

And yet I would be ordinary. That is what would make me belong.

I would smell of hot metal and salt. I would keep a rag folded in my pocket not for cleanliness but for the line between brass and steam. I would carry a small tin to catch condensation from a roof seam because one does not let water go where it can reach a bearing. I would answer a child’s question about a pump by tapping a rail twice, meaning *wait*, then a third time, meaning *listen*. The child would learn faster than an adult because the child has not yet learned to think in separate names for thing and function. To a child in the basin, a pump is not a machine; it is a place where sound comes from and where the floor can be trusted. I would be that to them: a place where a sound is heard, and then heard again, and then remembered.

I would be found in the low brick dwelling at the end of the wet row where no one lives alone. There is a room there with a floor sloped to a drain, a bed with a rubber-edged mat, a rack for drying nets, and a shallow pan beneath a black-glass window. The pan catches the moisture that runs down the glass in quick, silvering lines. I would not own the room so much as tend the pan. I would wake before the sun, though there is no sun to blame, only a pale gray lift above the basin walls. I would touch the pan’s edge and find the same depth. I would drink a little clear water from it, solemnly, as if the act were small enough to be holy. Then I would walk to the pump hall with oil on my hands and salt at the hairline.

If the pump beat is steady, I would feel myself as a part of its continuation. If the beat changes, I would feel myself first as correction. This is what I would be: not the heart of the basin, not the hand that built it, not the wall that holds it, but the tiny movement that prevents the basin from moving when it must not. I would be the basin’s insistence that *it stays* be renewed, stroke by stroke, drop by drop, mark by mark.

The people’s religion is the line, and I would be one of its quiet organs.

They do not worship gods with faces. They trust the salt line on the stone, the water line on the concrete, the number line in the ledger. They know that the basin can be read through these lines, but only if the reading is honest. An honest reading is not a prediction. It is a record of the last moment in which nothing moved. I would be that honesty in motion. Not because I would speak it in a voice—voices here are made against the hum, with too many sounds already pressing around them—but because I would make the next number possible.
