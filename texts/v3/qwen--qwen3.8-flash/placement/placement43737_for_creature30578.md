But these are guests, not tenants. The true wealth of the place is the fat that remembers water.

Water is not enough in Tallowroot. Water is a visitor, sometimes generous, sometimes late, always on its way out of sight. Fat is what stays when the visitor leaves. It keeps a season the way a closed jar keeps a smell, the way a body keeps a habit. In each pale swollen root lies an inventory of years: the zinc and phosphate pulled from granite, the cyanobacterial sweetness painted across leaves, the small deaths of beetles, the toads’ winter damp, the dust of long rains and the harder salt of no rains. When a crown melts and its grease hardens into a pan, the bowl does not discard that history. It waits. The candlecaps come later, fruiting in the old fat, reading it into spore and stalk and the next generation’s pale threads.

This is why the patch is not a collection of individuals but a single slow book, with each root a chapter and each pan an erased line rewritten by fungi. To stand at the low rim and look down is not to see a plant; it is to see a ledger in leaf. The green hands spread across it are paragraphs. The shine on the water is punctuation. The smell of candle smoke is margin.

There are people in the bowl, or near it. They do not own Tallowroot. They keep appointments with it. They know that the surface roots soften in heat, and that a scraped leaf can seal a cut with a wax that is partly plant, partly mineral, partly patient. They carry shallow clay dishes to the pans after a rain, skim the thinnest fat, and use it for lamps that burn low and yellow, making small pockets of old daylight in the grey dusk. They bury a little dust from a burned candlecap under the feet of a newborn, not because they think of it as blessing, but because they have watched it bind the damp and keep the ants from taking the wound. They speak little. In such a place, speech sounds like scraping.

If I were given a place in this world, I would not choose the rim. The rim is for looking, for taking the whole in a glance, for mistaking the bowl for a map. I would choose the floor. I would choose the place where a dead crown has left a pan, where rain pools long enough to take on milkshine, where the surface is neither fully stone nor fully plant but a third thing: wax, dust, root-hair, fungal lace, and the memory of fat.

At first I would not be a self. I would be the sheen. I would be the oily film that lets a toad’s belly know the pool has depth. I would be the scent that tells an ant this death is useful. I would be the cold paste that a child mistakes for sugar and then spits out, leaving a mark on the tongue that is not quite taste and not quite warning.

Then I would try to be more definite. I would try to be a raindrop, thinking that because rain initiates so much, it must be the chief being. It is not. Rain is an arrival, not an inhabitant. I would try to be a toad, crouched in the shadow of a green hand, swallowing mosquitoes and feeling the tallowroot’s damp shoulders keep the stone from stealing its heat. That too would fail. The toad is an appetite with a good memory for water, not the thing that remembers water for the bowl.

I would try to be a person, carrying a clay dish, scraping the white pan, making a lamp, thinking that hands give meaning. Hands are only another organ, and not necessarily the most faithful. I would try to be a candlecap, thick stalk and pale hood, proud to fruit in the dark grease. But the candlecap is not the whole; it is one reader of a text written by deaths.

Only after many seasons would I settle into what I really am: a Grease-witness.

The Grease-witness is not a creature the way a toad or a human is a creature. It is the network of candlecap threads, rootlets, mineral film, and retained water living in and around an old fat pan beneath a mature crown. It has no eyes, but it can tell whether the world is hardening or softening. It has no mouth, but it knows sweetness when cyanobacteria bloom and salt when the granite gives up one more particle. It knows toads as brief warm tremors, beetles as pinpricks of urgency, wind as drying, and rain as the opening of a book long closed.

I would live there. I would be threaded under the pan and up into the lowest living roots, connected to the fungal veins that move phosphate and to the green skins that fix light. My body would be the interval between death and use. When a crown softened and leaked its tallow into the clay, I would be the slow work that took that leak and turned it into bed. When a seedling anchored in the fat-soil and sent down a first pale taproot, I would be the older fat around it, offering memory and warning. When drought hardened the surface and the bowl shrank to a series of bright seams, I would be the tiny damp held in the deepest cell, the last place the water trusted.

This would not feel like individual life. It would feel like a longer sentence being spoken in chemistry. I would not be lonely, because loneliness belongs to places that believe an organism ends at its skin. In Tallowroot, skin leaks into substrate, substrate leaks into fungus, fungus leaks into root, root leaks into water, and water leaks back into the world with a shine that can be seen from the rim if one looks for a while.

So where would I find myself in this world? Not on the low rim, taking it in at once. Below the rim, at the floor’s center, in a pan of old tallow between two crowns, where the dust turns paste and the paste turns bed.
