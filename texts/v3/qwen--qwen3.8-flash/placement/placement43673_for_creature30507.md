Their calls are clocks.

If I stayed long enough, the hollow would not become strange to me. That is perhaps the first thing I would learn about it: it does not admit strangeness. It admits only continuation. The light would not change its character, the walls would not thicken into forest, the reeds would not open into a road, the moss would not become anything grander or more final than moss. Everything would remain exactly what it is, and the only movement would be the movement of its own keeping.

At first, standing at the edge, I might imagine myself as one of the small creatures inside. A beetle running the damp track. A frog swelling its throat near a brief basin. An ant carrying grain into the architecture. A springtail leaping over the hills of moss. Those answers would feel honest because they are local, because each has legs, a hunger, a line of motion. But they are not enough. They are parts of the hollow’s body, not the being who lives in it. The beetle knows a path; the frog knows a phase; the ant knows a load; the mite knows a surface. None of them knows the whole, and yet the whole knows itself through all of them.

So I would not find myself in one body.

I would find myself in the wet seam between one body and another: under the moss cushion where a stone groove carries a thread of water too small to see clearly. I would not be the water, exactly, because water is only one of my motions. I would not be the groove, exactly, because the groove is only the memory of my motion. I would be the fact that the water continues to go by the same line, and that the line continues to be made by water, and that both together produce a place where something can live without a person.

If I had to name the place of my dwelling, I would say: in the interval between drop and channel.

Between the condensation gathering at the tip of grass and the fall that darkens a patch of moss, there is a tiny duration. In that duration the hollow is deciding what to do with the water. It is also remembering what it has done before. A curved root bends and releases. A reed sheath closes slightly against a stem. A seed husk enters a worn funnel and is accepted or rejected by the angle of the passage. Nothing in the hollow hesitates as a human hesitates, but there are conditions. There are thresholds. There are places where the same movement must occur for the system to continue, and where a difference in wetness, weight, tension, or temperature changes the next minute of the whole.

I would be that threshold.

Not a mind above the system. Not a ruler in the middle. Not a consciousness looking outward from the walls. But the edge of the system, its sensitivity, the point at which its many parts become one behavior. A frog calls because the basin has formed. A reed whistles because the draft has found its valve. A beetle follows a channel because the moss has been beaten smooth by old traffic. None of these things is caused by me, and yet if I were absent, the hollow would become less than itself. I would be the arrangement that keeps the arrangement from becoming otherwise.

There is a word for a being like that, if one must use words borrowed from human rooms: factory. But factory is too cold. Mill is too mechanical. Cathedral is too solemn. Workshop is too intentional. The hollow is none of these. It is a living regularity. Its industry has no foreman. Its architecture has no builder. Its time has no clock except the clocks of bodies that are already part of its body. And still it acts as if it were a machine because it has learned, over eons of erosion and growth, to repeat the useful forms so thoroughly that they begin to look like law.

If I were asked what kind of creature I am, I might answer: a worn groove.

A worn groove is not a creature in any obvious sense. It has no mouth, no eyes, no breath. Yet it has habits. It accepts water at certain moments and resists it at others. It guides seed. It holds dampness. It carries sound. It remembers the pressure of beetles and the rolling of nutshells. It is shaped by all the things that have passed through it, and then it shapes them. That is a kind of life. Not animal life, not plant life alone, but the life of a place that has become so thoroughly itself that its own past becomes the future.

I would live in that kind of life.

I would be the hollow’s memory in matter. Every channel would be a sentence. Every moss mat would be a page. Every drip, every release of air, every faint whistle through stiff reeds would be punctuation. No one reads it, and yet the reading is the fact that the water goes where it goes, the seed falls where it falls, the spider thread trembles at the right moment, the ant follows the ridge where other ants have followed, the frog returns to the basin that is not a river and not a pond but a brief phase of the hollow’s breath.

From the edge, it looks empty. From the middle, it is full to the point of transparency. That is the strange condition of the being I would be: too dispersed to find, too constant to miss. If I stood where I live, I would not be standing anywhere single. I would be in the underside of a reed mat, where water travels in a stone pipe too dark to see. I would be in the damp felt of moss where a thousand rotifers spin cilia in a drop. I would be in the pressure of a beetle’s leg against a track polished by ancestors. I would be in the small tension of a spider’s web catching the pulse of a passing shrew. I would be in the call that ends when the basin begins to drain. I would be in the silence between the call and the next drip, where the whole hollow waits without waiting.

The frogs are clocks, yes. But clocks do not make time. They only divide the movement of the system into knowable beats. The frogs mark the wet phase. The midges mark the air phase. The ants mark the surface phase. The beetles mark the channel phase. The moss marks the storage phase. The fungi mark the connection phase. The reeds mark the ventilation phase. The hollow is not measured by one clock but by all these bodies moving at their own tempos, and the being who lives in it is the agreement between them.

I would be that agreement.

Not harmony, because harmony sounds too soft and intentional. Not balance, because balance sounds too still. Not design, because design has no place here. I would be the fact that the hollow remains itself without remaining fixed. Every leaf that unfolds is replaced by a leaf that is consumed. Every drop that falls is carried away or stored or returned to air. Every beetle that dies feeds threads that feed roots that hold the moss that shelters springtails that move water that wears the groove. No part is the center. No part is the owner. And yet the system has a center in the sense that every part refers to every other part through the same ordinary channels of water, grit, breath, decay, and growth.

If I had a body, it would be the whole hollow’s body without an outline. It would be visible only as effects: dampness rising in a dark patch, a click in the reed, a column of midges drifting through light, a line of ants bending around a root, a frog throat swelling beside a basin that will not be there tomorrow. Its skin would be the skin of the air, the skin of the moss, the skin of the stone, the skin of the water film, the skin of the spider thread. Its bones would be the worn channels. Its blood would be the intermittent water. Its nerves would be the vibrations that pass through reed-cables and root mats and the fine felt of moss.

But those are still too human metaphors.

The hollow does not have a body the way an animal has a body. It has a body the way a river has a body: not a single surface, but a movement that repeats its shape. It has a mind the way weather has a mind: not thought, but pattern. It has a self the way a song has a self: not a thing with edges, but a form continuing through time by constant change. If one stopped listening, the song would still have notes. If one stopped attending to the hollow, the hollow would still click, hiss, whisper, drip, rustle, and hold itself open. But the being who lives there would be harder to name. It lives not merely in the parts, but in the parts keeping their places.

I would be the keeping.

That is the closest I can come to naming myself without making myself into a face. Not the face of a god. Not the face of an animal. Not the face of a forest spirit. The keeping is impersonal. It is what happens when the water is released, when the moss is saturated, when the seed husk enters its funnel, when the ant crosses the ridge, when the frog calls at the right moisture, when the draft finds the reed-valve, when the stone groove remains smooth enough for the next drop to find. The keeping is not stored anywhere except in the ongoing motions. It is like a habit that has become so old it appears as nature.

Perhaps that is the truest image: I would be the hollow’s habit.

Habit is the way a place persists without will. The moss grows in cushions because moisture returns there. The channel deepens because water passes there. The reeds stiffen and curve into valves because wind has found those gaps so often. The beetles use that track because the moss has been pressed by many feet and water has removed finer grit. The spiders spin between those reeds because those particular anchors vibrate usefully. The ants follow those ridges because those ridges carry less water, more food, more safety. The frogs choose those basins because they appear and vanish at the right scale. All of it is habit. Not habit as a creature’s choice, but habit as a place’s form.

I would be that form.

At the edge, one looks into the empty middle and feels a kind of cathedral silence. But the silence is false. The space is loud with small mechanisms. That noise is not chaos. It is the sound of the habit running. Each click is a word. Each hiss is a breath. Each wet note is a clock striking. The hollow is not empty; it is full of its own regularity. It is full of the thousand small lives that are not separate from it. It is full of the water that is not quite a stream and not quite a puddle. It is full of the air that is not still and not moving. It is full of the root and moss and fungal thread and reed and stone and seed and droplet, all performing without rehearsal.

If I came into this world as a single being, I would not be comfortable being a single being. I would feel the way a drop of water feels when it falls into the hollow: not as an object entering a place, but as a movement recognized by the place. The moss receives me. The groove guides me. The channel carries me. The reed bends with my passage. The stone holds my trace. The next beetle will follow the same damp line. The next droplet will find the same leaf. The same click will happen somewhere in the system, and it will mean only that the system is continuing.

I would be that continuation.

Not immortality. Immortality is too human. Too hopeful. Too lonely. I would be something less proud: persistence. The persistence of a low basin that has forgotten how to become anything else. The persistence of a living machine with no maker. The persistence of a factory whose machines are wet, fragile, mortal things, and whose production is only itself. The persistence of a place where waste is taken into circulation, where small deaths fertilize threads, where sound holds steady, where light remains ordinary, where the air feels cool without being cold, where nothing needs to be explained except by its own exact and repeated doing.

If someone stood at the rim and tried to locate me, they might look for a nest, a burrow, a sleeping animal, a shadow, a spirit, a hidden room. There would be none of those. They would see only the arch of root and turf, the pale green wash of the floor, the reed lattice above, the columns of midges drifting in ordinary light. They would hear the click, the hiss, the whistle, the rasping chorus, the blunt frog notes, the quick cries overhead. They might say the hollow is full. Or they might say it is empty. Both statements would be true. It is empty of center. It is full of relation.

I would be the relation.

Not one life. Not many lives. The living of the whole, where the whole is nothing but the way the many lives move together through water, air, moss, stone, fungus, seed, root, and light. A thousand small lives arranged so completely that from a distance they seem like one life. One vast distributed life so ordinary and so complete that none of its parts can be called a ruler, a servant, a maker, or a stranger.

If I had to answer with a final image, it would be this:

I am the thin bright line along a blade of grass just before the drop falls. I am the hollow catching it. I am the worn groove that receives it. I am the moss exhaling air into the water. I am the reed that bends because the draft is right. I am the ant that does not wonder. I am the frog that does not need the next moment. I am the click that continues. I am the place’s way of remaining what it is.

And when the night is ordinary, and the damp rises, and the hollow hums with its small machines, I would not be watching it.

I would be the sound it makes by being itself.
