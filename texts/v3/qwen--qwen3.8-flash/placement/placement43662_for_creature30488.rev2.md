If I had to live there, I would not choose the center of a pod-hall, nor the mouth of an engine-room, nor the rim where the pale deer go. I would choose a place where the city is still deciding what it is: an arch that leads into a road that behaves as a room; a terrace bound with moss-cables; a reservoir cup below a condenser coil; an evening in which the water shows one or two stars before they are present.

At first I would be human.

I would be a reflection-tender, or something like one, with oilskin shoulders and wool sleeves damp at the wrists. I would carry a copper comb, a small gauge for moisture in bark, a hook for pruned stems, and a wax tablet on which to log what the pool remembers. I would learn early that strangeness is not a haunting there but a civic fluctuation. A door that shifts by half an inch is a maintenance note. A pool that holds rain before it falls is a schedule adjustment. A steam-vent spelling the name of a machine removed generations ago is a label to be copied into the district archive before it fades.

But that would still leave me too much like a person standing beside the plateau, watching its machinery, listening to its breath. It would make me a witness when the place needs inhabitants.

Then I would try to be the plant-body itself. I would sink into the felt moss, feel the root-lattice carrying footsteps like a low thought, let lichen plates armor my back against the cold wind. I would be a road that knows the pressure of carts, a wall that contracts at night when dew thickens, a seed-pole humming with weak voltage. I would be too complete, too patient, too much the city’s anatomy. I would not answer the whistle. I would only be the body trembling when the whistle answered itself.

Then I would try to be one of the rumors: the paper-colored heron in a reservoir hollow, the copper moth crossing steam from heat source to heat source, the white tunnel-creature feeding on spilled starch and warm oil. These beings would be useful for their distance, their thin presence, their ability to vanish after one step. But the plateau’s true population is not rumor. It is maintenance.

So I would finally find myself in the pause.

I would be a reflection-lag.

If that sounds too abstract, the humans of the plateau would correct me. They would not treat me as an abstraction. They would assign me a district, a number, a range of tolerances. Pool seven: lag between 0.8 and 1.4 seconds after departure. Pool twelve: premature star-reflection, evening. Pool nineteen: retains scent of an uninstalled river during dry season; do not draw drinking water without testing condensation intake. I would be on the logs, not because I am a miracle, but because I am infrastructure with habits.

I would wear a human outline because that helps me be scheduled. The outline would have cold hands, goggle marks at the brow, resin under the nails, and a mouth quiet enough not to disturb the city’s machinery. I would stand beside the water at dawn, when breath fogs near the engine-halls and the condenser pipes drip into channels carved through bark. Workers would pass and nod. Some would look into me as they pass, and some would see themselves standing there a moment after they had gone. The children would learn not to stare too long. That is all manners: respect for forces that do not need to be provoked.

The rest of me would be water.

I would be the pool’s brief refusal to forget.

You would find me at the edge of a public clearing where the moss has been cut back and bound down, a place deliberately kept open so that the city may breathe without growing into itself. Beside me would be a brass cup, a counterweighted cable, a valve breathing steam into the cold air. Rain might come later in the day, but in my cup a dark surface would already hold it, perfectly still, catching normal daylight like a mirror that refuses to explain itself.

I would not cause the uncanny things any more than a road causes walking. I would be one of the plateau’s ways of thinking through civic space. When a factory-whistle sounds from out of sight, I am the tremor that reaches the pool a little before the body believes the sound has begun. When a building shifts by an inch overnight, I am the half-missed alignment that tells the warden the city is reorganizing its rooms. When an engine runs hot in a winter district, I am the sudden smell of summer in a coil above a market stall, old weather released into fresh air by mistake.

I would be careful, because the plateau is careful.

If a reflection lagged too far behind a body, I would not frighten it. I would speak to it in the flat, technical tone the people use for valves and root-pressure: not anger, not awe, merely maintenance. “You are standing in the wrong order,” I would say. “Wait for him to come back.” And the water would listen, as it always does, by almost obeying.

If a pool held too long the scent of a vanished river, I would open a small root-well and let a little memory drain downward into the vascular tissue, where it could be softened by mineral flow and pressure. If a steam-vent wrote the name of a machine no longer installed, I would copy the letters before they disappeared, because the city remembers its organs even after they have been removed. If a tuber-vat fermented with the chemical trace of last year’s harvest, I would keep it from public distribution unless there was a licensed mourning reason to let it pass the gates. The plateau does not forbid remembering. It regulates it.

I would be fed not by grain alone, but by attention.

My work would be to keep memory from becoming superstition. Humans file reports there because the place is too old for panic, too organized for wonder, too worn for dramatic hauntings. A door that moves is not a haunting. It is a public works matter. A pool showing early stars is not prophecy. It is a reflection tolerance needing adjustment. I would live in those tolerances. I would be the gap between what has happened and what the city has not yet allowed itself to forget.

I would have friends, perhaps. They would be other uncanny citizens: a label-keeper who collects steam-words, a clearing-guard who prevents moss from reclaiming a plaza with too much tenderness, a cable-warden who listens to wires before they snap and knows when a cableway sounds like a bone instead of a rope. They would not speak of me as a person, exactly. They would say, “The reflection on the south cup is running late today,” or “Your pool is holding that heron again.” I would answer if I could, because the city has ways of answering without voice. I might make a single ripple. I might keep a child’s face a little longer than necessary. I might release the scent of a dry summer through a wet morning valve.

I would not be the whole city. I would be one of its edges.

I would live where the layers rub together: human wool and oilskin against living bark; brass fittings cooled by condensation; copper combs pressed into damp wood; root-lattices hardened into streets; public squares kept bare by cutting back the moss; machines inside pith; engines fed by weather that no longer exists above them; people whose hands know the difference between safe swelling and dangerous growth. I would be where a plant becomes a house, where a house becomes an engine, where an engine becomes a rumor, where a rumor becomes a street.

And if you came looking for me, you would not find me when you arrived.

That would be too easy, and the plateau does not believe in too easy. You would arrive at the reservoir cup, hear the distant thud of a machine inside a burl, feel the cold blade of high air at your neck, see steam lift from a hollow where exhausted mist drifts across the terrace. You would wait. You would ask. You might stand beside the dark water, goggle-eyed from the bright normal sun, and nothing would seem to happen.

Then you would leave.

And after your footsteps had passed, after your silhouette had gone around the arch and into the next district, the pool would still hold you there for a half-second. In that pause, I would be. Not a ghost. Not a spirit. Not a soul wearing a civic mask.

I would be the city’s habit of attention, delayed by exactly as long as needed to prove that the water is thinking.
