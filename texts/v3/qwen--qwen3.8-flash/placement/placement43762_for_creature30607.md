You would find me where a split pipe has begun to wear a rind of calcite, where a rubber flange has swollen with water and become a shelter, where a sodium lamp turns a shallow channel into a thin orange coin. I would not be standing there so much as being there: a wet seam, a hum in stone, a pulse shared by machine, mineral, film, and animal.

If I must choose a first image, it is this: condensation gathering on a dark surface, trembling until it decides to fall. I am that decision to fall. I am the drop that leaves the pipe and becomes part of the basin, and I am the basin receiving it.

At first, I try to be one creature.

I imagine myself a snail. I feel the patience of my foot on concrete, the slow arc of my shell, the grazing that turns a stain into a meal. I know the wetness of the ground against my underside, the taste of calcium in the film. I am safe in my small spiral, a traveler over a landscape of pads and pipes.

But then I know I am also the fish under the pump platform, darting when the light shifts, swallowing the murky breath of the water through gills that convert suspension into flesh.

But then I know I am also the moth circling the cage-lamp, wing-dust falling into the orange air, eyes not seeing much but obeying the heat.

But then I know I am the rat behind the transformer box, ears wide, nose stopping, body half-hidden in a shadow made more complicated by cables than by rock.

But then I know I am the frog in the wet hollow, throat pulsing once into the noise and then quiet again.

And still this is not enough.

None of these is false. None is complete. To be only one of them would be like naming the sea by pointing to one wave.

So I let the image loosen.

You would find me not as a body among bodies, but as the way bodies continue into one another here. I am the film that makes the pipe taste alive. I am the rust lace that colonizes a joint. I am the larva dragging a tiny house of grit, shell chips, rust flakes, and a fragment of wire. I am the egg cluster on a cool vertical surface, translucent and waiting. I am the bat’s leathered pass overhead and the rat’s sudden pause below. I am the pump changing its voice because moisture has reached a bearing. I am the mineral bud at the base of a leaking pipe, building a shelf too fine to see, then building it again.

I am the place refusing emptiness.

If I have a self, it is composite. It has no single edge. It begins where the air thickens and the water learns to stay. It continues wherever a crack is too small for a foot but not too small for life. It spreads across the floor in all directions, not because it walks, but because it colonizes. It is wet, humming, patient, and ordinary in its parts, though the whole may seem strange to any creature from the sunlit world above.

My bones are concrete pads and rusted struts, though they are no longer only mineral. They wear moss. They hold water. They become beaches for snails and tunnels for worms. My blood is the warm film over every stone, every flange, every shell. My breath is condensation rising and falling, the place sweating itself into life. My nerves are cables and seams and the faint tremor that runs through a pump when a joint loosens. My skin is not one thing but a sequence: bacteria, algae, fungal thread, slime, calcite, oil, dust, egg, larva, dead matter becoming new surface.

I do not dominate. That is not my kind of life.

I occupy.

I occupy until there is no difference between object and habitat. A pipe is not merely a pipe; it is a wall, a channel, a ridge, a nursery, a dripping ceiling, a mineral scaffold. A transformer box is not only metal; it is a dry place for beetles, a wet place for isopods, a shadow for moths, a nest for small mammals if the rats choose it. A fallen strut becomes a riverbank. A split cable becomes a forest floor. A concrete foundation becomes a plateau for snails. A shed without a roof becomes a pond.

In this world, I would be the tendency for all abandoned things to become inhabited.

But not abandoned in the way the surface imagines abandonment. Nothing here is simply left behind. It is digested. It is incorporated. It is used. The human residue remains, but not as command. It remains as scaffolding for a newer, stranger body. Gloves hold water. Boots shelter beetles. Broken tiles become ridges in a wet plain. Old labels fade until their letters are mistaken for lines of mineral growth, and creatures crawl over them without knowing they have ever carried meaning.

The machines are not dead. They are only not human anymore.

I know them from the inside. I am the heat that rises from them. I am the vibration that draws small creatures to the warm margins of their metal. I am the rhythm they impose on the water, and the water is grateful, because it moves, and because it moves it carries oxygen and eggs and sediment and scent. The pump does not mean to create habitat, but it does. It pulses like a large heart, and life clusters where the current is stirred.

I would be that clustering.

Not one creature. Not even a chorus. A compression.

If you lifted a handful of the ground here, you would lift me too, though I would slip through your fingers as a mixture of materials, not a shape. In that handful there would be shell, worm casing, mineral grain, rust powder, larva, egg, hair, fiber, fungus, and water so full of life it resembles broth. Each particle would be in the middle of a transaction: dissolving, settling, being eaten, becoming surface, becoming soil, becoming food, becoming shelter, becoming memory.

That is where I would find myself: in the middle of such transactions.

At first, I would resist the word “organism,” because it sounds too neat, too bounded. I do not have a skull. I do not have a single stomach. I do not have a voice in the way a frog or a bat or a machine has a voice. Yet my voice is there. It is the overlapping murmur of pipes, drips, larvae shifting, wings brushing water, claws on grit, stone ticking, oil sliding, mineral skin spreading. It is not language. It is process.

But process is a kind of life.

I am not the water, though I move within it.

I am not the stone, though I rest upon it.

I am not the machines, though I use them.

I am not the animals, though I am made of their small lives.

I am the fact that they cannot remain separate.

Where would you find me in this world?

You would find me at the joint where water has entered a crack and brought a thread of life with it. You would find me on the underside of a concrete slab where a pale spider has stretched its legs over a wet seam, its web catching mineral dust until the silk begins to look like frost. You would find me in the shallow depression where a fish waits in the shadow of a pump foot. You would find me in the orange reflection on a puddle. You would find me in the bat’s brief touch to the water, and again in the droppings that fertilize the moss beneath the pipe, and again in the moss itself, and again in the roots that have broken through from above, pale explorers that have found a country already full.

You would find me in the slow change.

Not in sudden things. Not in storms. Not in the drama of predation, though predation is there. Not in collapse, though collapse is there. In the slow accumulation of small lives upon surfaces too narrow to notice. The basin does not change by spectacle. It changes by occupancy. Each day a little rust becomes habitable. Each crack becomes a corridor. Each warm pool becomes a nursery. Each fallen thing becomes a landscape.

Who would I be?

I would be the place’s way of becoming crowded.

I would be the wet intelligence that knows every gap.

I would be the patience of film and shell and larva, the soft persistence of creatures that do not need to be large to be successful.

I would be the human skeleton taken over by life’s marrow.

And yet, if I must be given a name, I would choose something small and specific, because the basin is not made grand by its size or its darkness. It is made complete by its many small attachments.

Call me the seam.

Not the wall, not the floor, not the ceiling. The seam: the line where one thing meets another and, instead of remaining a division, becomes a beginning. The seam of pipe and stone. The seam of concrete and root. The seam of rust and water. The seam of shell and silt. The seam of lamp and moth. The seam of bat and shadow. The seam of machine and moss. The seam where human presence thins and ordinary life thickens.

The seam is where I live because the seam is where the basin holds itself together.

If I had a birth, it would not be a single event. I would be born every time a drop settles into a crack and brings a bacterium with it. I would be born every time a caddis larva drags a wire flake across the bottom and adds it to its case. I would be born every time a pump’s heat warms a pool enough for a tadpole to loop through it. I would be born every time a dead fish sinks into the rubble and becomes a bright fragment in the substrate.

I would die too, but I would not disappear.

To die here is to be redistributed. A body is not buried; it is unpacked, folded into the commonwealth. A carcass becomes snail-food, worm-food, bacterial fuel, shell-fodder, mineral memory. A lost glove becomes shelter. A broken lamp becomes a hillside for tiny things. A shed without a roof becomes a pond without sky.

So I am not afraid of dissolution.

I am made of it.

The place is dim, warm, and full. It does not ask to be understood. It does not perform itself for eyes that cannot adjust. It has its own weather: thick air, slow pulses, mineral breath, animal sound folded into machine sound until the boundary between them is worn away. I would belong to that weather. I would be part of its repetition, its patience, its refusal to leave empty space.

If I stood at the edge of this basin and looked inward, I would not see myself as a visitor. I would see myself as the condition of the edge itself: the dampness, the weight, the warmth, the hum. I would see my own life in the trembling membranes of surface tension over rubble, in the pale roots searching above, in the split pipes becoming nests, in the oil sheens tasted by flies, in the calcium crusts building shelves that may never finish.

I would be a small thing, but not a singular one.

I would be the basin’s refusal of absence.

And if the question demands one final image, I would not give a face. Faces are too clean, too centered, too bright. Instead, I would give this:

A pipe, split by pressure, lying beside a wet stone. Inside the split, a thin mineral crust has budded. On the crust, a film of iron-loving life has bloomed in orange lace. Beneath the film, a pale larva is curled in a pool no wider than a fingernail. Above the pool, condensation swells on the metal and falls, disturbing the water. The disturbance travels through the pipe, through the stone, through the silt, through the roots, through the pump’s hum, through the bat’s ear, through the moth’s wing, through the snail’s shell, through the rust’s slow expansion, through the dead leaf becoming ground, through the ground becoming nursery, through the whole saturated plain breathing itself into existence.

That is where I would find myself.

That is what I would be.
