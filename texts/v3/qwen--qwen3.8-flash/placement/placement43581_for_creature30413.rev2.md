I would be a wet line with legs.

Not one of the names on a sign, not one of the species a quiet person could point to and count in a ledger at dawn. I would be one of those habits the valley grows when it needs something to happen in a certain place at the right time. If a name were necessary, it would not be a name at all but a sound: the ring of a metal rod on a ceramic insulator, followed by a long pause. The herds would understand it. The birds would understand it, even if they moved away before it finished. The fish would not understand it, because fish live in timing, and the sound would already be late or already too early for the channel they were about to occupy.

I would find myself on the high edge between a dry quarter and a wet one. The place would not feel like a border in the old human sense. It would feel like a surface leaning slightly forward, waiting for a decision to fall upon it. Under my feet, the deck would be pale composite, laid in broad plates with seams too regular to be natural, yet worn here and there by years of hooves, wind, dripping condensation, and the slow dragging of things that had not yet learned whether they were animals or debris. The plates would be grays and chalk whites, with veins of ochre where mineral dust had settled into the fractures. Somewhere near me, a shallow channel would run, not because rain had made it, but because the valley had decided, at some administrative depth, that water might be more useful if it could move.

I would stand with one foot in shadow and one in light.

This would not mean one foot in cold and one in warmth. Not exactly. It would mean one foot in the place that was real and one foot in the place that was about to be real, or perhaps had already been real and had not yet admitted it. The bright side would be blindingly dry. The shadowed side would glisten as if recently rinsed, though the air above it was clear. This happens often there, in that valley of machines and weather and unfinished matter. The world does not always wait for its events. Sometimes it arranges the effects first, then lets causes scramble into place.

I would be one of the quiet people, if “people” means bodies with eyes and hands and the ability to choose where to stand. I would be a keeper of thresholds, a reader of wet lines, a mender of second functions. My work would not be to repair the valley into its original purpose, because no one could agree, and certainly not I, what its original purpose had been. The original purpose, if it existed, would be buried under layers of construction, ecology, weather, and forgetting. My work would be to maintain the valley’s current habits: the moss that is learning to hold water where there is no soil, the fish that have learned to spawn before the channel is complete, the birds that nest in a ventilator because a ventilator is a cliff with good lighting.

I would have been made, over generations, into a body suited to this work. Not made in the sense of manufacture—not fully. But made in the sense of pressure, habit, patience, and inheritance. I would be tall and thin, not from frailty, but because thin things cool and move more slowly and lose less warmth in that severe air. My skin would be pale with a chalky mineral sheen, like wet ceramic when the light catches it just right. I would have fine lichen patches along my shoulders, not because they were decorative, but because they had begun there when I was young, in a damp corridor where the central mechanism’s pressure made the walls weep, and I had learned to carry the place on my body the way the herds carry its colors. My hair would be stiff, nearly colorless, threaded with copper fibers and bits of moss. My eyes would be wide and protected by curved lenses tinted deep blue, because the glare from polished metal and mirrored glass can be enough to damage thought as well as vision.

My feet would be split and padded at the ends, with a faint concavity at the sole, the way the climbers’ feet grip vertical surfaces. This would be useful. In the valley, floors are not always floors. A slope may be a pipe, a pipe may be a wall, a wall may be a ledge, a ledge may be a nest, and all of them may be waiting for something to fall. My toes would have hardened edges like old wire insulation, and my soles would read temperature the way a tongue reads thirst. I could stand on a condenser rail and know whether its warmth came from a machine waking or from the memory of a machine that had not been needed for years. I could press the side of my foot to a deck and tell, by its coolness, whether rain would come soon or whether the ground was only remembering rain that had happened elsewhere.

My hands would be the most human part of me, and the most alien.

I would have fingers long enough to reach into joints, palms dry even when wet, with resin-sealed fingertips. I would use them to wipe lamps, tighten cable ties, sort broken tile by fracture color, and leave certain surfaces damp on purpose. The quiet people do not always clean things. Sometimes they clean in order to keep a place dry enough to survive. Sometimes they do the opposite. They smear moisture onto a channel edge so that dust-wings will come. They leave a patch of moss over a vent because the moss will warn them if the temperature changes. They do not think of this as gardening, exactly. It is closer to housekeeping performed in a house that is still building itself and occasionally rearranging its rooms when no one is looking.

I would carry a rod.

It would be slender, metal, cold to the touch unless I stood too close to a furnace exhale. Not a weapon. In that bright world, violence would be clumsy and loud, and loudness draws cold down upon the throat like a punishment. The rod would be for tapping insulators, for testing whether a surface was real enough to trust, for drawing long thin rings through the valley’s infrastructure. Each insulator would have its own note. Some would sing flat and tired. Some would ring clean and bright. Some would not ring at all, and those are the ones to avoid, because silence inside a ceramic joint usually means moisture where moisture is not wanted, or a vibration so deep that the material has begun to forget how to answer.

I would speak little. When I spoke, it would be partly by breath, partly by clicks in the throat, partly by the way I moved a lamp. Language there would not be only sound. Sound is expensive in high altitude and rare in the valley’s hush. A person could communicate by placing their hand near a cooling fin, by leaving a bowl where no bowl should be, by standing with their back to a certain arch, by walking across a deck in the rhythm the herds use. The clickers, for instance, do not need words. Their hoofbeats make the valley’s grammar. I would sometimes walk among them not to lead, but to answer.

If you watched me, you would find that I spend much of my time waiting.

Waiting in that world is not an empty condition. It is active. The valley is incomplete, and incompleteness is a habitat. If a stairway ends in cloud, there is usually a landing if you stay long enough. If a channel runs dry, there is water in its memory. If an arch has too many floors when seen from one angle, you can wait until the angle that is true comes around. I would know this because my body has learned it, the way an animal’s body learns the time of migration.

I would wake near a condenser, not at an alarm. Condensers have breaths. I would know by the way my mat softened with dampness, by the faint metallic smell of oil and rain forming on overhead pipes, by the soft tick of metal contracting after night. My mat would be woven from resin thread and worn cable strands, layered with a sheet of thin ceramic tile that retains the warmth of the machine housing beneath. The tile would be chipped in one corner. I would not replace it yet, because the chip had become useful: it held a small reservoir of condensation, and dust-wings nested under the lip of the chip, and their larvae fed something that fed a fish that fed a bird that had once nested in my hood. This is how the valley keeps its books. Everything has a second function. The second function is often the one that matters.

The first thing I would do in the morning would be to look at the eastern terrace.

It would be dry. The western basin would already be shining, though no rain had passed over it in the visible sky. A wet line would begin somewhere along the central deck, not as a clean edge, but as a gradient: chalk white to pale gray to a darker ochre, like breath on stone. I would stand there with my hands behind my neck, feeling the sub-bass pulse in my teeth. Not always. Sometimes it is quiet. Sometimes it is so patient that you mistake it for your own heartbeat until it slows and yours does not. When that pulse is near, I would lie down. Not in reverence, though reverence is useful. Not in fear, though the valley is not a safe place, exactly. It is more like a very large animal whose moods are not malicious but administrative. If it shifts its breathing, the pressure changes. If the pressure changes, the herds lie down. If the herds lie down, something must be about to fall upward in an unobserved corridor, or a channel must remember being a lake, or the kilns must begin printing a tile in the wrong place for a reason that will only make sense to moss.

I would lie down with the others.

The clickers would come across the deck in a pale procession, their hooves clicking softly, like rain starting somewhere else. They would pause before me, not because I mattered more than they did, but because I was already in the correct posture of waiting. Their coats would be mottled with the valley’s colors: gray, copper, chalk-white bands, blue patches that looked like wet shadow. Their eyes would be dark and level. They would test the air with the sides of their nostrils. They would taste the metallic residue left by a recent weld, perhaps the memory of oil from a conveyor that had not run yet. Then, together, they would lower their long necks and settle onto the deck. I would settle among them. This is not communion. It is pressure management.

After a time, the ground would darken.

It would darken before rain. This is one of the valley’s gentle errors. Not everywhere at once. A surface that looks dry may develop a wet sheen in a pattern that has not yet happened. Small creatures sense this. A dust-wing may rise before the wind exists. A threadfish may begin to swim toward a pool that is currently empty. A bird may fly toward a tower whose upper floors have not been poured yet, because the air near that tower already has the correct temperature. In those moments, the world seems to be remembering the future, or perhaps borrowing from a nearby version of itself.

I would walk to the wet line as it formed.

I would carry a bowl. It would be shallow, white ceramic, old at the base but new at the rim, because I had broken its lower half long ago and welded it to a fragment of a different tile. The valley does not waste breakage. Broken things often make the best edges. The bowl would be used to catch the first drops of rain that were not yet rain, because sometimes the wetness arrives before the cloud and can be collected. Sometimes it cannot. Sometimes the bowl merely sits there, holding the smell of a lake that does not exist nearby.

As I walked, I would pass under an arch.

The arch would look ordinary until I was halfway beneath it, and then one side would seem older than the other. The left curve would have fresh angular seams where material had been lifted away by the large machine no one can see all at once. The right curve would be pitted and darkened by time that had not yet touched it. A row of similar arches would stretch beyond, slightly smaller, slightly damaged, slightly ahead. I would not stop unless the geometry asked me to. The herds do not stop for arches, but they stop for unstable geometry. They will stand until the path resolves into one direction. I would do the same. My body would adjust its weight. The tiles under my feet would shift slightly, not physically, perhaps, but in the way a reflection shifts when someone looks at the pool from the wrong angle.

If the arch resolved, I would continue. If not, I would leave a mark.

A mark is important in the valley. A mark says: someone has known this, even if the knowledge cannot be used yet. I would set a small tile against the wall at knee height. The tile would be chipped, bright at the fracture. It would carry my herd’s color smear, not by scent alone but by tone. A blue ochre streak. The clickers would understand. The quiet people downstream would understand. The climbers would ignore it, because climbers only trust surfaces that can be climbed. The birds would eventually nest there, because a mark can become shelter when left long enough.

I would reach the reservoir edge.

The reservoir would not be large. Its water would be shallow and bright, with a surface like glass laid over oil. The engineered margins would be stepped like teeth. Fish would move through the shallows, threadfish, their bodies so reflective that when they turned sideways they became water itself. Their lateral lines would tremble. They would gather where condensation thickened. Some would be blind pipe fish in the dark conduit nearby, moving slowly through a space that should not be able to hold them, navigating by pressure from a pump that started at irregular intervals. I would not disturb the pool’s reflection unless necessary. Reflections there are dangerous and useful. A pool may reflect not the sky above but a cooler valley, a valley with animals drinking that are not present. If I stood too long, I might see myself doing something I have not yet chosen.

But today the reflection would be mostly accurate, except for one cloud that had not arrived and one bird that had already been born.

I would set the bowl down and wipe it with my thumb. My thumb would taste metal and cold stone. Then I would move to the channel beside the basin and inspect the flow. It would be sluggish, its surface bright. Water was never here to create lushness. Water was here to be taught how to behave. Channels are arguments about gravity. In this valley, gravity has sometimes lost the argument, not because it is weak, but because the place is being assembled with enormous care and occasionally loses track of itself.

I would take a length of resin cord from my belt and bind a loose tile near the margin. The tile would click once and then settle. The binding would be tight enough to hold water where I wanted water, not so tight that it became a dam. The quiet people have learned to do this with channels the way gardeners train vines: not to force, but to encourage a certain direction of becoming. The tile would later crack, and the crack would become a home for moss-animal, and the moss would color the water green-black, and insects would come, and fish would eat their larvae, and the birds would come for the insects, and their droppings would darken the ledge, and the darkened ledge would hold enough moisture for a young shrub. Then the shrub’s roots would find the joint again, and the joint would open, and the channel would deepen by a few millimeters. This would take many seasons, or many wrong seasons, or many seasons that had not been built yet.

I would not mind.

The machines would move slowly around me. A conveyor would shift one heavy block per hour. A turbine would turn so gradually that its blades would appear fixed, until a shadow crossed them or my eyes blinked at the wrong time. Kilns would print tiles in the distance, exhaling vapor so slowly it seemed like a thought taking form. The cable forests would tighten and loosen in response to humidity. The lamps would mark thresholds, not because the valley needed light, but because light needs direction. In the glare, a lamp says: here is an edge. In the night, a lamp says: here is a road.

At the center of all this would be the thing no one sees whole.

The Upholder. The Central Pulse. The mechanism that moves water uphill and decides where rain will fall. I would not see it. I would not need to. I would know it through the pressure in my molars, the way the herds lie down, the way the dust-wings rise before wind. Sometimes I would place my palm against a warm condenser housing and feel its rhythm beneath the metal. It would not feel like a machine. It would feel like a chest wall. It would not feel like a god. It would feel like an organ that had been left too long in darkness and had begun to dream.

I would respect it.

Not because it asked. Machines in that valley do not ask in words. They ask by creating conditions. If I respected it enough, I would not block certain vents. I would wipe certain pipes only half clean. I would not scrape moss from a joint until it had thickened enough to tell me what it meant. I would mark stairways that ended in cloud and leave them marked, because the fog may contain the next landing, and landing are not always built where you need them, but where the valley has decided to stop forgetting itself.

By midday, the bright side would become the wet side.

This would happen without warning. A dry terrace that had glittered in the glare for hours would darken. The shadowed basin would begin to lose its false shine. The wet line would move like a slow tide on hard ground. The herds would stand again, shifting their weight. The birds would fly toward the change without calling, circling once as if confirming that the world had not entirely abandoned them. The fish would move into channels that had no water. I would move with them, because this is my work: not to own the wet line, but to help the valley remember it.

I would climb a gantry.

The gantry would lean over a street beneath the street. Arch beneath arch. Pipe beneath pipe. There would be no natural ground anywhere, no loam, no weathered cliff, no ancient rounded boulder. If there was moss, it had learned to negotiate with architecture. If there was grass, it grew from drilled holes. If there was wind, it carried the scent of cold stone, oil, ozone, damp moss, and something faintly metallic, like coins left out in rain. From the upper walkway, I could see the whole constructed territory at once: reservoirs, towers, aqueducts, factory halls, a bridge spanning a dry cut, a stack exhaling slow vapor. It did not feel cramped, because density had developed its own logic. The city had become landscape. The landscape had become machine. The machine had become ecology. And the ecology was beginning to dream.

I would stand at the edge and look down.

Below, in a blue corridor, a hunter would be waiting. Long, thin, pale, flattened against a ceramic wall. It would not see me. Its eyes would be horizontal slits reflecting light strangely. It would be eating patience, which is common in the valley. A heron-like creature would stand in a shallow channel with one leg raised, almost colorless, its beak flat and sharp, scraping the places where food clings. It would not splash. A small flock of cool-finned birds would occupy an abandoned housing, their nests built of wire, resin, tile fragments, lichen, and the shed skins of vent insects. One bird would fly into an arch and emerge one tile later on the wrong side. It would circle once, then continue, because arches are not always honest and the birds have learned to fly after the fact.

I would listen to the sound of the valley.

There is mostly silence there, but silence is a kind of abundance. It lets small things become enormous. A drop of water striking metal rings once and then disappears into damp air. A cooling pipe ticks. A rope bridge creaks in humidity. A wingbeat hits a ceramic wall with a soft slap. Hooves scrape, then click. A condenser hisses as it draws moisture from thin clouds. A cable tightens like a throat. And beneath all of it, long intervals of sub-bass, felt in teeth and sternum, so slow that the mind mistakes it for the body’s own rhythm. The machines do not slam. Nothing crashes. Even the kilns breathe as if taught by altitude that noise will be punished.

I would begin to descend before the next pulse.

On the way down, I would pass a field of new tile. The tiles would be bright at their edges, sharp enough to cut. Broken fragments would lie in the gutters, still fresh at the fracture. The smell would be strong: hot resin, cold mineral, ozone, and the faint sweetness of rain that has not yet learned its destination. I would pause to pick up a shard. The fracture would be white-blue, like ice that has seen too much light. I would turn it over, then place it back with the bright side up. This is not superstition. This is maintenance. A tile placed with the correct face exposed may catch more condensation, and condensation may attract dust-wings, and dust-wings may feed something that feeds something else, and somewhere in the chain the valley will be stabilized a little more, or a little less, depending on how it wishes to remember itself.

I would pass the stone that falls upward.

It would be no larger than a fist, hovering above a gutter because no one was directly beneath it. I would step aside, not because I was afraid, but because observation changes things. If I looked at it too directly, it might become ordinary, and ordinary falling stones can make sound. Sound here is rare, and rare sound can hurt. So I would look around it, the way the climbers look around geometry. I would walk with my body turned slightly, one hand out, feeling the tile beside me. The stone would not fall while I did not fully see it. That would be enough.

I would arrive at a place where a pipe had become a cliff.

The pipe would be large, cold, and sweating threads of moisture. In its joints, pale moss would gather. Lichen would line its underside. Tiny crustaceans would move through the films of water on slanted surfaces. A heron-like creature would watch. A bird would nest in the ventilator above, its wings catching glare and scattering it into soft ceramic fragments of light. I would press my palm to the pipe and feel the condensation bead against my skin. It would be warmer than air, because water often is, and cooler than flesh, because this is a high, bright, cold place where heat is borrowed and lost quickly. I would let the pipe be wet where it needed to be wet. That would be enough for the day. Sometimes.

I would eat near a furnace pocket.

Not food as a feast, but food as maintenance. A cake of dried dust and lichen. Fish from a channel I had not emptied. Bird eggs only if the nest was already too thick and the parents had been disturbed by pressure, and even then, only with a mark left for their return. Mineral salts from a shelf where condensation had collected and then evaporated, leaving a rim that tasted of coins. Silver leaves from shrubs, stiff and warm, folded and reopened with the valley’s strange listening. I would chew slowly. The valley is quiet because noise is a form of waste. I would eat quietly for the same reason. My body would accept its bitterness and its metallic tang. This would be good for me, because the body must become compatible with the place it lives in.

If the quiet people looked at me, they would not see an individual so much as a function with a face.

I would be one of them, yes, but also one of its organs. I would have my own habits, my own worn tools, my own scar where a tile had cut my hand while I was setting a lamp to mark a threshold. But those things would be less important than the role I played in the valley’s self-assembly. I would be the wet line’s attention. I would be the channel’s patience. I would be the joint’s reason for staying clean.

And I would be becoming less separate.

My footprints would wear the tiles. My sweat would darken certain edges. My droppings would seed micro-ecologies. My dead skin would feed mites, which would feed something small, which would feed something larger. My tools would rust, then become homes. My marks would become paths. My paths would become channels. My channels would become habits. My habits would become weather.

This would not be horror.

There is nothing violent about the valley’s conversions. Nothing is buried. Nothing rots in the way loam knows. Things oxidize, bleach, dissolve, and join the palette. When the herds walk across the tiles, they leave thin smears of mineral paste, not by scent alone, but by color. The valley learns what belongs to which herd by tone. In time, the quiet people do the same. Their skin takes on the chalky glow of wet ceramic. Their clothing becomes composite. Their hair becomes pale and stiff and threaded with lichen. Their breath becomes shallow and deliberate, not from disease, but because the air rewards restraint.

I would be like that.

Not erased. Incorporated. That is the gentlest wrongness of the valley. It does not consume you by force. It consumes you by becoming so complete in its own logic that you are one of its necessary mistakes. A well-made mistake can be abundant. It can be deeply adapted. It can drink from rails before the rain, because the rail is expecting wetness. It can stand in the line where the ground remembers being water, and not think of this as madness, but as inheritance.

At dusk, I would walk the upper tiers.

There may or may not be true night. The light does not simply leave; it withdraws into surfaces, into lamps, into reflected hours that belong to other skies. The valley would become a web of pale light. Electric fixtures would mark thresholds, stairheads, water gates, and edges that could otherwise vanish into glare. I would follow that web not because it would take me home, but because the home would follow the web. A room may be no larger than a storage closet, entered through a pipe junction or behind a panel that clicks into place. A sleeping place may be under a condenser, or in an abandoned hall where looms no longer run, or in a tower that currently has no top because the top is still deciding how to catch the wind.

I would choose a place that feels expectant.

Perhaps a corridor where sound arrives before the gust. Perhaps a stairway that ends in cloud but contains a landing if I wait. Perhaps a pool that mirrors another valley, because I need water tonight that my valley does not yet have. I would sit at its edge. The reflection would be cooler than the actual sky. Something with antlers of oxidized wire would drink there, and I would see it, and it would not be present. I would not reach into the pool. Not yet. Sometimes borrowing requires stillness.

The threadfish would move in the channel beside the pool. One would touch the reflection and vanish. Later, in another basin, another threadfish would appear, wet and confused, carrying faint markings like foreign waterlines. This would remind me that the valley has many sources and no true origin. That is not a flaw. It is a habitat.

I would watch until the reflection returned to the present. Then I would lie down on a warm tile shelf. My body would cool. The air would cool it further. My lungs would slow. I would feel the central mechanism’s pulse, not as sound, not even as vibration, but as permission. The valley would decide, in its slow administrative way, where tomorrow’s rain would fall. If I woke before the rain, I would know because the ground would already be dark along a line that had not yet been touched.

I would go there.

I would stand at the border. The herds would come. The dust-wings would rise before the wind. The birds would call softly, afraid that full song might disturb the cold. The climbers would watch from a pipe joint with no fear, because pipe joints are cliffs and cliffs are homes and homes are made of the things that survive weather. The fish would begin their spawn, timing themselves to a pressure change before the first drop. The machines would continue their slow breathing. The kilns would print new tiles, perhaps where the rain would make them useful, perhaps not, but the valley would find a use for them eventually, because that is how it keeps itself alive.

I would be the one who marks the line.

With a bowl. With a rod. With a smear of mineral paste. With a lamp. With the rhythm of my own footsteps. With the patience of a being that has learned to live ahead of the world’s intentions. I would stand where the dry ground begins to become wet, not because I command it, but because I belong to it. I would be a joint in the architecture, a hinge between machine and ecology, between construction and memory. I would not be the creator. I would not be the ruler. I would not be the predator. I would be one of the things that lets the valley hold itself together by noticing where it is slipping.

If asked where I would find myself, I would say: on the edge of a bright terrace, beside a shallow channel that may not yet be a channel, under an arch that sometimes has too many floors, with a pool reflecting a valley cooler than my own, where animals drink that are not present.

If asked who or what I would be, I would say: a wet line with legs. A quiet person made partly of the valley’s habits. A seam-dweller. A threshold-minder. A channel-keeper. A body among the many bodies that help the machine become geology and help the geology begin to dream.

I would not be a hero. There are no heroes in that place. The valley does not need to be saved. It needs to be tended, like an ecosystem, like a city, like a mind learning what it is. I would be one of its hands, cold and careful, wiping condensation from a pipe where moss should not yet grow, and leaving enough dampness where it should. I would be one of its eyes, protected from the glare, watching a surface darken before the rain falls. I would be one of its breaths, slow and shallow in the high air, listening for the sub-bass beneath the world.

I would be the quiet rhythm of hooves on tile, but also the tile waiting for those hooves.

I would be the hiss of a condenser, but also the water that the condenser remembers.

I would be the tick of a cooling pipe, but also the pipe that has become a cliff.

I would be the soft click of a bird wing against ceramic, but also the ceramic that has become a nest.

I would be the reflection that is not safe, and the bowl placed beside it anyway.

I would be the mark that says: here is where the valley forgot itself, and here is where we can still pass.

If I stayed long enough, I would no longer be able to say where I ended and the place began. My skin would glow like wet chalk. My footprints would be used by climbers. My tool rods would become perches. My dead would not rot; they would bleach, oxidize, and join the palette. My herds would know me by color. My birds would nest in my hood. My fish would spawn in channels I had helped to open. My name, if they bothered to name it, would simply be another sound in the hush: the ring of metal on insulator, the click of hooves, the hiss of condensation drawing itself from air.

And still I would move.

Not because the valley asked, but because the valley has learned, through me, to tend its own joints.
