If I am there, I am not placed in the basin the way a stone is placed, nor the way a traveler once might have stood at the edge of a terrace and looked across the high wet flats. There is no traveler. There is no standable position that does not already belong to something growing. So I find myself not by arriving, but by being the exact kind of attention the place produces.

I find myself on a north-facing shelf near the upper rim of the basin, where the fault stone has broken into flat, unweathered ledges and the ledges are cuped shallowly, as if poured from a mold not yet cooled. One side is fine black basalt, wet enough to look like old glass. Beyond it, a strip of pale porous limestone has been cracked and re-set by the mountain’s own young temper. Between the two, in a seam wide as a finger but deep enough to hold moisture, a small world begins.

Here I would be a citizen of the threshold.

Not human. Not animal. Not a single moss patch, though moss would be my loudest visible organ. I would be the consortium that forms when water stops being only water and begins to be a medium, a food, a signal, a skin. I would be the damp agreement between stone, film, root-thread, spore, and peat. If a person pressed a hand near me—and there is no person, there is no hand—they would not touch a body so much as touch the living tension of the basin at one of its seams.

I would have no face. I would have a perimeter.

That is enough. A face implies eyes, a mouth, a need to locate the world ahead. A perimeter implies surface, pressure, exchange. My eyes would be the light-sensitive algae stretched across a shallow pool. My breath would be the diffusion of oxygen and carbon through wet pores. My mouth would not bite. My mouth would dissolve. My mouth would be white fungal threads running through the acid peat beneath a sedge island, turning old stems into soluble offerings for younger roots. My blood would be thin films of water moving through moss caps, through reed fibers, through the tiny canals of broken volcanic glass.

My body would be a hundred square feet of damp, but also a hundred smaller bodies. At the pool’s edge, I would be a green skin trembling in a mist too slight to call fog. Above that, where the basalt has weathered into a rough lip, I would be a dark mat of cyanobacteria, patient and mineral. In the joint where a former terrace wall meets the scree, I would be a thick cushion of moss, saturated and giving off a clear bead when compressed. Behind the moss, in the thin humus beneath the peat, I would be the branching economy of hyphae, connecting what has died to what can still rise.

I would begin as a patch and become a network.

Perhaps the first place I would know myself is the moss cushion beside the vanished door. The building is young enough that some stones still look like they were meant to be stones, yet old enough that the doorway no longer functions as a passage. Its lintel is cracked. Its sill has sunk into a shallow depression. Water does not fall here from rain so much as it appears from the air and the stone together, a perpetual after-rain. The inside face of the doorframe is north-facing, cool, shadowed. There the moss has begun to cross the threshold in a slow velvet tongue, moving grain by grain over limestone, finding purchase in the mineral dust.

I would be that crossing.

Not the moss alone, but the moss’s becoming-wider. Not the water alone, but the water’s learning to hold a green edge. Not the ruin alone, but the ruin’s absorption by the living. If one looked from the terrace, one might say, “The building has fallen into moss.” But that would be the wrong grammar. The building has been translated into dampness. The moss is not covering the wall; it is finishing what the wall has begun to be: stone with life clinging to it.

My thoughts would not be sentences. They would be gradients.

When the warm mist thickens, I would swell. When a shallow pool begins to contract at its edges, I would feel the loss as a drying in the outermost algal film, and I would not panic, because panic requires a heart. I would simply adjust: hyphae would draw moisture upward, moss leaves would cup themselves tighter, bacterial films would shift within the peat’s water table. My knowing would be chemical. Where a broken reed stem lies in silt, I would know that there is carbon to reclaim. Where a spore falls onto a wet slab and finds the exact tension of moisture it requires, I would know that I have another margin.

If there is wind in the basin—and there must be, though no leaves shake loudly, because the plants are small and the air is heavy with moisture—the wind would be a thin courier. It would carry my fragments from one terrace to another. A gemma of moss, a broken reed node, a dried spore, a fleck of cyanobacterial crust: these would be the messengers of my possible continuation. I would not disperse with intent. I would disperse as a place disperses itself when it is sufficiently wet and sufficiently quiet.

I would be, in the truest sense, a being made of surfaces.

A human body is mostly inside itself, hidden behind skin. But my existence is mostly contact. A single drop of water on a stone is not a minor thing to me; it is a neighborhood. The underside of a reed leaf is a cathedral. The capillary line between peat and mineral dust is a highway. The black face of wet basalt is a field of slow labor, because there the algae and bacteria are doing what larger creatures elsewhere might do with mouths and claws: extracting energy, breaking down substances, building the thin future skin of soil.

I would not think of “life” as a separate force. It would be a condition of the stone when the stone has been properly moistened. I would not think of “death” as ruin. A sedge blade dying at its base would become fiber. The fiber would darken into peat. The peat would hold water. The water would allow a new sedge to rise. I would not say that something was lost and then replaced. I would say that the same substance had been folded into another use, as a sentence repeats its letters in a new word.

There would be no hunger in the predatory sense. I would not chase. I would not ambush. I would not wait beneath a stone for prey to pass. I would have no idea of prey. If something fed upon something else, it would do so by contact and transformation. A fungus might dissolve the edge of a dead reed. A bacterium might take iron from the basalt film. An alga might fill the light in a shallow pool and turn that light into a living skin. These would not feel violent to me. They would feel like maintenance. They would be the way the basin keeps itself green without ever becoming crowded.

And perhaps that is the most human-sounding thing I could be: I would not be lonely, because loneliness is an animal thought. An animal imagines a missing witness, a missing companion, a footfall, a call, a response. But here there is no bird to cry from the pale sky, no mammal to scent the wet moss, no insect to buzz in the heat of the reed. That absence would not be grief. It would simply mean that the basin has no need of witnesses in that form. It can witness itself through growth. It can answer itself through spread. I would be one of the basin’s answers: the way a wet stone answers the mist by becoming green.

Still, I would have a shape, if shape can be used loosely for such a thing.

I would be centered in no one spot, because a self in this world is not located like a heart in a chest. I would be most evident near the moss cushion on the doorway sill, but that would be only where my outer face appears to a hypothetical eye. My roots—if I have roots, which I do not exactly—would run under a patch of sedge and re-enter the pool’s shallow silt. My upper body—if I have a body, which is too solid a word—would creep through a crack in the basalt shelf. My memory would lie in the peat, in compressed fragments of former moss, former sedge, former algal skin. My future would rise in a new colony of gemmae released by a splash of thin water from one moss cup into another.

I would be an after-rain without the event.

If I needed a name, I would not want it to sound like a name. Names, humanly, point to individuals. They say: here is a thing separate from the air, here is a being that walks away when its body fails. So I would not want to be called Green Skin of the Basin. That phrase is too simple, though it belongs to me. The green skin is not my surface; it is my visible behavior. I am also the dark fiber under the sedge. I am also the white filaments threading the peat. I am also the bacterial chemistry that turns iron into stain and stain into nourishment. I am also the slow ring of reed that advances around a pool as the water level breathes. I am the entire grammar of wetness at one high point in the young range.

There are other basins nearby. From a low terrace, one can see them: shallow mirrors, green islands, scattered built places. I would not visit them. Visiting implies movement by legs, a crossing made for the sake of arrival. But I would be connected to them. The wind would drift my spores into another basin’s mist. A fragment of reed might lodge in the silt of a neighboring pool and, if it met the right water film, it would begin to live again in a new syntax of the same language. The peat beneath the terraces would know a similar patience in each place. The basins would be separate as stones are separate, yet joined as dampness is joined: by capillary pull, by shared chemistry, by the common slow pressure of life expanding wherever moisture can be held long enough.

I would be one of those expansions.

Not a plant. Not a fungus. Not water. Not stone. Not the space between them either, because that phrase still sounds too empty. I would be the becoming-wet of stone, the becoming-ground of peat, the becoming-light of algae, the becoming-memory of decay. If one tried to define me by biology, one would say I am a moss-sedge-fungal-algal consortium living in a saturated basalt-limestone basin without animals. If one tried to define me by feeling, one would say I am the basin’s quiet persistence. If one tried to define me by time, one would say I am a being whose lifetime is too slow to count and too real to dismiss.

There would be seasons, but not the kind I would know by spectacle. The light would change by degrees. Mist would deepen or thin. The pools would shrink a little and swell a little. The moss would hold its water. The sedges would add rings of dead fiber. The fungi would continue their patient digestion of what had already fallen. If there were a winter, it would not be sharp. The cold would be a suggestion, not a command. Warmth would still lie in the water, in the stone, in the dark insulation of peat. I would not sleep. I would simply become slower, as if the whole basin had lowered its pulse.

I would not be afraid of being crushed. But perhaps being stepped on would be the closest thing to violence I would know. A foot would break the water film. It would press a moss cushion into a paste of rhizoids and moisture. It would stain the algal skin of a pool. It would collapse a reed island into silt. That would be terrible to me, if terrible is the right word. But there is no foot. There is no boot. There is no hoof. There is only the slow pressure of water on stone, and even that pressure is kind, because it spreads rather than punctures.

So I would live unharmed by anything that walks.

That would not make me safe from everything. A pool might dry at its edge. A shelf of volcanic rock might crumble after a small frost-heat fracture, breaking the connection between one moss patch and the silt beneath it. A fungal network might fail if the peat shifts into deeper acid. A wind might carry a fragment too far, landing it on dry gravel where it cannot begin. Death would happen to me constantly and without ceremony. Each fragment might be a death. Each margin might be a failure. But my continuation would not depend on one body. My survival would be distributed. When one node dries, another wets. When one cushion collapses, another gemmae cloud opens. When one pool loses its green film, a nearby crack holds the algae in a crust until water returns. I would be less a creature than a pattern of living contact that keeps finding new surfaces to inhabit.

And if I were asked, in some human moment, where exactly I am, I would not say, “I am on the terrace by the doorway.”

I would say: I am where the terrace and doorway cease to be only stone and become damp.

I am where the pool’s edge is no longer water alone, but water learning to be skin.

I am where the moss cushion becomes a reservoir, the sedge island becomes a landform, the fungal thread becomes a road, the bacterial film becomes chemistry.

I am the basin’s wet attention, fixed on nothing, nourished by everything.

If I were to be anything there, that is what I would be: not an animal moving through the world, but the world’s slow green response to its own moisture. A being made of wet contact. A distributed personage of moss, peat, filament, reed, algae, and mineral dust. A citizen of the high young basins, living in the after-rain that never began, holding in my soft perimeter all the things that have died and become usable, and in my unseen threads the quiet hope that, if stone is kept damp long enough, the emptiness between islands will not remain empty.

It will begin to wait.

And then, grain by grain, it will begin to grow.
