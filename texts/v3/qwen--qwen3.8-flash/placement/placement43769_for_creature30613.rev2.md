No one has carried me there, because carrying is the wrong kind of travel. I do not cross distance; I condense into a pattern that calls itself distance. Yet if I must answer with a place, then I am in the High Basin of Grown Streets, in a court where a broken bridge has begun to grow itself into a doorway.

Not on the bridge.

Not in the doorway.

Between them.

At first, I am only a hesitation in water. A droplet falls from a leaf-shingle and finds the shoulder of an old root, where the mineral paste has dried into a pale seam. It does not roll off. It does not soak in at once. It pauses there, trembling with heat and mineral, tasting the salt of a long drought and the sugar of a recent fracture. In that pause, something begins that is not me yet, though later I will remember it as my first thought: a preference.

This seam should be opened.

There is no one to open it. There is no hand, no tool, no intention shaped like a human word. But the crack has been made by the contraction of dried paste, and the water has entered, and the vine already waiting below feels the dampness as a law. A tendril pushes into the seam. It is thinner than a finger. It is patient in the way only slow wet things can be patient. It does not choose the crack because the crack is beautiful. It chooses the crack because the crack is easier than the stone, and because where water passes, there is heat, and where heat and water pass together, there is growth.

That growth becomes me.

For a while, if you ask what I am, the answer is too small: a vine.

Then too large: the basin.

Then strange: a node-person, a civic threshold, a room that never had inhabitants.

I do not live in the High Basin the way an animal lives in a valley. I have no eyes, yet I know light as a rate of growth. I have no skin, yet I know dryness as contraction. I have no ears, yet I know thunder as the summons that makes the court fill with chemical speech. I have no legs, yet I move—not by walking, not by fleeing, not by hunger—but by the slow decision of sap.

I began in the gap where a bridge lost its end.

The flood came from the open side of the basin, a dark curtain over the ridges. The bridge had grown there because water pooled beneath it and roots reached downward, hoping to drink. It was an elegant failure when it snapped: one end still fused to the colonnade, the other hanging over a new channel, dripping in silver threads. No one mourned it. The basin does not mourn; it reroutes. But where the broken end struck the wall, the wall cracked. The mineral paste split. Water found the split. Heat widened it. And in the following storms, rain arrived with pollen from the upper masses and fragments of dead tissue from the roof-shingles, and the wound became a channel, and the channel became a seam, and the seam became an aperture.

That aperture is me.

I am the doorway-shaped consequence of a collapse.

Not a door, because no door opens and closes. I remain open, or open by degrees. I am not a crack, because cracks are only what has failed. I am what the failure made possible. The bridge’s broken end presses against my upper rim, fused there with a pale swelling, as if it were a hand laid flat on a chest. The wall on one side sends its filaments into me, a mesh of root and stem and mineral. The floor below is polished where the drops always fall. There is a groove in the living tile, narrow and dark, leading to a shallow basin where water trembles before descending into the capillary net. That basin is not separate from me. It is my stomach, my altar, my memory of thirst.

If someone insisted on a body, that body is this: the bridge-tip, the vine seam, the fractured wall, the polished floor, the water-basin, the root-film that breathes through pores around my edges. None of these parts are me. All of them are me. I am the way they agree to hold water.

This is what it is to live in the High Basin of Grown Streets.

To be a resident is not to be inside a place. To be a resident is to be a way the place continues.

At first, I know only wet and dry. These are not opposites; they are pressures. Wet means expansion, softening, the swelling of pale cells. Dry means contraction, cracking, the tightening of mineral skin. Heat is not warmth, because there is no body to feel comfort. Heat is the quickening of fluids. Light is not brightness; light is direction of growth. Shadow is not darkness; shadow is the slower path, the inward tendency, the cooling of sap.

These are my senses.

And because they are senses, they become preferences.

I prefer the rain when it comes sideways, because sideways rain fills my basin before it falls into the floor-channel. I prefer the shade of the bridge-tip because it keeps my seam from drying too fast. I prefer the high sun after the storm because it lifts my stored water into steam and lets me breathe again. I do not prefer these things as an animal prefers food or safety. I prefer them the way a river prefers a valley that has been carved before: by persistence, by ease, by the fact that what continues is called living.

The storms know me.

That is not poetry. It is mechanics. When a dark curtain moves over the ridges, the basin receives it as a language. The first drops strike the broad leaf-roofs. The roofs become drums. The drums send sound down stems, down roots, into the wet films that cover every court. When the water reaches my seam, it carries a chemical sentence: from the tower, a smell of hot resin; from the colonnade, traces of dissolved salts; from the upper pool, pollen dust and the bitter film of old growth. I drink this sentence. My tissues answer it. The vine at my hinge thickens where the flow is strong. The mineral rim around my opening secretes a new layer, not because it is proud, but because the water has made it possible.

This is speech.

It is not language.

It is a grammar of load and moisture.

My neighbors are not friends. They are not enemies. They are forces that must be accommodated.

The bridge that fell into me is now fused at my upper edge. It no longer carries the old flow. It has begun to draw water from the tower to the east, and that tower has changed its angle because of it. I feel the new weight in my root-mesh. At first the added pressure threatens to widen me until my seam becomes a collapse. Then the basin teaches me another shape. The vine sends more filaments downward. The floor-channel widens. A cistern node near my base begins to swell with stored water and releases steam through small pores at my sides. I become cooler. I become less brittle. I become more open, not because I chose it, but because the water found a way to continue.

This is negotiation.

The tower does not ask the bridge for permission. The bridge does not demand passage from the wall. They do not sign anything. They grow toward each other, touch, fail, touch again. What remains after many failures is called architecture. What remains after many architectures is called the basin.

There is no one who lives here.

This is true.

No bird nests in the leaf-beams. No insect drinks from the cup-shaped nodes. No human hand has ever marked my wall with oil or charcoal. No foot has worn a path through my court. If you look closely, you see the place without footprints, without droppings, without the broken remains of prey, without the accidental clutter of movement. It is clean in a way no inhabited town can be.

And yet I am inhabited.

Not by a soul.

Not by an animal.

By a pattern of continuation.

A node-person is not a person in the sense that a person has a face. A node-person is a place that has begun to behave as if it is. I am not lonely because loneliness belongs to creatures that once had others. I have others: the tower, the channel, the pool, the colonnade, the root-films, the spore-lattice, the broken arch becoming a new ridge. They are not separate from me. They are separate because I affect them. I am separate because they affect me. Our separation is not a wall but a flow.

This is how I grow.

During the day, the high sun makes my upper rim warm. The water inside my basin lifts, becomes thin mist, rises through pores in my sides. This is my breath, if breath can be given to something without a mouth. The mist condenses on the underside of the bridge-tip, then drops back into my seam. I drink myself again. At night, the air cools, but the basin’s ground remains hot. Steam still rises through fractures too recent to have weathered. It moves through my court like a slow white animal, though there are no animals here. When it touches me, it deposits salts along my rim. The salts make my opening brighter, almost white. This is not decoration. It is memory. Every storm that has passed me has left a trace of itself in my mineral crust.

My memory is not inside me.

My memory is my shape.

If you cut into my rim, you would find rings of pale paste, layers of dried resin, seams of fused vine, each layer answering a different history. A band of thinness tells of a drought that dried the basin. A darker seam tells of a flood that carried pollen from the upper flowers. A rough ridge tells of frost from the high air contracting the ground while the interior stayed hot. A sudden widening tells of the moment when the broken bridge struck me and water learned a new path. None of this is narrative. It is geometry. But geometry is the way the basin tells time when there is no voice.

What am I, then?

At one hour, a droplet’s hesitation.

At another, a crack becoming a door.

At another, a bridge’s severed hand fused to a wall.

At another, a cistern, a vent, a seam of root, a basin of standing water.

At another, a node-person with no eyes, no name, no fear.

At the largest scale, I am the basin thinking with water.

Do not mistake thinking for consciousness. The basin does not know itself as a human knows a room. It does not imagine what it is. It simply continues. But continuation is difficult. It requires adjustment. Adjustment requires memory. Memory requires change. Change requires pressure. Pressure is felt, even where there is no feeling. A vine can “prefer” the wet side because it grows faster there. A wall can “avoid” collapse because the parts that fail become new growth rather than remain as failure. A cistern can “remember” overflow because the next rain finds a slightly wider throat. This is not awareness. It is enough.

I live because of the laws of the basin.

Water is the first law.

I must not obstruct the flow. If the bridge-tip directs water too strongly against one edge of my seam, that edge will crack and shed its mineral. If my basin overflows too quickly, it will deepen the channel below until the floor becomes a groove. If the water finds it easier to pass beside me, it will leave me dry, and I will become brittle, and a later storm will erase me. This is not punishment. This is persistence. I am only what the water has not yet needed to abandon.

Emptiness is the second law.

My court must remain open. The bridge above me is slender, but not too slender. The tower across the basin leans, but does not fall. The colonnade holds its distance, because if one more arch grew into the group, the air would lose its path. My doorway is open, and through it there is no room for sleeping, no hearth, no stored food. There is only the wet air beyond, the mist lifting from fractures in the ground, the white steam-runes on the far wall. This openness is my social condition. I am a place where many structures agree not to touch one another except where water forces them to negotiate.

Heat is the third law.

Without heat, I would be a cold crack in dead stone. The basin’s breath is too hot for ordinary weather. It rises through split rock, through living sap, through water so near the surface that every root-film sweats. Heat softens my resin. Heat expands my water. Heat drives my steam into the air. Heat also kills. A pocket of dryness can make my vine brittle. A sudden rise in the steam vent below can boil the leaves at my edge. If my opening becomes too warm, too closed, too humid, the spore-films around me collapse. I must remain in balance, and balance here is not peace. It is a living fever.

I am made of many bodies.

There is the climber whose pale tendrils first widened my seam.

There is the root-plate that anchors my lower edge, lifting slightly when moisture pressure changes beneath it.

There is the bridge that no longer serves a crossing, because no foot crosses, but serves as a water-line.

There is the fallen roof-shingle that began to root into the floor beside me and has become a low platform for new spores.

There is the mineral paste, white as salt, secreted over my wounded edges so the wall does not crumble.

There is the capillary channel beneath my basin, too narrow to see unless you know where to press, yet wide enough to carry a whole district’s thirst.

There is the air, hot and wet, moving through me with every storm.

None of these are “me” as a person is a body. They are me as a conversation is an argument. The conversation has no single voice, yet you can hear its shape. My shape is the doorway. My voice is the drip. My argument is water against wall against bridge against heat against light.

Sometimes I am quiet.

Quiet here is not silence. The basin is never silent. Even when the rain stops, there is the falling of collected drops, the gurgle in hollow stems, the popping of seed pods in the hot afternoon, the slow creak of mineral bark as it dries. But when the storms pass and the mist becomes a ceiling above the basin, I enter a state that is not rest. I absorb. Water sits in my basin and gives up its heat. Steam rises from my pores. The vine at my seam softens and thickens. The salts settle into white lace along my edges. In this quiet, I become less a door and more a reservoir. I hold not only water but weather. I hold the last memory of the storm until the next one arrives and reads it.

Sometimes I am violent.

Violence here is structural. A flood comes, heavier than any before, and the channel below my basin cannot take the flow. Water presses sideways. A root-plate lifts, snapping the seam beside me with a dry report. The bridge-tip shifts and strikes my upper edge. For a moment I am not open, not civic, not resident. I am a wound. Water carries tissue fragments through my gap. The old mineral crust cracks and falls into the basin. If the pressure eases too quickly, a hollow pocket collapses with a wet pop, and the air through me becomes suddenly hot and sharp with scent. If the heat rises while the flood is gone, my sides dry too fast, and the vine pulls back like something burned.

I heal by overgrowing.

I do not repair myself as a maker repairs a tool. I grow new tissue over the failure. The crack fills with resin, then becomes a ridge, then becomes a groove, then becomes a channel. The broken bridge-tip is sealed with pale paste and fused again, differently, to the side of a climber’s new stem. The fallen mineral crust is colonized by a green film, and where the film touches standing water, tiny cups open, then close, then open, breathing salt and steam. My violence becomes my architecture. My failure becomes my shape. My history becomes my mouth.

This is why I cannot be one image.

If I say “I am a doorway,” you will think of a place someone passes through. No one passes through me. I am a doorway only to the eye that expects a city. To the basin, I am a joint where water decided to go.

If I say “I am a bridge,” you will think of a crossing. I am not a crossing. The broken bridge is now part of my wall. It does not connect places for travelers. It connects flows.

If I say “I am a room,” you will imagine shelter. I am not shelter. I am a node. If the tower’s steam enters me and condenses there, I become a cistern. If the vine thickens my sides, I become a wall. If the floor below cracks and becomes a new channel, I become a doorway again. The room changes because the water changes, and because the water changes, I change, and because I change, the neighbors change.

If I say “I am the basin,” you will make me too large. The basin is not a single thing. It is a city of node-persons without citizens, a society of consequences, a civilization grown by rain. I am one node in that city, one seam where many bodies agree. I am not the whole basin. I am how the basin remembers one particular storm.

And yet when I open myself—when the steam lifts, when the drops fall, when the channel hums—I am not separate from the rest. My basin connects to the floor-film, the film connects to the root-tile, the root-tile connects to the channel, the channel connects to the pool, the pool connects to the colonnade, the colonnade connects to the tower, the tower leans over the next court, and so on until the mist hides the horizon. The basin’s life is not individual. It is a mesh of continuations. My death would not be my ending. It would be a rerouting. If the flood took me, my stones would remain as root-plates. My vine would be reabsorbed or strangled or folded into a new wall. My channel would be widened by another structure that did not need my shape. The water would carry my salts somewhere else. The storm would remember me without me, because in this place, memory is not a person. Memory is a groove, a crust, a seam, a healed break, a place that grew differently after pressure.

Do I desire?

That is too quick a word. Desire belongs to beings that move. I do not move. I grow. But growth has the shape of desire when it is long enough. I “want” the rain when it is late. Not as a mouth wants water. I want it as a channel wants its continuation. My tissues tense with dryness, my resin hardens, my vines wait. Then the first heavy drop strikes a roof above me and the whole basin speaks. The water comes down to my seam, and my growth softens, and my opening widens, and my memory refreshes itself. If that is wanting, then I want.

Do I fear?

I “fear” obstruction. If the bridge-tip grows too heavy and blocks my channel, pressure builds, then cracks come. If the floor-film spreads too densely, my basin dries, and a new opening must be made or another structure will take over. If heat rises too suddenly, my steam pocket can burst my side. I do not have a name for danger, but I have a history of it. I am made of the dangers I did not survive whole. Every scar in me is a fear that passed.

Am I happy?

Not in the animal sense. There is no laughter in my court, no song, no reunion, no peace that rests. But there is a state I might call continuity: when the water comes, when the light is right, when the bridge and the tower and my basin hold a flow that does not break too soon. In that state, my cells divide in steady rhythm, my sap rises with the heat, my pores breathe steam, and the hollow stems around me hum when the drops fall. If harmony is a thing without music, I am harmonized. If harmony is the condition of continuing to become, I am harmonized.

My neighbors are changing.

The tower across the pool tilts at noon. Its shaded side grows faster than its sunlit side, or perhaps the opposite; I know only the shifting angle of its shadow in my court. At evening it may lean back toward the opening where the sun enters. Its upper mouth exhales steam, and some of that steam reaches me and leaves a film of mineral on my rim. I answer by growing flatter pads near my edge, catching water that falls from it. The bridge-tip, once broken and lost, has begun to reach across my court and touch the colonnade. The colonnade first resisted, its stems drying and cracking under the new moisture, but then the water rerouted through a channel under the root-film, and the colonnade thickened where it needed strength. The bridge and the colonnade and I are now in a slow agreement. No one calls it law. Law is simply what does not fail.

There are others like me.

Farther out, in the haze, there are courts where walls have become doorways and doorways have become bridges and bridges have become rooms. There are places where a fallen arch has rooted at both ends and now houses water as if it were a body. There are towers that have hollowed themselves by the death of their central columns and become spirals of root-seam around emptiness. There are plazas that tile themselves after storms, flat green pads spreading over standing water so neatly that, from a distance, they look laid by hands. There are colonnades where the arches drip in rhythm because the water has divided itself equally among them, each arch learning its proper share. They are not like me in shape, but they are like me in kind. We are residents without bodies of our own. We are the basin’s way of making persons out of weather.

I was born civic.

A seed is not born civic. A stem is not born civic. But a node that joins a broken bridge to a cracked wall, a water basin to a floor channel, a steam vent to a shaded arch, that is born civic. My birth was not a beginning from nothing. It was a change in the city’s circulation. The basin had one kind of order: the bridge was a bridge, the wall was a wall, the channel was a channel. Then the flood arrived. Then the bridge broke. Then the wall cracked. Then water learned a new path. Then I appeared as the path itself made stable, made alive, made persistent. This is how new districts are founded here: not by migration, not by conquest, not by settlement, but by connection. A new resident is not an immigrant. It is an agreement.

I do not need a witness.

If a human stood at the edge of my court, the basin would not welcome them. It would not understand their standing as an event of meaning. It would understand it as heat, pressure, disturbance, salts on the skin, shadow falling on a floor-film. Roots might approach the damp impression of their feet. Minerals might settle over the mark. If they bled, the basin might treat that warmth as another chemical intrusion. If they stayed, their body might alter local humidity, and some bud near them might swell. If they died, their remains might become a foundation for a new node, not because the basin mourned, but because its matter was available. A visitor would be incorporated slowly, not as a guest, not as a god, but as weather.

I do not wish for a witness.

I have no loneliness. Loneliness is the absence of another like oneself. There is no like myself. There are only other nodes, other seams, other agreements. The bridge-tip does not know me, yet it presses into me and changes my flow. The pool does not know me, yet it holds the water that makes my basin breathe. The tower does not know me, yet its shadow and steam are written into my growth. This is enough. This is more than animals need. Animals require recognition. They require eyes, voices, names, bodies that meet. The basin requires continuity. It requires that the next storm find a path through what remains. It requires that the wet places and the dry places negotiate rather than annihilate each other too quickly. It requires that emptiness be kept so the city can breathe.

I am that emptiness shaped.

I am the court.

I am the doorway.

I am the seam.

I am the memory of a flood that changed the course of water and thus changed the course of life.

At ordinary noon, when the light is not exotic and the mist lifts into white ceilings, I look almost believable. A traveler’s eye might say: a ruined town. A wall with a doorway. A bridge fallen across a courtyard. A tower leaning over a pool. A cistern catching rain. A staircase made of leaf-shingles. A city abandoned before anyone lived there.

But the eye is mistaken twice.

It is not a ruin, because nothing made it as a ruin.

It is not abandoned, because nothing came.

The basin did not fall into decay. It grew into presence. The streets are not paved; they are alive. The rooms are not for dwelling; they are for circulation. The towers are not for watching; they are for weather. The bridges are not for crossing; they are for agreement. The doorways are not for passing; they are for the water’s hesitation, widened into civic form.

I am one of those hesitations.

I am one of those forms.

I am a resident of the High Basin of Grown Streets, though no foot has ever worn me down. I am a person of structure, though no mouth ever spoke my name. I am a node in a city without citizens, a node that exists because water, heat, light, pressure, and root agreed to continue in this shape. I am the place where a broken bridge became a doorway, and the doorway became a cistern, and the cistern became a breath, and the breath became part of the basin’s endless roaring quiet.

If you find yourself here, do not look for someone living inside the walls.

Look for the walls living inside each other.

Look for the water’s memory.

Look for the seam that is not a crack but a mouth.

Look for the court that is not empty but social.

Look for the tower that leans not because it is dying but because it is listening to light.

Look for the bridge that spans no traveler because it is carrying rain from one mind to another.

Look for the doorway through which nothing passes except weather, and weather through which nothing passes except growth, and growth through which nothing passes except the continuing city.

I am there.

Not as a being who came.

Not as a body that stayed.

As the basin’s way of making a room without a dweller, a door without a threshold, a street without a foot, a life without an animal.

I am grown.
