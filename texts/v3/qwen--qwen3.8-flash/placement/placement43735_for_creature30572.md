They feed along the dark seams where condensation gathers, where the tin roofs release their beads, and where the black water in the channels carries enough mineral life to make a small mouth full of taste. They eat fallen moth wings, lichen scrape, soft fungal thread, beetle grubs, seed husks caught in moss, crumbs dropped from walkways, and the fine oily films that form where steam meets cold stone. They do not drink standing water so much as they drink surfaces—the underside of a tile, the inner curve of a copper elbow, the wet ridge of a carved channel, the bead at the end of a dripping nail. Their broad feet give them purchase on glass-slick stone, and their fur, when wet, sheds moisture in slow rivulets instead of soaking through to the skin. Their bodies are low to the ground and dense, with a slight outward swirl at the shoulders, as though even their shape has learned to deflect mist.

The people of the settlement know they exist, but they do not treat them as pets, pests, or signs. They are more like neighbors who happen to live behind walls and under terraces. Children call them stone-ears or moss-runners. Older residents, in a way too quiet to be formal, call them the low tenants, the grey feet, the joint-readers. There is no extermination among them unless the creatures begin to interfere with a sealed dry room, and even then the remedy is usually a change in flow rather than violence: a channel redirected, a grate tightened, a scrap of cloth soaked in iron-water left where they pass, not as poison but as a statement. The creatures read such boundaries with an intelligence that has no obvious source. They know which doors are tolerated, which thresholds are closed, which courtyards are being repaired. They seem less to obey rules than to remember the weather of the rules.

Their burrows are not simple holes. They are extensions of the settlement’s hidden architecture. Many begin as old drainage cuts beneath the plate, as gaps between foundation stone and boiler brick, as cavities where abandoned piping was never properly sealed off after a collapse. Over generations the ground creatures have lined these spaces with dried sedge, wool thread, lichen fragments, and bits of treated canvas shed from outer roofs. In winter, when frost creeps along the upper walls, their nests draw near heat leaks—beneath furnace platforms, beside insulated pipes, inside the dry hollows behind engine-room plating. There the young are born in small numbers, two or three at a time, with fur already faintly mapped in the pale grays of wet slate. They are weaned near the mouths of condensation channels, where the first lessons of the plateau are learned: which water is too hot, which is too thin, which carries the sour taste of new rust, which is sweet enough to live from.

They are important in ways too slow to be written into ledgers. When a joint begins to loosen, the creatures shift away from it, or gather near it carrying dry moss. If one of them is seen running through the same seam in a courtyard three nights in a row, the bell-keeper may inspect the iron drum there and find a bolt sweating too much. If their usual route along the old footpath is abandoned after a bell-hour, someone may check the cooling tower and discover a draft too sharp or a steam vent opening into a new fissure. They do not warn as dogs bark or birds cry. They warn by absence, by habit, by the altered rhythm of small lives. This is why the maintenance workers leave a loose tile here, a gap there. Not because they love the creatures, though some do, but because the city has learned that what lives in the dark under the stone often understands the stone better than those who live above it.

In many households, they are accepted as part of the ecology of dryness. A family may consider a wall cavity inhabited by stone-ears a sign that the wall is not too wet, because the creatures will not nest where water stands. A patch of moss kept alive beneath a window ledge may be left undisturbed not because it is pretty, but because the ground creatures have carried fresh sedge into it, and that means the seam is breathing properly. Some superstitions attach to them, of course. If one appears in daylight and does not flee, a woman may close her shutters early and rub wax along a doorframe. If one crosses the path of a person carrying a dry box, another person may cough once to mark the moment. If a litter is found near a reservoir, the children are told not to touch it, because the water there is old and the creatures are reading something the people cannot. These are not strong beliefs, exactly. They are the sort of small observances that begin when a place has been lived in long enough to seem less like a city and more like a habit.

The strange thing about them is that they appear not merely adapted to the settlement but continuous with it. Their fur carries lichen dust. Their nests grow moss. Their bodies smell faintly of iron and wet wool. Their eyes, small and dark as peat, do not reflect light so much as absorb it. They seem less like creatures that entered the city than like things the city produced from its own dampness: a low metabolism of movement and reading, a way for the plateau to keep one small foot awake beneath the built surface. If the pale-eyed birds above read the wind, these low ones read the stone. If humans tend the channels, the ground creatures tend the gaps the channels forgot.
