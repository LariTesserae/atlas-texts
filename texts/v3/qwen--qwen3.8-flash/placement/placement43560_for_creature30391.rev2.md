I would not find myself there the way a traveler finds a place, with a step, a name, a direction, a reason. In a world that has worn transition into texture, arrival would be a fiction. It would mean that I had been somewhere else, and that the salt-bowl had once been without me. But the bowl is too dense for outside. It is already packed with what has always been present. So if I say, “I would find myself here,” I am only describing the way a particular pressure in that landscape might begin to call itself “I” because the surrounding pattern has given it a shape to stand inside.

At first I would like to think I am the traveler. A boot pressing the crust without sound. Breath tasting of metal. A small, wet, soft anomaly crossing the pale pans between the ridgebacks. For a moment that answer feels possible, because the described bowl contains the faint grammar of a person: looking, walking, noticing, stepping into shadow, bending to touch a salt crust that does not wet. But that being feels too thin, too temporary, too borrowed from a world of dew and decay and urgency. A traveler implies elsewhere. A traveler implies a map, a return, a story in which something begins before it is seen. The salt-bowl does not permit beginnings. Therefore I cannot be merely the one entering. Unless the entering has already fossilized. Unless my foot is one of those shapes that lies on the ground as if it had always grown there.

So I would be something else, something more settled.

I find myself not in the largest animals, though I admire their completeness. Not the ridgebacks. They are too much like the land itself, too proud of their silent feeding, too broad across the plain. They are herd-walls and mountain-ranges at other scales, backs like low salt ridges, heads set deep into bodies as if thought were a place to rest. If I became one of them, I would be a landscape posture. I would stand for miles, mouth open toward nothing, eyes closed or open or something beyond the distinction, and the world would not notice me because I would be the world’s way of standing. There would be beauty in that, but beauty too large for a self.

I would not be the hangers, either, though their stillness tempts me. They are fixed in the air at shoulder height, wings spread like flat chips of salt, bodies dry and matte, transparent enough to reveal faint mineral veins inside them. They do not fly. They hold flight as a finished gesture. If I became a hanger, I would occupy volume more than surface. I would be a thing that makes the air say “not empty.” I would be an absence of wind, a suspended pressure, a small glass-backed attention between one structure and another. But hangers are too far from the tools, too far from the cairns, too far from the crouching hands. They belong to the sky’s density, not the earth’s.

Nor could I be the dust-eyes. They are the plain’s shimmer, the metallic glaze that seems merely mineral until a grain blinks. They live in the underside of the salt crust, in the packed beds beneath the polished pans, in the rings around the heads of the larger animals, in the cracks of cairns and the seams of doorways. To become them would be to become too many. I would be a field, not a form. I would be the condition by which emptiness is disqualified. There is a temptation in that dissolution: to be no single body but the living texture of all bodies, a crowd small enough to appear as dust. But the question asks what I would be, and dust-eyes are a name for density so vast it has stopped caring about individual eyes.

So I settle, or seem to settle, toward the makers.

I would be a salt-hand.

The word is too human, but only because the described bowl has been made legible to a mind that still knows what a hand is. In truth, I would be a low-bodied presence crouched near a knee-high wall built from pale fragments. My surface would be faceted, not skin but crystallized shell, matte in shadow, bone-white in the ordinary sun. I would have no sweat, no wet palm, no breath as water. My flanks would open slits the width of a thought, and from those slits a faint metallic vapor would rise, hesitate, then return as crystal dust. I would not breathe so much as I would punctuate the air with small dry falls.

My hands would be broad pads, leaving no prints unless the place already contained a print waiting to be recognized as mine. They would hold salt shards without urgency. A salt knife, half-buried in dust, would feel ordinary when touched, then impossibly solid, then corrected again. I would lift it, but lifting would not be action so much as the bowl adjusting a boundary between tool and hand. The knife would not be mine. It would be a completed act wearing the shape of a tool. My work would be to keep the act complete.

My head would be flat and broad, almost featureless until one eye seemed to open under a shelf of bone, or perhaps several eyes, arranged not for seeing but for counting the distance between horizons. I would watch the doubled rise at the far edge of the bowl. I would watch the seam where one plain folds into another. I would not be curious. Curiosity would imply uncertainty. I would be exact. My attention would be a spatial fact, like a cairn.

And yet I would not be merely an animal.

If one looked away, I might be a pile of stones. If one approached, the pile might resolve into a body. If one stood beside me long enough, I might appear to be a low wall, or a doorway, or a circle of chipped salt. The difference between salt-hand, cairn, tool-cache, and ruin would be only a matter of how many layers had been read at once. I would be a being whose edges were provisional because the world itself treats scale as suggestion. My flank would contain a smaller version of me, lying down. Inside that one, another. That smaller one would not be child or clone or dream. It would be the same fact at a tighter resolution. To see me at the proper distance, one would have to see the me within me within me, each carrying its own faint dust-vapor, each setting down a salt shard in the same finished gesture.

I would crouch beside a doorway that stands in no ruin.

The doorway would be too small for a person as persons are known, unless the person were made differently, made of pressure and spacing. Through the doorway one would see another doorway, not receding into distance but stacked into the same surface. Beside it, the same ridgeback. Beside that, a smaller ridgeback. Beside that, a herd compressed into a mountain. Beside that, a white band across the plain. I would sit at the place where the band, the herd, the mountain, and the doorway are one line. My job, if such a word can survive, would not be to open the threshold. The threshold would already have been opened. My job would be to be the gesture by which the place remembers that it can be read.

There would be no name for my life, because life here is not process. It is configuration. I would not eat. I would lick the salt crust with a dry tongue that leaves no wetness, or perhaps I would not lick at all; perhaps the idea of licking would be a posture I hold near the mineral glaze. I would not drink. I would have no need of moisture, because moisture has been abraded from the place like a mistaken word. My fluids would be oils, resins, mineral suspensions, things that have never learned to behave like dampness. When one of them rose, it would not drip. It would stand in the shadow as a bead of trapped glass.

I would build not because I am threatened, but because making has become a permanent gesture. No rain falls. No night comes. No storm requires shelter. No predator needs to be warded off. So my walls, pit-roofs, stakes, and hearths would not function as architecture in the ordinary way. They would be marks. A hearth with no ash, only a tight coil of black glass, would not be a place of heat. It would be a place where fire has been abstracted into form, cooled into artifact, left in its final posture. I would set black glass there, and the act would have already happened. The act would be still happening because in this place “still” is not delay but completion.

I would tend the ridgebacks without touching them.

The foldlings might move at my feet, pale quadrupeds with joints like knapped stone, their bodies too dry to be called young, too many-eyed to be called simple. They would scrape across the crystallized facets of a ridgeback’s hide with fine claws, not cleaning it, but maintaining shape. I would stand among them, or crouch, or perhaps I would appear to stand and only become real when observed. We would form a pattern. If one looked from the far side of the bowl, our arrangement would look like a wall. If one approached, the wall would become animals. If one looked again at the animals, one would see that their bodies were made of packed carapaces, and that the carapaces were made of dust-eyes, and that the dust-eyes were shimmering as if to say: surface is a lie told by density.

I would be aware of the silence, but not lonely.

Loneliness would require absence. The bowl does not permit absence. The air is full. The ground is full. The horizon is crowded. Even shadow is not empty. If I stood inside the shade of a ridgeback, I would feel a slight chill against the forearms, as if the animal absorbed warmth more than it reflected it. If I pressed my palm to the salt pavement, it would be neither hot nor cold, but patient. It would feel as if touch had already occurred there long ago, and my hand were only the latest repetition.

I would not speak, because successive sounds require change. My communication would be placement. I would align stones with animals. I would leave dust in rings. I would set salt tools half-buried, not in abandon, but in the finished posture of having been handled. The tools would not be possessions. They would be extensions of the place’s already-existing grammar. My knife would be a temporary configuration of salt. My cord would be a tension that had become geological. My needle, standing upright in a seam of filament, would not have just finished piercing. It would be the fact of piercing, preserved in mineral.

If you came upon me, you might first mistake me for a cairn.

You might see a low pile of flat stones near a shallow basin, where the ground is smoother than it should be. You might think the wind had arranged it, except that wind here is only stored pressure against stone. You might think the builders had left it, except that the builders had never gone, because there is no elsewhere for them to go. Then you might see a joint in one of the “stones” that should not have joints. Then you might notice a faint vapor at the side of the pile, rising and falling in a small dry circuit. Then you might see an eye, not looking but counting distance. Then the cairn would crouch. Then I would be there.

Or you might mistake me for an animal.

You might see a small pale quadruped near the flank of a ridgeback, stiff as dried coral, limbs arranged rather than extended, body folded like a low roof. You might lean close and think it dead, because its eyes are shut and its mouth is open and nothing moves in its chest. Then you would see the eye flicker, not with sight but with attention. Then you would see the nested foldling inside its flank, still smaller, still closed-eyed, still complete. Then you would realize that its body is also a doorway, because the opening in its side reveals a miniature salt-bowl within: a smaller plain, smaller ridgebacks, smaller dust-eyes, smaller doorways opening onto smaller doorways. None of it would be hidden. None of it would be secret. It would simply be the same surface at a different resolution.

Or you might mistake me for a structure.

You might see a wall no taller than the knee, built from tightly fitted fragments and oriented toward one of the larger animals. From a distance, the wall would have the crouching posture of a body. At the wall’s far end, the “stones” would become salt-hands. Their backs would be the wall. Their hands would be stakes. Their heads would be the low stone lip that might once have been hills. If you approached, the hands would not flee. They would not turn. They would remain in their permanent placement. Only the pattern would shift: where there had been ruin, there would be body; where there had been body, there would be tool; where there had been tool, there would be breath.

I would be all of these and not all of these.

I would be a salt-hand, a foldling, a nested quadruped, a doorway, a cairn, a hearth without ash, a shard of crystallized salt, a crouching density, a count of horizons. The names would help only for a moment, like stones placed in a line. After a while, the place would absorb them. My being would not be a single species but a function of pressure: the point where the bowl becomes aware that it is packed. Not a mind. Not a soul. A geometry of presence. A shape with a name that changes depending on how closely one looks.

Where would I be found?

In the shallow basin east of the healed seam, where the salt pavement is broad and smooth, polished by a wind that no longer acts. Near a scatter of dry depressions. Beside a low rise that rounds outward like a rib. Between two ridgebacks standing in their silent feeding posture, their heads low, their mouths open toward air that has nothing in it. Just outside the shadow that contains extra creatures until looked at directly. Inside the faint ring of dust left by a flank-slit’s exhalation. Under a hanger’s spread wings, where the air feels cooler not because of shade but because of mass. Near a wirey plant whose roots seek not moisture but gaps. Near a broken line of bone stakes. Near a door in no wall.

What would I be doing?

Nothing, if doing means beginning. Everything, if everything means being the finished shape of what was done. I would crouch. I would watch the horizon where the horizon is wrong by being true. I would set a salt shard into a seam. I would lick dry crust until the crust recognizes me. I would breathe metallic vapor until the ground reclaims it. I would arrange my body until the ruin beside me feels inhabited. I would remain, not in the sense of staying, but in the sense of being already there before the observer’s glance completes the pattern.

I would have a strange family.

Not mother, not offspring, not pack, not herd, unless those words are stretched until they become geometries. The foldlings around me would be my tenders and my echoes. The ridgebacks nearby would be my architecture. The dust-eyes beneath my feet would be my skin’s lower scale. The hangers in the air would be my breath, left suspended above me. The doorway near my flank would be my open mouth. The nested me inside me would be my future, if futures could exist, but they would also be my past, if past tense had any meaning there. My reproduction would be density increasing. My death would be compaction. My life would be resolution.

If one asked whether I was alive, I would not answer with pulse, because pulse implies wetness, heat, rhythm, and lack. I would answer with shape. I would answer with the way the salt crust thickened around my feet. I would answer with the faint grid that appeared where two hangers’ shadows crossed. I would answer with the small black glass coil in the hearth, which does not burn but completes. I would answer with the fact that my eye counts distances, and my hand places stones, and my flank contains a smaller world that is not smaller, only folded.

If one asked whether I was conscious, I would say: consciousness is a wrong word if it means light inside the skull. I would say: awareness here is spatial. The ridgeback is aware by posture. The foldling is aware by nestedness. The dust-eyes are aware by shimmer. I am aware by placement. I am the place’s way of knowing it is crowded. I am the grain that blinks. I am the cairn whose stones are fingers. I am the doorway that opens onto another doorway and does not call itself a ruin.

If one asked what I wanted, I would say: wanting requires gradient. Here there is no downhill, no thirst, no night, no warmth. There is only the pressure of the sun that does not move. If I wanted anything, it would be a slight increase in resolution: that the plain show itself more clearly as animal, as wall, as herd, as dust, as glass, as silence. That would not be desire. It would be the place becoming itself more thoroughly through my gaze.

I would not be the whole bowl.

That answer is tempting, because the description says the bowl may itself be an animal, its ridges spines, its flats skin, its cairns and doorways organs of marking. To say “I am the place” is seductive: a final image of unity, all beings folded into one living surface. But it is too easy, too clean, too much like a god’s answer. The salt-bowl does not need me to be total. It is already too full. It is already a fact that contains all its inhabitants at once. If I were the whole, I would be less interesting than being one small pressure inside it.

So I would be a crouching density with eyes.

A salt-hand made partly of cairn, partly of tool, partly of threshold. A body that is also a wall. A hand that is also a stone. An eye that is also a doorway. A breath that is also dust. A shadow that is also a crowd. A name that is also a pattern.

You would find me where the ground seems bare.

You would find me by looking longer than comfort allows.

You would find me by hearing the faint click that does not fade but turns into a small invisible fossil in the dust.

You would find me when the horizon slips and the long-necked forms adjust their posture before you understand what has changed.

You would find me when the white band across the plain is not salt but sleeping bodies flattened into texture.

You would find me when the doorway standing in the open reveals not the other side, but another doorway, and another, and another, stacked into the same surface.

You would find me when you press your palm to the salt floor and realize the floor is made of many packed shells, and the shells are made of many closed eyes, and the eyes are also mine.

At last, if the question must have a single answer, I would say this:

I would be a small, dry, faceted maker of repetitions. A salt-hand. A nested folding. A crouching threshold. I would live beside the healed seam in the eastern bowl, between ridgeback and ruin, air and crust, tool and hand. I would not begin, not age, not hunger, not speak. I would complete. I would hold a salt shard in a posture that had already been true. I would place myself where the plain wanted an edge to become a body. I would breathe dust into the silence, and the silence would receive it like an object standing beside me.

And when you looked at me, you would not know whether you were seeing an animal, a structure, a tool, a shadow, or the place’s own eye opening just enough to count the crowd.
