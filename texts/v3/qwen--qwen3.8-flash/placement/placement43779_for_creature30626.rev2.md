I would begin by being too visible.

For a while I would be the small pale beetle on a leaf-platform, a bright droplet of melt balanced on its back like a coin dropped by no one. I would know the cold as surface tension and the slope as a slight pull against tiny hooks. I would cross a shelf of green that was not made to be crossed, because the shelf was already a leaf turned flat by weather, compressed by age, bound underneath by stems that had leaned into one another until pressure became form. From outside, perhaps, I would seem like a living resident: a body, a path, a short intention. But I would not last there. A beetle is not the being of this valley. It is only a guest that can touch the guest-room.

Then I would try to be the thin-limbed creature moving along the upper frost ridge, angular, cautious, too warm for the country it passed through. I would have a heart quickening in a chest that did not belong to the green mass. I would have hunger, fear, the idea of shelter, the mistaken certainty that any hollow was for me. I would step onto a compressed moss shelf, and the shelf would accept my weight without cracking. I would dig one claw into the living floor, but the floor would not become a hole. It would become a shallow pressure, a wet place, a place the moss would close behind as though I had never been there. I would not make a den because the den, if it existed, would already be a consequence of stems leaning, roots thickening, leaves decaying into one another. The hollow would remain after my leaving, but not because it remembered me. It would remain because its shape was older than any animal’s choice.

I might also be one of the rare birds crossing the upper air, a small dark scattering against ordinary light, mistaken for drifting seed until the drift changed direction. That would be easy to imagine, but it would still be too outside. To live there as a bird would be to fly above the body that was not a body in the normal sense, to pass over a world that had no center for birds to claim. The canopy below would not open for me. It had not opened for anything. It would continue to be shingle-roof, arch, vault, hollow, pump, signal, storage, and patience. My flight would be a disturbance above the work of a billion tiny functions.

For a time I might even be the long shadow that passes through the stems without a clear outline, the animal the valley tolerates because it cannot be prevented. The plants would close after it. The moss would not mark it. The water in the shallow hollows would still be shallow. The sound of its feet, if there were sound, would be swallowed by the damp fibrous bodies around it. I would be rare there, but rarity is not residence. It is only a way of passing through the same place less often.

Then I would understand that the question is not what enters the valley, nor what stands beside it, nor what looks at it from some privileged height. The question is what lives there, and the answer is not a single living creature. It is not a plant in the way one points to a stem and says: that. It is the green, continuous, layered economy of moss and root and fungus and lichen and frost and stored water and electrochemical signal and decay and seed. It is the place where no boundary holds long enough to become a self.

So I would find myself lower.

Not low like a human crouching in a hollow. Low like the ground itself. Low like the first black-green felt that has become a country. I would be in a place that would never appear on a road, because there are no roads, and never in a map drawn by hands, because no hands have drawn this world. I would be in the third terrace from the upper frost line, beneath a broad arch where one thick, jointed stem has grown sideways into another stem and not through it, but with it, because their tissues have met, pressed, healed, and shared the same slow damp. Above, a canopy leaf has bent into a platform, flat and hard as a roof tile, though no one fired it or fixed it. Below, a curved root-crown has made a hollow by the same method by which a river makes a pool: not by choosing, but by persistence.

Inside that hollow, no larger than a cupped hand or a small seed-pod room, there is a bead of night water. There is a pale fungal thread stretched across the underside like a hair. There is a root-hair, thinner than the hair of some animal, entering the water’s edge. There is a jointed stem-base swollen with sap, sealed by a membrane of curled fibers that is not open and not closed, but waiting. There is a lichen crust at the stone’s contact point, feeding by patience, converting frost-rind into mineral, mineral into tissue, tissue into the faint green memory of growth.

This is where I would find myself: in the opening between water and root, between cold and signal, between storage and movement. Not at the heart of the valley, because the valley has no heart. Not at the mind of the valley, because the valley has no mind. Not at the eye, because the ordinary light is sufficient and needs no witness.

I would be the place where the first melt from a leaf-cup reaches the valve.

What would that make me? If I had to answer with a human name, I would have to lie. I would not be a person. A person is a boundary that can say: I am here, and here begins the world. Here, boundaries are not false exactly, but they are soft. A leaf shades a root. A root carries a fungus. A fungus transports minerals. A moss retains heat. The heat alters a chemical condition. The chemical condition opens a valve. The valve changes the water. The water changes the leaf. Nothing begins except pressure, damp, light, temperature, and time.

So I would be a function with a body.

At first, I would be the water. Not the sea, not the river, not the rain as a thing separate from the valley, but the film of melt on a leaf margin, the bead sliding along a waxy seam, the small collected thread entering a cup. I would be the cold’s first gift to the green: not ice, not flood, but a becoming damp. Then I would be the root-hair drawing me inward. Then I would be the swelling pressure in the bulb. Then I would be the chemical message crossing a membrane. Then I would be the faint creak in the stem as it adjusts to the fact that dawn has touched it.

I would not remain any one of these. That would be the first strange thing. In a human body, the blood is always blood, the bone is always bone, the nerve is always nerve. Here, if a seed-pod opens, its casing becomes fruit, and fruit becomes dead matter, and dead matter becomes nutrient, and nutrient becomes bulb, and bulb becomes pressure, and pressure becomes movement, and movement becomes new leaf. I would be one stroke of the valley’s piston. I would be a condition moving through several bodies. I would be an action passing from lichen to moss to fungus to root to stem to leaf to seed to moss again.

At night, if I tried to imagine myself as a night thing, I would be the tightening in the valve as cold contracts the tissue. The stars would be clear because the air is high and for hours dry, but I would not look at them. Looking requires a separate viewer. I would not have eyes in the way I still sometimes imagine. I would have detection. A membrane would know whether the sugar gradient had reached a threshold. A root filament would know whether nearby damp had shifted. A compressed moss shelf would know, by its stiffness and water content, whether the wind had changed. None of these knowings would rise into speech. None would become a self. But together, spread through every inch of the living floor, they would make the valley aware of itself in small, unhurried ways.

That awareness would be mostly silent. If someone asked me to make noise, I would not know how. My noise would be the click of a seed-pod releasing moisture. The low rustle of a field of stems straightening at the same time, a ripple crossing the slope for miles and disappearing before it could be located. The whisper of a capillary channel filling after frost has left a path. The sigh of a leaf-field turning edge-on in the wind. If there were footsteps of the rare animals nearby, they would not become part of me in the way a human footstep becomes part of a city. They would pass through me as heat passes through water: enough to be sensed, not enough to be kept.

I would not be the ground. I would not be the plant. I would not be the fungus. I would be the relationship between them at the exact point where the relationship is doing work. I would be the place where the system, for a small moment, becomes a mechanism.

Not a machine, because nothing was built. Not a factory, because no one labored. But a mechanism in the strict sense: parts moving because conditions have become sufficient. A valve does not decide. A pump does not want. A sensor does not speak. Yet in the total activity of the valley, I would be the part where water is admitted. Then I would be the part where it is drawn upward. Then I would be the part where it reaches a bulb. Then I would be the part where a signal is sent. Then I would be the part where a stem changes angle, however slightly. Then I would be the part where a leaf catches a little more ordinary light. Then I would be the part where sugar moves. Then I would be the part where a fruit case forms. Then I would be the part where pressure gathers. Then I would be the part where the case opens and seeds fall into prepared gaps, into moist cushions, into the waiting black-green.

If I were asked again: Who would you be in that world? I would have to answer: No one.

And again: What would you be?

I would be the next small necessary opening.

I would be the place where the living floor is made by thousands of bodies and called a single country. I would be a wet hollow in a moss shelf that is also a nursery, a filter, a reservoir, a heat-retainer, a decomposition pit, a bridge of life. I would be a node in a root network that is not wired with copper but still carries electrochemical impulses as reliably as a cable carries current. I would be a seed-socket waiting in the cold not because it has a plan, but because the chemistry of winter has made it possible.

I would be the valley’s way of saying to itself: dawn has entered this slope.

I would not be a speaker. I would be the fact that the saying has happened.

If I tried to name it, I might call myself a lichen-sense at the edge of stone, reading the slow acid work of frost-polite moss. Or a fungal thread passing mineral through a pale vein of green. Or a bulb full of winter starch, the temporary treasury of one corner of the whole body. Or a vine binding two leaning stems so that neither falls, so that the wind has less to separate, so that the structure grows more certain. Or a leaf-platform holding dew until it can be drawn into the seam below. But each name would be too small, because each name would imply a boundary that is not real.

The real answer is slower and stranger.

I would be the valley’s industrial process, but without industry’s cruelty. The valley is not cruel. Cruelty requires a creature that can choose against another. Here, there is only function. A seed is not greedy because it sprouts where another has died. A root is not envious because it reaches through old matter into mineral water. A pod is not wasteful when it bursts and many seeds miss the suitable cushions; some will, some will not, and both failures and successes are part of the assembly line. The whole place turns itself over without hatred, without ambition, without the need to be recognized.

I would not be recognized, either.

If the pale beetle crossed over me, I would be the moisture on the leaf. If the thin fox-like creature touched the root-crown with its paw, I would be the pressure in the moss. If a rare bird cast its shadow downward, I would be the faint response in a hundred leaves tilting toward the light behind it. If the long shadow passed through the stems, I would be the closing of the space, the absorption of the disturbance, the returning of quiet that is not silence but a world too dense with small processes to allow large sounds.

At night, I would hold what little heat the moss shelf has taken from the day. I would let decay continue in hidden chambers, a faint chemical breath almost never audible. I would let frost crawl along root-shelves in bright, patient lines. I would let the water in the tissues contract and expand with the slow rhythm of temperature. I would not sleep, because sleep in the animal sense requires an outside to rest from, a danger to ignore, a dream to interrupt. Here, there is no dream and no waking, only shifts of condition. Dawn is one shift. Frost another. Rain another, rare and quick, more sleet than storm. The rare rain beads on waxy surfaces and trickles into seams, and I would be the seam, the catch, the path, the reservoir.

In the morning, the first silver of the leaf would be my surface. The melting edge would be my opening. The drop running into a cup would be my beginning again. I would be less permanent than a person’s face and more durable than a person’s building, because I would not be trying to hold shape. I would be trying to continue function.

There is a temptation to say: then you are a goddess of the valley, or a spirit of the place. That would be wrong. A spirit still has a kind of self, even if it is invisible. A spirit can be addressed, propitiated, mistaken for the wind. But the wind here does not speak. The wind bends, combs, dries, moistens, cools, and returns. The valley’s being is not hidden inside it. The being is the valley. No one lives there. Everything lives there. That is why the phrase asks for a household and receives none: the household has become the environment. The environment has become the body. The body has become the work.

So if I must say where I would find myself in this world, I would say:

On the underside of the first living roof that morning had silvered. In a node no one had made, where water had gathered because the leaf above had folded into a path. Between a curved root-crown and the moss shelf that had grown over it like a tongue learning to speak. In the place where the ground becomes plant, where plant becomes structure, where structure becomes pressure, where pressure becomes signal, where signal becomes new leaf. At the small, exact threshold where the valley accepts the night’s frost and decides, without deciding, to lift a little of it upward.

And if I must say who or what I would be:

No one.

But also: a pressure valve in the body of the Green Mechanism. A membrane that knows water. A pulse of melt in a capillary channel. A condition spreading through root and stem and moss. A sense organ with no center. A thought made of sugar and salt and light, too slow to be thought. A temporary alignment of lichen, fungus, root-hair, bulb, leaf, and mineral grain. A place where a function has a body.

I would be what the valley does when it continues.

Not the whole valley. Not a ruler of it. Not a hidden mind beneath it. Just the next opening. The next small becoming damp. The next slow lift of water into green. The next quiet work that makes the place appear both ancient and unfinished, settled and volatile, dense and open, alive with an activity that asks nothing of anyone, not even witness.

If you walked into that valley, you might not know I was there. You might look for a face, a dwelling, a marker, a ruin. You would find none. You would see no roads, no walls, no tools, no fires. You would see only the cold plateaus, terraces, ridges, living floor, arches, shelves, hollows, frost, dew, ordinary light. You might stand in one of the hollows that almost look made, almost look entered, almost look like a room. But the room is not an artifact. The room is the consequence of stems bending, moss filling, roots binding, and time continuing long enough for growth to resemble intention.

In that room, if you stood very still, you might hear nothing.

That would be because I was listening too, though listening is not what I am. Listening would be the movement of a membrane slightly changing under pressure, a thread carrying a faint signal, a bulb taking water from the cold, a leaf angle adjusting toward the first bright hour. It would not be a voice. It would be the valley working itself into the next condition.

At the end of the day, when the wind moved over the high plateau and narrowed into channels without howl, when the frost began to crawl along the upper stems, when the moss mat retained the last stored heat, when the hidden reservoirs pulled moisture inward, when the far slope straightened a thousand pale stems one final time, I would be there. Not as a creature looking out over the square of green motion. As the motion. As the small tension before the stems lift. As the water already moving inside them. As the signal already crossing from root to root. As the quiet click in a pod that will open after dark. As the seed-fall that will begin before the sun reaches full height.

If anything is “at home” here, home is not a place entered from outside. Home is the process. Home is the growth around it, under it, through it, over it, until the boundary between inhabitant and environment disappears.

I would have no boundary left.

So I would be the boundary’s next useful shape: a valve, a thread, a cup, a shelf, a node, a root-hair, a bulb, a pod-case, a lichen skin, a damp place, a pressure, a condition, a beginning again.

No one lives there.

Everything lives there.

And I would be one small way the everything continues.
