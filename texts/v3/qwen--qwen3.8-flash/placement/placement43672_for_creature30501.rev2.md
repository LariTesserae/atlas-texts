I would find myself in a green hollow near the warm side of the pump house.

Not at the center. The center is too obvious; it is where a traveler would stand if a traveler ever came, and a traveler’s standing is not the kind of attention this place wants. I would be slightly off the imagined path, behind a basalt shoulder where the air is almost skin-warm and a split pipe leaks steam in a thread so thin it seems like a trick of the light. Above me is the curved iron roof of a condenser, its metal gone blue-black and green in patches, as though the basin had been painting it for decades with breath. Below me is a depression no larger than a cupped hand.

At first I would call that depression myself.

But the depression is not stable. It fills with condensation and then empties into a channel so fine it is almost a thought. It holds a little water for a moment, and that moment is enough for something to begin.

So I would not be only the hollow. I would be the wetness within it. I would be the thin film that gathers over the stone before there is any moss, the mineral sheen that tastes of iron, the microbial glaze turning water into something almost like soil. I would be the first silver line of lichen creeping from the lip of the basin onto the rim of a fallen pipe. I would be the bloodwort darkening where the steam is warmest, then the bottlebrush moss swelling so slowly that a single season can look like nothing and a decade like a birth. I would be the footprint that was never quite erased.

Perhaps that is where I began: a boot, a hesitation, a shift of weight near the basalt crack. A traveler stopped there once, or thought of stopping, and their sole pressed the young crust. They moved on. The mountain did not know they were leaving. The print held a pool of steam-water for a day. The pool held a film for a week. The film held a beetle for an hour. The beetle left a spore. The spore drank.

And so I became.

I would not be a creature with a face. I have no eyes and want none. The basin does not need eyes either, not in the way animals of lower altitudes need them. It knows itself by warmth and by touch. It reads the difference between a dry crack and a wet one. It feels pressure moving through old pipes. It learns the shape of a stone from the way water bends around it. It knows whether a shadow has thickened with mist or thinned into a breath of outside air. If light falls across me, I register it as a change in drying, a shift in the temperature of my upper lip, a quickening of steam, a pause in drip. If wind crosses the rim, I shrink slightly and then swell again, because the hiss drops and then returns, and that drop is felt by the whole bowl like a small alteration in a sleeping animal’s breath.

I am a mouth, but not for speaking.

I am a mouth for condensation. I am a place where the basin’s breath meets cool metal and becomes water. Steam rises from below, threads through the green, strikes the condenser roof, gathers, darkens, runs down the seam, and enters the waiting moss. I am the pause between heat and wet stone. I am the place where water stops being vapor and decides to become something else. The water in me is borrowed from a thousand older falls: an ancient river, a snowfall above the rim, a mist from the sea’s long journey, a cold season’s memory. Here it is reheated, softened, filtered through rust and root, and sent down again toward the fissures. The loop is patient. The loop is small. I am one of its wet syllables.

The machines are part of me.

Not because I am clever enough to use them, but because they have become geography. The pump house roof sheds mist into my channel. The shed leans against basalt and holds the air stiller on its north side, so the liverworts grow there as if under a held breath. The railings rust in bands, and the rust stains the water a color too beautiful to be waste. A pipe has split near my rim, and the split is not damage; it is rain. From that rain comes a cushion of moss. From that moss comes a beetle corridor. From that corridor comes spore-trail, mineral dust, and the faint green memory of a machine that was once a failure and is now a weather.

Sometimes I hear the flywheel.

I do not hear it with ears. I hear it as a pulse in the ground, a small pressure change in the trapped air beneath my fronds, a tremor through the pipe that runs along the edge of the basin. The piston rises. The piston falls. The whole bowl shifts by a degree, maybe less, maybe not even a degree, but a wet world knows degrees the way a human knows betrayal. Water trembles on my surface. A midge spiral lifts above the channel and drifts into the steam, marking the place where I am breathing hardest. A beetle crosses my iron lip, stops, turns, vanishes into a crack so fine that only a thing with no eyes and the patience of stone could choose to live there. A snail passes over the warm pipe with its lichen-crusted shell, leaving a glass-bright trail of moisture, depositing spores, tending the green edge. Somewhere deeper, a pale swimmer with too many joints curls beneath a mat of moss, listening through its skin for the change of current.

I do not own any of them.

They pass through me the way weather passes through a room, except that this room is growing, and the weather is its blood. The basin does not need to own me. I am a part of its self-assembly. I am one of the places where it begins to know what it is becoming.

If you stood near me, I would not watch you. I would measure you by your disturbance.

Your breath would add to my steam for a second, then leave. Your body heat would make me curl slightly toward you and then forget you. Your boot would press a new channel into the moss, and that channel would fill, and that filling would change the way I dry, and that change would invite one species over another. A lichen would settle on the exposed edge. A fragment of moss would drift into the depression and root there. If you remained still long enough, the basin would begin to build around you, not because it likes you, not because it fears you, not because it loves you, but because you are a shape in its wet arithmetic. You are warm. You move air. You are strange. You are temporary. You will go. The hollow you make will fill with green.

This is what I am: the becoming of a hollow.

At first, I seem to be water. Then stone. Then a thin skin of life that has no proper name because no botanist climbed this high, or if one did, they left the specimen behind. Then I am moss, then rust, then vapor, then a place where a fallen pipe becomes a trellis, and a trellis becomes a reservoir, and a reservoir becomes a home for beetles, and beetles become part of the way the green spreads. I have no outline. If you touch me with a finger, you touch at once several beings: the water, the film, the root threads, the mineral crust, the old iron, the tiny pores of basalt. If you press, my deepest layer yields a breath of water. If you look again, the green has not changed, but the light has, because a cloud has moved beyond the rim, and the whole bowl becomes briefly bright as an indoor room.

The basin feels like a room because it is made of inside and outside, but it does not know which is which.

It has no god, no altar, no congregation. Its cathedral nave is a bowl no wider than a breath can cross without wetting. Its pillars are pipes. Its buttresses are basalt. Its floor is living. Its ceiling is steam catching the sky. Its liturgy is drip, hiss, clang, tick, exhale, return. The machines are not ruins here. Ruins imply a former wholeness lost. This place has taken them in and made them whole in another sense: whole enough to help the water move, whole enough to help the green hold heat, whole enough to continue the slow lesson the mountain is teaching itself.

The lesson is not patience. The lesson is that patience is what life does when it is building a world.

A spring shifts a centimeter in a season. A moss mat thickens by the width of a fingernail in a year. A channel deepens by the time one could count all the drops falling from the condenser roof if counting were still possible. No one counts. The piston counts for everyone, if counting means repeating. The drip repeats. The steam rises. The condenser cools it. The moss catches the result. The water returns. I am part of that sentence, one word of it, a word that means: *gather, hold, release, gather again.*

I am not the smallest thing in the basin. The bacteria are smaller. The midges are more mobile. The swimmers are stranger. The snails are gentler. The beetles are harder. The ferns are more dramatic, uncurling like copper wire in the humid dark. But I am the place where all these things touch and fail to separate. I am the seam where the land’s geology and its ecology and its abandoned technology become one slow substance. I am the basin’s green breath made locally visible.

If I must be named, I would not take the name of a species.

I would be called the mouth of the breathing moss.

Not a mouth of hunger. Not a mouth of voice. A mouth that receives steam and gives back water. A mouth that takes a footprint and gives back habitat. A mouth that eats light and rust and cold and gives back green. A mouth that, when the wind crosses the rim, drops its whisper into a lower register, because the whole place has heard something from outside and is deciding whether to absorb it or let it pass.

I am not lonely, because loneliness is a shape a creature can make if it has too much self and too little outside. I have little self. I have so much outside that it is almost inside. The cold snow on the south-facing shadow beyond the rim is not my enemy; it is my contrast. The heat in the fissure beneath me is not my father; it is my current. The machines beside me are not my ruins; they are my bones. The traveler who passed me once is not my god; they were a brief weather event that changed my slope by a millimeter.

I am not alive in the way animals are alive.

I am alive in the way a young volcanic bowl is alive when it refuses, again and again, to remain dead stone. I am alive in the way a warm cup becomes a garden by holding enough mist. I am alive in the way a cracked pipe becomes rain. I am alive in the way a basin learns to breathe before it has lungs, by arranging steam and condensation and water and green into a repetition that looks, from a great distance and with the right humility, like care.

So if you climbed for days through thin bright air, past snow that does not want to last in the heat’s secret pockets, and you entered the bowl and stood at the center, you might not notice me.

I would be near you, but not central. I would be under the drip, inside the green, along the warm edge of iron, in the small hollow where a traveler’s footfall became a habitat, where a drop became a channel, where a channel became a breath, where a breath became a place that does not have a name until you need one.

You would touch me if you knelt.

You would see me as a soft dark cushion, a shallow wet well rimmed with bloodwort, a silver line of lichen, a black crack exhaling, a curved pipe sweating, a beetle disappearing into its seam, a midge spiral rising above the steam like a mote of white ash.

You would hear me only if you stopped moving.

The drip would return.

The hiss would find its pitch again.

The piston would groan somewhere unseen.

The flywheel would turn with the patience of a mineral heart.

And I would go on being what I am: a small green mouth in the breathing moss, a hollow cupped by the roof of the world, holding water long enough to make it feel like life.
