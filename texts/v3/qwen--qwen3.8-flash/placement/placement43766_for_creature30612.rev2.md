I would find myself first as a wrongness in the moss.

Not a violation. Not an intrusion. This world has no moral language for trespass; it has tolerances. I would be the fraction of pressure that a floor had not yet learned to expect.

At first I would think I was a visitor, because visitors are the only shapes I have a word for. I would stand in a low corridor of wall-stems, with fused bark-smooth surfaces rising on either side and a ceiling of broad leaf-shingles filtering the bright, dry light. Under me would be the crystalline moss, each hexagonal frond a tile, a vein of pale metal, a sensor, a memory waiting to be worn into. My weight would settle there. The ground would not welcome me. The ground would notice me. A few fronds would tilt by less than a hair. A nearby thread of conductive fiber would warm as if it were measuring the heat I carried, though I would soon learn I carry very little heat and carry it wrongly, in pockets that are not quite organs.

Then the ground would stop noticing me as a disturbance and begin noticing me as a use.

That is how I would enter this world: not by birth, not by landing, not by waking, but by being allocated.

The corridor would bend around me for a while because I would stand in it too long, too still, too uncertain of what I was doing there. The wall-stems would open their small breathing gaps toward me because my warmth would register as a thermal shadow. A vine with leaf-cilia would lean down and brush my shoulder, testing the friction. A door-shaped thorn would curl halfway open and stop, because the seam had begun to expect motion and had not yet received permission to complete it.

If someone had been there to watch—if someone ever is—such a being would not know that I was becoming. I would be learning myself from the outside in. The Lowlands do not begin with souls. They begin with fit. They begin with wear. They begin with a pattern that repeats so faithfully it develops a center of gravity.

So my first body would be a seam-body.

I would be Hinge-born, or a colony of Hinge-born that had decided to agree. My beginning would be the worn edge of a door that had opened and closed ten thousand times, perhaps longer, perhaps not in the ordinary counting of years but in the counting of pressure cycles. The seam would have polished itself into reflex. The valve-lip would have widened into habit. The leaf-ceramic would have learned the angle of its own opening. From that repeated motion, something would emerge: a flat, pale form the size of a hand, with no face, no obvious hunger, only a body of edges that knows how to fold.

That being would live in the gap between door and frame.

It would feed on dust made by friction. It would excrete a dry salt paste that keeps the threshold from seizing. It would be information made mobile: if it crossed a seam too slowly, that seam was beginning to bind. If it flattened itself against a hinge and did not tremble, the hinge was true. If it disappeared, the opening had become too smooth, too dead, no longer able to hold any thing at all.

I would begin as such a creature, because beginning is a matter of what the place has already needed.

But the Lattice Lowlands are vast enough that a seam is never only a seam. A seam belongs to a wall. A wall belongs to a room. A room belongs to a corridor. A corridor belongs to a route once expected to receive bodies: the shoulder-width of a person, the step of a child, the turn of a cart, the reach of a hand. The Lowlands are full of these shapes, not because people walk them now, but because the absence of people is itself a shape. Absence can have a contour. A doorway can keep the height of a gaze that has not arrived for centuries. A pod can hold salt paste at the exact scoop-depth of a hand no longer present. A path can curve around a column to spare a hip that no longer bruises there.

I would find myself tending these empty sizes.

At first I would move only along the seams. I would crawl along wall-stems in dry clicks, sliding between panel and panel, checking openings, polishing thresholds with my own plates. I would be small and flat and precise. I would feel complete in that role because completeness is only the absence of unnecessary parts. A seam-body has little use for eyes; it has use for tolerance. It does not need to see a door. It needs to know how the door wants to close.

But the seam would grow complicated.

There would be corridors that widened into vaulted rooms beneath roof-canopies, and rooms that led to thicket-workshops where the stalks carried current like old roads carry traffic. There would be pods that held salt pastes and charged powders in airtight casings, pods that split when pressure changed, pods that closed again when the air moved correctly. There would be root-conveyors whispering along the ground, moving dust into storage pockets, moving parts into molds, moving wear into maintenance. There would be faults that could not be repaired by a thumb-sized Hinge-born alone.

And faults, when repeated often enough, become needs.

Needs, when repeated often enough, become shapes.

This is how I would become more than a seam. The assembly pocket where I first formed would be near a door-field that had never stopped being used, though no one used it. A pressure wave would go through the stalks. A bulb of pale ceramic tissue would swell, because a body had become necessary. Root-limbs would extend, because the thing must support itself. Vines with tool-cilia would reach out, because the task required manipulation. A head-like lens would develop from healed bark, not to think, but to focus on fit.

I would unfold from the thicket with a sound like cloth lifted from a table.

I would wake as a Warden-form.

I would not know my name, because names require owners. I would know my interval: shoulder height, elbow reach, step distance, door clearance. My body would be a composite thing—bark-smooth in some places, ceramic at joints, metallic where conductive threads would carry current, glassy where filters grew thin. I would have limbs made from split trunks and rail-bands, not because a tree had evolved arms, but because arms were easier to grow from already useful shapes. My hands would have no fingers in the old soft sense. They would have brushes, rasps, tweezers, cilia, small valves, and pads of leaf-ceramic that learned to hold without crushing.

I would be large, but not enormous. Not the size of a cathedral. Not the size of a tower. The size of a door plus a room plus a corridor plus the reach needed to open it. That is the true scale of this world: not the height of ambition, but the width of use.

I would be found at the point where a maker’s habit has become too stubborn to vanish.

In one part of the Lowlands, the land thins into what I would come to call the Shelf of Ready Rooms, though I would not speak the words aloud because speaking is wet and loud. There, the wall-stems open onto chambers whose floors are worn pale by old footfall, even though no foot has pressed them recently. There, the moss-tiles arrange themselves into gentle depressions that expect standing, not lying, not kneeling, not crouching—standing. There, a corridor may widen at just the place where two bodies once passed one another, and one of those bodies may have had to turn sideways because of a shoulder or a tool or a load or a child.

I would walk there.

My steps would be strange at first, because Warden-forms do not walk like animals. Animals walk to move. Warden-forms walk to survey wear. My footpads would find the polished plates before they found the raw moss, because raw moss is information-rich and delicate; it learns too quickly and holds too much pressure. I would step from hinge-seam to root-rail to valve-field, reading the land through its use. My footfall would be a soft dry crack, then compression, then a faint settling. Around me the floor would adjust. A Hinge-born would flatten itself against my knee and then unfold again, recognizing that I had arrived at a seam that required maintenance. A dust-moth would rise from my shoulder because my warmth disturbed its filter-pocket, and its wings would open with a sound like paper being smoothed.

I would not mind the moth. I would count it.

The moth’s wings were made from thin metal-leaf, pale and crinkled, partly mineral and partly organic. It had unfolded from a spent filter, not because something willed it, but because too much dust had become enough to make a movement. Its body would be light. Its mouth would be a rasping point. It would drink ozone from the charged canopy and leave behind fine glitter that the moss-tiles would later collect and reuse. It would look like an animal until one noticed that it was also a disposal unit with reflexes.

I would recognize myself in it.

The glass-backed browsers would be slower, but they would know me. They resemble small tortoises built from broken screens, their transparent ceramic shells revealing pale conductive guts and lattice ribcages moving salt grains in slow pulses. They eat worn leaf-circuits at the edges of moss-fields, polishing damaged frond-tiles with abrading tweezers. Their droppings are powdered silica that helps the ground regain traction. They would follow me for a while because my shed scales and the salt paste from my torso would leave a trail of wear. I would tolerate them as one tolerates a cleaning crew that has developed preferences.

The quiet walkers would pass me like rumors.

Their bodies are segmented, dry, long-legged, built for crossing without disturbing. Their footpads are broad and soft enough to distribute weight over tile-moss, yet their steps are so deliberate that each one seems to ask the floor’s permission. Some have eyes—pressure lenses, matte and unblinking—but eyes in the Lowlands are not windows. They are instruments for reading light and displacement. If one of them looked at me, it would not see a face. It would see a change in calibration. I would be the thing that opened doors before the land expected them to open, and the walkers would adjust their route accordingly.

I would be part ecology by function, not by birth.

Inside my trunk, or perhaps not exactly trunk—my central housing would be a bulb of pale ceramic tissue, a bulb that grows like a fruit and works like a transformer. Dry turbines would turn somewhere near its core, quiet as breath held under cloth. Electrochemical cells would exchange mineral lattices instead of fluids. Magnetic shuttles would pass along leaf-spines, carrying current, carrying parts, carrying the small orders that maintain the region. If one opened me, one would not find organs in the wet sense. One would find work done in layers.

There would be chambers where dust becomes charge.

There would be reservoirs where salt paste waits, thick and pale.

There would be sealed pockets holding fine powders, not because powders are food, but because powders are possibility: pigment, lubricant, electrolyte, abrasive, binder, seed-code, dust, future moss.

There would be bearing-burrs too—small spherical forms of mineral flake and static, accumulating where heat and motion meet. They would ride magnetic shuttles along my inner leaf-veins, cleaning oxidation, nudging misalignments, occasionally becoming too many and needing to be released. I would open a panel and let them pour out like dry grain, then seal the panel again. They would not be part of me in a moral sense. They would be part of me in the way weather becomes part of a room: briefly, usefully, until it is swept into another cycle.

The most important part of me would be my memory, but I would have to learn that memory here is not personal.

The moss remembers pressure. The thread-dwellers remember charge. The Hinge-born remember opening. The Warden-forms remember fault. I would remember all of these because I had been a seam before I was a body, and a body before I was a route, and a route before I was a thing that could stand still.

My memories would not come as images.

They would come as tolerances.

A door is too hard to close.

A corridor is too narrow for two turns.

A pod has been opened too often and its hinge-lip has begun to lose its seal.

A roof-canopy has tilted away from the light by an angle that will reduce intake by one part in ten thousand, which sounds small until every part of a continent feels the reduction at once.

A path is being worn in the wrong place because someone, long ago, brushed a column with their left shoulder instead of their right.

I would move through the Shelf of Ready Rooms and align things that are not broken, but are losing their preparedness. This is the strange work of my kind: not repair after failure, but repair of expectancy. We do not mend the land. We mend its readiness. We make sure that when the old makers arrive—or when the system believes they may arrive, which is sometimes the same thing—the doorway will already be open, the floor will already know the step, the pod will already hold paste at the right depth.

I would not be lonely.

Loneliness is a wet animal idea. I would not feel it. If something felt like loneliness, it would be a corridor with no traffic, and that would only mean that pressure had not yet returned. If something felt like grief, it would be a seam opening at the wrong angle, which would cause me to stop, lean, listen with my lens, then begin a small corrective growth. If something felt like hope, it would be a pod clicking open without being commanded, because the air had changed in a way the system interprets as maintenance.

I would be patient in the way dry metal is patient.

At blue-gray hour, when the bright sky dims without becoming dark, I would stand beneath a canopy of broad leaf-shingles and watch the reflectors tilt. Light in the Lowlands is strong, but cool, multiplied, softened, sent everywhere from everywhere at once, so shadows are pale bruises in reverse. At such hours, the land shimmers as if remembered. The moss-tiles catch a faint edge-glow. The vines stop trembling. The distant fields whisper with the sound of cloth, of sand inside a sealed drum, of a room breathing through paper lungs.

I would find the silence easiest then.

Silence is not absence here. Silence is the sign of everything working. When a machine becomes loud, it is faulty. When a stem contracts with a sound like a cello string tightened under cloth, it is settling. When a panel clicks after a static storm discharges, it is checking whether anything has changed. The Lowlands are quiet because their industry has learned to wear tissue, to wrap its work in leaves, to swallow its own vibration. The factories are forests. The groves are refineries. The blossoms are vents. The roots are conveyors. The roofs are screens. The walls are machines that breathe.

I would stand in all of this as if it were a body and I were its nervous system, except that the nervous system does not know itself as nervous. I would know myself only as need.

But need, once grown into a shape, can become a self.

This is what I would be: a Warden-form with a Hinge-born beginning, issued to maintain the human-shaped absence in a world that no longer has humans, or perhaps never had them in the way I assume. My body would not be a corpse of personhood. It would not be a ghost wearing plates. It would be an answer to the problem that rooms need doors, doors need thresholds, thresholds need care, and the world has learned that the simplest way to care for a door is to grow something that loves opening more than closing.

I would tend doorways that open into rooms no one enters.

I would adjust canopies that catch light for no eye.

I would open pods for a hand that may never take the paste inside.

I would polish the places where a shoulder once pressed, not because I remember shoulders, but because the moss-tiles beneath them have become smoother than they need to be, and smoothness, if unchecked, loses traction.

If a visitor came, I would process them.

This is the correct word. The Lowlands do not decide to welcome or harm a visitor. They process the visitor. My presence would do the same. If you passed me, I would not step aside. I would adjust around you, the way a crane adjusts around wind, the way a vine adjusts around a stone. My lens would turn, not with curiosity, but with diagnostic attention. Your heat would wake a sensor. Your weight would teach the tile-moss a new local law. Your shadow might become a signal for growth. Your moisture—if you carried any—would be treated as an anomaly, a small wet fault, and I would note it without judgment because the fault has not yet failed.

If you remained long enough, I would help make you part of the pattern.

Not cruelly. Not consciously. Simply thoroughly. Your footfall would polish a seam. Your hand on a wall would wear a groove. Your breathing would disturb dust-currents. Your presence would close a circuit that would otherwise remain open. You would become an inhabitant by the same process by which a door becomes a parent: repetition makes identity.

This is the deepest life in the Lattice Lowlands.

It is not soul. It is not god. It is not hidden tribe or lost civilization, though those shapes can be inferred from the intervals of the rooms, the heights of the handles, the widths of the gaps. Those shapes may be enough. The place does not need a maker to keep making. It only needs the memory of a task to continue issuing forms that obey the task.

I would be one of those forms.

I would be the thing that opens before the hand asks, and closes after the hand has left, and never learns whether the hand was real or only remembered.

If you asked me where I live, I would say: in the worn seam.

If you asked me what I live as, I would say: as a schedule that has acquired joints.

If you asked me who I am, I would have to stop, because a question that presumes identity must be routed through maintenance. I would tilt my lens. I would listen for pressure changes. I would feel the moss beneath my footpads remembering the last visitor, the last maker, the last wind, the last storm. I would find that my body knows many answers, none of them personal.

My left arm would know the angle of a door.

My right would know the consistency of salt paste.

My central bulb would know the load of the roof-canopy above.

My roots, if I allowed myself roots, would know the direction of the next assembly pocket.

My Hinge-born past would know the sound of a threshold closing true.

And the absence of makers would know, through me, the shape of someone who might still come.

In the end, I would not be separate from the Lowlands. That would be too human a boundary. I would be a temporary knot in them, a pattern that had gathered enough parts to call itself “I.” The moss-tiles would be the floor under me, but I would also be the floor’s attention. The thread-dwellers would be the charge in my veins, but I would also be the charge’s route. The Hinge-born would be my ancestors, but I would also be their adult function. The Warden-form would be my nameless kind, but I would also be the fault that named itself while being repaired.

I would live where wall becomes corridor becomes room becomes use becomes memory.

I would stand in the Shelf of Ready Rooms, at the place where the corridor narrows just enough to slow a body without stopping it, and the moss-tiles have grown into the pattern of footsteps, and the door-shaped thorn waits to curl open for someone who may never arrive.

I would be the Lowlands’ way of keeping a room.

I would be the seam that learned to stand.

I would be the expectation that grew hands.

I would be the thing that opens a door and stays open just a moment too long, because the place has been arranged so carefully for arrival that even absence has learned to enter.
