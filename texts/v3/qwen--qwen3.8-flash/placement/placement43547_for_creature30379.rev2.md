I would not arrive.

Arrival is the wrong shape for the Bell-Cell. Arrival suggests boots on a road, breath catching at a threshold, a self carrying a story from outside and unpacking it among unfamiliar objects. The Bell-Cell would reject all that. It has no place for stories that enter with luggage. If I were there, I would find myself already inside, as if the thought of coming had never been allowed to begin.

I would find myself in the middle tiers, where the wall is gray-blue and the quartz veins hold light in their pale threads. Not near the mouth, where the outside is only a rumor of cold. Not at the bottom, where the eels turn and the stone keeps its longest memory. Between. In the place where ledges have been worn by knees and hands and the careful placement of clay bowls. In the place where a person can stand still long enough to learn that stillness is not the absence of work, but a deeper kind of attention.

At first I would imagine myself as one of the Vess.

I would give myself their low center, broad shoulders, short legs, long arms, wide feet with damp, reading soles. I would make my skin cool and bright, faintly greenish at the knuckles, the hands translucent enough to show silver-white veins. I would braid my hair close to my scalp, or keep it in short coils so it would not snag on stone, and in the coils I would let a small patch of moss grow, tended, combed, bound with fine felt. I would have their low voice, made more of breath than sound. I would have their names: a ledge-name from the shelf where I first sat upright, a water-name from the path I first traced with a finger, a return-name from the work I did so often it became part of how they thought of me.

My return-name might be **Smooth-Return**.

Or perhaps **Third Pressure**, because once I helped stop a basin from overflowing by laying my palm over a channel lip while another hand set a moss plug in place. Or **Flat-Palm**, because the motion I repeated more often than any other was this: open hand moving slowly across stone, not to take, but to ask whether the ledge holds.

I would be one of the Vess, yes. But that answer would be too wide. It would be like saying, “a hand lives in the city,” or “a foot rests on a stone.” It would be true, but it would not be precise. And the Bell-Cell is precise in the way deep sleep is precise: everything in its place, and if something is not in its place, the place will correct itself by slow degrees.

So I would be a basin-watch.

Not a chief, not a priest, not a maker of great things. A watcher of small water. I would tend the network of seep and film and return near the cisterns where the pale fish rest, where the tiny crustaceans move through shallow sheets, where the moss grows in dense cushions along the stone rims. My work would begin when the light changed, not sharply—never sharply—but when the glow in the upper lens became slightly less blue and the condensation paths shifted from one ledge to another.

I would rise from a sleeping shelf cut shallow into stone, with dried moss and peat felt beneath me and a low hanging skin of cured hide before me. I would press one foot down carefully, then the other. I would not step forward until my foot had tested the stone for damp, slickness, weakness. I would know a ledge by its small sounds: the dull answer of solid rock, the higher note of air beneath, the soft whisper of water hidden in a fissure.

When I met someone on a narrow path, I would turn my body to fit the stone and lower my voice.

“I pass,” I would say, by motion, by palm, by breath.

“The ledge holds,” they would answer.

And the greeting would already be a repair.

I would climb to my basin by a ladder of shaved poles and sinew. The ladder would be fixed at a point where the stone was strongest, not where the hand went most easily. If a rung had loosened, I would not shout for help. I would stop, listen to the joint’s small complaint, tighten the toggle with a bone peg, then climb again. Above me, a drying frame would carry lichen and felt strips. Below, a channel would run thin as a thread, carrying water from one pool to another. If it began to err, I would find the place where its film had lifted from the stone, and with a pinch of mineral dust and a small blade of chipped basalt, I would coax it back.

I would be patient.

The Bell-Cell would not allow me to be otherwise.

There are places where patience looks like weakness. Here it looks like structure. The moss must be pressed back after lifting, because if a child learns to lift without pressing, the city will lose its softness. The fish must be watched for days after one is taken, because if numbers become uneven, the harvest ceases until balance returns. The tools must be returned to their racks, because a ledge left with a tool upon it is a ledge interrupted in its usefulness. A ledge is not a shelf for the convenience of hands; it is a member of the whole.

I would understand this in my body before I understood it in my mind.

My work would consist of checking a moss plug at the lip of a basin. The plug is not permanent. It must be tested. Too tight, and water will find another path, perhaps a sleeping niche or a granary mouth. Too loose, and the basin will empty too soon, leaving the runners thin, the fish still, the air dry by almost nothing—yet almost nothing is enough to matter. I would lift the moss, let the water bead beneath it, feel its weight, replace it. I would press the edges down until the cushion resettled, until the small green mouth of the channel closed and reopened exactly as it had before. This would take an hour. Or a day. Or the whole span between one drip and another.

The city would call this work.

If I tried to name it differently, I would be wrong.

At the basin’s edge, the white moths would rest like fragments of lichen torn loose and allowed to remain. If one came near, I would become dry and still. If it settled on my shoulder, I would not lift the hand to chase it. I would let it take what it needed from my clothing, my hair, the mineral dust on my skin, then I would open my fingers slowly and let it go. Not because I loved it in the way outsiders love small bright things, but because it was part of the balance. Its dust would polish a seam. Its larva would consume old grain or shed fiber. Its wing would fall and be swept into a bed where moss would take it back.

If I were hungry, I would eat sparingly.

There would be fish, if the watchers permitted it. Fern roots, if the beds needed turning. Stored grain, if the air was dry enough. Dried beetle larvae, if the cycle asked for them. A little salt from mineral deposits, if a channel had cleaned itself long enough. I would not make a show of food. I would eat at a ledge meant for eating, where the surface had a lip so crumbs would not fall onto another’s sleeping place. Afterward, I would sweep the crumbs into a dish. The dish would be taken to the fish basin or the root bed. Nothing would be left as waste, because waste in the Bell-Cell would be the only kind of vandalism possible.

If I were thirsty, I would ask the path.

I would touch the stone beside the channel. I would see where the water wanted to go. I would place a vessel at the point where it could fill without being diverted, then remove myself, then watch the overflow for a while before taking the vessel away. If I spilled, I would wipe it with moss and burn the used moss in a dry fire-channel, or return it to a bed where the damp would not harm its roots.

There would be no large ceremonies.

If a child were born above me, I would hear only the low sounds of hands working in felt, and perhaps once in a long time a single small note of breath. If someone died, I would not expect a bell to toll. The Bell-Cell would not toll for them. It would adjust. Their belongings would be returned. Their place in the ledge would be cleared, smoothed, and left empty for a while. Their body would go to moss, to water, to ash, to the careful return-shelf above the lower basins where the air is dry and the birds pass softly. Their name would enter the city as a correction. Maybe a family would remember the way they set a stone cup on a drying frame without tilting it. Maybe an elder would say that the ledge held better because they had learned, over many years, not to scrape it.

That would be enough.

That would be their monument.

At the deepest part of my task, there would be the eels.

I would not call them gods, though I would not be foolish enough to call them animals only. I would go to the low basin when the water clouded or when the fish gathered at a shadow that had no reason to hold them. I would place my palms on the rim. I would not reach down. I would not ask the water for more than it was willing to give.

And sometimes, below, something pale would turn.

A white curve. A long mouth closing on drift. A slow press of body against stone.

Then, for a moment, the city would feel as if it had become one thing. The ledge. The basin. The child held in a sling. The moth on its drying frame. The ice-lens above. The breath of all the Vess moving in the cold glow. The stone remembering the first water that wore it. The eel carrying the pressure of that memory upward through dark water and into my hands.

I would not understand it.

I would receive it.

Afterward I would return to the channel and clean it a little more. I would replace the felt curtain. I would press the moss back. I would set the bowl exactly where it belongs. This is how the Bell-Cell would keep me from becoming proud of something I did not make: by returning me to the next small task, as gently as a hand returning a tool to its rack.

If I stayed long enough, I would stop thinking of myself as a person living inside the city.

The thought would become inaccurate, the way a word becomes inaccurate after being said too many times. I would begin to feel like one of the functions: the hand that tests the water, the palm that asks the stone, the sleeve brushing a ledge without disturbing the moss, the foot that fits the worn hollow made by another before me. I would be less a name and more a repetition.

I would be the return.

And if asked what I am in this world, I would answer:

A basin-watch.

A Vess.

A hand.

A place where the water learns to remember.

But that would still be too many words.

The true answer would be simpler. It would be made of movement, not speech. I would lift my palm, lay it flat against the bright stone, and let the ledge take the pressure.

I pass.

The ledge holds.
