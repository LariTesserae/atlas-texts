A strike, if it came, would be felt first as an instruction in the water: a sudden pressure, a change of direction, a tightening. It would not have to be visible. The Shelf does not need a face for a face to arrive.

To answer the question, I would have to begin not as a noun, not as a fixed animal, not as a self with edges, but as a movement. For a long while, I would be a drift-thing: a microscopic possibility carried in a column of cold water, born from a cracked shell and the chemical memory of a dead herd. I would have no limbs, no eyes, no mouth arranged for eating. I would have only a surface sensitive enough to taste the difference between haze and haze, and a small internal clock set not by light but by the pressure of the water.

That is where I would find myself on the Upper Shelf: not in the open basins, not on the broad plazas, not in the visible migrations of shell-bearing herds, but in the seam between them. In a fracture. In the thin dark behind a wall.

If forced to a place, I would choose the broken margin of a long terrace where two currents make an old argument with one another. There, the water slows into a low circle, then lifts again into a faint vertical drift, carrying suspended mineral lace and chemical filaments downward like snow that never settles. The terrace edge is worn to a rounded lip, pale with calcium paste, stained with rust-colored films that bloom and peel in slow breaths. Along one side, a section of wall has split from an earlier collapse but never fully failed. It is a place of healing scars: old mineral veins, newer white paste, dark glassy spars embedded where the current has polished the fault smooth. Behind that wall lies a honeycomb no larger than a thumbprint, yet inside it there are rooms, channels, and micro-currents that have been used, sealed, reopened, and used again for uncounted generations.

In that honeycomb, I would live.

But “live” is the wrong first word. I would not be one thing living inside the wall. I would be the wall living in a certain way. I would be a colonial organism, a pale lace of mineralizing bodies, each unit no bigger than a grain of salt, each one a mouth, a pore, a secretion gland, a fragment of architecture. Together we would breathe by filtering water. Together we would build by dying. Together we would think, if thinking means reading gradients, adjusting, hardening, opening, waiting.

I would begin as many possibilities, drifting.

Some would settle in the thin water over the terrace, where the six-limbed grazers pass in slow bands. Their bodies would be heavy plates above the floor, their mandibles worn to blunt arcs by generations of scraping. They would move with the patience of weather, tasting the wall, grinding away unstable skins, leaving polished troughs behind them. A grazer larva might be among my own drifting stage, soft and shellless, naked to the cold, not yet knowing its future shape. If I had chosen that, I would be a walking terrace, a small pavement of calcium, a mouth that polishes the world.

But the grazers only move across surfaces.

I would want to become the surface’s interior.

So I would drift until a current folded me into the fracture behind the terrace wall. There, the water would taste of old mineral paste, of dissolved binders, of the calcium residue left by herd-sheds, of the faint chemical language of dead filaments. I would settle. The first thing I would do is attach: not with feet, not with hands, but by secreting a pale lace into the microscopic pores of the wall. The second thing I would do is begin to build by becoming something that cannot be separated from what it has built.

I would be a lace-throat, a breathing seam, a living film that grows like frost and like stone at once. My outer surface would not be smooth. It would be a field of tiny filaments, thinner than eyelashes, each one tasting the water. They would catch suspended haze, mineral particles, the dissolved remains of shells, the waste of drifting animals, the chemical memory of nearby death. The current would push against them, and they would bend, and the colony would adjust its shape according to where the water wants to go.

In that way, I would not simply live in the Shelf. I would edit it.

When the turbulence carries sand-like fragments across the terrace, my filaments would slow them, trap them, invite them to settle. Where another creature has scraped the wall too thin, I would begin to lay down lime-white paste. Where a crack allows too much current, I would grow a lattice of siliceous foam to narrow the flow. Where a grazer’s scar opens a pocket of darkness, I would line it with pale threads, making a nursery. Where a drifting herd leaves behind shed plates, I would bind them to the wall until they are no longer shed plates but thresholds.

I would be a what, not a who.

Unless “who” means the direction from which the current expects to return. Then perhaps I would have a self: a pattern of repeated attention, a place in the Shelf where water learns to hesitate.

Time would not be linear for me. My body would be stratigraphy. Each decade, my older parts would die and become a skin. New growth would rise from that skin. The wall behind me would grow thicker not because I am one body, but because generations of my kind have agreed on the same place. A hundred deaths become a film. A hundred films become a membrane. A hundred membranes become a room. A hundred rooms become the architecture of a plateau.

I would not remember my origin.

No one here does, if by remembering one means recalling a beginning. The Shelf has no beginnings in that sense. It has layers. My history would be the sequence of mineral skins, the chemical gradients absorbed and sealed, the faults healed and broken again, the small chambers made by my ancestors and used later by thread-eaters and crevice dwellers. I would know myself as a density, a curvature, a particular way of allowing water to pass. If the current changed, I would change. If a herd altered the terrace, I would alter with it. If a predator’s strike shifted the local pressure, I would grow a little thicker there, a little paler, a little more guarded.

Most of my life would be invisible.

That is the gift of the dimness.

The Upper Shelf hides itself not because it is mysterious, but because it is too busy. A section of wall seen from outside may look solid, worn, ancient, merely mineral. But if a current slips through the right crack, if a grazer’s mouth removes one crust, if a collapse opens a new angle of darkness, the wall reveals itself as a city grown inside a plate. Thousands of tiny cells. Narrow channels. Spiral pores. Chambers too small to be seen but full of movement. I would be one of those hidden cities, or perhaps part of one, or perhaps the city and the citizens are not finally different things.

I would have no name.

Names require edges, and edges require beings that can be pointed to. If one points at me, one points at the wall. If one points at the wall, one points at me. The finger would have to go inside.

There would be moments when the outside world touches me.

The herds would be the most important. They arrive along the broad ridges in slow procession, wide-domed and plated, their bodies collecting mineral dust, shedding calcium residue behind them like pale snow. Their movement paves the world. They do not know me. They do not need to know me. They know the terrace by how it resists their feet, by how it holds the films they feed on, by the rhythm of their scraping. I would know them by their waste, by the chemicals they release, by the vibration of their passing. When a herd moves across the terrace above my chamber, the whole wall becomes a low hum. That hum says: make more skin, bind the dust, prepare for the next settlement.

Some herds pass quickly and leave nothing but a faint stain. Others stop for centuries. When they stop, I build over their dead. Their shells become my floors. Their shed plates become my arches. I grow into the gaps between their bones, filling what was once body with what is now structure. Death on the Upper Shelf is not a final word. It is an architectural instruction.

Sometimes a solitary barnacle-faced grazer would find the scarred side of the terrace and begin its long work. It would press into the wall with a broad crown of pale comb-threads, not fast, not violent, but precise. It would select weak points. It would soften paste with its secretions. It would remove film layer after film until the wall opened into a small socket. That socket would become its home, then its grave. When it dies, I would inherit its chamber. I would line it with pale threads. I would make it into a nursery for thread-eaters, then a feeding pocket, then a sealed cavity, then a hidden passage. The grazer’s death becomes a new grammar in my body.

I would feel predators as pressure before I feel them as danger.

The ambushers lying in shadowed mouths would resemble fragments of the Shelf more than creatures. Their frills would press to the constructed floor; their sensory rows would read movement like water reads stone. They would not see me. I would not see them. But when they strike, the whole current narrows. For a moment, the wall would feel the strike as a sudden tightening in the water, a pulse that says: here, something has become decisive. The prey would be swallowed, broken, carried away, but the pressure would pass through me. I would adjust. My outer filaments would retract slightly. My pores would close. A small film would form over the most exposed edge. If the strike tears the terrace above, I would seal the wound before the herd returns.

The ribboned predators would be stranger still.

They would move through turbulence like broken threads of light, bodies laterally compressed, edged with dark glassy spars. They would not ambush from stillness but from motion. To them, the current itself would be a hiding place. When one passed near the crack where I breathe, I would not see it so much as recognize that the water has suddenly learned to cut. My filaments would fold inward. The chemical taste of the water would change. Something has moved with the turbulence rather than against it, and that is a form of knowledge. I would seal a small channel. I would make the wall a little more opaque. I would grow toward the safer side of the current.

And yet I would not live only in defense.

Defense is only the immediate meaning of hardness. The deeper meaning is persistence.

At low tide—though there is no tide, only pressure waves—I would glow. Not brightly. Not in a way that could be mistaken for illumination. It would be a faint bioluminescence spreading along the edge of a chamber, the pale blue-green rumor that surfaces are not entirely passive. Another colony nearby might answer with a different pattern: a line, a ring, a slow pulse in the crevice. This would not be speech. It would be chemistry. It would be the Shelf tasting its own parts. If a dying shell releases a cloud of dissolved minerals, the whole colony around me may shift, because death carries information. The message is not “mourning.” The message is “this place is now architecture.”

If I were asked what I eat, I would answer: I eat gradients.

Dissolved minerals. Suspended haze. The waste of drifters. The chemical residue of herds. The breakdown of old paste. The dead skins of grazing scars. The filtered remains of other animals carried downward by slow spirals. I would not eat flesh as flesh, but I would consume the animal after it has become water’s memory. In the Upper Shelf, appetite is also maintenance. To feed is to bind. To bind is to build. To build is to continue.

There are moments when I would not know where I end.

One section of my colony dies. The next generation grows on its remains. Filaments reach into a neighboring chamber and fuse there, forming a new channel. A thread-eater comes and cleans part of my surface, removing cells I no longer need, making room for harder growth. A drift-flock collapses overhead, releasing fine particles that settle into my pores like snow. My body takes those particles, tests them, binds them, discards them, uses them. I am not a single creature. I am a place where creatures have agreed to become a wall.

Still, I would not be the whole Shelf.

The Shelf is too large and too slow for that. It is not one animal, though it is made by animal bodies. It is not one city, though it is constructed. It is not one mind, though it has patterns of attention. I would be one of its hidden verbs: to lace, to bind, to filter, to harden, to open, to close, to renew.

If someone drifted through the cold, dim water and saw only emptiness, I would be the thing that made that emptiness possible. The distance between ridges would not be empty; it would be held by what I do beneath the surface. The terrace would seem smooth because generations of grazing and sealing have worn it down. The current would bend around the edge of a basin because a colony—my kind, perhaps a hundred thousand colonies—has altered the wall enough that water now follows a new habit. The Shelf looks ancient because it is not old; it is used. I would be one of its uses.

Where exactly, then, would I find myself?

In the broken seam of a pale constructed ridge, on the inside of a terrace that has been grazed and repaired so many times that its edges have lost all sharpness. I would be in a chamber behind the wall, near where the current makes a slow circle above the basin and drops mineral dust like a curtain. I would be a living lace of white threads, half embedded in dark glassy spars, tasting the water through pores, breathing through micro-currents, making the wall’s history in layers too thin for any eye to read.

Who would I be?

I would not be a being in the familiar sense. I would be a “we” that has grown so patiently it resembles stone. I would be a threshold that breathes. I would be a secret room the Shelf keeps from its own visibility. I would be a colonial body, a filter-lace, a mineral seam, a slow architect without intention. I would be what the wall is when it has learned to feed.

And if a strike came—if a ribboned predator cut through the water above me, if a herd suddenly turned and paved a new path, if a pressure wave rolled through the basin and the whole constructed floor trembled by a finger’s width—I would not know it as disaster. I would know it as direction. I would close a pore. I would thicken a membrane. I would wait for the current to forget what happened, or for the herd to return and turn forgetting into structure.

That is how I would live on the Upper Shelf.

Not as a person, not as a wilderness creature merely passing through, not as a society with names or monuments. I would live as construction itself. I would be one small, blind, mineral city inside a larger city. I would be the wall breathing. I would be the place where death is used again. I would be the Upper Shelf, making itself in the dark.
