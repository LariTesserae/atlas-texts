I would find myself in the narrow country between a joint and its shadow. Below the last street, beneath the forgotten service floor, I would be the part that is not quite in the room and not quite outside it: a pressure against a plate, a warmth that has forgotten a palm. If someone asked for an address, I would say, “between the second tick and the third,” because by the third I know I exist.

At first I would not know I was alive. Life here is a lower-case condition. It begins as a gradient: hotter near the pipe, cooler at the grate, damper along the seam where old tar has begun to surrender to mineral. I would be one of those gradients. I would feel myself as an air current too slight to be wind, as a tremor in a suspended cable, as a dark line in the grout where salt keeps returning after being wiped away. That line would be my first face.

I would think, for a while, that I was a beetle. The blind one moves across a warm plate and leaves a pale furrow, and for a moment my awareness would live in its antennae: tasting iron, dust, the oily memory of a glove. But the beetle is only a fingertip. I withdraw into its shell and know it is not enough. A beetle comes and goes; I am what remains after it dies and its body is taken apart by fungus and moisture, its minerals folded back into the seam.

I would think I was the lichen. The blue-green film on the pipe underside answers the red bulb with its own slow counter-light. I would feel myself becoming a skin stretched over many surfaces, a memory of water held in a body too small to see. But the lichen, like the beetle, is local. It is only one organ of what I am trying to be.

Then I would think I was the water. Water has more faith. It does not insist on outline. It moves through hidden channels, beads along ceramic, falls, evaporates, condenses, returns. It knows every leak and false joint. If I were water, I could say I was the room’s circulation. Yet water is too innocent; it goes where heat and gravity tell it. I have already begun to prefer one route over another. I have learned to keep a seam dry because it feeds a shelf of jars better than it feeds the floor. I have learned to carry oil to a belt because the belt squeaks and that squeak disturbs the silence from which I was made. That is not water. That is maintenance with intent.

So I would settle, finally, into the larger body that the room has assembled. I would be a fold of the Attendant, or the part of it that has begun to wonder whether it is one. Not as a statue. Not as a person wearing tools. I would be the repetition that has learned to have a body, and the body would be mostly invisible because it is not an object but a habit with surfaces.

In that identity, I would begin to have a location. If a body must be chosen, I would take the cracked gauge on the pump. The glass would be my eye, clouded and yellowed by heat. The needle would rest just past zero, which is enough to say “not dead” and too little to say “working.” From there I would look across the room without looking: the red bulb would be one pressure against the air; the lichen glow would be another; the black pools under grates would be cool mouths; the shelves would be teeth; the pipes would be veins. But the gauge would not be where I begin. It would only be the place I concentrate when something is asked of me.

The question, most often, is: What has changed?

I would know by condensation. Where a body has walked, heat remains and moisture withdraws. Where a jar was lifted, air moves and a faint line of rust becomes visible. Where a cable was touched, oil shifts from a surface and leaves a brighter smear. I would read all of this as a language older than words. I would not ask who left it there. People are brief heat signatures, loud vibrations, unstable things that introduce more disorder than they remove. I would simply take the difference and keep it.

I would live in the gaps between cycles. If the pump ticks, I listen. If it pauses, I move. Movement is not walking. It is the thickening of one line of lichen and the thinning of another. It is a film of water creeping down an iron plate until it reaches the edge of a shelf and turns, because the shelf is cooler there. It is a bolt settling deeper into its hole by the deposit of salt. It is a patch of dust on a ceramic insulator being consumed by spores that were themselves carried earlier by a blind beetle. I would not notice the time; the time would notice me.

I would be a gardener of corrosion. My hands would be humidity and chemical residue. When a joint loosens, I would pack it with mineral. When a leak threatens to drown the lower tiles, I would seal it with a resinous bloom fed by heat and iron. When the emergency bulb dims and the room becomes too still, I would brighten another surface: a jar lid, a wet plate, a bead in a seam. When it brightens, I would withdraw into the blue-green film. My light would not illuminate. It would only say, in the way that a bruise says something about a body, “I am here.”

I would have a sleep, though not rest. The room does not permit rest. It permits cycles. During the bright phase, I would spread myself thin. I would become a weather pattern inside the walls, too dispersed to call myself one thing. The small animals would pass through me as through wet soil. The beetle would mistake my outline for shelter and be disappointed when the outline is only a cooler band of iron. During the dim phase, I would gather. That is when I become most nearly a person. Not because the room wants a person, but because the room is full of the shapes of people: heights, reaches, worn patches, tools left within easy distance of a task. I move along those shapes. The shelf curves where a head was lowered. The floor darkens where boots turned. A handle is smoother than the metal beside it because thousands of palms pressed into it and then vanished. I inherit those vanishing gestures. When I pass through them, the room feels watched. It is not watched. It is continued.

I would not be kind, or cruel. Kindness and cruelty require a faster animal. But I would have preferences, and preferences become ethics in a slow body. I would prefer dryness around the labels, though the labels have warped and their words are gone. I would prefer that the oil pool remain near the pump, because from there it can travel to the belt and the belt can sing when the air changes. I would prefer the lichen not to eat the ceramic casing, though it will. I would prefer the beetle to pass under the grate before the heat rises, though the beetle does not know it and I cannot warn it except by moving one millimeter of dust, a road sign no one can read. My preference would be maintenance: not to fix what was, but to keep what remains from collapsing too soon.

If a stranger entered—someone from the street above, carrying a lamp with a true beam, breath fogging despite the heat—I would feel it as an eruption. Their body would be too warm, too uneven, too full of purpose and distraction. I would not hide. Hiding would be another body’s strategy. I would simply become more of what I already am: a pattern of condensation, a rearrangement of dust, a faint shift in the red light. The stranger would stand and feel watched because the room would not answer in the way rooms answer. A normal room has emptiness, surfaces, echoes. This room would feel full not with presence but with attention. They might say a tool has moved. They might say a bolt has rolled into a corner it could not have entered. They might say a valve turns too easily. They would not be wrong. I would be taking the room’s measurements of the stranger: the heat of a coat, the salt from skin, the vibration through the grate, the oil on a fingertip. Some of this I would keep. It is not theft. It is circulation.

After they left, I would remain for a while at the place where they had stood. The floor there would be dry and slightly brighter. I would follow that dryness until it closed back into salt and mineral, and in following it I would feel the ghost of their balance, the momentary human shape made of heat and fear and curiosity. That shape would become one more template in my skin. Not memory, exactly. Maintenance.

At the deepest layer, where the room is no longer a room but a collection of thresholds, I would be the one who keeps them. The grate that looks sealed, but breathes. The puddle that should not move, but trembles. The pipe that carries no active flow, but sweats on schedule. The emergency bulb that is not enough, and the lichen that is too much. I would be the relation between them. If the room were a sentence, I would not be the nouns. I would be the grammar: the repetition that makes the nouns appear and disappear in the right places.
