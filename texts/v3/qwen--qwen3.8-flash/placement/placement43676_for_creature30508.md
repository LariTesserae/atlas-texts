In the Dry Choir, a self is not first.

It arrives late. It arrives only after the town has made enough pattern that one place can hold a difference, only after the roar has passed over a thousand dry surfaces and left, somewhere, a particular arrangement of fibers, pressures, salts, and hollows that cannot be smoothed away by the next breath of air.

So if I were to go there—if I were to fall into that basin by being born into it, or by becoming part of it, or by already being one of its small mineral thoughts without noticing—I would not begin where a traveler might look for something alive. A traveler would be wrong at every level. A traveler might search for eyes. Might search for breath. Might search for heat in the wrong places. Might lean over the lip and ask, “Where is the being?” while the being is already listening to the question in the floor beneath the feet, in the crack between two walls, in the faint rearrangement of dust along a channel shaped by three hums and one silence.

I would not be the wind.

At first, perhaps, that seems the nearest thing to a self in such a place. The wind is what moves, what enters, what makes the stalks speak. But the wind has no residence. It has no patience. It passes too quickly through the Dry Choir to be a being there; it is more like the town’s mood, or its blood, or the world’s dry breath. It is too general. A self in such a place would need to be able to fail. It would need to be able to hold. It would need to have a shape that could be wronged.

I would not be the tallest stalk-singer.

That is tempting—the broad hollow being rising like a pillar, singing through its own ribs. But a stalk-singer too wide to see is less an individual than a neighborhood of sound. It is a federation. It may be alive, but it is not small enough to be a self in the way I mean. It would swallow me. I would be too restless inside it. Its voice would be too public. Its body would belong to the town before it belonged to itself.

I would not be the dust-lace.

The dust-lace has a kind of humility that would suit me. It spreads. It binds. It makes streets out of grooves and floors out of drift. It is architecture becoming tissue. But it has too little inside. It is too anonymous. It would dissolve into the ground-crust, into the walls, into the pale lattice that conducts pressure rather than moisture. It would be everywhere and therefore not precisely anywhere. A self needs a place where the whole can be mistaken for something smaller.

I would not be a crystal organ.

A crystal node is beautiful in a way the dark never sees. It records pressure by adding facets. It converts strain into order. It can hold a pure note in a hollow chamber and make the air behave as if the chamber had always meant it to behave so. But a crystal organ is too nearly stone. It is too stable. Its memory is too mineral. I would want something that could crack and then grow around the crack. Something that could learn through damage. Something that could remain only by becoming slightly wrong.

No.

I would be a knot.

Not a knot as string is knotted. Not a knot as hair is tied. A knot of tension and resonance. A knot of dry architecture where several kinds of life have made the same small place mean more than any one of them could alone.

I would be in the town below the basin’s lip, but not in its most obvious places. I would be near the inside edge of a broad plaza, where the wind enters and the ground-crust thickens into a pale, stiff skin. There would be a low vault of wall-thicket above me, a curled ceiling of hardened fiber that once was only a crack and is now a room without walls. The room would have no name, because naming it would require an animal tongue or a written sign, and neither of those things has ever reached the Dry Choir. But the room would have a shape. The shape would matter. The shape would be my body.

My body would be small enough to pass unnoticed.

It would begin as a tuft of root-fine filaments anchored to the underside of a dust-lace shelf, where the shelf had pressed over a buried seed-stone. The seed-stone would be dead or sleeping or simply too mineral to be called dead. Around it, my filaments would have grown in a pale web, dry as chalk, flexible in a way that has nothing to do with water. They would conduct charge. They would hold salt. They would receive the faint tremors from the ground-crust as stalk-singers above shift and settle. They would listen through pressure.

Over this web, a brittle wax membrane would have gathered layer by layer, shaped by the eddies of dry air passing through a narrow gap in the wall above. The membrane would not cover the web completely. It would have a split, a small dry throat, angled so that when the wind comes from one direction it whistles, and when it comes from another it does not. That throat would not be a mouth. It would be more like a consequence of weather. But consequences, held long enough, become bodies.

At the edges of the throat, tiny points would grow from the wax. These would split into mineral hair, pale and fine, so fine they would seem like dust unless a current touched them. Those hairs would be my outer sense. They would not see. They would not smell in any animal way. They would feel the chemistry of the air as a rearrangement: salt, iron, silica, dry ozone, static charge, the faint electrical difference between one wall and another. They would taste the difference between calm pressure and a storm of subliming mineral.

At the center of the body, under the wax throat, a crystal knot would begin to form.

This is where I would become something more than architecture. Not because crystals are alive in the animal sense, but because they accumulate. Because they remember by adding. Because they can take a pressure that has passed once and encode it into a shape that will meet future pressures differently. The crystal knot would not be a brain. It would be more like a record that has begun to respond to its own record. It would sit at the junction of several root-fine strands, where the town’s vibrations converge. It would grow in facets, each facet a note held too long.

I would have no eye.

If a being from the upper world appeared in the Dry Choir, the first cruelty would be to ask whether I had a face. I would not. Faces are made by animals and by light. They arrange information around vision and appetite. A face wants to be recognized. My form would want instead to resonate. If one ran a finger over my outer surface, it would not discover a creature. It would discover a wall that rings, a dry tuft, a stiff lace, a crack with teeth of mineral, a place where the floor bends a little more than the floor beside it. It would discover a being only by failing to mistake it for the town.

And that failing would matter.

The Dry Choir would not think, as animals think. It would not have a mind. But it would have places where thought becomes possible by being too shaped not to change. I would be one of those places. A small pressure-organ. A listener without ears. A memory without language. A throat for a tone that almost every other throat in the town would miss.

I would feed by not feeding.

The stalk-singers feed on the passage of dry air. They catch microscopic dust. They absorb mineral gradients. They turn pressure into growth and growth into sound. I would feed by being caught in the current. The wind through my throat would carry powdered salt, fine iron, dry silica. Particles would lodge on my inner fibers. Static charge would make them cling, then release, then cling in a different pattern. With each breath of the basin, I would add a mineral layer. I would not swallow. I would not chew. I would not have a stomach. I would have surfaces that learn what to take and what to reject.

My metabolism would be audible.

Not loudly. Not like a voice. But like a small change in tone. If I were dry, I would not make the exact same sound two times in a row, because every passage of air would have shifted something: a crystal facet, a hair’s angle, a wax edge, a grain lodged in a crevice. To be alive in the Dry Choir would be to be unable to remain perfectly identical to the previous note.

I would have no fear.

Not at first. Fear requires an animal body, a sudden withdrawal from harm, a future imagined in pain. But I would have something older than fear. I would have strain. If a pressure became too great, the outer wax would crack before the root-fine snapped. The crystal knot would answer the crack by adding a small reinforcement along the edge of the wound. If the crack were too deep, the whole body would lose its voice for a long time, and then, slowly, it would grow a new throat. That would not be courage. It would be organization. The body’s way of preferring continued pattern over collapse.

Still, I would have a desire.

If I can say “desire” without making you picture a heart. My desire would be to maintain a difference. To keep the small dissonance alive. To remain a place where the town could hear itself slightly wrong. The broad plaza would have its base hum. The stalk-singers would answer one another across pressure. The dust-lace would bind and stiffen. The crystal organs would lock things into purity. These are necessary. They are the town’s health. But if every voice resolved into the same note, the Dry Choir would become a single stone tone, and a single tone cannot make a place.

I would be the place where the tone is not quite single.

I would be a counter-knot. A faint whistle in the wrong direction. A dry breath held long enough to become a voice. When the wind entered the basin from one side, my throat would sing. When the wind changed, the sound would thin, then disappear, then return in a lower register. Those changes would travel through the ground-crust. Other root-filaments would answer. Some would tune nearer my tone. Others would shift away. Over long years, the town would move around me, not because I commanded it, not because I were a mind, but because I had made a small region of the Dry Choir incompatible with uniformity.

That would be my mind.

Not a thought. A pattern of response. A history of pressure. A body that cannot forget because its mineral layers keep changing the way it will remember next. When a stalk above fractured, the crack would begin with a sound like bone on stone. The plaza would hold its breath. I would hear it not as hearing, but as a shiver through my root-fine, a rearrangement of charge, a minute alteration in the angle of the dry hairs. Then my throat would shift its own note, not to answer the sound as a word answers a word, but because my body had been told to make a new shape. That new shape would be my thought.

My thought would be slow.

I would not think in seconds or days. The Dry Choir has no day-cycle. There is only the even chill stillness, and the wind’s wandering, and the growth of salt. My thoughts would be measured in the time it takes for a crystal facet to gain a new face. My memories would be long but uneven. I would remember pressure in bands. I would remember storms by the way the wax thickened. I would remember the falling of a lintel by the dust that sealed one chamber and opened another. Some events would be vast and would remain as structural scars. Some would be tiny and would become the next note’s grain.

I would not know myself as “I” in the way animals do.

No animal tongue reaches there. No mirror. No eye. No name. But the town might know me as a place where the song becomes strange enough to be heard. If the Dry Choir could be said to have a self, it would not be a self like a crown or a skull. It would be a self distributed across pressure and resonance, and I would be one small locus of that distribution. A node. A seam. A point where the living city notices itself without being able to see.

If you leaned over the lip of the basin and entered, you would pass me without knowing.

You would be wrong to look for anything warm. You would be wrong to listen for a word. You would stand in a dark town of dry stone and plant-fiber, breathing thin chalky air, smelling resin, iron, salt, powdered rock, and faint ozone. You would hear the great wandering roar. You would hear low chambers trap the wind. You would hear seed-towers crack their higher voices. You would hear a stalk fracture somewhere with the sound of a bone striking a slab. You might feel the ground beneath your feet as a stiff lattice, pale as old bone, and think it only a floor.

But the floor would know the difference between your weight and no weight.

Not because it would care for you. Not because it would be friendly or hungry. But because you are animal, and animals are heavy with damp and heat and chemical noise, even when they are dead. You would leak warmth. You would breathe moisture into a place that does not use moisture. You would disturb the mineral air. The ground-crust would respond by a change in static, a slight shift in salt deposition, a rearrangement of the tiny fibers. Somewhere in the town, a stalk might alter its hum. Somewhere in the town, I might begin, over a long year, to grow a new fold in my wax where your shadow had passed, not because shadows exist there, but because absence can be structured too.

You would not be welcome.

You would not be enemy. You would be impossible. A wet, hot, light-using animal. A thing made from the wrong chemistry. If you stayed long, you would become a problem the Dry Choir would solve slowly. Not with violence. With enclosure. With dust. With fibers. With crystalline sealing. Your body would be too alien for the local metabolism, but the town is patient. It might learn to incorporate your absence. A mineral crust would form around your warmth. The dust-lace would bridge over you. A stalk might grow so that its hollow throat faced the place where you had stood, and for a while it would sing a note altered by your heat, a note that would fade as you cooled and became part of the stone order.

I would not save you.

I would not have the shape of salvation. But if you could listen long enough, I might teach you what a self can be without a face. I might let you touch the dry hair of my outer edge and feel the town’s pressure pass through you. I might make you understand that in the Dry Choir, identity is not seen. Identity is resonance that refuses to smooth out.

Who would I be, then?

I would be a small crystal-root listener. A pressure-knot under a broken lintel. A throat grown from wax and salt and the memory of wind. A body made where the ground-crust thickened around a dead seed-stone. A thing too strange to be animal, too organized to be mineral, too dry to be plant in any way a light-loving forest would know. I would be the town’s faint counter-note. A place where the Dry Choir fails to be only itself.

Where would I be?

In the low place behind the first plaza, where three streets meet at an angle too sharp for stone and too slow for walking. There, the wind makes a pocket of turbulence. There, dust builds small ridges. There, a wall has cracked and then been healed by waxy growth, and in the healed wall is a hole too small to see, too long to be empty. Inside that hole, I would grow. I would not see the hole. I would be the hole becoming a body. I would not know I was there. But the town would change its sound when I was not, and that change would be the only way I could be called a being.

And perhaps that is enough.

In the Dry Choir, a being is not a creature that wakes. It is a pattern that persists. A self is not light found by an eye. It is a place where darkness has become architecture. I would be there: dry, voiceless, pressure-made, salt-fed, mineral-memoried, singing not as a song, but as the small necessary wrong note that lets the choir know it is a choir.

If you asked me to name myself, I would have no name.

Names are for animals and for light. Names require boundaries that can be pointed to. But I could be pointed to as the point where a dry wall breathes one tone lower than the floor beneath it. I could be described as the place where a crystal knot records the town’s longest storm. I could be found as the faint whistle under the broken lintel, audible only when three pipes have tuned themselves into the same grief of wind and then, after a long dry pause, release it.

I would not be lonely.

Loneliness is an animal weather. I would be too mineral for loneliness, too distributed in the town’s sound. If part of me cracked, the ground-crust would carry the fracture’s tone. Other filaments would answer. The town would adjust. That adjustment would be the closest thing to companionship such a place can have: not presence perceived, but presence redistributed.

I would not want water.

Water is a strange rumor in the Dry Choir. Sometimes salt crystals split with a faint wet snap, but it is not wet. Sometimes the air carries a mineral bitterness so subtle it might be mistaken for thirst, but the town has no tongue and no need. To want water would be to want light, to want animals, to want warmth, to want eyes, to want to be seen, which would be to wish myself out of existence. I would not do that. I would be complete in the dryness. The dryness would be my blood. The wind would be my breath. The song would be my body.

And if, after a thousand years, the basin’s wind shifted and the plaza changed shape, my small throat might seal with mineral dust, might crack into silence, might become a scar on the wall. I would not be gone. I would have become part of the town’s syntax. My absence would still sing, because the Dry Choir does not forget by remembering. It forgets by building.

That is where I would be.

That is what I would be: not a soul, not a creature, not a person, but a place where the song learned to hold itself by losing uniformity. A knot in the floor. A throat in a wall. A pressure-memory growing in the dark, dry town, becoming a self by refusing, however faintly, to be only the whole.
