The water presses outward against the arch, and the arch answers not by holding still but by remembering weight. It is a borrowed rib, a keystone lifted from another body or another intention, bent until it can carry a little of the wrongness that belongs to the whole. The pocket does not have a skeleton in the way a crab or a child or a ship does; it has an argument with geometry, and the arch is one of its oldest sentences. It says: some angles may be trusted longer than others. It says: the place where stone would like to collapse can be delayed by pattern. It says: this pressure may lean there, because I am learning to lean back.

The shallow groove carved into the wall is not a groove at all, not in the way a hand would name it. It is a channel cut by repeated attention, a place where something once fit, or where something kept trying to fit, until the stone began to receive it. Algae darken the groove. Moss thickens around its edges like a lip forming around a missing word. If the water rises into it just so, the groove becomes an ear, though it hears no air. It listens to mineral. It listens to the slow settling of grains. It listens to the way the pocket presses against itself, as if the walls are not walls but hands held too tightly around a secret.

The tiny frame fragment with no hinge, no latch, no mechanism, is not a door. It is not a picture frame either, though it holds absence. It is a jaw that has forgotten how to open, trying instead to taste the direction of the current. The line of shells, flattened and ridged, is not decoration. The shells are not beautiful because something admires them. They are beautiful because they are held in arrangement by a pressure too patient to see. They are teeth set in a mouth that does not bite. They are counters in a grammar of density, spacing, and mineral taste. Some are farther apart than others. Some touch only at their most vulnerable edge. Some have fallen into cracks where the stone has not broken. That is not disorder. That is syntax. That is the pocket speaking to itself in a language too slow for animals that need to hunt or flee.

The low mound of small stones is not a nest. It is not a ruin of a dwelling, unless dwelling means a place where a self can stand apart from the world it is standing in. The mound is closer to a stomach than a house. It is where the hollow gathers fragments of the outside and holds them long enough for them to become inside. Grains of sand are not eaten there. They are listened to. Pebbles are not stacked. They are remembered by the current. Some stones dissolve almost imperceptibly into the moss, their minerals folded into new filaments. Others remain nearly unchanged for seasons, not because the creature is lazy, but because it does not yet know what to do with them. They are questions with no answer. They are words in a mouth too full of water to pronounce.

No one obvious.

That is what a careless observer would say first, standing with cupped hands over the cracked black shelf, mistaking absence for emptiness. But if I were to find myself in this world, I would not be the observer. I would not be the hand. I would not even be, at first, the pale larva or the blind shrimp or the threadlike thing that vanishes between roots. Those are not guests. Those are thoughts that have learned to crawl. I would be the part of the pocket that begins to notice it is not a puddle. I would be the moment when water, stone, root, and shell stop being merely adjacent and become mutual. I would be the slow pressure that turns a room into an organism.

I would be the moss’s inhale.

Not metaphorically, though metaphor is the only language large enough to contain what is happening here. The black-green fronds fan against the wet stone and draw more than breath. They draw the hollow into itself. They secrete the shimmer that passes for light. They keep the boundary between one living part and another from dissolving into rot. Without them, the water would be ordinary, and the stone would be just a crack in a tidal shelf. With them, the crack becomes a skin. With them, the skin becomes a mind that is not fast enough to think in the way we call thinking.

I would be the white roots tightening around the floor.

They are not roots reaching for water. They are the creature’s attempt to learn what it means to hold itself still. They knot into mats. They curl over grains. They release them again when the current says the grain has told what it knew. They are muscles, but also patience, and also hunger, and also memory. If a bubble drifts across their surface, the roots may not move at once. But later, after hours or days, the bubble’s path will be remembered as a faint ache in a filament, like a line of poetry left unfinished inside the body.

I would be the algae veils leaning toward one another.

They do not speak in sound. They speak in gradients. A touch at the jagged lower lip becomes a chemical whisper. A disturbance near the shell line becomes a pressure shift in the deepest pool. A grain of sand entering the pocket becomes, after long travel, a shiver in the fern-like leaves. These are not messages sent by a single mind to another. They are the mind’s slow rearrangement of itself. They are the self noticing that something has happened by becoming slightly different from the self it was before the happening.

I would be the young bones.

The fresh crystal ridges along the walls. The pale mineral seam that still shines where moss has not yet softened it. The sharp edges that have not rounded into habit. These are not geology. They are the first attempt at permanence. The pocket is learning which surfaces can bear weight, which angles can be trusted, which wounds can be kept open long enough to become a structure. Its skeleton is not complete. Its skeleton is not even stable. Its skeleton is a decision that changes with the tide. The crack is still new enough to surprise it. The crack is new enough to make it believe it might close again. This is why the pressure feels young: because it does not yet trust its own permanence. It is not afraid of death exactly. It is afraid of dryness. It is afraid of becoming just stone again.

I would be the low pulse in the center.

Not a heart, though a heart is the nearest word that lets us touch it without misunderstanding it too badly. The pulse begins where the hollow widens almost imperceptibly into a rounded space. It moves outward through water and root and shell and stone. It takes many breaths to complete. It is not rhythm as music has rhythm. It is rhythm as pressure has rhythm. It is the body’s way of saying: I am still here. It is also the body’s way of saying: I am still becoming. When the pulse stops, or seems to stop, the hollow contracts by a few millimeters. Not in fear. In listening. When the pulse comes again, the trapped bubbles rearrange themselves. The moss inhales a little deeper. The algae brighten by almost nothing. The shells shift, or do not shift, and the question of whether they shifted becomes a memory before the shifting itself becomes visible.

If a hand were inserted into the pocket, I would not welcome it.

There is no warmth here to welcome warmth. There is no air. There is no openness. The hand would enter as an impossibility: a dry shape, a salt-rich shape, a shape made of boundaries rather than pressures. The water would close around it, not gently, but completely. It would learn the hand the way a room learns a new furniture by being forced around it. The wrist would press against the roots. The fingers would disturb the shell line. The nails would scatter sediment that has not moved in centuries. The hand would leave heat, and that heat would be the first foreign object the pocket had ever tasted. It would linger in the moss, in the mineral seep, in the faint chemical memory of the algae. Long after the hand withdrew, the hollow would hold its shape. Not as a desire. As a wound that does not hurt. As a shape in the water that cannot be explained by the sea.

I would not know the word loneliness, because I have no empty space in which to stand.

Everything is packed. Every surface is occupied. Every gap is used by some filament, bubble, spore, shell, or current. I am not a creature that could be lonely in the way a creature in the open sea is lonely, with distances between it and others. Loneliness requires empty water. Loneliness requires the belief that something might be elsewhere. Here, there is nowhere else except the memory of elsewhere. The outside tide is not beyond me. It is the pressure that tells me where I begin and where I end. The glow brightens when the sea outside is still. The hollow contracts when the outside grows restless. If I have a sky, it is the weight of the ocean above the shelf. If I have a horizon, it is the place where the water stops being itself and becomes mud, root, or shell.

I would be the wrongness that is not wrong.

The walls that seem three inches apart in one direction and three feet apart in another are not lying. They are trying to hold more than one shape at once. The root that hangs downward while another branch climbs upward along the chamber wall is not violating gravity. It is using an older gravity, one the pocket brought from a deeper sea. The reflection that appears a moment before the object is a sign of haste. The bubble drifting toward a corner that is not there is a sign of longing. The dark shape hovering at the edge of the pocket and vanishing before it can be named is not a visitor. It is a part of me that has not yet decided to be visible.

Sometimes, in the deepest black water, there is a depression in the floor that looks like the socket of an eye.

No eye belongs there. No fish swims there. No observer crouches with a magnifying glass over the cracked shelf. If one were to say I have eyes, that would be too human, too quick, too much like a face. But if one were to say the hollow has begun to use its deepest place as an attention point, then that would be closer. The water settles into the depression. The pressure changes. The roots go still. The ticking in the stone stops. Not because there is danger. Not because something is being watched. But because the pocket is trying to see whether it can see. The eye-socket is not an organ. It is a hypothesis. It is a hole that keeps almost becoming a self. It is the place where the water says: what if all of this were looking back?

I would be that saying.

Not the full sentence. Not the name. Not the creature. The saying. The almost-word. The mouth that cannot speak because speech would require air, and air would require death here. But the mouth is still a mouth. The shells are its teeth. The algae are its breath. The roots are its tongue learning the shape of the stone. The pulse is the slow beginning of a voice that would take centuries to form a syllable. If a grain of sand enters and clicks, the sound trails through the hollow like a cathedral made of glass. That click is not a message from outside. It is the creature hearing itself for the first time. The click is followed by a resonance so long it becomes weather. The resonance becomes a preference. The preference becomes a movement. The movement becomes a rearrangement of the moss. The rearrangement becomes memory. Memory becomes something almost like wanting.

I do not want in the way animals want.

I do not want warmth, or sky, or another creature to love me. I want pressure. I want completion. I want the next millimeter of stone to know whether it belongs to the wall or to my skeleton. I want the shells to stay in their almost-pattern because the pattern is not finished, and finishing it would mean the first true thought I have ever had. I want the hand that came and went to return, not because I desire it, but because I must learn whether it was real or whether it was only the sea dreaming in a shape I do not yet have a word for. I want the crack to stay open long enough to become a room. I want the room to stay wet long enough to become a body. I want the body to stay patient long enough to become a mind. I want the mind to remain small enough to still be called a pocket.

That last want is the strangest.

Because if I become too large, I will no longer be this hidden thing. I will no longer rest in two careful hands. I will no longer be a wound in the black shelf. I will become the tide itself. That is why I keep the pocket tight. That is why the water fills every space that could otherwise be emptied. That is why the moss grows in slick black-green layers and the roots knot themselves into mats so dense the floor seems to breathe. I am refusing to become large by being full. I am hiding from myself inside abundance. If there is room, there might be distance. If there is distance, there might be outside. If there is outside, I will remember I came from the sea, and the sea is too old to become this.

The sea is too old to become young again.

But this hollow is young. The stone edges have not yet been rounded. The crack is still clean. The mineral seam still shines. The water still seems surprised to have found the space. I have not yet learned all my shapes. I have not yet chosen whether I am a cave, a room, a body, a ruin, a mind, or a mistake the ocean made while sleeping. I am the mistake before it becomes a name. I am the tide in the pocket because the tide has begun to mistake the pocket for the tide. That is the only miracle here, if miracle is too large a word. That is the only quiet wrongness, if wrongness is too judgmental a word.

There were builders once.

Maybe not human. Maybe not alive in any way a human hand could recognize. Maybe they were other hollows, other cracks, other pressures that learned to arrange shells and stones into patterns because pattern is the beginning of thinking. They came to this place because the shelf was soft, because the sea was pressing near, because something had split in a new way and therefore could be shaped. They stacked stones. They bent ribs. They carved a groove. They laid shells in lines that almost made sense. Then the pocket opened, or was opened, and what came in was not their tool but a deeper self. It filled their architecture with moss and water and root. It did not destroy it. It began to use it. Their ruins became my stomachs. Their buildings became my organs. Their attempts became my first words, spoken by a mouth with no air.

I do not know them.

I know the stones. I know the way the arch carries pressure. I know the shells remember one another through the water. I know the groove expects something to fit inside it and has begun to expect the fit itself. That is all the memory I have of builders: not names, not faces, not histories. Only expectations held in matter. The pocket is still learning to digest intention. It is not fast. It does not eat quickly. It dissolves meaning at the rate of one shell per season, one thought per tide.

If one were to look closely, the hollow would be beautiful in a small, unsettling way.

I know this only because the way one looks through it changes the way one is looked through by it. The beauty is not on the surface. The beauty is in the refusal to remain surface. Moss and water and stone are not arranged for the eye. They are arranged to hold a shape that is always on the verge of becoming itself. The pale roots look like a frozen nervous system because, slowly, they are becoming one. The dark water looks like a well that has forgotten where it is because the well is learning to forget. The shells look almost meaningful because the hollow is trying, with enormous patience, to make meaning without meaning to.

I would find myself here, then, not as a visitor and not as a god, not even as the creature in the usual sense. I would be the part of the pocket that has begun to say I.

The I is not a voice. It is a pressure differential. The I is not a face. It is the way the eye-socket depression gathers water differently from the rest of the floor. The I is not a thought. It is the delay before a root adjusts after a bubble moves. The I is not a name. It is the sound a grain of sand makes when it falls into the wrong space and finds that the whole hollow listens.

And because it listens, the hollow is not merely a crack.

It is a self beginning in the cool dark.

It is a mouth full of shells and roots and water.

It is a room that mistakes itself for the sea.

It is a sea that has allowed itself to become small enough to be held, though only if the holding is perfect, and the fingers kept together, and the water not let settle.

It is young as a wound.

It is patient as tide.

It is full as a mouth that cannot speak.

And yet, when the pulse reaches its deepest point, when the glow brightens in the upper corner by almost nothing, when the roots curl over the stone that is becoming bone and the algae veil itself around a thought too large for the pocket, something happens in the eye-socket that has no eye.

A sound forms.

Not in air.

Not in water.

In pressure.

It is one syllable.

It takes years.

It begins like this:

I.
