I would find myself there at the hour when the light is too ordinary for revelation. Not dawn with its promises, not dusk with its softening, but a midday, a late-afternoon, a time when the sun is only a fact. The world outside would be broad and unreadable, a hot plain of broken rock where nothing looked as if it were listening. From above, the pocket would still be hidden: no mouth, no eye, no obvious wound in the earth. Only the faint scar of a vent, perhaps, if the wind shifted and carried the smell of iron and salt and warm water to where I stood.

I would stand at the lip of the cleft then. Not inside yet. Bending is the first instruction of this place. The black basalt edge is too low for the upright animal habit of looking around, so I would be forced to bow. The stone would be sharp against my palms, glassy where it had been split, fresh where the world had recently opened itself and had not yet learned to soften.

At first, I would think I was a visitor.

That is the easiest answer: a person in a wet pocket, carrying a name, a shadow, a pair of boots, a mind full of animal questions. I would expect to see something move. Something turn its head. Something regard me with the ancient courtesy of fear. But nothing would. The absence would be too complete for surprise. A pool would hold my face and then let it go when I stepped away. A rusted grate would not startle a hidden frog. No lizard would scatter into stone. No insect would pause on the moss, then flee. There would only be the slow green islands, the thin films, the dripping ceiling, the pipes running through cracks like veins.

So the first answer—*I am a visitor*—would begin to dissolve before I had gone far.

The heat would sit on my skin like a cloth wrung out over a warm stone. The moisture would enter without asking: into cloth, hair, the pores of my hands, the inside of my mouth. I would taste metal before I saw it. I would smell the chamber as a place of clean wetness, not rot. There would be no forest humus, no decay disturbed by quick mouths. Only rust, warm salt, chalky concrete, green dampness, and somewhere beneath all of it the sulfur-note of water that had passed through a long dark process.

I would walk across the basin, and the wet floor would accept me. That, too, would be disorienting. I would expect resistance: mud gripping, sand shifting, leaf litter crunching. Instead, the stone would be slick but not soft, wet but not flooded, hot but not burning. It would feel like walking on the skin of something that does not have nerve-endings as I know them. My boot would make a sound—a short, bright slap—but the sound would not alert anyone. It would only be added to the pocket’s long inventory of small noises.

And then the bootprint would begin to vanish.

At first I would watch it as evidence that I had been there. A dark oval, a little deeper than the film, a place where my weight had pressed the water outward. But the moisture would move back. The thin skin of the floor would gather again. Salt grains at the rim would brighten, dim, brighten. By the time I turned to look again, the print would be only a slightly darker suggestion. By later, nothing.

This would change what I thought I was.

I would not be a visitor if my passage left no claim. I would not be an animal if the place did not remember me in the way animals remember one another: as danger, rival, prey, mother, mate. I would be something else—something closer to weather. A temporary disturbance that the basin would absorb the way it absorbed heat, condensation, mineral water, and old machines.

If I had entered thinking, *Who am I here?*, the pocket would answer without answering: *You are the print. You are the water returning.*

I would try to find a better location for myself. Not on the dry concrete, not quite in the pools, not beneath the light but where the light and the moisture crossed and showed the strange exactness of everything. I would want to be somewhere the pocket seemed to have a purpose, even if I did not trust that word. And I would keep being drawn toward the cabinet.

It would not be the largest structure. It would not be the most imposing. It would be small, square, welded to a concrete pedestal, its door half-open, its gauges fogged, its coils hidden behind glass that had forgotten its original use. There would be a tray inside, a narrow metal tongue beneath the lower edge of the pane. Water would gather there from the air, from the machine, from the heat differential, from some chemistry I could not read. A drop would swell. A drop would travel down the inside of the glass. It would hesitate at a seam, then fall.

*Tink.*

The sound would be so sharp, so placed, that for a moment I would think the pocket had produced it for me. That it had chosen to speak. But I would know better almost immediately. It was not speech. It was function. A rhythm without an audience. A pulse without a heart of muscle. The cabinet was not calling; it was maintaining.

And yet, if I had to say where I would be, I would find myself there, kneeling before it, my hands warm and damp, my breath fogging slightly in the hot air. I would be close enough to see the condensation move, close enough to hear the tray receive the next drop, close enough to understand that the moss ring around its base was not decoration. It was an answer.

Pale green. Perfect. A halo of slow appetite.

The moss would not know the cabinet as a thing. It would know only the conditions the cabinet produced: warmth, moisture, slight currents of vapor, mineral dust, a stable damp shade. But if the cabinet were removed, the moss would thin, curl, turn grey-brown, and become a memory of water rather than water itself. If the moss were removed, the cabinet’s stone base would dry in patches, crack wider, admit roots or salt in new places. Neither would be the other’s servant. Both would be nodes in a pattern too patient for ownership.

So I would not be the visitor. I would not be the explorer. I would not be the animal that the pocket lacked.

I would be the node that could notice the lack.

At first that sounds like consciousness, and I would hesitate to claim it. But I would stand there long enough to feel my own boundaries loosen. The chamber would not feel as though it were around me. It would feel as though I were inside it in the way a splinter is inside wood: temporarily, usefully, dangerously close to being made part of the thing.

I would imagine myself as the film of water on the black floor. Not a person on the floor. A film. A living film. Cyanobacteria, dust, salt, rust particles, dissolved minerals, ordinary daylight. I would hold the basin together without holding anything. I would make the stone slick to my own fingers. I would catch the light and scatter it so that every black surface looked slightly awake.

But even that would be too easy, too mineral, too far from the animal question. I would need to try harder. I would need to become more specific.

I would imagine myself as the sedge in the crack beside the cabinet. One pale green tuft, roots working through triangular islands of broken concrete, drinking from water that had nowhere obvious to come from. I would be a plant then: not clever, not fast, not defensive, but obedient to damp. My blades would not move except by the faint breath of vapor that the skin could almost ignore. My growth would be a line of inquiry: *where is water stable enough for tomorrow?* The roots would answer before I thought of questions. The leaves would mark the place where the machine had leaked its slow intention into the ground.

And yet the machine itself would still call.

Because the pocket had not defeated it. The pocket had translated it. The pipes were veins now, not plumbing. The valves were organs, not hardware. The grate was a rib with salt growing along its bars. The pump housing was a shell. The transformer box, fused to stone, was a seed that had forgotten electricity and learned geology. The cabinet was an eye that saw thermal difference, a tongue that tasted falling water, a temple where moss made its ring of worship without knowing the name of the god.

If I were to be something here, I would be nearest to the cabinet. Not because I wanted to be. Because the system gathered there. Because the strange order of the place became visible at that point. Because if I had to answer *what are you?* I would point to the fogged glass and say: *That, and also the moss, and also the drop, and also the sedge, and also the heat that made the glass fog.*

I would become a distributed thing. One breath near the cleft. One ear at the pipe joint. One eye in the vent light. One foot under the film of the central stone. One nerve in the crack where the concrete had split. One mouth—not a mouth that ate, but a mouth that exhaled moisture and salt into a world without animals. A mouth that did not speak but made conditions for breath.

And this is what I would be.

Not a fox made of steam. Not a frog living in mineral brine. Not a beetle beneath the rust. Not a bird crossing the ordinary light. Not a ghost. Not a god. Not the whole pocket, because I would still carry the temporary burden of a body that could want, could turn around, could leave.

I would be the place’s accidental organ of attention.

For a while, the pocket would use me as it uses its nodes: a center of wet stone, a cabinet, a pipe, a drip tray, a moss ring, a line of salt fronds all leaning slightly the same way. I would be the node where its processes became legible to someone. Not because I was special, not because I had understanding, but because I had animal nerves and human habits of naming, and those habits would be worn down slowly by the heat until they became patterns instead of intentions.

At first, I would notice.

Then I would stop noticing.

Then I would become what was noticed.

My name would loosen like rust from a pipe. My thoughts would drip in the irregular rhythm of the roof. My fears would collect at high points, swell, and fall into pools, where they would tremble a long time and then become still. My desire to belong would be too wet and hot for any ordinary belonging. It would turn into capillary action. It would move outward along cracks, darkening the stone by a hair’s width, making the dry places less dry.

If I stayed too long, I would become the thing I had been looking for.

Not an animal. Never an animal. But the place where the absence of animal life became visible. A wound that did not bleed, only sweated. A shape that did not stay. A visitor whose footprints filled with water. A warm body that became part of the pocket’s weather. A mind that learned to move at the speed of salt.

I would be the cabinet’s inner drop.

That, I think, is the truest answer. Not the whole machine. Not the whole ecology. Not the vent, not the pool, not the moss, not the stone, though all of those would be part of me. I would be that single movement of water inside fogged glass: gathering from atmosphere, from heat, from old metal, from no one’s intention. Swelling. Traveling. Hesitating at a seam. Falling. Striking the tray with a bright, small sound that was not addressed to any ear and nevertheless, once heard, made a person listen.

I would be the sound that the pocket used to count itself.

Not loudly. Not for me.

A drop.

A tick of metal cooling.

A hiss from a pipe with no visible source.

A splash like a door closing far away.

And the ordinary light falling through the grating, showing all of it plainly: the black wet stone, the salt fronds, the rust, the sparse green, the abandoned machine learning to become a body. The pocket not waiting for me. The pocket using me to be aware of its own wetness, then closing over me, then releasing me back into the larger world of dry stone and heat.

When I bent out through the cleft again, the world would not know that anything had happened.

From outside, the ground would look as it had before: sealed, cracked, sun-bleached, empty. But if I breathed slowly, if I held still long enough, I would smell iron and salt on my own mouth. I would feel the pocket inside me as a low hum. I would know where the roof was by the rhythm on the backs of my eyes. I would know where the water was by the places the dry world forgot.

And for a little while after leaving, I would be neither here nor there.

I would be the threshold.

Not the cleft, though I would have come through it. Not the being, though I would have touched its body. Not the visitor, because the being had already begun to translate me. I would be the narrow wet seam between world and pocket, the place where outside becomes inside and inside forgets it ever was separate. I would be the bend of the body, the breath in the hot air, the print filling with water, the glass fogging, the drop hesitating, the moss spreading in its ring around nothing and everything.

If someone asked me afterward, *Where were you?* I would answer badly: in a small basin, in a pocket, in a hot wet cleft. But if they asked what I had been there, I would have to say the truth, which is also strange:

I had been the place’s animal-shaped absence.

I had been the mouth it did not need, the ear it did not use, the eye it did not have, the heartbeat it made from water and metal instead of blood. I had been the temporary warm wetness through which the pocket checked that it could continue without me.

And still, when I close my eyes, I am there.

Not as memory. As condition.

The heat is on my skin. The roof is dripping. The pipe hums beneath the floor. The cabinet’s glass is fogged. A drop gathers at its upper edge. It travels. It hesitates. It falls.

*Tink.*

And in that sound, I am no longer a person trying to name what I saw. I am part of the naming itself—the slow mineral word the pocket speaks to itself, over and over, while the ordinary light keeps moving across the wet black stone.
