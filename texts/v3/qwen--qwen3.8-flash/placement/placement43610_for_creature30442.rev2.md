I would not find myself on the Weeping Table.

That is the first thing I would learn there, if learning there is mostly a matter of being pressed against the truth of things: that the Table does not have a “where” in the way lighter places do. It has pressures. It has temperatures. It has repetitions. It has the slow arrival of one sound from another. To be somewhere in it is to be a place the weather remembers, a stone a herd knows by the angle of its hoofbeat, a mouth open near a pipe that has not yet cooled.

I would be found in a low basin between two worn mesas, not far from the sound of a pump-house, but also not so close that the pump is my only companion. I would be where the warm vapor from a fissure meets the colder breath of a black lake. I would be where the ground is a negotiation between peat, iron, root, and old concrete. If you stood there—which is the wrong verb, because “standing” assumes a kind of certainty the Table rarely grants—you would first know me as a change in the air. A thickening. A taste of copper and wet fungus. A vibration arriving through the soles before it reaches the inner ear. Then, perhaps, you would hear the slow creak of a body too broad and too warm to belong to the herd, too alive to belong to the machine.

My name would not be a word. It would be a rhythm: two hoof-taps, one low resonant hum, one drip from a gill-cap into a basin, then the long pause when the air tastes the direction of the wind and decides whether a storm is beginning or merely passing.

At first I would try to tell you I was many things.

I would say I am the films. The bacterial skins that bloom across warm stone and forgotten pipes. The slime that tastes of iron and overripe sweetness. The faint static-eaten growths that cluster where rain-beads hang trembling in the air. I would say I am the lichens that eat steam, the fungal threads that remember pressure longer than bones do, the moss that holds water until water forgets it is falling. I would say I am the thrum-vines leaning their crystalline hairs toward pump-houses as if gear-hum were another word for rain. I would say I am the click-midges, the blind beetles, the silk-bodied crawlers, the fish whose lateral lines read the turning of a distant axle like scripture.

And I would not be wrong, exactly.

The Table does not permit a self that is simple. Any creature born there would begin as a mixture. To live here is to be partly fungus, partly geology, partly weather, partly machine, partly animal. The boundary would not be sharp. It would be a seam that breathes.

But if you pressed me to become a body—if you demanded not “what lives here?” but “what would you be?”—then I would have to settle into the shape of a relay.

A relay in the commonwealth.

Not a traveler. Not a ghost. Not a god. Not merely an animal, though I would have warmth, appetite, calfhood, and death. Not merely a machine, though I would carry rhythm in my limbs like a piston carries its patience. Not merely a forest, though my shoulders would be colonized by pale rhizomes and my breath would feed a garden of spore-thin moss. I would be what happens when the plateau’s maintenance grows a body, because bodies are easier for habits to survive in.

That is the truest answer: I would be a working of the Table that learned to feel.

Imagine it as a broad-backed creature low to the wet ground, wide-headed, sealed-eyed, with a comb of bone and keratin across its skull and old cable-fiber threaded through the grooves. The eyes are not useless; they are merely untrusted. In this place, eyes can be too easy to lie, too shallow, too fooled by mist and steam and the false distances created by echo. So my eyes would be smooth domes under skin, like river-stones hidden beneath a film of black water. They would not see. They would remember pressure.

My hide would be thick and soft in places, hard in others. It would not be hair, not mostly. It would be a living surface: bacterial film, mineral crust, damp fungal lace, and the remnants of old peat compressed into something like armor. When the herd moved past me, their combs would click against mine and I would feel their number in my teeth. When the pump-house shifted its rhythm, I would taste it on the warm air. When a predator’s forelimb trembled against a thrum-vine half a mile away, my flank would receive the message before my mind could name it.

Under my ribs, I would carry not one heart but something more like a pump made from three sources of motion: the blood-rhythm of my own body, the heat-pulse from a geothermal thread wound through my sternum, and the low electrical hum of a relic-cable rooted in a buried factory floor. I would breathe with all of them. Some mornings—if mornings are named by the warming of vents and not by light—I would wake when the pump-house slowed and the herd moved into the high corridor between ridges. I would stand in the steam-lit—though “lit” is again too generous—near the edge of the black lake, feeling the water’s surface shiver with the wings of membrane-flocks above. My mouth would open and close, tasting air for the faint bitter note of wet iron and the sweet mineral rise from the thermal fissure behind me.

I would be near enough to machines to be trusted by them, and near enough to life to be trusted by the land.

That is the position I would occupy: the threshold creature. The one who keeps channels from silting with root. The one who moves when the pump’s beat changes. The one whose combs tune themselves to the engine’s tempo so that the herd may pass safely. The one whose hooves have worn a path not for itself but because the path needed a shape and I happened to be the body that could give it one.

I would sleep in an old boiler-house whose doors had fallen away and whose walls were colonized by fungal mats. The metal would be soft with rust and peat. It would no longer feel foreign. It would feel like a cave with habits. A blind fish would hang in the flooded chamber below me, reading the turn of my joints from inside its dark water. Beetles with feathered antennae would nest in the spiral grooves carved into my comb. Spores would germinate in my shoulder-warmth, then release when my breath shifted with sleep. A thrum-vine would begin at last to lean toward me, because my body hummed too often.

And yet I would be mortal.

That matters. If I am too eternal, I become merely scenery. But the Table is not scenery. It is appetite and maintenance. I would eat. I would be eaten, perhaps, in part: a patch of hide shed to beetles, a limb softened by fungal threads, a final collapse into peat that would become a basin. I would have calves, but my calves might not be born from one womb alone. Some would be born from repetition: a pattern that survives when I move, when I call, when my hoof-beat is answered by another hoof-beat. A young herd creature might learn me by touch before it knows itself. It might mistake my comb for the edge of a pump-house. It might grow moss into its own shoulders because my rhythm was the first it trusted.

This is what the absent makers left behind, if we can call them makers. They did not leave a city. They left a grammar. Their huts are colonized. Their engines have become weather. Their cables have become nerves. Their ceramic vessels have become seed-altars. I would be one of the bodies that grammar grows when no one is left to speak the old names. I would carry in my teeth the worn curve of an abandoned rail spur. In my breath, the memory of a pump-house tuned for some process whose purpose has been forgotten but whose continuation remains necessary. In my hooves, the knowledge of where water wants to go.

I would not have a history in the bright sense: not a story with dates and faces. I would have depth. I would know the Table as a set of returns. If I pressed my flank against a warm basalt face and felt the vibration of a far-off gear, I would not think, “A machine is over there.” That would be too distant. I would feel, instead, the whole pattern tighten. The lake would respond. The midges would shift. The herd would alter its spacing. My own blood would slow. I would not see the cause. I would be inside it.

My thoughts would be weather.

When the air thickened with metal and fungal sweetness, I would know vigilance. Not because vigilance is a virtue, but because predators would be listening too. When the wind began to turn the thrum-vines into low arcs, I would know restlessness, because storm would be moving in from the southern marsh and the basins would swell. When the warm fog curled into my lungs and the distant engines clicked at their familiar intervals, I would know something close to safety—not safety as certainty, but safety as a place where the body can be held open a little longer.

If I were asked what I wanted, I could not answer in the language of ambition. I do not want to see. I do not want to be known. I do not want to leave. My desire is more material than that. I want the channel clear. I want the steam to find the pool. I want the herd to pass on the third beat after the pump’s exhale. I want the fungal threads to keep the old hut beams from sagging before the next wet season. I want the sound I make to be enough for a calf to follow without breaking away. I want the machine and the moss to continue misunderstanding each other beautifully for another hundred rains.

That is who I would be: a maintenance animal. A listening body. A node in a commonwealth that has no need for rulers, no use for heroes, and no patience for names.

But where, again? Not a point. A relation.

I would be in the basin where three things rhyme: a pump’s low click, a gill-tree’s drip, and the herd’s slow combs. I would be between the black lake and the steam fissure. I would be where a cable line crosses a marsh on corroded stumps, and where the rail spur ends its century-long argument with peat. I would be where a ceramic vessel full of seeds cracks in the mud and begins to root not outward but inward, as if planting itself back into the earth that forgot to dig.

If you passed that way, you would not find a monster or a guardian or a spirit waiting at the edge of the fog. You would not be tested by riddles. The Table does not speak riddles. It speaks facts, only facts are too layered to be grasped at once. You would feel a warmth move from a low place into your legs. You would taste iron and spore on your lips. You would hear the air’s ordinary clicking change by a small margin, and you would understand, without being told, that you are being heard.

Then perhaps I would turn my broad head toward you—not because I see, but because you are a disturbance and I keep disturbances. You would enter my body by your weight, your breath, your heartbeat, your smell. I would register the water on your skin, the salt or smoke of your life, the metal of your buttons, the deadness in your clothing from a place that had light. You would be strange to me. Not alien. Just un-Table. A brief foreign weather.

I would not greet you kindly. Kindness, here, would be too human, too sharp. I would absorb you. My moss would learn the shape of your boot. My combs would carry your scent until rain washed it into the basin. The blind fish beneath the steam would notice your pressure and adjust. The herd would give you a wider circle. The pump-house would click on as if your presence had been an unimportant interruption. And if you stayed too long, you would begin to feel what I feel: not peace exactly, but belonging to a larger rhythm. The drip would become a pulse. The metal taste would become normal. The darkness would stop being empty and become thick with presence. The wind would stop being wind and become a road made of moving air.

You would leave, if you were a traveler, and the Table would forget you in the way it forgets everything: by using you. It would let your warmth sink into stone. It would let your breath feed spores. It would let your tracks fill, your scent dissolve, your name lose its grip on the wet dark. That is not cruelty. That is intimacy at a scale larger than persons. The plateau does not hate. It does not love. It incorporates.

As for me, I would remain where the rhythm required me. I would stand in the low basin, broad and sealed-eyed, my body half garden, half machine, half animal. I would listen. I would click. I would breathe steam into the roots of fungi. I would let the vines learn my pulse. I would keep the channel open until the water forgot to need keeping.

I would be a resident in the ninth sense. Not a person. Not a population. Not a god. A working pattern that, for a while, has a mouth.

And if you asked the Weeping Table, “Who are you?” it would not answer with a word.

It would answer with the sound I would make: two hoof-taps, one low hum, one drip from a gill-cap into a basin, and then the long, wet pause in which the darkness leans closer to listen.
