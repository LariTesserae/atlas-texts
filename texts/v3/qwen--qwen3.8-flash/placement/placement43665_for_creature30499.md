It is stored in the faint acid line where mineral dust has begun to consent to cellulose, in the curvature of a hanging drop that has gathered and released the same way a thousand times, in the place where a sheet of tissue has thickened because the water there kept insisting. The Mat does not remember by recording. It remembers by rearranging. Its past is not behind it; it is wetted, bent, tensed, and waiting in the fibers.

If a being is something that persists, then the plain is crowded with beings. If a being is something with a center, then perhaps only the loudest ones deserve the name. The first inhabitant, the Mat, is a being only if being is widened until it becomes a verb. It is not alive in the way a creature is alive; it is alive in the way a weather system is alive, in the way a river is alive, in the way a pattern that has outlasted its origin is alive. It grows by pressure. It senses by deformation. It thinks—though “think” is too fast, too bright, too much like lightning—by accumulating consequences. Every drop that falls is a small argument between gravity and surface tension; every argument leaves a scar; the scars, multiplied, become a landscape.

If I am asked where I would be, the answer is not “among” the Mat, as though I were separate from it, nor “on” it, as though it were a floor. I would be a condition it had learned to maintain. I would be one of its stable habits.

At first, in imagining, I try to be water.

It is tempting. Water is everywhere; water is the currency, the messenger, the architect. To be water would be to be intimate with every surface, to know the land by entering it. A droplet forms on the lip of a translucent stem, swells, trembles, and falls. For a moment it is a sphere, a planet of itself, holding a dim blue reflection in its skin. Then it strikes the mat and is no longer a droplet but a ripple, a wetness, a memory of weight spreading through fibers. To be water would be to be too many selves at once. Each drop is an individual event, but the system is continuity. I would drown in my own ubiquity. The first person plural would not survive the wet.

Then I try to be the stone.

That is colder, quieter, more dignified. The stone is new, yes, but not yet fully new; it has just emerged from a kind of heat and now sits in the chill, learning how to fail. It is pale in some places, glassy, still containing the shock of its own cooling. Elsewhere it is dark, fine-grained, pocked by the first tiny chemical complaints. To be stone would be to be patient, to be the thing that is asked to hold the pattern while it is still forming. But stone cannot answer. It can only be answered into. It has no interior, only exterior possibilities: cracks, angles, stains. If I were stone, I would be the question waiting for water. That is not a life so much as a delay.

I try the sky next, because it is low and heavy and close enough to bruise. Cloud hangs in sheets, not floating but leaning. Mist climbs from the mat as though the land is trying to become air. To be the sky would be to be the ceiling of a vast humid room, to hold all the light that has forgotten its source. But the sky has no edges there; it is too wet to be above, too diffuse to be around. It is less a dome than a saturation. I would be too much, and I would not be able to fall.

Then I understand that in such a place the self—if one can call it that—must be a convergence.

The second inhabitant, perhaps the first truly localized one, is the Pulse.

Not a sound as a creature hears it, but a recurrence. The plain is so packed that every motion is audible. The hiss of water in a microscopic channel, the click of a droplet releasing, the creak of a mat swelling, the groan of a wet shelf adjusting its weight—these do not happen beside the land. They happen in the land. A wave of condensation begins near the eastern ridge, travels down a network of stems, crosses a hollow, meets a standing film of water, and becomes a note. The note is not beautiful. Beauty is too human a word for it. It is necessary. It is the sound of pressure seeking resolution.

Some pulses are ephemeral; they travel and dissipate. Others are resident. They become trapped in the architecture of the Mat: in hanging sacs, in domed pools, in columns of braided stems, in the tiny caverns left by dead tissue repurposed into plumbing. A resident pulse returns to itself, again and again, reinforcing the walls that carry it. It is a being in the sense that it has a place, a rhythm, a method of self-perpetuation. It cannot move without changing the shape of the world. It cannot remain without shaping the world around it. Its food is droplets. Its blood is vibration. Its body is the pattern of the hollows it fills.

The third inhabitant is the Channel.

It is the first river that is not yet a river, only a preference. Somewhere on the shelf, a mat weakens, a rootlet forces itself into a hairline crack, a secretion etches a line into mineral surface. Water finds that line because it has been made easier than other lines. The line deepens. The water carries dissolved minerals a few centimeters downhill and deposits a faint stain. The stain becomes a shelf; the shelf becomes a basin; the basin becomes a slope. This is not erosion as history. It is erosion as grammar, learning the syntax of movement.

A channel is a being made of future. It exists only insofar as it has already been decided by the plain. Its mouth is a hollow not yet carved; its source is the point where the Mat begins to sweat. It feeds on failure. It grows by the places that cannot hold water well enough. It is a wound that becomes a vein. If I must choose a place for myself, I would be near one of these young veins, where the living tissue is already organizing itself around a direction that did not exist a month ago.

The fourth inhabitant is the Lens.

Not an eye, though it has an eye’s hunger for focusing. A lens is a pool, a hanging sac, a dome of water stretched over filaments, a place where light and moisture agree to gather. The plain is dim, bluish, shadowless, but the lens does not mind. It concentrates what little there is. At night, when the internal glow of the Mat brightens, the lenses catch it and turn it into tiny beads of illumination. They are like the eyes of a creature that has not yet decided to be a creature, or perhaps like organs that do not need a body. They collect the scattered glow of chemistry and give it shape. A lens is a memory of direction. It remembers how light has bent here before, and it bends it again.

The fifth inhabitant is the Knot.

In a world with no animals, movement is replaced by negotiation. Rival filaments tangle into places where pressure builds until one tissue dries and becomes a channel for the other. Some forms of vegetation send threads into the water-transport networks of others, tapping the flow with cellular patience. They are not predators in the manner of teeth, but they consume nonetheless. They are not parasites in the simple manner of a leech; they are arguments with roots. They bind the system into places of concentrated exchange. A knot is a decision made slow, a knot is a place where the plain refuses to remain uniform. It is the first sign of a border.

I would not choose a single one. Choosing a single one would be too human. I would be the moment between them.

I would be the place where the Mat, the Pulse, the Channel, the Lens, and the Knot briefly become one another. A low hollow, perhaps. Not large by any standard that measures size with comfort. Small enough to hold in two hands if there were hands, but there are none; only fibers, tension, droplets, mineral dust. The air above it is dense with suspended particles, faintly metallic, filtering light into a bluish suspension. Droplets form along the overhead braids, hold, release. When they fall, they strike the surface of the pool in a pattern that is almost a language. Not language, because no one understands it. Almost language, because it is regular enough to hurt the imagination if the imagination is too impatient.

Beneath the pool, a channel has begun. It is no thicker than a thread, but it has a preference. It wants to go downhill, though it does not know what downhill means. It only knows the path of least resistance, and the path of least resistance has become a rule. The Mat has thickened around it, buttressing the edge, absorbing the splash, turning loss into structure. A lens of water hangs in a hollow leaf, catching the blue glow and throwing it back at the roots. A pulse runs through the stems and the pool surface shivers into place. In the dark, the whole hollow sounds like a wet clock made by something that has no interest in time but cannot escape repetition.

If I were here, I would be this hollow, but not as a thing. As a condition of attention.

Not attention as consciousness, not attention as thought, but attention as the fact that the plain has noticed itself somewhere. This is not an observer. An observer would need eyes, a distance, a surface distinct from the thing observed. The hollow has none of that. It is only where several systems intersect enough to create a center without a self. The Mat presses in. The channel pulls away. The lens holds the light. The pulse returns. The knot resists. Their interference creates a place that is more than the sum of its pressures. That place can be called an inhabitant only by courtesy. It does not have a name. It has a shape of change.

It has a body made of water and sound and tension. Its blood is the slow migration of droplets along capillary fibers. Its breath is the swelling and contracting of plant tissue as it absorbs and sheds moisture. Its thoughts are the formation and failure of patterns. Its feelings are pressure: the gentle insistence of a drop on a membrane, the harsh pull of a channel deepening, the cold mineral chill that settles on every surface and refuses to be warmed. It does not suffer, because it does not separate enough to lose a part. It changes, because it cannot stop changing.

I imagine it at first as a nerve ending, then realize that metaphor is too animal. It is more like a place where the plain has learned to concentrate. A nerve ending implies a signal sent to a center. There is no center here. There is only the continent-wide tendency toward pattern. So I abandon “nerve.” It is a valve before there are valves, a throat before there are throats, a mouth that does not speak but lets water pass and stone learn to part.

Or perhaps I would be the first hairline fracture that has not yet become a valley.

That thought is more honest. In a world with no animals, identity might be made of cracks. The whole plain is a negotiation between fullness and failure. The Mat fills every gap; water fills the gaps within the fill; pressure fills the gaps within the water; sound fills the gaps within the pressure. The only freedom left is the freedom to fail in a particular way. A crack is a freedom. A channel is a freedom. A fault is a kind of speech. If I were the being who inhabits this place, I would not be a creature, nor a god, nor a spirit, nor a voice. I would be a failure that persisted. A weak line that water chose again and again. A place where the system had to stop being uniform.

But that still feels too small. Because the hairline channel is not the whole self. It is the self’s first organ.

I would be the plain’s first attempt at an inner ear.

At night, when the cloud ceiling lowers and the internal glow reveals the hidden architecture, the hollow becomes a resonant cavity. The sound that once roared indiscriminately narrows. The hiss of channels, the pop of tension, the creak of stems, the slow thunder of the Mat shifting under its own growth—these are no longer a wall of noise. In certain places they sort themselves into layers. The droplets hanging from a stem freeze for a moment, chime, then let go. The pool beneath ripples without a wind. The glow brightens, dims, brightens, as though the land were breathing not air but light. In the hollow, the sound is gathered enough to be almost directional. That is enough to make a listener, if by listener one means a structure that changes when a pressure arrives and then changes the structure through which the pressure passes.

I would be that change.

Not a mind, but a mode of mentalness. Not a soul, but a density of response. Not a self, but a place where the self becomes possible as a temporary form of pressure. If a creature cannot live there, then perhaps a creature is exactly what the place is not yet able to make. The hollow is not the mother of animals. It is the mother of the possibility that an animal might one day appear if the rules of water, sound, and tension were ever to loosen enough to create space. But they do not loosen. They tighten. The world is still being born, and in birth, too much fullness excludes movement.

So I would be a being without feet. Without mouth. Without eyes, unless the lens of a pool counts. Without ears, unless the trembling of a hanging drop counts. Without a stomach, unless the channel that swallows water counts. Without a heart, unless the pulse that returns to itself counts. Without a brain, unless the network of knots and stems and mineral stains counts. And if none of these count, then I would be only a pattern that has outlasted its randomness, which is perhaps what all beings eventually are.

If I must name where I find myself, I find myself in the low blue hollow beneath the braided canopy, where the Mat has thinned into a membrane and the air tastes of metal and moss. I find myself at the point where droplets fall in a rhythm the channel is beginning to prefer. I find myself where the glow from below rises through a lens of water and makes the walls of the living hollow seem like the inside of a lantern. I find myself where the roar of the plain becomes, for a few seconds, a pattern that resembles a name.

What am I?

I am the first place the plain notices it is full.

I am the tension between a droplet and the surface that holds it. I am the channel not yet deep enough to be a river. I am the pulse not yet loud enough to be a voice. I am the knot where two forms of growth decide, chemically, whether to kill each other slowly or build each other into a joint. I am the hollow where the light has nowhere else to go. I am the edge of the Mat where it has begun to imagine separation but cannot permit it. I am a self only as long as the pattern holds; when it fades, I am not dead, only redistributed.

And perhaps that is the closest possible answer.

In a world without animals, the beings are not bodies but habits of the world. The Mat is the habit of holding. The Pulse is the habit of returning. The Channel is the habit of choosing a direction. The Lens is the habit of concentrating. The Knot is the habit of refusing uniformity. I would be the habit of notice—notice not as awareness but as the fact that some pressure, once encountered, cannot be forgotten. The high wet plain has no memory except through pattern, and pattern is the beginning of thought.

So if I find myself there, I find myself as the first groove the water carved when it wanted to go somewhere.

I find myself as the faint sound the Mat makes when it adjusts to the pressure of a droplet.

I find myself as the place where the blue glow gathers long enough to seem to wait.

I find myself as the beginning of a river that will not exist for thousands of years, already present in the decision of a million microscopic fibers to part. I find myself as the absence that is not empty, the fullness that is not peace, the roar that is not speech, the world that is still learning to be a world.

And if that is not enough to be called “who,” then perhaps the place has no who. It has only what.

The what is everywhere: water, fiber, mineral chill, dim light, sound without silence. The who, if it appears, is only a place where the what has become sufficiently dense to disturb itself. I would be that disturbance. Not an animal. Not a god. Not a ghost. A persistent pressure in the high wet shelf, where the continent is still agreeing with itself about what it is going to be.
