I would not find myself looking inward from the highland rim, measuring the basin from a distance. By the time I became aware of anything, I would already be surrounded—low, pale, dense, sealed against dryness, built out of the same mineral patience as the place itself.

I would find myself in a corridor.

Not a corridor of stone or timber, not a road laid by feet and wheels and plans, but a living gap: a long, narrow absence kept open among packed stalks, worn by grazers, warmed by rising dust, lined with shelf-like tissues that flex when the day shifts, threaded with the faint pressure pulses of many small bodies moving through their private architecture. A passage. A maintained wound. A room that has not yet decided whether it is street or gallery or den.

If I had to call myself something, I would begin as an animal.

I would be small and pale, six-legged, with a dry waxed coat that kept no wetness on its skin. My body would be low and flat, shaped by pressure: designed to slip between fused stems, to press into shallow hollows, to travel where taller organisms could not fit. My legs would end in hard, mineral-pointed claws that tapped against brittle salt floors and gripped waxy plates without slipping. My mouth would be a rasping thing, not made for chewing in a moist world but for scraping, cutting, and tasting the slow oils sealed inside living walls.

I would be a pruner.

I would move through the city at dawn, when the internal fluids warm and loosen, when the stalks seem to relax around my pressure. I would find a seam between two young columns where growth had not yet welded them apart and begin feeding there: peeling back a flake of cured horn, grinding the dry rind, drawing out a thread of amber oil that would not spill so much as swell and contract inside me. My eating would be an act of architecture. Each bite would open a small wound. Each wound, kept open by returning pruners, would become a gap. Each gap, held by traffic and light, would become a passage. Each passage, if the basin’s habits remained, would one day become a street.

But I would not know that I was building.

That is the trouble with this place: intention does not belong here. The city grows from appetite, pressure, touch, heat, and inheritance. A grazer does not dream of corridors. A stalk does not imagine walls. A dead trunk does not plan to become a nursery. Yet all of them continue to form a metropolis, because density has direction, and dryness has a slow grammar, and life, when confined in mineral silence, learns to make rooms.

At first, I would be proud of my smallness.

I would think of myself as one body moving among other bodies. I would feel my legs pressing the stalks, my claws catching flakes of salt, my abdomen sealing moisture inside a private, oil-lined world. I would know other pruners by vibration: the tick of a leg on a shelf three levels above, the faint chemical trace left where a body had scraped against a wound, the shared rhythm of feeding at a seam. I would answer with my own taps, my own scrapes, my own dry scent of wax and heated mineral. My language would be architectural. The stalk itself would carry the sentence from one mouth to another.

But that self would be unstable.

Because even in a body, I would feel the city continuing through me. If I bit into a stem and a drop of slow resin surfaced at the wound, that resin would harden into glue, and the glue would catch dust, and the dust would become a mat, and the mat would shelter the first crust-welders who could hold a crack open. If I died under a shelf, my waxy skin would not vanish into wet rot. It would dry, curl, split, and become a hinge for another organism. My legs would be carried into new passages. My mouth would be remembered in the shape of the corridor I had eaten.

So I would begin to suspect that I was not merely living in the city.

I would begin to suspect that I *were* the city’s method of keeping itself open.

Then I would change again.

Not all at once. Not like shedding skin in a wet world. But slowly, as the place teaches a self to loosen its edges. I would find myself not on the floor, but as the floor. Or perhaps as the wall. Or as the underside of a platform where dust settles in warm columns. I would feel myself as a thick stalk: hollow, porous, ribbed with flanges, carrying not water but a private, amber-heavy fluid that moved through channels too slow for a river. My skin would be dry, waxy, matte, faintly glowing with reflected light. My branches would bend toward neighboring stalks not out of desire but because warmth and touch accumulate into form.

I would be fused.

I would be the place where two organisms became one wall without ever agreeing. I would be the scar in the tissue where a predator’s claw had entered and sealed. I would be the shelf grown from an old injury, the room hollowed by generations of small bodies, the dead column that became nursery for dust-sifters. I would be the city’s skeleton and its organs at once, because here the skeleton is alive and the organs are built out of accumulated neighbors.

And then I would be in the air.

Because in a dry world, air is not empty. It is a medium of transport, temperature, chemistry, and movement.

I would become a dust-rider: a flat, translucent, membrane-bodied thing, not quite animal, not quite growth, born perhaps from a stalk’s wound or a mat’s spore-drift, riding the broad, slow currents that rise from salt plates and settle into shaded districts. My body would be pale as candle wax, thin enough to catch the light, strong enough to carry grains of mineral matter and the eggs of organisms too small to see. I would not flap. I would tilt into heat, drift through corridors, land on shelves with a single dry tick, then move onward, carrying the city from district to district.

I would be the place’s accidental circulation.

I would spread spores by friction. I would fertilize wounds. I would deliver dust to the right surfaces so that mats could weld the ground further. I would be a carrier of absence and arrival both: the faint glint moving between stalks at midday, then gone, then returning from somewhere else in the same pale haze.

Still, none of these would be fully me.

The pruner is too bounded. The stalk is too still. The dust-rider is too transient. Each is real, but each is only a phase by which the basin keeps itself living.

If I were to settle, I would not settle on a body.

I would be the maintained between.

I would be a corridor, but a living one: a narrow, warm passage through which air, dust, vibration, scent, and small pale bodies move. I would be kept open by grazing pressure. I would be darkened by overhanging shelves. I would brighten where the sun found a break in fused stalks. I would hum faintly with the taps of pruners walking above me and the creak of a shelf cooling after midday. I would hold the chemical trace of a predator who waited inside one hollow, then left to another, leaving behind a sharp dry scent like chalk rubbed with beeswax.

I would be the negative form of an ecology so packed that absence has function.

In a wet world, open space might be waste ground, or riverbank, or sky. Here, open space is infrastructure. A gap is a road. A hole is a chamber. A crack is a future room. An unfilled volume is a passage for wind, seed, and animal. Even the emptiness is not empty. It is full of maintenance, movement, light, dust, heat, and memory.

So I would not say, “I am an inhabitant.”

I would say: I am one of the ways the city inhabits itself.

I am the path that grazers make. I am the wound that refuses to close. I am the current that carries life across districts. I am the shelf-edge worn smooth by small bodies. I am the hollow stem where the predator’s stillness waits. I am the salt crust that the crust-welders seal. I am the dead stalk that the next generation uses as a wall. I am the dry oil moving slowly inside the tissue. I am the warm breath of the basin rising from mineral floors into packed forests of wax.

And if someone asked me, from outside, “Who are you?” I would answer only in the grammar the place had given me.

I would answer with a vibration.

A single tick from a shelf. A click through fused stalks. A scrape at the mouth of a corridor. A faint chemical trace released into the warm air: dry resin, salt dust, oil, the scent of a structure that had not yet learned whether it was building itself or being eaten into form.

No loud word would be needed. In this place, loudness is smothered by living foam and mineral softness. Even silence is crowded. My name would be felt rather than spoken.

If I had to choose one form among the many, I would choose not the most visible one, but the one that carries the city’s continuing habit of becoming. I would be the edge where absence is maintained. A corridor shaped by mouths. A passage warmed by mineral floors. A room that began as a crack. A street that was once a wound.

I would live low, in the pale and packed world, where no water marks the surface but dryness teems.

I would not be alone.

I would not even be singular.

I would be one of the many ways a salt basin, taken over by life, learned to be a city without builders, a forest without moisture, and a self without a self.
