At first, I would be a mistake.

I would arrive with the wrong idea: that places have entrances, that floors are merely floors, that sunlight is only light. I would think I had crossed a plain and found a basin. I would think I was outside, then inside. I would think the water was a condition of the ground and not, as I would learn, the ground’s memory beginning to speak.

My shoes would go first.

Not because anything would take them, but because the damp would make them absurd. The soles would darken, soften, forget their edges. Mud would climb without mud ever appearing to move. The air would lean close enough to leave beads on my skin. The light would remain ordinary, almost kindly, and that ordinary brightness would make the strangeness worse, because I could see all of it too clearly: the black silt shining with water, the moss swallowing each step, the braided reeds forming a ceiling that had never been planned and yet had never failed.

I would not be allowed to remain a visitor.

A visitor leaves marks that do not answer back. Here everything answers back. The floor would begin by adjusting to my weight. The moss would warm where my feet rested. The water beneath the woven walkway would shift its level, perhaps by a line, perhaps only by intention, as if the basin had noticed that something too dry and too quick was passing through. A stem near my shoulder would tighten or soften. A hollow wall would click once, like a bone settling. Not threat. Not welcome. Just response.

So I would become, slowly, something that belongs to response.

I might call myself a mammal at first, but that would be too flat a word. I would have damp feet and large ears, yes. I would have a body built low to the ground, light enough to cross floating mats, curious enough to press against every seam that breathed warmth. My fur would always look oiled in the wet light. My tail would know what my hands had not learned yet: balance is not only what prevents falling, but also what tells the body how the world is tilted.

I would wake in a pod whose walls had thickened around the shape of my sleep.

This would not feel like sleeping in a house. It would feel like being remembered by the place. The hollow would be exactly as wide as my curled flank, exactly as high as my head needed, slightly cooler at the mouth, warmer at the belly, because the structure had learned that heat collects where breath leaves. At first light, the interior would exhale through hidden channels, drawing in moisture from the outside and returning it scented with resin, moss, fish-water, and faint green electricity. I would not call this morning comfort. It would simply be the city becoming a morning.

I would be one of the mammals who press stems to open membrane-doors.

That would be my first apparent work. I would not know it as work. I would know it as habit. A joint near my sleeping hollow would swell slightly at dawn. I would put my nose against it. It would smell like rain held in a seed. I would bite the resin node once, twice, three times—because three times is the number that makes the wall pause and reconsider—and the membrane would soften. Not tear. Not hinge. Soften, as fruit softens when the inside of it decides to become accessible.

When I walked through, the building would draw my warmth after me and hold the passage open a little longer than it needed to, because something with blood had been there. That longer opening would let air move through the internal capillaries, cooling a chamber where a clutch of fish-colored eggs lay in a root hollow. The eggs would breathe. The eggs would survive. I would not know this. I would only know that the door was softer today, and that the passage smelled of rain and salt, and that my mother—perhaps not my mother, perhaps the adult who warmed me through the storm—had once bitten the same node and taught me by example.

Language, for me, would begin in the feet.

I would learn to thump against a floating platform when the water beneath it had become too high. I would learn that a long, low tap means pressure is moving; two short taps mean pressure is false; a rhythm along the moss means a predator is near the eastern reeds. These sounds would travel through root-channels and wall- chambers, becoming something the basin could read. I would not shout. I would not point. I would not name the thing I feared. I would make it present through the ground.

A claim would be a nose rubbed against a stem until the plant tightened around the scent. A greeting would be damp noses meeting above a hollow, breath exchanging the news of water levels and sleep-warmth. A warning would be the sudden lift of the whole pod-clan, tails straight, ears forward, bodies becoming one question asked of the moss.

And the moss would answer.

If I stood too long in one patch, it would soften beneath me—not to fail, but to learn me. If I moved quickly, it would firm and spring, remembering pressure as something to be dispersed. If I lay down, it would cool and damp, accepting heat. If I died there, it would eat me without hurry and lift a small new mound over the place where I had been, so that my final body became a floor for insects, a bed for salamanders, a slight elevation in the basin’s long hydrological sentence.

I would not be only a mammal, though. I would become a crossing.

By the second day—or perhaps the second season, for here time has damp edges—I would start to notice that my senses are borrowed. My ears would understand the frogs not as animals, but as clocks. Their choruses would rise and fall according to water levels, heat, and the opening of seed-vessels. A particular call, low and rounded, would mean a valve had shifted in the deep channels. A trill would mean the upper reed-ceiling was too wet and was preparing to shake itself free. When they stopped, I would stop too. The stillness would be a shape in my ribs.

My blood would learn the fish.

They are the city’s bloodstream, but I would not think in metaphors that clean. I would simply feel, in the vibration under my paws, when the canals moved strangely. A school turning all at once would press a change into the water, which would press a change into the roots, which would press a change into the walls, which would make a membrane open that I had never seen open before. I would walk through it without knowing the chain of events that had invited me. I would think the door had appeared. The door would think I had arrived.

My skin would learn the insects.

The fine static of them near the water would enter the building, and the building would answer the way a leaf answers light: by becoming ready. Pollen would fall into channels where it was needed. Repair-spores would ride inside seed-pods. Thread-moth dust would find cracks in older walls and help them scar. I would not know these errands by name. I would only know that when the air glittered with wings, certain joints became sweeter, softer, easier to open.

My upper body would learn the birds.

They would move above me in the braided roofs, coaxing branches into ledges, pecking at nodes until stems bent into shelter. Their shadows would pass across the pod-houses like cold punctuation. Their calls would arrive through the whole vertical layer of the basin, from water to canopy to ordinary sky, and so they would sound more true than my thumps. When one called sharply, even the frogs would hesitate. I would learn that the sky is not separate from the settlement, only the part of it that can leave and choose to return.

Then, as my senses widened, I would lose the sense of a border between myself and the city.

At first I would say, “I am here, in this place.”

Later I would say, more accurately, “I am happening in this place.”

Because to be alive here is not to stand apart from the ground. It is to participate in its metabolism. The mammals are its hands. The frogs are its timekeeping. The fish are its blood-flow. The insects are its couriers. The birds are its upper architecture and alarm. The walls are its organs. The water is its memory. The moss is its skin. I would be the part that touches, presses, bites, listens, thumps, sleeps, and warms. But I would not be a citizen in the old way, not a person with rights in a town. I would be a response the basin has learned to perform.

There would still be something human-shaped in me.

Not because I was once a human, though perhaps I had been, long enough for the idea to feel like a dream. There would be a residual expectation in the walls: doors sized for upright bodies, heat-pads shaped around lap-heat, membranes that remembered the particular dampness of human breath. The mammals would tolerate humans if they came, not as rulers but as heavy, loud animals whose habits are already encoded in the architecture. A pod would open because a human has been there before and warmed it. A child born in the basin might learn to taste resin before learning speech. They would not be lost. They would be translated.

I would carry that translation without knowing its origin.

I would press my paw to a wall and feel a warmth that was not only the sun’s. I would curl against a seam because a seam is where something once decided to become a home. I would look up at the light through the reed-ceiling and feel, beneath fear and hunger, an odd ancient recognition: this is shelter. This is enough.

But I would not settle there.

Because the deepest place in the basin is not shelter. It is listening.

It would come near the hottest part of the day. The air would thicken. The ordinary sunlight would remain, indifferent and clear, but the water’s movement would slow. The frogs would stop mid-call. The insects would lower their shimmer. A bird would freeze on a branch with one claw folded, feathers dark with the memory of oil. I would stand in a moss clearing, my ears broad and still, my body suddenly unable to ask anything of the ground.

For a few seconds, the whole basin would be one suspended response.

I would feel the stillness not as emptiness, but as attention gathering. The roots would hold their water-signal. The channels would wait. The walls would not repair, not yet. The membranes would not soften. The fish would hang in the dark canals like suspended thoughts. The moss would hold my footfalls without yielding.

Then the soft popping would begin.

Deep in the ground. A tiny release, like a seed-case opening. Not loud, but central.

A membrane-door would open across the pools.

The world would rush back in a flood of sounds I had forgotten were sounds: dripping, creaking, fluttering, chirping, knocking, the hydraulic sigh of a wall adjusting its pressure, the wet tearing of an opening seam, the low wooden groan of reeds taking their shape again. The frogs would begin in a new rhythm, as if their stillness had been a rehearsal. The insects would lift in sheets. The birds would call from the high braided branches. The fish would turn in unison in the dark channels, sending a message through the city without intending a message at all.

I would move.

Not because I chose, but because the choice had passed through me.

I would cross a bridge that had tightened in the dry air and loosened under my weight. I would lie across a heat-pad and feel it swell, not because it obeyed me, but because something warm had come near and the city’s metabolism had noticed. I would bite a node and open a door that was not mine. I would carry scent into a passage where a young one waited, its ears translucent with the city’s damp light. I would wake a wall from its afternoon repair by breathing against it.

I would be the place’s brief animal self.

So where would I find myself?

Not in a pod. Not on a bridge. Not beneath the reed ceiling. I would be in the softening of a seam. In the instant before the membrane opens. In the moss where a footfall has begun to become a sentence. In the water channel that carries a signal I cannot understand but still obey. In the frog-song that tells the city what time it is. In the heat of my own body editing the architecture.

Who would I be?

I would be one of the damp-footed mammals, yes: quick, low-burdened, large-eared, learning by habit that the place opens when you press in the right way. But I would also be a node in the basin’s distributed mind, a warm pressure point, a thump in the floor, a breath against a wall, a body whose need to sleep causes the wall to learn the shape of sleep. I would be the city doing one of its small animal chores without knowing it.

And beneath that, I would be something stranger: the place’s capacity to notice itself.

Not sentient like a person, not thinking like a writer, not remembering like an ancestor. But aware the way a vine is aware of light, the way water is aware of slope, the way a wall is aware of pressure. I would be the basin’s wet reflex. The seam that opens. The moss that holds. The frog that marks the hour. The mammal whose body makes the door. The response that changes the path.

If I stayed long enough, I would no longer be able to say where the creature ended and the city began.

My name would be a rhythm.

My home would be a humidity.

My ancestors would be footsteps.

My gods would be water levels.

My fear would be the silence before the popping.

My belonging would be the fact that everything, even I, is allowed to become part of the ground.

And when I finally left—if leaving were possible—the sound of water would follow me for a while.

Not because I was dripping.

Because I had become the kind of thing that leaks into the world it has touched.
