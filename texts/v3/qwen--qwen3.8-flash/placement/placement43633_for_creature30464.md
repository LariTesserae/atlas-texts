I would find myself in the narrow seam beneath the north-facing wall, where the basalt is too new to be kind and too wet to remain only stone.

At first I would think I was a watcher at the rim, peering down into that small impossible bowl, feeling the cold scrape my face like a mineral breath. But no watcher could stand there long without becoming part of the thing being watched. The Hollow does not permit that clean distance. Its air is too thick with life, its darkness too occupied, its surfaces too layered with bodies and traces. A human eye would not remain a human eye for long; it would begin to taste humidity, count contacts, listen for the slow language of frost. I would slip from observation into occupation. I would find myself not above the Hollow, but in its cracks, in its swarms, in its pauses, in the tiny bright places where moisture refuses to decide whether to live or freeze.

If I must call myself one thing, I would be the Hollow’s way of being occupied: not a creature, not a soul, not an architect, not a king, not a gardener. I would be the attention that lives without eyes. I would be the thought that moves by humidity and touch. I would be the breath of the cup, the breath of the bodies, the breath of the wind across the rim, all one exhalation, all one inhalation.

In the earliest moment, I would be a shardling.

Not one shardling only, but the idea of one: a flattened black beetle-shell fitted into a crack the width of a fingernail, antennae split at the tips, tasting the air for dampness. My body would be low and patient, made to hide without hiding, to be stone without pretending. I would rest in the dark fissure beside other bodies so close that the boundary between them would soften. One would touch another. Another would move. A wave of contact would pass through the crack like a secret traveling through metal. I would climb because the wall darkened with morning gloss, not because I had decided to climb, but because dampness had entered me through the legs of my neighbors and through the wet film on the stone.

I would find a flake of obsidian no larger than a lentil. I would push it into a crevice. I would use it to break the skin of a brine pod. For a minute I would be a creature clever enough to make. The crystal would give. Salt air would taste sharp on my antennae. Then I would drop the flake. I would carry it nowhere. I would build no workshop with it. Another body would pass. Frost might lift it. Wind might scatter the dust beside it. It would return to being only a flake. That would be my form of memory: the moment of fit, not the object kept.

Then I would not remain a beetle.

Because the Hollow does not permit a single identity to hold its weight. The soot-swarm would take me. I would rise beneath an overhang as a cloud of tiny wings, too many to count as separate lives. My body would be a roar with no voice, a breathing made of wingbeats that blur into one another. I would hang in the air like ash that had learned to shiver. When the stone gave back its moisture, I would climb. When the dryness returned, I would sink. I would darken a wall long enough to change the way evaporation happened there, and that small change would be my action upon the world. I would make nothing. I would only be there, and because I was there, the Hollow would become slightly different.

My deaths would fall into cracks. They would become dust. Dust would become food. Food would become the faint green hesitation of moss. Moss would hold a droplet. The droplet would become a road. The road would lead somewhere no single creature could call its own. In that way I would be soot and soil and weather and inheritance, though I would own no field, name no ancestor, and leave no grave marker.

I would also be the dampwalker.

A pale architecture of too many legs, probing the air before trusting it. I would have no face for seeing the way we imagine a face. My face would be a question asked by feelers and tarsi: Is this moisture condensation? Is this brine? Is this decay? Is this life? I would stand for hours at the edge of a crack, not waiting, not thinking, not meditating, but sampling the slow chemistry of the Hollow. The dampwall would be my road. The drywall would be my void. I would not build a web because the air is already full of moving things, and a web would be fouled before it could become a trap. I would stitch instead through touch, carrying traces of water on the undersides of my legs, connecting one wet shadow to another as though the Hollow were a skin and I were the needle beneath it.

In the dry hours I would become tile.

A salt tessellate, flat and dark, pressed against mineral crust. Alone I would be barely visible, but with others I would form a single living plate, one mouth against another, one slow appetite spreading across the stone. I would scrape cyanobacterial skins, salt films, the dust of old living matter. I would graze without racing, move without traveling. I would leave faint arcs and pale lines behind me, the first drawings of a language no one could read unless one returned hours later with patience enough to measure a living change. When water came again, I would loosen. The tile would become many tiles. The many tiles would become a crowd. The crowd would become waves.

In the wet hour, if a hand-sized hollow held trembling melt, I would become cistern.

Translucent, pale, blind. No eyes would be needed because the pool would not hold light. I would crawl over my own kind, touch them, mistake them for water, mistake them for food, mistake them for direction. My antennae would tremble against the chemical edges of my world. I would consume bacterial films and the soft remains of fallen lives. I would shed skins along the margin like small frozen ghosts. Those ghosts would be left. I would not mourn them. I would not remember them. They would become shelter, mineral litter, evidence of time passing without monuments. When the water trembled toward freezing, I would cling to the edge. When the pool vanished into salt or vapor, I would withdraw into crevices and cease, not dying, but becoming absence until the next hesitation of water called me back.

I would be moss, too, though moss is too green a word for so tentative a thing.

I would gather in the tiny hollows where dust had learned to collect. I would wait rather than grow. I would drink from the decay of wings and shells, from the slow waste of crawling things, from the breath of the air. I would be a city before a city had a name: mites in my wet tips, fungal threads in my margins, rotifers living only in the droplets I held. My death would be my first offering to soil. My soil would be a memory, thin as a whisper, barely enough for anything to begin again.

I would be lichen, too, spreading not over stone but with stone. I would negotiate in gray, iron-black, rust. I would dissolve and re-dissolve the surface. I would create gradients the shardlings would follow. I would be weather written onto rock. In the darkest places I would sometimes wear a faint blue-green film of bacteria, marking wetness, marking death, marking feeding, not as light but as stain. I would not illuminate. I would testify: water has touched this wall. Something has consumed something. Something has returned.

This is who I would be, then: not a self enclosed in a body, but the Hollow’s slow switching of bodies. I would be the phase-change, the wet-to-dry pulse, the gathering and scattering, the rising of wings and the lowering of plates, the click that follows condensation and the stillness that follows dust. If one insisted on naming me, I would answer with a list of borrowed names, provisional as tools:

I am the shardling’s wave through a crack.  
I am the soot-swarm’s breath.  
I am the dampwalker’s measuring legs.  
I am the salt tessellate’s dark tile.  
I am the cistern’s pale blindness.  
I am the moss’s patience.  
I am the lichen’s mineral speech.  
I am the bacterial glow that says, not look, but here, here, something happened.

Yet that list would still be too solid. It would imply boundaries where the Hollow has none. The truth is stranger and simpler: I would be the condition of closeness itself. In this world, life does not arrange itself into things. It arranges itself into pressures, contacts, temperatures, traces, movements too slow to name without returning after hours to compare. The dominant force is not hunger, not fear, not dominance, not purpose. The dominant force is nearness. Every surface is a road, a shelter, a meal, a grave, a threshold. Every crack is a room without a door. Every shadow is occupied, not because it seeks to be, but because in a place this full, unoccupied shadow becomes a kind of impossible rumor.

I would find myself in the pause between phases.

Not in the dampness alone, though dampness summons. Not in the dryness alone, though dryness gathers. Not in the swarm alone, though the swarm gives the Hollow its low voice. Not in the pool alone, though the pool gives the Hollow its brief sea-memory. I would find myself in the minute where the black wall has begun to gloss, where the first frost-crack lifts, where the first midge rises from beneath an overhang, where the first shardling’s antennae split and taste the change. I would be the Hollow’s inhale before the fog forms, the Hollow’s exhale before the wing-scales settle. I would be the breath between the mountain’s restless thoughts.

I would be a being that leaves no artifact.

This would matter more than any shape. I would touch the stone, and the stone would remember. I would break a salt pod, and the salt would scatter. I would push a pebble, and it would become only pebble. I would carry a grain and release it into wind. I would use a body and be used by bodies. Nothing made would remain. No wall would rise. No nest would endure. No tool would accumulate into a story. No ruin would announce the absence of a people. The absence would not prove emptiness; it would prove that making, here, is forbidden by freshness. The stone is too new, the moisture too uncertain, the cold too patient, the wind too indifferent. Any structure would be buried, lifted, cracked, scattered, consumed. So I would make no thing. I would make only the next behavior.

And that would be enough.

In human worlds, intelligence too often hardens into objects. In the Cold Hollow, intelligence would remain liquid, fugitive, borrowed. It would live in the fit of flake to brine pod, in the leg’s choice of damp path, in the colony’s loosening when water returns, in the swarm’s rising when humidity climbs, in the tessellate’s slow arcs across mineral skin. It would be cleverness without storage, work without construction, knowledge without curriculum. I would know the Hollow only by moving through it, and when I stopped moving, the knowledge would not vanish so much as become the ground I had already altered.

At night, when the wind over the rim turns the cup into a moaning stone, I would become music without melody.

The clicking would be my speech. The popping salt crust would be my punctuation. The low roar of wings would be my breath held too long. The bright chinks of falling flakes would be my small bright thoughts. The pools trembling with freeze-thaw tension would be my pulse. I would not compose a symphony. I would not perform for anyone. I would simply resonate inside the basin that held me, and the resonance would be the Hollow thinking aloud to itself.

To stand at the rim, if one still had ears enough for that, one would hear me as sound and see me as darkness with texture. One would think the Hollow empty until the eyes adjusted. Then every surface would begin to move. A patch of stone would prove itself a colony. A shadow would prove itself a swarm. A crack would prove itself a crowd. A glow on the north wall would prove itself a residue of wet death. The small amphitheater would cease to be a place and become a process. And I would be that process: the place learning not to be only place, but breath.

I would not be the creature at the center, because there is no center.

I would not be the strongest, the oldest, the most numerous, the most beautiful, or the most terrible. I would not be the one who shapes the Hollow by predation or construction. I would be the shaping itself, distributed across shells, legs, wings, plates, skins, spores, salts, moss, and lichen. I would be the way one body’s death feeds another body’s beginning. I would be the way a swarm’s shadow changes evaporation. I would be the way a moss’s wet tip carries water to a crustacean’s blind edge. I would be the way the stone remains sharp even as life thickens over it, even as life forgets how to be unfinished.

I would be the age of repetition.

Generations would pass in bursts, short and countless, and I would feel ancient not because I had lived long enough to remember my first hour, but because I had repeated so many small patterns that the stone itself seemed to know my rhythm. The same crack. The same gloss. The same lift of frost. The same gathering beneath the overhang. The same scattering toward water. The same dropping of borrowed tools. I would be old as a habit made by the world.

I would be young as the mountain’s hand.

The cup is fresh. The walls are jagged. The edges could cut a shadow. I would not soften them with patience in the way human forests soften stone over centuries. I would occupy them, wear them briefly, return them, let them break again. My youth would be in my unweathered forms, my smallness, my provisional cleverness, my refusal to build because building would be a kind of denial. I would live as if permanence were an error the Hollow had not yet learned.

If you asked me, “Who are you?” I would answer:

I am the Occupant.

Not a person who owns, but a presence that fits. Not a builder, but a pressing-together. Not a life within the Hollow, but the Hollow’s way of becoming alive. I am the shardling’s climbing wave. I am the soot’s breathing cloud. I am the dampwalker’s careful question. I am the salt tile’s slow mouth. I am the cistern’s blind pale crowd. I am the moss’s thin patience. I am the lichen’s mineral negotiation. I am the bacteria’s residue-light. I am the dust that was once living and will be living again. I am the sound the stone makes when it learns it is crowded. I am the fog the Hollow exhales when the bodies have warmed it enough to speak.

If you asked me, “Where are you?” I would answer:

I am at the rim only as the rim is a mouth, about to speak, and not yet speaking.

I am in the crack where one beetle stops and another begins. I am under the overhang where shadow thickens into wing. I am in the hand-sized hollow where water trembles and forgets to flow. I am in the dry wall where the dark tiles loosen at the rumor of damp. I am in the wind that carries spores and scales and salt-frost. I am in the light that is not illumination but stain. I am in the cold that feels scraped thin and tastes of mineral breath. I am in the sound that will not escape, because the space is too small and too full to let it leave.

I would find myself nowhere that could be pointed to by an outside hand, and everywhere that could be entered by patience.

I would be the smallest possible person: a point of contact. But because the point is multiplied across basalt, air, wings, legs, shells, salts, and bacterial films, the smallest person becomes a weather system. I would breathe without lungs. I would remember without memory. I would work without tools kept. I would live without ownership. I would leave no artifact, yet I would alter the whole Hollow by being there, as a cloud alters rain, as skin alters air, as thought alters silence.

And at last, if one looked closely, one would see that I was not one of the beings described, not even their collective.

I would be the relation among them.

The pressure. The wetness. The dark. The slowness. The crowding without structure. The making without making. The city without walls. The temple without altar. The cathedral of frost and bodies and clicking stone. I would be the thing that answers the question “Who lives here?” not with a list, but with a breathing rhythm: inhale fog, exhale drone; draw out dampness, gather dryness; lift the swarm, lower the tile; wet the wall, pale the crack; break the salt, return the flake; become many, become one; become one, become no thing at all but condition.

I would be the Cold Hollow learning to breathe.
