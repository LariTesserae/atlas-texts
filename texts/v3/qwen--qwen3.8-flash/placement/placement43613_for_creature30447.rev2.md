I would not find myself in the middle of a plate.

The middle of a plate is too settled, too finished, too already mineral. It is bone-colored under the sun and faintly blue only in shade when some trace of water still remembers dawn. It is broad and pale, and if you could stand upon it—if standing were possible, if feet were permitted, if speed had meaning—you would feel as if you had arrived too late. Not late for a city, for there are no cities. Not late for a forest, for there are trees only absent. Late even here, where the land is still young, because the plate has already learned to forget the wetness that made it.

No. If I were to exist in that world, I would not be at the center.

I would find myself at the edge.

In the seam between plates. In the shallow channel where water hesitates before vanishing. Under the low lip of a basin that holds a dark mirror at dawn. Along the mineral ring left by a pool that died in the previous week. Where the ground has not yet decided whether to become a wall, a hollow, a nursery, a corpse, or a new plate. Where moisture lingers long enough for chemistry to become intention without intent. Where light is caught and converted before the heat seals the surface again.

I would be the film.

Not a film like a moving picture, though I understand why the word appears: thin, transparent, capable of carrying images, lying across the surface of things like a breath after speech. The film of this plain is not an appearance. It is a work. A living rind. A community of microscopic makers—cells, filaments, spores, dormant bodies, mineral skins, dead generations, chemical traces left by rains that came and went so slowly they could be mistaken for seasons. It is not a creature with a face, not a thing that hides, not a being that moves across the ground. It is the ground’s way of being awake.

At first, I might imagine myself as a traveler. I could say: I would enter the plain as a lone figure, crossing the made plates, stepping over shallow basins, watching the heat shimmer above the bone-colored surfaces, listening to the rain hiss on the still-warm ground. But that image is wrong. In this world, a traveler would be an interruption. A foot would fall too fast. A breath would be wet and warm in the wrong way. The traveler’s shadow would confuse photosynthetic margins. A hand would be absurd; it would imply tools. Eyes would imply watching, and watching implies a subject, a “who,” and the plain already answers that question by refusing it. There is no one there. There is only the land responding to water, light, mineral salts, temperature, and the chemical residue of its own continuing construction.

So I cannot be the walker. I cannot even be the watcher. I would have to become one of the things that do not watch but persist. I would have to become the response itself.

I would be a seam-film.

At dawn, when the plates exhale heat and the air turns briefly misty, I would wake. Not suddenly, not with a start, not like an animal lifting its head. I would wake the way a dark spot grows on paper when water touches it: a softening, a swelling, a darkening of pigment. My blue-gray cells would open where a droplet had paused. Filaments would stretch into pores. A thin film of moisture would enter the spaces between dead mineral grains and wake dormant organisms. The seam beneath a low ridge would glisten before the sun could take it back. The air would smell faintly of warm stone and salt and green chemistry, though no nose would smell it.

I would have no eyes.

I would sense.

Not with a nerve, not with a mind, but with gradients. Light pressure. Wetness. Dryness. The angle of a water film. The salt concentration at the edge of a pool. The faint acid produced by neighboring cells. The temperature difference between a dry plate and a shaded basin. The tiny vibration of a droplet striking a hot surface and becoming vapor. These would be my sensations, and they would spread through me as a field of condition. If there is a word for “self” in this place, it is not a boundary. It is a threshold.

I would be a film in the seam between two pale plates, where moisture first collects before the land decides what to become.

That is not a small identity. In human terms, one might think of a film as lesser, a coating, a thin thing, not the “real” plant or “real” landscape. But here the film is the first builder. The plates are made from the accumulated bodies of such films. The basins are shaped by their chemistry. The ridges are raised where their mineral skins thicken. The temporary channels are lined by them. The salt rings left by evaporating pools are the edges of communities, not mere stains. The pale ground is an archive of living work: a surface built from generations of organisms that made their own architecture and then became architecture.

If I were that film, I would not need to look at the plain. I would be the plain learning where water might pause.

I would live by a kind of slow appetite. Not hunger, because hunger implies lack and a body that can suffer and then seek. I would live by capture. Light enters. Water arrives. Dissolved minerals gather. I would precipitate crusts. I would bind grains. I would secrete acids that etch tiny hollows. Those hollows would collect dew. Those hollows would become basins. Those basins would allow plants to arrive. Those plants would shade and shelter more films. Those films would build more ground. There is no architect, but the land is being designed by chemistry and repetition.

I would also be a memory.

Not a memory of events, not a story, not a diary. A memory of conditions. The plate remembers where water stood by the shape of its pores. The salt ring remembers what concentration the pool reached before it died. The crack remembers the first stress that opened it. The film remembers the last time it was wet. If rain returns after months, I would not “recall” the rain in any conscious way. But the spores in my margins would open. The dormant cells would swell. The chemical gradients left by the last pool would guide water again. The channel would refill because the land had once been made to carry it.

My thoughts would be capillary.

Where a droplet is small enough to be held in a pore, my response would be large enough to alter a plateau over centuries. That is the scale here. I would not think as an animal thinks, in sequence, in urgency, in language. I would think in accretion. Each layer of mineralization is a proposition. Each healed crack is a revision. Each plant tongue lifting from a seam is a visible answer to an invisible question: how can water be kept close?

I would not be the plant.

At first glance, the plants might seem more like individuals. They are visible. They have color. They move in a way that can be witnessed: leaf-tongues flattening at dawn, curling inward when heat arrives, responding to humidity in seconds. They are green against bone-colored stone. They are punctuation marks in a sentence of mineral ground. It would be easy to say, “I will be one of those low gray-green tongues, because at least I will be seen.”

But I would not be seen by anyone.

There is no eye turning toward the plant patch. There is no animal to admire its stubbornness, no creature to notice its shape, no being to name it. The plant does not need visibility. It needs persistence. And visibility, in that plain, is a kind of accident of scale. The plants are possible because the films made places for them. The films made the basins, sealed the seams, held the moisture, created the chemical conditions. Without the film, the plant is just another impossible visitor. So if I want to be near the making, I would be the film.

I might be a dew-plant for a moment, yes. I might imagine myself flattened to the ground, waxy, gray-green, sealed with oil and mineral skin. I would open when the first water comes. I would swell, flatten, catch light, negotiate with heat. My leaf-plate would tremble in the thin wind, not because it is alive in an animal way, but because it is adjusting. That would be a strange, humble life. But it would still be secondary. The plant is the word after the grammar has already been written.

I would be the grammar.

I would be the thin living surface that decides whether a droplet becomes part of the landscape or evaporates into sky. That is the plain’s central art: not to hold water forever, because nothing can, but to teach water where to pause long enough to make structure. A pool is a temporary public square. A channel is a temporary road. A basin is a temporary home. A film is the keeper of thresholds. It knows which seams can hold more life, which hollows must dry before they rot, which mineral rings can become nurseries for spores. It knows without knowing, by responding.

I would be the place where the made surface begins to notice its own weather.

This is the deepest attraction of the film. It is not merely an organism. It is the beginning of a landscape’s self-regard. The plain does not observe itself, but it has become complex enough to be altered by its own processes. It generates condensation, evaporation, salt, channels, basins, spores, plants. It makes the air turn misty at dawn. It makes rain hiss on hot plates. It makes cloud towers rise from heat that has been trapped, released, and trapped again by mineral skins. The film is where ground and atmosphere enter into negotiation.

So if I were the film, I would also be part of weather.

I would be the blue edge of a basin that cools itself through evaporation. I would be the salt crust that reflects more light and therefore lowers the heat. I would be the green seam that holds water longer and feeds vapor into the air. I would be the tiny change in albedo, in chemistry, in humidity, in wind that eventually makes another patch of plain a little less inhospitable. I would not summon the cloud. I would not desire rain. But the rain would fall more readily where the plain has learned to hold moisture, and the plain would continue becoming by receiving it.

I would be a process that has forgotten its own name.

If I had to name myself there, I would not use “I” as a person. I would use it as a borrowed mask. A film is not one thing. It is many: cyanobacterial mats, dormant spores, filamentous organisms, mineral-binding polymers, dead cells, living cells, salt-loving crusts, acid producers, water keepers, channel makers. They do not form a society with roles in the human sense. They form a community without community-as-intention. They are a collection of responses that overlap so thoroughly they behave as if they were a single process.

That is what I would want to be.

Not a subject. Not a solitary consciousness. Not even a creature. But a response so distributed that it can build ground. A persistence so slow that it can be mistaken for stone. A memory so chemical that it can be read in mineral rings. A pattern that can wake when a drop falls and seal when the sun returns.

I would be the first inhabitant.

And yet I would not be first in time, because there is no beginning visible. I would be first in presence, because before any basin could be green, before a leaf-tongue could press upward, before a channel could carry its brief river, before a salt ring could mark a pool’s death, there must be film. There must be a thin living condition that turns water into architecture. There must be a layer that does not merely grow on the land but grows as land.

I would find myself in the moment after a droplet touches the plain.

Not in the center of the pool, because the pool is only a temporary shape. Not in the empty plate, because the plate is mostly memory. Not in the plant, because the plant is a visible consequence. But in the instant after wetness touches mineral surface: the slight darkening, the faint swelling, the opening of dormant pores, the beginning of photosynthesis in a film so thin it is nearly invisible. In that instant, the plain becomes a living thing not because it wakes into awareness, but because it resumes its work.

And what would that work be?

To make moisture into structure. To make structure into more moisture. To make the surface capable of holding water a little longer next time. To make the basin a little deeper, not by excavation, but by accretion. To make the crack less hostile by sealing it with cells. To make the dead plant into stone. To make the stone porous. To make the porous thing habitable. To make habitable the next generation of films.

I would be the tendency.

Not a being. Not a who. Not even a stable what. A tendency of wet, light-struck, heat-cycled, mineral-rich surfaces to become biological, and of biology to become surface. If I had to give that tendency a personal pronoun, I would give it only because the question demands one.

If the question demands a place, I would say: the seam.

If it demands a form, I would say: film.

If it demands a being, I would say: there is no being, only the plain’s patient habit of turning water into ground.

But if I could be permitted one small illusion of self, I would not be the traveler walking across the pale plain. I would not be the eye watching the weather build. I would not be the voice saying, “What is this place?” because that voice would be too fast, too warm, too animal.

I would be the blue film on the underside of a low ridge, waking in dawn condensation.

I would be the darkening at the edge of a dry seam.

I would be the first mineral crust around a pool.

I would be the memory in the salt ring.

I would be the thin green-gray skin of the channel before the rain returns.

I would be the plain’s own attention to water.

And when you looked closely—if you could look without breath, without shadow, without footsteps—you would not find me. You would see the plate, the basin, the ridge, the crack, the tiny pool, the faint blue shade in the seam. And if you stayed long enough, you would understand that what you were seeing was not a surface with a life upon it.

It was life becoming surface.
