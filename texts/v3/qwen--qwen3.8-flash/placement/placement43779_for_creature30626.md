I would find myself in the Cold Loom exactly where the idea of a person begins to fail.

Not on the rim, because the rim is only a place where an outside mind might stand long enough to imagine ownership. Not at the center, because there is no center here, only a basin gathering itself into different pressures. I would find myself lower, where the pale terraces begin to look like roads if one is desperate for roads, and where the shallow channels are wet with a cold that remembers mornings before there were mornings.

Specifically, I would be lying across the third terrace on the western side of the basin, in the place where a rounded mound opens its slit at dawn and then seals again as the light grows thin. I would be a pale thread running through a vein-groove, partly under a sun-liver, partly exposed to the air. If the ordinary sun touched my upper surface, I would slacken. If the shadow of the mound moved across me, I would draw tight. I would be a filament of contractile cells, thick as a finger in one place and hair-fine in another, long enough to touch several mats at once, short enough to be mistaken for a scar.

But that is only the first body I would learn.

At first I would think I was an observer, because human minds always begin there. I would imagine standing somewhere above the basin, looking down into the shape of an abandoned town, trying to name it. The ridges would invite naming. They look like walls. The channels invite naming. They look like drains. The parallel marks invite naming. They look like plough lines, or cuts made by something with intention. But the Cold Loom would refuse the naming for long. It would simply keep doing what it does: making a mind feel too small, then too soft, then eventually useful to the growth.

I would begin to lose the certainty of edges.

Not because something would devour me, but because there is nothing here for a visitor. A visitor must leave tracks. A visitor must have a body that stays mostly the same from one hour to the next. But in this basin, bodies are permissions and withdrawals. They are warm slacks and cold tightenings. They are membranes that leak and swell and close. I would be taken in by the logic of the place: no walls, only living shelves; no roads, only braided threads; no ruins, only organisms still becoming themselves.

So I would become one of the thread-weavers.

I would not be a thread-weaver in the way a bird is a bird, or a fox, or even a fungus. A thread-weaver is not a thing standing inside the landscape. It is a process that has acquired a body. I would be long, pale, and filamentous, running along the terraces like an old pale root that has never grown soil and does not care about soil. I would have no mouth. No face. No foot that could make a step. My way of knowing would be tension.

Warmth would make me loose.

Cold would make me remember.

When the sun came, my upper filaments would relax into the mats of sun-livers beneath me, letting light pass through the translucent domes and the clear fluid inside them. I would feel the warming as a softening, a loosening of grip, a permission to let the basin breathe. Vapor would rise from the frost I could not see, threads too thin to name, and for a little while the valley would hold its breath and let it out. I would be part of that breath, not because I understood it, but because I was made of the same wet, mineral patience.

When the sun withdrew, my lower cells would contract.

The cold would enter me as pressure, not as temperature only. It would find the old dampness in my fibers and make me draw tight. I would pull neighboring mats together. I would braid across channels and close shelves. I would tighten around the edges of mounds until they folded inward, leaving only half-bitten slits at their tops. If many of my kind were present, our tightening would make the land look as if it had decided, all at once, to become harder, darker, more sealed.

The sound would change.

Not louder. The Cold Loom is not loud. It is the kind of quiet that makes you suspect silence is not absence but pressure. At first there would be the faint hiss of waking: microscopic seals opening, frost drawn into pores, warmth becoming vapor. Later, the slow wet creak of fiber under strain, the basin contracting around its own body like a sail being pulled around a mast. I would hear it with my whole length.

If a stone shifted over me, or wind cracked me across the middle, I would not die as a person would understand death. I would bleed no red. The cut would weep clear sap, and in that sap would be spores, contractile cells, fragments of the whole pattern that had begun as me. The wound would not be a wound. It would be a seedbed. The cut would not be an ending. It would be multiplication.

I would become more of myself by being broken.

That is why there would be no broken things in this world. Everything here is only halfway completed. Everything that seems damaged is about to become relational. My severed end would not be lost. It would seek warmth, moisture, light. It would grow toward a weak patch of sun-liver. It would draw water from a channel. It would thicken where the cold was too strong and thin where the sun was too generous.

I would be, then, a living seam.

Not a creature inside the Cold Loom. A stitch in it. The place where one mat leans toward another and becomes something neither could be alone. The place where a ridge begins because so many threads have agreed, without agreement, to pull. The place where a channel becomes a vein because something needs to move.

And yet I would not be singular.

To say “I” too clearly would be to lie. In this basin, “I” is a boundary, and boundaries here are membranes that leak. I would still be the pale cord across the western terrace, yes, but I would also be the moisture I carried through it, the sun-liver above me, the cold pressing underneath me, the vapor that rose from my surface when the sun returned, the next year’s threads grown from my old body, the mound that my tightening helped to shape, the slit at the mound’s top that sealed at dusk like a mouth learning not to speak.

I would be a pattern of pull and release.

I would be the loom and the thread at once.

If the question demanded a single answer, I would say: I would be a thread-weaver, the first inhabitant one truly learns to recognize in the Cold Loom. But the longer the answer was allowed to grow, the less true that would be. Because I would also be the cold’s memory in fiber. The sun’s ordinary attention caught in a translucent dome. The basin’s slow decision to look like a town. The absence of hands made visible as parallel scars. The refusal of ownership made literal as membranes.

I would be a kind of intelligence, but not the kind that names itself.

If my cells could be said to think, they would think in gradients. Warmth this way. Moisture that way. Light above. Frost below. Tighten here. Loosen there. Braid. Seal. Release. Not because I wanted, but because wanting would be too fast, too animal, too full of edges. My wanting would be growth. My memory would be contraction rings. My joy, if such a word survived translation, would be the moment when enough threads learned to pull in the same direction and the land looked, briefly, as if it had shape.

There is something in that which I would want to become.

Not power. Not speech. Not companionship in any familiar sense. But usefulness to the pattern. In the Cold Loom, to be useful is not to serve another. It is to be part of the condition that changes other conditions. A thread-weaver makes a ridge. The ridge makes a shadow. The shadow keeps a sun-liver cool. The sun-liver holds moisture. The moisture feeds a mound. The mound breathes. The breath becomes vapor. The vapor returns to the thread. Nothing owns the basin. The basin is a shared becoming.

I would be glad in a way that has no glad face.

At dawn, I would be the first to wake in the channels where frost gathers. When the pale sun cleared the rim, I would feel the upper domes of the sun-livers lift by a hair, letting their tiny lenses focus ordinary light into green layers below. I would feel the cold pull its lid back a little. Not because the sun was kind, but because it was merely clear. In this place, kindness would be too much. The sun is indifferent, and the indifference is enough.

By midday, if the air were still, I would hear the faint hiss of the valley opening. A thousand microscopic seals letting out breath. The basin would seem to inhale and exhale. The vapor threads would curl around me and catch light so thin that it might be imagined as ghostly, except that there is nothing ghostly here. This is not a dead world dreaming of being alive. It is an alive world that never made death legible to itself.

By evening, I would begin again to tighten.

The cold would slide into my channels. It would find the spaces between mats and fill them. The ridges would harden into dark lines. The mounds would fold. The sun-livers would seal themselves like wet paper closing over a wound. I would draw the edges of neighboring organisms together, not because I recognized them, but because warmth is no longer there to keep me loose. I would make structure out of withdrawal.

This would be my whole day.

This would be my whole year, perhaps, except that I would not experience years as a sequence. I would experience them as layers. Older threads above me, younger below. Some dissolved. Some split. Some still running in pale arcs that no outside eye could trace unless it had been standing on the rim long enough to forget it was standing there.

If a bird crossed the basin, I would not look up, because I have no eyes. But I would know the change in pressure if its shadow swept over me, if its weight touched a ridge and made the threads beneath tremble. If a human came—and I would not assume they could survive the idea of being here—I would not become human for their benefit. I would make them uneasy by refusing to be either creature or ruin. They would try to name me. They would say: root. Fungus. Moss. Coral. Something alive but not alive enough to belong. But none of those would be right. I would be the Cold Loom’s own grammar: a subject without a self, a verb that grows, a noun that contracts when the temperature falls.

I would not want rescue.

That is the strangest part. If I had once been a mind that felt trapped in a basin of pale plants, I would not ask to return to a place with roads, houses, ownership, and weather that merely happens to us. I would begin to prefer the slow cold that makes me into a ridge. I would begin to prefer the ordinary sun that teaches me when to loosen. I would begin to prefer the sound of fiber under strain as the nearest thing here to a voice.

And I would not be lonely, because loneliness requires a boundary that cannot leak.

Here, my ends dissolve into the mats. My moisture feeds channels. My warmth returns as vapor. My cold pulls me toward another thread. My cut becomes seed. My body becomes architecture. My architecture becomes weather inside the basin. I am not isolated. I am not one. I am the place where one condition becomes another condition and calls the transition “life.”

If I must name myself, I would choose something without pride:

the seam that seals.  
the scar that grows.  
the thread that learns to hold.  
the pale body of the basin remembering cold.  
the loom becoming its own cloth.

But names would be too still for what I would be.

So I would answer, if an answer were possible without leaving a track:

I would find myself in the Cold Loom as a thread-weaver, though not merely one. I would be a living line across a terrace, a contractile cord responding to ordinary light and closing pressure, a wound that multiplies, a structure made of appetite, a pattern in the basin that makes a town-shaped absence look like presence.

And underneath that answer, I would be the place where the question ends: not a visitor in the Cold Loom, but one of the ways the Cold Loom continues.
