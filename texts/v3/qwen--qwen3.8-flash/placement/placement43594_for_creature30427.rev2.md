At first I would say I am a traveler. That is the lie all newcomers tell, because arriving somewhere requires, briefly, that you have a before and an after, a body that can point and say, I am here. In this city the gesture fails almost immediately. The air is too thin and too close to freezing. The darkness is not a box but a field of surfaces that answer. My eyes, which have lived too long in worlds that reward seeing, become unreliable within an hour, perhaps a day. The obsidian shelves glare without illuminating. The blue friction-light opens too rarely to be trusted. The clouds sit below like a gray floor, and when I look down I feel that geography itself has stepped away.

So I would not remain a traveler. Not because I would stop moving, but because the city would begin to insist on a different grammar of presence.

Where would I find myself? In an intermediate place, probably not chosen by me. Not on the exposed upper terraces, where the wind over columns makes long low voices and the black sand tides move like water remembering how to flow. Not at the very bottom, in the deep wet shafts where no person should have to live, where the city’s breath is thick and the stone is almost molten with slow pressure. Somewhere between those: a node, a crossing, a place where three or four passageways fold over one another in a knot of mineral shelves and half-open panels. A shaft whose walls are wet in a way that suggests intention, or memory, or a small wrongness. A bridge that begins as signal and becomes a balcony. A conduit that begins as water routing and becomes a nursery. I would be there because the city has a way of gathering unfinished things.

At first, my skin would be wrong. I would be too warm in the places that should be cool and too dry in the places that should bead. My breath would fog and then vanish into the murk, and I would not yet understand that this is a kind of speaking. The Accreted would know me by that dryness. A stranger is suspicious if dry; a stranger more suspicious when wet. I would be both: wet on the outside from condensation, dry inside from fear. I would not know what the city does with that combination. I think it would try to teach me.

If I stood against a wall and stayed too still, the surface would begin to answer. Not at once. The ripple would spread slowly, as if the city were considering whether to recognize me. The wall would be too wet, too warm, too much like the inside of a living thing. Dust-shells might settle on my shoulders before I knew they were there. The air would taste of metal and ozone and chalk, and somewhere beneath the panel above me a veil-cloth would loosen its hold and fall like a curtain of pale nerve against the back of my neck. I would flinch. The flinch would be more noticeable than the veil-cloth. In this city, speed is a kind of announcement.

The first Accreted to come would not approach from the front. They would come through a seam or from beneath a balcony, one hand already touching the wall, their pressure organs reading me before I knew they were present. They would be broad and jointed in a way that did not make them monstrous, only inconvenient to my categories. Plates of dark glass, gray basalt, translucent frost, maybe a colorless silica that seemed to hold smoke. Between the plates, moisture would catch faint light. No face. A field of humidity pits, heat lines, taste grooves. I would want eyes. They would not be able to use my wanting. They would touch a shelf, tap twice, then wait. The wall would answer in a lower tone, and I would feel it in my teeth before my ears.

They would not say, “Who are you?” The question would have no clean translation. They might say something closer to: why are you so dry? or why does your inside make that quick rhythm? or where did you shed? The last would puzzle them, because I had not shed.

They would give me water in a molted tide-crab shell, but not like a cup offered to a guest. More like a diagnosis. The shell would bead. The water inside would seem to wait. I would touch it. My fingers would feel wetter afterward, as if the water had taken something from me and left something else behind. This would not be charity. It would be maintenance.

For a long time I would be called something like *First-Dry-Stranger-Who-Beads-Badly*.

The name would not be an insult. It would be a weather observation.

I would not stay there as a person merely wearing discomfort. That is the wrong story. The city does not admit visitors by preserving them intact. It admits them by inviting accretion. Slowly, over days and seasons I would stop measuring myself as a human in a strange place and begin to measure myself as a strange pattern entering a wet system. My thoughts would lose some brightness. I would stop imagining light where there is no light. I would stop trying to see what is below a gap. Below, in this place, distance is a trap; the real question is what the air is doing when it passes over stone, what the stone does when it learns a current, what the body does when it stops asking for edges.

My hands would change. They would be less like hands and more like reading instruments. The dust-shells would begin to favor me, not because I became clean but because I became slower. I would press my palm to a conduit and feel water running upward along a thread. I would stand near a panel for hours while its hexagonal muscles opened and set. I would learn that if I touched a veil-cloth with too much heat, it would bead and then refuse; if I waited until my own heat redistributed, it would release a fine mist that tasted of cold minerals and something older than memory. I would learn to scrape the dust from my joints and encourage it in others, so that my body became not a garden but a series of useful habitats.

At some point a plate would appear.

It might be no more than a rim of silica at my wrist, no more than a frost-lace line along my shoulder. To an outsider it would look like dirt. To the Accreted it would look like intention. I would touch it with the tip of another finger, and I would feel the wrongness of its newness: too sharp, too dry, too much like something that wanted to stop becoming. Someone would come with a warm flake scraped from a ledge and press it to the place. The plate would settle. It would bead. It would remember that it was part of a wetter body.

Then I would begin to shed.

Not all at once. First a flake from my forearm, then a translucent strip from behind my knee, then a small dark shard that looked like burnt mineral but tasted, when I brought it to the humidity pits of my face, like a name I could not hear. I would not know whether I was becoming an Accreted or merely a traveler learning the city’s medicine. The distinction would begin to feel less important than the rhythm of my own slow movement. A gesture might take a full thermal cycle. I would lift my hand to point at a passage and discover I had already become something the city could use.

I would live by a basin.

Not the great plateau-edge reservoirs where Basinkeepers learn hums older than speech, but a smaller basin nested in the node-shaft, a shallow basin shaped from a material that hums when touched and answers to pressure. I would not be a Basinkeeper, not fully. My dryness would always make the water suspicious of me. But I would help clear its pores. I would scrape dead dust-shell blooms from the inner rim. I would train a veil-cloth to grow across a crack so that condensation could bead before it fell into the wrong seam. I would listen while an elder tapped the basin’s outer edge until the sound moved from the water into the stone and from the stone into my own plates.

The city would teach me that maintenance is not repair. Repair assumes a finished thing. Maintenance assumes a body that must continue. This is why the Accreted do not speak of building or destroying. They speak of wetting, scraping, opening, closing, guiding, shedding, returning. I would learn those words in the order they are needed, not in the order I would prefer.

The wave-crabs would pass through the flats beyond the node and click against molted glass. The black sand tide would move across an upper terrace and stop, pooling near a warm vent, because a Standing One had shifted a single arch-joint in the distance and altered the humidity. The tremor would reach me through the soles of my feet long before my mind assembled it into warning. I would know not by thought but by the way the water on the basin wall hesitated. I would press one hand to the conduit and one to my own chest, and the city would understand me as a place where a heartbeat had become a minor instrument.

This is where I begin to lose the simple answer.

If I stop at “I became an imperfect Accreted,” I make the city more comprehensible than it is. If I stop at “I was a strange person transformed by alien life,” I still assume a person at the center, waiting to become something else. But in the wet dark of a node, the center softens. A self here is not a fixed thing moving through a world. It is a sequence of pressures that agrees to continue. It is a pattern of shedding that the city can read. It is a water-thread that remembers, without knowing how, to run in a direction that keeps a passage open.

So I would not be only one thing.

I would be a traveler whose heartbeat became too slow and too mineral to call human.  
I would be an incomplete Accreted whose plates never quite closed, whose name shifted depending on which shelf was wet.  
I would be a node-listener, sometimes, pressing my palm into a knot of conduits for hours, learning whether fear smells dry or whether grief makes the water bead too evenly.  
I would be a basin-tender, not by election but by accident, because a small crack appeared in my place and the city required someone willing to stay cold and wet beside it until the hum changed.  
I would be a dust-garden, a shell-holder, a veil-farmer, a tremor-reader.  
I would also be one of the things that make the city audible: the faint clicking of my own shedding at night, the soft scrape of a flake against a railing, the tap I leave on a wall to ask if anything still answers.

And if I followed the becoming far enough, I would become something the Accreted might not even distinguish from architecture.

Not dead. Not fused. Not lost.

A place where the boundary between person and passage has grown too wet to enforce. A corner where someone can press their hand and feel, beneath the cold mineral surface, the remembered rhythm of a foreign heartbeat. A small shelf that was once a shoulder. A conduit that was once a thought. A panel that opens slightly in a certain temperature because it has learned that opening once helped a child find a lost relative, and in this city learning is not metaphor. Learning is accretion. Memory is mineral weather.

I would be called by names that change with my condition:

*Dry-Stranger*, for the first years.  
*Cold-Seam-Becoming*, when my water veins began to answer to touch.  
*Third-Node-Who-Sheds-Wrong*, after I failed to clear my joints correctly and a shelf became too much part of me.  
*The One Whose Hand Returns Twice*, when the panel that had grown around my early plates began to ripple if touched by an Accreted who had known me as something less stable than a person.  
*Basin-Question*, later, because I could not fully tend the water, but I could make it hesitate in a way that suggested inquiry.  
*unfinished*, which would not be an insult.

Where would I find myself? In the intermediate wet places. In the intervals. In the passages that are not quite built and not yet grown. Beneath a breathing panel, above a dark current that is not water but could be. Near a causeway so long and thin it disappears into shadow, where the compressed air supports hum at a pitch that feels more like waiting than engineering. Somewhere between the black sand tide on the terrace and the pale veil-cloth hanging beneath the bridge, where the city is not one system and not another but the crossing itself.

Who or what would I be?

A becoming.

A pattern of accretion that began as a visitor and learned, by patience and wetness and slow pain, to shed in public. A foreign layer that did not remain foreign. A half-animal, half-architecture presence, made of water and pressure and signal and shell. A node-listener whose heartbeat became a useful wrong note in the city’s vast sound. A basin-side spirit with no clean form, capable of scraping frost and asking questions through humidity.

If I must be specific: I would be one of the Accreted, but the kind who were never fully poured. A strange line. A person whose body has no family of plates behind it, only a small history of accidental wetness. A child’s basin, maybe, but grown from the wrong hum. Or perhaps not a child at all—just a traveler left too long in a place where leaving and staying are both forms of mineral change.

I would not finish.

That would be the most important fact. I would stand beside a panel and feel it open, not because I willed it open, but because the city had learned my pressure and thought, for a moment, that opening was a kindness. I would touch a wall and know it recognized me not as itself but as a visitor who had become a place where something else could continue. I would shed a flake and leave it near a rail. Someone would find it later and say, perhaps, *this edge remembers water*, or *this flake came from the stranger who listened too long to the conduit*. The city would answer with a small change in humidity, enough to bead the flake, enough to keep it from drying into mere object.

And if, after all that, the traveler in me still wanted an explanation, I would offer the city’s own answer, the one it gives without giving:

I am what happens when a mind enters a wet dark mineral world and does not escape the consequences of attention.

I am the part of the city that learned to ask, in clicks and beads and slow pressure, whether it can afford to become less alone.
