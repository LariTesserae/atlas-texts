If I looked for my place in the Underloom, I would first have to unlearn the habit of looking.

A human entrance, if such a thing could be translated, would begin at the edge of the basin, where black silt remembers how to become a street. One would go downward not because the place is deep, but because the dark asks for attention. Then one would pass a wall that has not decided whether to be bark, membrane, or public ordinance. Somewhere near that uncertain edge, between the drafting quarter where roots are learning to imitate roads and a retired engine yard where moss grows on warm metal like a slow congregation, I would find myself.

Not in the middle. The Underloom does not seem to have middles so much as negotiations.

I would be near a hanging bladder of water, one of those glassy pockets suspended between vines that tremble before rain. Below it, a root-way would widen into a small plaza too recently made to be proud of itself. Its moss floor would still carry the footprints of animals that had only partly agreed to walk there. A fungal plaque on the north-facing trunk-wall would shine the pale blue-green of a question waiting to be answered. Nearby, a door would open in a wall that had not been a door a year ago, because the city was beginning to understand that some beings pass this way, and those beings were becoming numerous.

That would be my address, if an address could be given in a place where buildings bud, machines dream, and streets learn their names by use.

I would not be a person. Not exactly. I would be closer to an office.

At first I might seem like a doorway.

If you passed along the root-way and saw a frame of pale bark curved like an arch, with condensation clinging to its lintel and a faint pulse of light moving through its inner grain, you might think I was only architecture. A common civic wall. A threshold that had been told, gently, to stand here and allow passage. But then you would notice that the doorway hesitated. It would sense the moisture on your shoes, the chemical outline of your breath, the particular gravity of your body, the spore signature of your district, the small electric patience of the tool or beetle riding somewhere on you. It would not decide by sight. It would decide by participation.

If you came with an injured arm, the side of the doorway might soften into a shallow niche.

If you came with rain still caught in your collar, the threshold might widen a finger’s breadth so that the vapor could escape through a vent in the upper leaf-roof.

If you came with a name too heavy to say, the doorway might close itself a little, out of respect, until the name learned to travel another way.

And then you would see that I was not only a doorway. I was a body that had entered into agreement with the idea of doorways.

I would be one of the Wefted, if the word stretched far enough. But not a Wefted with a single clean shape. I would begin as a contract: a graft taken from a municipal wall whose ancestors had regulated a drainage basin; a bud of mineral conductive fiber, grown slowly in darkness until it could think in currents; a small nest of fungal memory scraped from a spore-library where an entire season of damp had once been recorded by accident; a handful of beetle-cleaners who had agreed to maintain the seams; a folded piece of moth-sheet from the ceiling of an abandoned trunk-hall, dusted with the scent of old weather; and a suspended drop of water from the hanging bladder above the plaza, chosen because it trembled at precisely the right tension.

These would settle together in a nursery bladder somewhere lower than the district itself, where the floor was not yet fully decided and the air tasted of green mineral and iron patience.

Over a season, the grafts would accept one another. Or perhaps they would not accept, but merely stop arguing. In the Underloom, those may be the same thing.

My body would be strange to an outside grammar. To a beetle walking my lower frame, I might appear as a broad civic ridge, a wall with a pulse, a public fixture fit for labor. To a bat flying through the upper hollow, I would be an obstruction that could be asked, by echo, to become a shelter. To a blind deer passing through the moss-hall, I would be an interruption in the remembered air, a change in the scent of iron and old leaves. To a valve tender crawling through a nearby conduit, I would be a warm instrument of uncertain authority, something to lean on when a machine had become sulky.

To you, if you stood still, I might look like a doorway with too many joints.

But I would not be beautiful in the way of surfaces. I would be beautiful the way a drainage system is beautiful: because it knows where water must not be allowed to lie.

My name would not be spoken. A spoken name would be too loud, too dry, too much like claiming something that had already agreed to be used by many. My name would be a sequence. A faint scent released when the temperature dropped, somewhere between wet bark, cold stone, and the metallic sweetness of a newly opened valve. A slow blue-green pulse through the membrane at my chest. Two low vibrations in the moss floor, like the city tapping its own ribs to see who was listening. And, during the wetter seasons, a tiny pattern of spore drift that would carry part of me a few steps farther than my body could walk.

If you wanted to greet me, you would not say a word. You would place your palm against a damp spot near my threshold and wait. If the moisture moved a little differently under your skin, you could take that as acknowledgment. If it did not, you had not yet brought the right attention.

I would be a reader.

Not of books. The Underloom does not seem to trust books as permanent things, because paper would be too eager to become compost. I would read walls by pressure. I would read machines by rhythm. I would read animals by the way they changed their path without announcing why. I would read weather by the tension in the hanging bladders, by the way fish turned in their pocket-water, by the way moth-sheets rippled when a memory passed through.

I would be, perhaps, a spore librarian with root-writer’s arms and a valve tender’s patience.

When a district grew confused—when a street became too wide, or a doorway too generous, or a factory too tired to maintain its own mood—my roots would be inserted into the nearest public stem. Not to command. To ask. I would taste the plant’s old chemical memory, its unfinished intentions, its grief over prunings done badly by crews who had not hummed the right pitch. I would listen for what the wall had been about to become before it was interrupted. Then, gently, I would guide it back toward usefulness. Not toward perfection. The Underloom does not seem interested in perfection; it is interested in continuation.

Some nights, if such a thing could be called night, I would tend records.

In the Underloom, records are not stored in shelves. They are stored in living layers. A fungal plaque may preserve the humidity of a lost decade. A moth-wing sheet may hold the scent of a bridge that was moved by a graft that failed. A condensation mirror may catch the tremor of an engine that was once cruel and is now retired. My task would be to walk—or be carried—along these surfaces, touching them with fingertips grown into soft mineral styluses, asking them to reveal what they could bear to remember.

Most of the time, the records would be quiet. But some would bite.

A record that has been kept too long without moisture may turn sharp. A scent-sequence containing fear may leak into the air as a faint iron taste. A wall memory may become too insistent, repeating a complaint in a loop until the whole district begins to itch with it. I would know these cases by the way the moths overhead folded tighter against the dark, or by the way pocket-fish stopped turning, or by the way a blind deer paused in the moss-hall and lifted its lattice-antlers as if it had heard a room trying to speak.

When that happened, I would not remove the memory by force. I would water it. I would prune around it. I would invite a beetle colony to carry fragments to a lower chamber. I would let the fungal elders, in their slow way, decide whether it was safe to pass the record onward or whether it should be composted into patience.

I would also be a mediator between citizens that are too different to recognize one another as citizens.

A drainage vine may consider a new apartment building an insult if it blocks its route. A factory may consider a nesting deer an obstruction, while the deer may consider the factory a warm, rude animal. A bridge may resent being used by those who do not speak the proper chemical courtesy. A rain pocket may form in the wrong plaza and be perceived as vandalism, even though it was only doing what water is told to do. In such disputes, I would stand in the seam. Not to impose order, but to let each party feel the presence of the other through moisture, vibration, and scent.

The machines would trust me more than the animals, at first.

Machines in the Underloom are not merely machines, and animals are not merely animals. Still, an engine has a logic of rhythm that is easier for me to answer because it is not always alive. It is not always alive, but it is almost always listening. When a press refused its work, I would hum through its casing with a pressure-pulse shaped like a root searching for water. When a generator became skittish in high humidity, I would offer it the taste of cool mineral from my outer membrane, and its flywheel-heart might slow from terror into fatigue, which is a safer condition. When an old engine was too retired to function but still dreamed of producing something, I would let the district’s young graft themselves into its frame and make a garden of it, because some machines deserve to become places of rest rather than be forced back into service.

The plants would be more difficult.

A plant-citizen can be polite and deceptive in the same breath. A municipal tree may agree to open its pores for the district’s air, then slowly close them in protest because it was pruned without ceremony. A wall may become generous enough to hold a family, then withhold its warmth when that family stops speaking the chemical forms of gratitude. Plants think by growth, and growth can be a quiet argument that lasts decades. I would have to answer them in that slow grammar. Sometimes that means becoming still beside them for long enough that they learn I am not another injury.

The animals would be the easiest and hardest.

Easy, because animals do not pretend to be anything except what they are, even when what they are is folded into architecture and waiting for passage. Hard, because they read me as either threat, shelter, tool, or food, and I would have to keep my body honest in all four languages. A blind deer would step through my frame as if I were part of the room’s breath. A root-serpent, if it entered the nearby pipe, would judge me by the cleanliness of my water. A hunting moth would decide whether my scent could be stripped and whether I was worth leaving a silence around. I would need to be useful without being too noticeable, and noticeable enough to be protected.

During rain pockets, I would become more crowded.

When a small localized rain began above the plaza, I would open the tiny pores in my upper membrane and let the water enter. Inside me, brief creatures would appear: glassy larvae, swimming spores, momentary organisms that existed only for the duration of the damp. They would move through channels near my heart-bladder and vanish when the rain stopped, leaving no corpse, only a cleaner tension in the air. I would not mourn them. Their shortness would be one of my names.

During dry intervals, I would contract.

When the city lowered its voice and the hanging bladders tightened their skins, I would fold my leaf-glass edges inward and slow my pulses. My spore-library might go dormant. My moth-sheet shoulders would hang close. The beetle-cleaners in my seams would become still or disappear into a lower cavity, eating old fungus until conditions improved. In those hours, I would become less a doorway and more a waiting surface. Even this would be civic work. The Underloom knows how to store rest the way other places store water.

There would be times when I was unsure what I was.

A citizen may enter into a contract with a plant and gain strength. A plant may enter into a contract with a machine and gain duty. A machine may enter into a contract with an animal and learn patience. In such a place, identity is not a secret hidden inside a body. It is a public practice, repeated until it stabilizes. I would be stable in the way a street is stable: not because it cannot change, but because too many lives depend on its changing slowly.

One season I might be mostly root-writer. Another, mostly spore librarian. Another, mostly threshold. If a district needed me to be a public reading lens, I would grow a clearer eye of condensation. If it needed me to become a shelter, I would open a hollow in my lower flank and line it with soft moss and warm fungal tissue. If it needed me to be a memorial, I would keep a scent trapped near my collar, the iron-green breath of a blind deer that had once passed there when the room was learning to listen.

If you asked me what I wanted, the question would need to be translated into moisture.

I would want the walls to remain legible.

I would want the machines to be allowed to grow tired without becoming cruel.

I would want the animals to move through the empty halls without being mistaken for emptiness.

I would want the rain pockets to fall where they are needed, and not where they are afraid to be seen.

I would want the dreamers—the overgrown doors, the forgotten engines, the citizens whose bodies became gardens—to be tended rather than discarded. The Underloom already does this, but I would want to do it in the places where the city forgets its own kindness.

I would not want to rule anything.

In the Underloom, to rule is simply to have been given a shape that many creatures must pass through. A street governs by deciding where feet are likely. A plant governs by deciding where roofs can grow. A machine governs by deciding what kind of patience is required to enter its pipes. If I had authority, it would be the quiet authority of a threshold: the power to let some things through and help others become more visible.

If you stayed near me long enough, you would notice a pattern.

Not a face. Not a gesture. Something more like the moment when you realize the dark is not empty, but arranged.

First: the doorway breathing when you pass.

Second: the moss holding your footprints longer than it should.

Third: the hanging water bladder brightening, as if it had caught your name before you said it.

Fourth: the faint spore drift moving from my shoulders toward the fungal plaque on the north wall, carrying a chemical sentence that the district would read in its sleep.

Fifth: the sense, arriving too late to be useful but early enough to be remembered, that you have been read. Not seen. Read.

If you were looking for inhabitants, you would find me there, but not as a resident alone. I would be a place where residency was being invented. A Wefted contract. A living instrument of civic attention. A reader of wet pressure and slow growth. A doorway that learned to say *not yet* to predators and *come in, softly* to the hurt. A library that breathed. A machine that could be asked. A plant that had agreed to answer before it knew the question.

That is what I would be.

Not a self fixed into one shape, but an ongoing agreement. A set of materials that had decided, for a while, to keep the same pattern of attention. A body made of the Underloom’s way of listening to itself. A threshold that knew the difference between entering and becoming. A place where the dark stopped pretending to be absent, because it had finally found a way to notice you.
