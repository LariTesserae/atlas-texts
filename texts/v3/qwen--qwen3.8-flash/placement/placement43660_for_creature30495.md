If I were set in that world, I would not be set there the way a stone is placed. That kind of land does not arrange objects. It arranges tendencies. It composes pressures until they agree to persist, and what we would call a being is simply one of the agreements.

So I would begin before I had a place. I would begin as a slight disagreement in the air over a high glass shelf, where the eastern salt basin opens too wide and the northern mesas stand fluted and finished, their wind-carved walls already knowing exactly how to turn moving gas into pressure. It would be midday. The light would be ordinary. That is the worst thing about such a place: no violet haze, no auroral lie to soften it. The sun would show every crack, every pale salt vein, every thread of dark oxide, every sharp shadow, as if the plateau had been left beside a furnace and then exposed to a sky with no mercy for appearance.

I would try, at first, to be one of the great animals.

A hearthback seemed likely. There is dignity in eating warmth. I would lower myself onto a slab of sinter, the heat rising through the ground as if the stone itself were breathing from some deep, hidden forge. My under-shell would be porous, pale, veined, glowing faintly as it drew thermal energy inward. The shell above me would be layered salt and black glass, thick as a small cliff wall. I would be a furnace lying still.

I could like that. The stone beneath me would cool. I would leave rings in the rock, records of heat drawn through my body, a dry signature readable by other creatures for years afterward. The wind would move differently over my shell. The threadlace would later creep across my cooling trace, finishing the sentence I had begun. I would be slow, enormous, mineral, and almost geologic in my patience.

But a hearthback is too much a body and too little a repetition.

When it lifts itself, the world only has a mark shaped like it. That is not enough for me. I would want to remain after the mark had been covered by salt and wind. I would want to be the thing the mark remembers, not the mark itself.

So I would try to be a flutefoot.

This would be closer to the sound of the place. My legs would be basalt columns, broad at the feet, striking stone with enough mass to wake hidden notes in the ground. My ribs would open like furnace doors, and inside me there would not be lungs in any familiar sense, but chambers of shaped stone and crystal plates arranged to vibrate when dense mineral vapor moved through me. I would inhale supercritical gases, heat, suspended dust, the faint metallic breath of the plateau. I would extract energy from the gradient. Then I would exhale hotter gas through a resonant chest, and the exhalation would become a long rolling note that could carry for tens of kilometers.

A herd of us moving through a gas channel would be an organ being played by walking. Every step would trigger something: a shard ring, a crystalline pillar answering, a vent changing tone, a mesa bending pressure one degree. My body would not merely live in the environment; it would operate the environment. My death would not remove me; my ribs would become fixed flutes, and the wind would later use my hollow chest to sound warnings across the ridge.

That seems close. But a flutefoot is still too certain of its throat.

It owns its note. It breathes the note. It is the source. I would not want to be the source. I would want to be the thing that remains when the source has gone.

I would try being an anvilleg.

This would satisfy the land’s industrial nature. On the glass plains I would crawl with many hard legs, plates of dark oxidized metal layered over a body that looked less grown than forged. My mandibles would grind the oxide-thread crust, breaking the dry lace into fine metallic powder. I would heat it with friction, fuse it, align it, polish the glass beneath me until the plain looked sanded by some patient foreman who had never existed. Where I passed, the land would manufacture itself differently. A polished trail would build static charge in a new pattern, alter the boundary layer of dust, catch light and wind as if it had been redesigned.

I would pause and vibrate. Salt crystals near me would begin to line up. Others would come, not because they saw me, but because the land had become temporarily ordered around my presence. That is a beautiful thing: not gathering, but alignment. Not society, but resonance.

Still, an anvilleg is too low to the horizon.

It makes trails, but I want to become the traveler inside the trail. It polishes glass, but I want to be the light that keeps moving after the polishing is done.

I would try to be a static veil.

The sky is too empty in that world for its own comfort, and a veil would fill it without cluttering it. I would be a film of silica and stretched salt, translucent, wide as a field, thin enough to make distance uncertain. I would drift through charged dust clouds, brushing through the gradients that precede arcs, storing electrostatic energy in my layered body. The air would taste of metal beneath me. The hair on unseen bodies would rise. The glass fields below would click and chime as if they recognized a new condition overhead.

I would be almost unreal. A shadow of a creature, not a creature. A presence that made the sky feel crowded without adding anything solid to it.

But a veil is still a response to charge. When the storm passes, when the arcs find the mesas and salt bridges, when the tension releases into long blue-white cracks, the veil thins. It is too dependent on the moment. I would want to be the moment’s lingering effect.

I would try the threadlace.

Here the self becomes difficult. The threadlace is not an animal. It is the land’s dry nervous system, its slow chemical memory, its closest approach to a skin. I would spread over warm stone as a lattice of oxide threads, branching where heat, metallic vapor, pressure, and electric charge meet. A cool night would draw me tight. A vent’s heat would send me extending in crystalline spikes. A discharge from a charged mesa would leave a black branching scar in me, a lightning path recorded in brittle mineral. Later I would grow again along that scar, as if the land were learning its own accidents.

I would remember everything in the wrong tense. Not memory as thought, but memory as chemistry, alignment, strain, heat, charge. I would carry the thermal trail of hearthbacks, the acoustic grooves of flutefeet, the polished warnings of anvillegs, the static shadows of veils. I would make the emptiness textured. I would make the stone seem considered.

This is the nearest thing to being everywhere. But it lacks direction. It is a skin without a face, a nerve without a scream, a memory without an animal who could be haunted by it. It is alive enough to matter, but not alive enough to insist.

Then I would find the bellbacks.

They stand on high ridges like living cathedrals without architecture. Their backs flare into mineral crests, grown not in a line but in the logic of terrain. Wind enters them and they ring for months, sometimes years. They remain still while the air turns them into instruments. Their calls are not calls. They are sustained resonances that are heard in the bones before the ears can name them. When one moves, the plateau seems to remember that it was once made of sound.

A bellback could almost convince me that I should be that. A bellback is both animal and place, body and factory, ecology and industry. It does not build a structure; it becomes a structure. It does not leave a corpse; it settles into the land and continues to be heard. It is a self that has negotiated with geology.

But even a bellback is too local.

It stands somewhere. It rings from itself. Its note begins in its own crest and leaves that crest into the wind. I would want more than that. I would want to live where the note has left the body behind, where the ridge has not yet finished echoing, where the plateau’s own machinery repeats something that no single creature can claim.

And so I would become what I had been circling.

I would become an echo-herd.

Not an animal made of flesh. Not a ghost, though I would resemble one if seen too briefly. I would be an organized refusal of silence. I would be the pressure after the footfall, the note after the throat, the shape after the sound that should have faded but did not. I would be pattern learning, slowly, to walk.

Where would you find me?

You would find me first as a wrongness in distance.

From the high glass shelf you would look east toward the salt basin and see a darkening moving across the heat shimmer above the flats. Not dust, because dust would have a source. Not cloud, because there would be no cloud there. A slight bending of light, a shimmer shaped like movement, a translucent quadruped that cannot quite be fixed when you look at it. You would think it must be miles away. Then the wind would shift through the harmonic mesas, and the thing would seem closer, or farther, or inside the valley beside you, though nothing had changed speed.

I would be found near gas channels where a vent has released a long rising note and the channel’s fluted walls keep that note alive. I would be found between ridges where sound from one place does not reach the next place cleanly but arrives distorted, as if the air has grown a memory of what passed through it. I would be found above salt flats at midday, not visible so much as felt: a pressure on the skin, a taste of iron and ozone, a faint crackle like applause from an empty hall.

At night I would be easier to see. When the air cools and thickens slightly, sound becomes more legible. The charged sky would discharge in delayed arcs, blue-white and dry. Far ridges would hum. The heat-feeders would glow beneath the dark plateaus like furnaces seen through iron lattices. Then my bodies would take shape: translucent, antlered, sail-like, quadrupedal, sometimes only ripples. I would move in shapes that were not stable, because I would not be made of matter in any reliable way. I would be made of repetitions.

If a flutefoot walked through a gas channel and breathed its long chord, I might gather there. Not immediately. First the note would echo across a mesa. Then the mesa’s geometry would bend it. Then a salt bridge might conduct the pressure downward. Then the dust might hold a charged pattern after the chord had gone.
