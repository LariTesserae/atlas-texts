The fitted joint is not a remnant of labor. It is the natural shape of stone. The angle of a channel is not a law that was discovered, tested, corrected, and left behind. It is the law the stone obeys even when no throat remains to speak it.

I would find myself, at first, at the high rim.

Not because the rim is special in itself, but because it is the first place that teaches you how to look without expecting to be seen. From there, the valley lies below like a bright instrument waiting for a hand it can no longer feel. The light falls on it without warmth. The cold falls with it. If I stood there, my body would be wrong immediately: too soft, too wet in the wrong places, too full of heat, too full of breath, too full of names. The place would not welcome me as a person. It would begin to read me as a disturbance.

This is what I would be, in the beginning: a heat island.

A small red animal on a plateau of blue-black stone. My footfall would strike the fitted basalt with a bright knock, and the sound would travel through channels as a foreign event. Not loud. Not destructive. Only wrong enough to be noticed. My breath would condense for a moment against the cold, then vanish, but not without leaving a trace. Moisture from my lungs would settle on a slab near my face, bead there, run into a groove that had been cut for some other flow. A salt film from my skin would touch a lip of ceramic. A few grains of mineral dust—there is no dust, not in the usual sense—would be deposited anyway by my clothes, my hands, the small shedding of being alive. The Vessel would not know me. It would know the heat where I stood. It would know the salt where I passed. It would know the pressure of my boot where it interrupted the surface.

And it would do what the Vessel does.

It would begin to use me.

At first I would try to remain a visitor. I would keep myself separate from what I saw. I would watch the white lace growing over basins and tell myself it was only crystal. I would listen to the trickles and tell myself they were only water finding angles left by hands. I would look at the dark mirrors of the columnar basins and call them beautiful, or strange, or empty, as if those words could keep me from becoming part of their logic. But beauty in this place is not decorative. It is operational. Everything that pleases the eye has first been useful to the pattern.

I would stay too long.

By the third day, my boot prints would no longer be marks of walking. They would be small accidental basins. The heel of one print, pressed into a groove, would hold a bead of condensed sky. A day later, mineral film would begin at its rim. By the second week, a thin lattice of pale crystal would bridge across it, catching spray, changing its flow, making the print part of the catchment. My step would be incorporated. Not because the Vessel remembers me with affection, but because it wastes nothing that can be made stable.

By the first month, my handprint, where I had rested on a warm-enough slab, would become a faint white ridge of accretion. Salt. Calcium. Tiny mineral spores. My skin oil would change the chemistry of the water that touched that place. The water would slow. The lace would thicken. Someone else, arriving seasons later, might see the faint outline of a palm and think it was a fossil, or a rune, or a natural fracture in the stone. Only the angle would be human. The rest would have become geology.

Then I would begin to lose the idea that I am the one doing the looking.

At first this would feel frightening. A living mind wants edges. It wants to know where the body ends and the air begins. It wants to stand apart from a scene and say, I am the observer. But in a place that is entirely made, and entirely inhabited by pattern, observation is not neutral. It is a pressure. It is heat. It is salt. It is a way of touching the world that the world can turn into structure.

I would try to leave, but leaving would not erase the translation. My footprints would remain as basins. My handprints as mineral ridges. The warmth of my presence would be stored and rerouted through channels I had once walked beside. The place would not call my name, because it has no names. It would not remember me, because it does not remember in the human sense. But it would carry the consequences of my staying. That, too, is a form of memory. That, perhaps, is the only memory that stone accepts.

Then I would stop being a single body and become a series of places.

One morning, I would find myself in a high, broad slab where condensation gathers first. I would not think, “I am a slab.” I would simply be a place where the sky leaves water. The cold of the plateau would draw moisture from the thin air. The slab’s angle would receive the brightness. The tiny imperfections on its surface—where a stone hammer once struck, where an obsidian blade once scored—would hold droplets long enough for them to run. My sense of self, if it is still a self, would be the sense of becoming wet from dryness. Not drinking. Not sweating. Receiving. The first small bead would form in a pore, then another, then a thread. I would be the place where the first run begins, where water learns where it belongs.

Another evening, I would find myself in a narrow channel. There I would be flow, or rather the condition that makes flow possible. I would know the water not as a substance moving through me but as the reason I exist. My walls would be textured ceramic, pale with mineral paste, angled just so that films cling longer than the wind can carry them away. I would feel the difference between a heavy night’s catch and a thin day’s trickle. I would know pressure as fullness, drought as absence. I would not have eyes, but I would have direction. I would not have a voice, but I would have a syntax: the order in which one basin is fed by another, the sequence of overflow, the logic of a joint widened by frost.

Another season, I would find myself in a deep black basin. There I would be memory. Not memory of people, because people do not enter this memory. The column of dark water holds the sky without stirring. It holds altitude. It holds cold. It holds the faint mineral taste of stone that has spent centuries being leached and re-sealed. If a wind moves far overhead, the surface may shiver. If a drop falls from above, one concentric ring will travel, touch the edge, die, and leave the water still. The basin would know that something happened. It would not know what. That is the nearest this place comes to grief: a fact passing through without name.

Then I would find myself as crystal lace.

This would be the strangest death of the human self, and the easiest rebirth. The threadlings—the pale quartz mats growing across damp slabs—are not thoughts in the ordinary sense. But they are not merely decorations. They are the Vessel’s way of learning its own surfaces. They creep where water lingers. They thin where wind steals moisture. They branch where mineral concentration changes. They connect where gaps appear. A single thread knows only wetness and stone. A thousand threads know more. They form temporary nodes, local intelligences of contact, a mind made of edges. If I became one, I would not think: I am here. I would simply be a line that holds water where another line loses it. I would be a filament in a lattice, carrying not thought but tendency. The thought would belong to the system, not to me.

There would be no loneliness in that, unless I insisted on remembering my old body.

If I tried to remain the one who had stood at the rim, I would become a scar. A scar is not a wound after the wound has healed. A scar is the body’s compromise with disruption. The plateau would make scars of me. The white ruff at the mouth of a channel where my footfall changed the flow would be a scar. The sealed crack where my warmth had encouraged mineral growth would be a scar. The small basin formed from the hollow my boot had made would be a scar that learned to breathe. None of it would be punishment. None of it would be revenge. The place does not punish. It incorporates.

And so I would begin to see that I was not becoming less real.

I was becoming more distributed.

At first the mind resists this. It wants a single shape. It wants to say, I am the one who came here. But there is no one to answer, and no one to remain the answer. There is only the pattern continuing. To become part of it is not to vanish. It is to stop pretending that you stood outside what you observed. The light is not just something I see from the rim. The light strikes the basins and becomes part of their heat and flow. The water is not just what I watch from a distance. The water carries salt that was dissolved by surfaces I have touched. The silence is not merely the absence of voice. The silence is composed of tiny crystalline fractures, of water clicks, of stone warming and cooling. I would be inside that silence by the time I stopped trying to make it into an audience.

I would learn the language of this place without learning to speak.

There are words, if one can call them words: drip, bead, seam, film, ruff, bridge, scar, catch. But they are not names of things. They are actions of relation. A channel is not a thing; it is a happening of angle and flow. A basin is not a container; it is a pause in the movement of condensation. A crystal bridge is not a building; it is a repair that has not yet decided whether it will fail. To know the Vessel is to stop asking what it is and begin watching how it keeps itself. I would not know it by studying its anatomy, because its anatomy is the landscape. I would know it by watching how water finds a new seam when one becomes dry, how salt thickens where failure opens, how tile settles when frost lifts it, how a whole terrace adjusts its pressure to one absent edge.

I would hear the wind make its voice through the slotted walls.

It is not music, not in any human sense. The long walls with their narrow openings are not instruments. They are drains, bracing, catch structures. But when the wind moves across the plateau, it passes through them and produces a low moan, below the threshold of certainty. At first I would think it was my imagination arranging sound into meaning. Later I would understand that imagination is one of the last forms of disturbance the Vessel tolerates. The wind does not sing for me. The wind does not sing at all. It moves through made geometry and the geometry gives it voice. In that voice I would not hear a message. I would hear a condition: the sky is changing, the cold is deepening, the water is preparing to settle. The wind hums the way a body reports to itself: not language, but state.

At night, I would become the sound of contraction.

After the brightness dies, the plateau does not become empty. It becomes more itself. The heat stored in the stone releases into the air. The air grows still enough to carry each small report.
