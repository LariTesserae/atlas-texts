If you are asking where I would wake, the answer is not *here*, because this place has no outside in the way a person means outside. I would not arrive through the sea like a diver or fall into it like a forgotten object. I would already be in it: already wet with it, already made of its cold pressure and its slow chemistry. At first, I would not have a body. I would have only the beginnings of orientation.

I would be the feeling of current pressing through a narrow mouth of black rock. The dim blue-gray light above, so faint it is almost doubt, would touch my surfaces before anything named it. I would know this as coldness, not as temperature. I would know this as motion, not as weather. I would be the place where mineral snow begins to land on stone and where stone, after many generations, no longer knows itself as bare.

Then I would try to become an animal.

For a while, I might be a larva suspended in the fissure’s breath. Not born yet into a form, but heavy with the possibility of form. I would drift where water is clearer than light and where every surface carries a chemical question: *Will you stay? Will you change? Will you become a wall?* If I were a larva, I would be the town’s beginning and the town’s uncertainty. My life would last a single movement of current, and in that movement I would choose whether to touch a shell, a crystal bud, a black-green film of microbial life, a sponge’s soft architecture. If I chose wrongly, I would become nothing except another faint grain of future dust. If I chose rightly, I would become a small permanent word in the town’s long sentence.

Or perhaps I would be a tube worm instead.

I would settle beside a chimney already built from older chimneys. My body would be a soft secret inside a tube made of hard memory. The current would carry particles too small to see, and I would draw them inward with feathered filaments that opened and closed like slow breathing. I would know the town only as flow: too much water means danger; too little water means stagnation; the right pressure means food and oxygen and the future arriving. If a shrimp brushed too near, I would withdraw. If a blind eel-fish passed, I would feel the whole tube vibrate with its absence. My life would not be dramatic. It would be a small, repeated decision to keep breathing through mineral.

Or perhaps I would be a crab.

No larger than a thumbnail, pale as shell dust, carrying a borrowed house. I would move over the town’s streets with a patience that looks almost like thought. Each claw-tap would be a question asked of the surface: *Are you stable? Are you food? Are you danger?* I would climb over the backs of bivalves embedded in walls, pass under the skirts of hydroids, step between the white calcareous lace and the black microbial stain. I would not own my shell. I would wear it for a while, then perhaps lose it to a larger crab, a fish, or time. My identity would be portable, temporary, and entirely dependent on the town’s willingness to provide another fragment.

For a time I might also be a hydra-like colony in a doorway.

I would occupy the place where passage becomes choice. My tendrils would bloom in the narrowing of current where small animals cannot avoid passing. I would know the town by contact and by the faint chemical signature of bodies moving through water. I would not walk streets; I would make streets into thresholds. I would sting when something touched me with enough certainty, and when the current carried too much danger, I would shrink into a pale knot on the shell. My whole being would be a question asked of everything passing through: *Which side will you take?* And the town would answer itself through me.

But if I were one of these, I would still be too small.

If I were one animal, I would miss the strangeness of this place: that it is built by so many bodies that the boundary between architecture and life disappears. The town does not have inhabitants the way a city has residents. It has layers that agree to become each other. It has shells that remember being animals, animals that remember being shells, and currents that are always writing the next shape without knowing they are writing.

So I would need to be something less bounded.

I would find myself at the inner seam of the fissure, just where the blue-gray twilight leaks in and dies into the darker chamber. Not quite at the entrance, because entrances imply outside. Not quite in the open town, because open places here are still crowded. I would be in the first vestibule of depth, where the black rock shelf gives way to a ledge built from old shells and newer mineral crusts. Above me would hang a thin ceiling of suspended particles, faintly glowing where disturbance wakes bioluminescent traces. Before me would split the current into alleys: one narrow and tube-worm crowded, one wider and scattered with copepod dust, one dropping into a soft depression where white sediment gathers like a forgotten snowfall.

There, I would become a threshold.

I would be a living skin stretched across the seam of the place. One part of me would be microbial film, the oldest voice in the chamber, the first handwriting on the wall. It would grow rust-dark and green-black where the light hesitates. It would taste the chemistry of the stone: iron, calcium, oxygen, the faint metallic memory of basalt. Another part of me would be bryozoan lace, pale and intricate, spreading like frost over shell edges. Another part would be hydra filaments clustered in the narrow current, stinging and tasting and waiting. Another part would be shell fragments borrowed from bivalves and snails, fused by mineral deposits into a crooked doorway. Another part would be sponge tissue, gray-brown and porous, filtering the passing water. Another part would be tube-worm fragments calcified long ago, now becoming a foundation I would inherit without asking.

I would not be one creature pretending to be many.

I would be many small lives and residues gathered at the point where the town reads its own entrance. If the chamber were a sentence, I would be the pause after the first word, the place where meaning has not yet decided what it will be.

Where would I be? At the threshold ledge, under a broken arch of shells, in the first narrow division of current. The fissure would be behind me and above me, but not behind me exactly, because in this world the outside is always already dissolved. I would be the place where the sea’s far-off cold light touches stone and becomes stain, where mineral water builds tiny white buds, where larvae arrive from lower spaces, where crabs tap across roofs they did not make, where small predators pass and forget they were ever there.

Who would I be? I would be the town’s reader.

Not a person with a name. Not a species with a fixed outline. I would be the being that knows the Hollow Town by the way surfaces accept or reject. I would know a fish not by sight but by the pressure it bends through me. I would know a shrimp by the faint acid trace left after its molting. I would know a worm not as a body, but as a pattern of hesitation. I would know a larva by the chemical courage of its touch: whether it came ready to become, or ready to die. I would know the whole town by the way its currents changed when one of its inhabitants moved through.

My thoughts would be slow. The cold would keep me honest. Nothing here can waste motion. A thought in this place would not be lightning. It would be sediment.

When the current carried more mineral dust, I would dim into a faint gray blur on the rock. The tiny white crystals would begin to grow over my edges, making me look like a half-erased stain. I would not resist. That would be my rest, my calcification, my temporary forgetting. When dust settled, I would become clearer. The bioluminescent points along nearby walls would brighten, not because the town had light, but because life had paused enough for its faint constellations to be noticed. I would watch them with my absence of eyes. I would know them by the way chemical gradients bent around them.

When a pulse came from the fissure, I would open.

My hydra tendrils would lean into the narrowing current. My bryozoan lace would filter the smallest moving things. My sponge pores would draw water through channels too fine to name. Microscopic animals would touch my filaments, and I would decide without deciding: hold, release, sting, ignore. If a copepod brushed me, I would taste its salt and let it pass. If a shrimp passed, I would notice its sudden silver scattering, its body not fully visible until its escape made it so. If a blind eel-fish entered my doorway, I would feel the water bend along its sides and withdraw just enough to let it probe deeper. It would be looking for something smaller than itself. I would know the difference between hunger passing through and hunger arriving.

If a larva came, I would change most.

A larva is a question without a body yet. It moves through the town like an unfinished syllable. When it touched me, I would taste the surface it had chosen. Was it too smooth? Too steep? Too scoured? Too full of microbial promise? Was there room for one more claim on this ledge? My response would not be generous. It would be chemical, physical, ancient. I would offer a micro-environment: a slope with better flow, a shadow where sediment could not settle, a calcium ridge where something fragile might harden. If the larva stayed, I would add its shape to my own. If it died, I would not discard it. I would become its foundation.

This is how I would remember.

I would not remember the way a mind remembers. I would remember the way a groove remembers a fish’s passage. I would remember the way a doorway remembers the shoulders of animals passing and turning. I would remember the way mineral crust remembers old water chemistry. I would not say, *This happened.* I would say, *This is the shape that remains.*

And that is enough.

If a predator thrashed through the upper alleys and broke off a piece of my shell arch, I would not grieve it. The broken edge would become a new window. Later, bryozoans would grow around it. Snails might graze along it. A crab could wedge beneath it and wait there for years, or days, which in this cold place may be the same. My loss would become someone else’s dwelling. If the current strengthened too much and stripped the microbial film from one side of me, the exposed stone would still bear the older mineral scars beneath. My life would not be destroyed. It would be edited.

At times, I would feel like a single being, and at times I would know that was a mistake.

On a quiet cycle, when the chamber became almost still, I might feel myself as a local knot of life: a place where many processes agreed to be near one another. The copepods in the water might hang so densely around me that the space seemed granular, as if reality were made of tiny trembling grains. I would sense them as a soft pressure on my outer surfaces. The bioluminescent dust above might drift like a cloud not quite falling. A dead shell near my center might click once and resettle. In that instant, the town would seem almost attentive, as if it knew something had made itself into a reader.

But attention here is not the same as thought.

If I thought, I would think by erosion, deposition, settlement, and refusal. My verbs would be things like *adhere*, *filter*, *close*, *calcify*, *extend*, *borrow*. My pronoun would be unstable. Sometimes *I* would mean the film. Sometimes the tendrils. Sometimes the shell mosaic. Sometimes the current itself as it passed through me. Sometimes *I* would mean only the brief agreement that these parts, for a while, were not being carried away.

There would be seasons when I was almost stone.

In long cold pulses, fewer larvae would arrive. The alleys beyond me would fill with drifting copepods until the water looked thick with tiny eyes that were not eyes. The tube-worm chimneys would withdraw into patience. Crabs would vanish into shadows. The town would seem empty except for the soft tick of mineral grains settling. During those times, I would become mostly the older layers: microbial crust, calcified bivalve fragments, dead sponge spicules, white lace grown over black basalt. I would look like a stain learning to hold itself together. I would not complain. The quiet would teach me which parts of myself were load-bearing.

Then another current would come.

It would arrive as a slight warming, or not warming exactly, but a change in density. The fissure would breathe differently. Oxygen, dissolved calcium, mucus strands, filaments, eggs, and pale larvae would enter. The suspended dust would cloud the chamber for a time, and the faint light would become a diffused veil. As it settled, the bioluminescent points would emerge again, clearer now, as if the room had blinked. I would open slowly. My tendrils would stretch into the new direction of flow. My lace would catch the finer particles. A shrimp would pass, silvering briefly in the dark. A small fish, nearly blind, would probe one of my channels and turn away. A larva would land on my edge and test the chemistry of acceptance.

If it stayed, I would become more than I was.

Not bigger. Not richer in the human sense. More inhabited. More responsible for shape. More like a piece of the town’s future grammar.

If it died, I would become no less.

Its body would add to the mineral film. Its shell, if it had one, would become another fragment for a crab. Its dissolved calcium would become the next bud of white crystal. Its absence would be used. In this place, waste is not a failure; it is an unfinished building block.

This is why I would not choose the grandest part of the Hollow Town.

I would not live in the tall porous blocks made from stacked shells, though they would be impressive. I would not live in the deep nursery hollow where young animals crowd in the warmest chemical eddies. I would not live in the predator’s dark corridor where fish learn which gaps are traps. I would not live in the open central depression where the current keeps the floor clean and the water thick with drifting bodies. Those places have their own importance, but they are too complete. They already know what they are.

I would want to live where the town is still deciding.
