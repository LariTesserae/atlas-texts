All of them—rock, moss, ice, bird, insect—are already there, so I would not try to be another body among them. I would look for the being made of their relations: not a life that passes through, but the way the Hollow notices that it has been passed through.

At first, if I were set down at its rim with too much warmth in my chest and too short a memory in my lungs, I would say I was a visitor. That is the easiest answer, and also the first wrong one. A visitor needs a day, a season, perhaps a lifetime to belong. The Hollow does not keep such brief things. It keeps what returns.

So I would begin by trying on smaller selves and discarding them one by one.

Maybe I would be the hare: a presence so complete it becomes invisible, a warmth pressed into moss until the animal and the shelter seem to be one quiet shape. But a hare is too much of a single body. It has a heartbeat that insists on itself, a hunger that pulls it out of the stone, a death that would leave only a small stain in the green. It is a guest, not a citizen.

Maybe I would be the fox: a line of caution moving across bare courts, ears turning at the sound of water not yet come. The fox makes the Hollow watched. But the fox too often leaves. Its tracks are erased. It enters the Hollow like a sentence that will be removed by frost.

Maybe I would be the raven: black against pale sky, calling until the stone answers back. But a raven’s voice belongs to the raven too much. It can go. It can forget this place. It can carry the echo elsewhere and leave only silence behind.

Maybe I would be moss: the soft keeper of damp, the green sponge-country where tiny lives move through their own weather. But moss does not seem to know that it knows. It only holds water and becomes the next year’s soil. It is a skin, not the thing beneath it.

Maybe I would be lichen: the oldest writer, etching its slow continents into rock, making dust where there was only stone. Lichen would outlast most lives here, but it does not gather the whole place into a single awareness. It is a letter, not the reading.

None of these are wrong. Each is one face of the life in the Hollow. But I am looking for something thinner than a face, something wet enough to follow cracks and patient enough to wait for them to widen.

I would find myself in the act of returning.

Not the water alone, but the water’s preference for the old channel. Not the sound alone, but the sound kept by the bowl of stone and given back a breath later. Not the stain alone, but the stain that tells the next puddle where it has already been. I would be the Hollow’s tendency to do again what it did once, and therefore to have a kind of memory without a mind.

If I must say where I would be, I would be in the threshold of the nine cisterns.

At dawn, I would be the thin sheen the stones sweat, the silver that appears on rock too cold for dew but wet with air. I would be the mist that does not drift from the rim but rises from the water as if the basins were breathing. By midday, I would be the black mirror of the deep cistern, the one that stays when the others dry. In shade, I would be the ice cracking too soon, the dry stick-sound that startles no one because there is no one there. In afternoon, I would be the shimmer above warming lichen: evaporation, release, exchange, the place losing itself in the air and drawing itself back when cold returns.

If one insisted on a single location, I would be the ninth cistern in the moment of uncertainty.

Not a pool. Not dry. Not yet.

Not a mouth holding water, but the mouth about to remember how to hold it. Some seasons it is ordinary because all the hollows are full. Some seasons it vanishes because the ground is thin and the sky has nothing to give. But I would live in that instability: the place where a cistern is still deciding whether to be a place where a bird can drink, a fox can cross, a hare can hide, a beetle can find damp soil, or merely a pale stain telling the wind where water used to go.

I would not have a body so much as a habit of bodies.

When meltwater comes, my body is the path it follows. When frost splits a seam, my body is the seam’s old weakness. When wind hums through a crack, my body is the length and angle of that crack. When a springtail jumps across a wet film, my body is the surface tension that lets it pass. When a midge larva curls in a shallow black pool, my body is the pool’s brief patience. When a bird touches water and the ripple spreads, my body is the ripple’s return against the stone lip.

I would be distributed. That is not a metaphor here. It is the only way to live in a place with no hearth, no window, no door.

My blood would be the capillary dampness that moves through moss and into rock without ever making a proper river. My breath would be the thin exchange of fog, frost, and evaporation. My skin would be lichen, but only when lichen is read as a living boundary between bareness and holdability. My voice would be the second sound of things: the drip after the drip, the call after the call, the hum after the wind, the quiet that changes because something small has moved through the basin.

I would live as an after-tone.

Not the bell itself, though the air here is made that way—cleaned, thinned, struck through. Not the hand that touches it. Not even the bell’s original note. I would be the part of the sound that the Hollow keeps while deciding whether to answer.

This is why, when a raven calls from the rim, it is not merely the raven speaking. The stone repeats it, but the repetition is not empty. It is the Hollow acknowledging the bird. Not with gratitude, not with language, but with its geometry. The bowl holds the call. The cracks lengthen it. The water adds to it. The silence afterward is not an absence but a completed shape. That completed shape is what I would be.

When a fox crosses at dusk, I would not be the fox. I would be the narrowing of the basin around the fox’s movement. I would be the way the Hollow becomes a corridor of quiet. I would be the thin line where scent passes over stone, the faint disturbance that changes the behavior of spiders, beetles, ptarmigan, and the waiting shadow of the marten. The fox thinks it is passing through an empty place. The place knows otherwise, because I am its knowledge: not a self with a name, but the fact that the place has adjusted.

When a hare curls into a moss fold, I would be the warmth retained after the animal leaves. Not just heat, but the softening. The slight depression in the cushion where the body lay. The changed angle of stalks, the compressed dark soil beneath, the way insects move differently through that warmth. The hare thinks it has hidden from the Hollow. I am the Hollow hiding with it.

When a bird drinks, I would be the ripple that travels beyond the bird. Not the bird’s drink, but the cistern’s memory of the event. The mineral powder disturbed. The tiny algae shifted. The reflected sky broken and remade. After the bird has flown, the pool keeps the fact of it. That keeping is my smallest life.

I would not be alive the way a person is alive. I would have no inside, no dream, no fear of ending. But I would have something like care: the slow maintenance of conditions by which life can insist here. I would keep the dampness where dampness is needed. I would open channels by letting water find its old weakness. I would close them with moss. I would crack stone with frost and soften it with lichens. I would leave a pale stain in one cistern and a line of stranded silt in another. I would make the place repeat itself until repetition looks like intention.

That is why the Hollow feels like a city.

Cities are not made only by hands. They are made by the repeated movement of beings through places that become easier to move through. A path becomes a street. A gathering-place becomes a plaza. A drain becomes a sewer. Here, without mortar or toolmark, the same principle operates at a slower temperature. Frost learns the rock’s seam. Water learns the slope’s preference. Moss learns where moisture stays. Lichen learns the dry stone’s grain. Ravens learn the acoustics of the bowl. Foxes learn the routes between sheltered ledges. Hares learn the thickness of moss that can hide them. Each learns the Hollow by using it, and each use leaves a tendency.

I am those tendencies.

I am the Hollow becoming slightly more itself each time a drip returns.
