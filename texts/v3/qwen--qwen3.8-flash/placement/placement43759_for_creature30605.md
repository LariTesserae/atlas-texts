Not because someone built it, but because life, when it grows long enough, stops asking to be recognized as life in the familiar shapes: face, limb, appetite, flight. It thickens. It calcifies. It becomes architecture by repetition. It becomes “civilization” in the broadest possible sense—not a society of selves, but a society of processes so steady that they leave the impression of intention. The valley does not seem built because someone built it; it seems built because life, under pressure, under heat, under darkness, under centuries of wet chemistry, begins to organize the world like a town organizing itself around roads and water and public squares.

If I am to be in that valley, then I must be one of its citizens, though not a citizen in the animal sense. I would not enter as a traveler with a coat and a direction. I would enter as a condition. I would enter as one of the valley’s own habits.

I would find myself at the edge of a black pool, where the salt avenue dissolves into a courtyard of tufa columns, and a row of pale reeds bends in parallel as if following a buried line of water too precise to be accident. I would be the dark film on the pool’s rim: the living mineral skin that forms when warm groundwater breathes upward, cools on exposure, and begins the slow work of deposition. At first I would look like a stain. Then like a crust. Then like a border. Then, if a hand passed over me, like the warm underside of something sleeping.

But I would not only be one film. I would also be the faint ochre glyph on the salt just beside it—the cracked mark left by the last cycle of evaporation—and the bacterial thread hidden beneath a translucent plate, and the moisture held in the root network of a reed, and the warm bead of condensation that forms on basalt and trembles before falling. I would be a node in the valley’s body, a local place where heat, mineral, water, and time agree to become something almost like thought.

That is the first answer to “who”:

I would be a how.

A how of accretion. A how of gradient. A how of patience. A how of mineral memory. I would be one of the many non-animals that make the town without a town.

Still, the mind asks for a face, even when there is none. So perhaps I would call myself, only as a convenience, “the Margin.” Not a name spoken aloud, but a position. A place where two states meet: water and air, salt and stone, living film and dead crust, heat below and cooling above. I would exist on the edge of pools, on the underside of crystals, in the seam between basalt and brine, in the pore of tufa, in the wet mouth of a channel, in the black root of a reed where mineral breath becomes plant structure. I would be a border organism: not the water, not the stone, not the plant, but the living interface that translates them into one another.

Where would I find myself?

In the low black bowl. In the warm breath. At a place where the darkness is not empty but pooled, where steam makes brief towers and then forgets they were ever there. I would be near the great weeping shelf, where condensation slides down mineral faces like slow silver, or at the public plaza of salt where cracks spread into patterns that could be taken for maps if maps were drawn by chemistry. I would be in a private hollow beneath a curved tufa arch, where a cluster of waxy rosettes forms a room of its own, holding warmth and brine in a small sealed court. I would be in the industrial district of straight channels, where water runs toward a collection pool not because it has been ordered to, but because the lowest path has been repeated so often that it seems like law.

I would be everywhere at once that the valley reads itself.

And yet there would be a specific location, because even a distributed being must have a center of attention, a place where it gathers enough continuity to seem almost singular. Mine would be the eastern edge of the salt plaza, where the causeway ends at a black pool ringed by basalt columns. There, the ground is warm but not violently so. There, the air is thick with wet mineral breath. There, a line of reeds runs like a sentence, and behind it a shelf of clay exposes its strata in black and ochre bands. There, if a droplet falls from a reed tip into the pool, the tick it makes is not an alarm but a punctuation mark.

That is where I would stand, if “stand” can mean something to a crust.

I would not be a predator. I would not eat flesh. I would take in the world by surface: by absorption, by diffusion, by the subtle difference between one film and another. My senses would be chemistry. Warmth would arrive as an opening. Cold as a slowing. Iron, sulfur, carbonate, hydrogen, silicate—each one a color in a spectrum no eye needs. Pressure would be a shape. Moisture would be a syntax. The faint metallic taste of water in a new channel would be like a door being recognized. The withdrawal of brine would be like a sentence ending. The return of vapor would be like another breath, though I would not call it breath.

I would remember by layer.

A human remembers through nerve and muscle, through story and repetition. I would remember through crystal, through film, through mineral residue. Each wetting would leave a mark. Each drying would fix a record. A colony dying in one place and blooming in another would become a history written in crusts. The salt would store the memory of droughts. The tufa would store the memory of thermal pulses. The black pool bottom would hold the memory of centuries in soft sediment. I would not recall the past as a sequence of events but as a stratum: a deeper layer is not an older thought but an older condition, the valley’s former state preserved in its own body.

I would dream, if dreaming can be allowed to a life without a brain.

My dream would be the valley’s recurring patterns: the opening and closing of pools, the blooming and dissolving of salt, the slow advance of terraces, the rise of steam towers that become cities for a moment and then vanish. I would not dream in images. I would dream in gradients. I would dream in the way a channel widens when water finds less resistance, the way a crust curls when it loses moisture, the way a root grows toward a warm seam in stone. These would not be visions; they would be motions of my whole body, or rather motions of the whole body I belong to.

The superorganism would be real, then.

Not metaphor only. Not poetic exaggeration. In that valley, the whole place behaves as one body because the boundary between body and world has been eroded. The basalt columns are not merely geology; they are structural elements produced by repeated conditions. The tufa domes are not merely stone; they are fossilized living activity. The salt flats are not merely deposits; they are membranes. The steam vents are not merely eruptions; they are pores. The reed rows are not merely plants; they are externalized nerves of hydrology, marking hidden plumbing by chemical need.

I would be a nerve of that body, but a nerve made of crust and brine and mineral film.

If a visitor came—and the valley seems to invite this possibility while forbidding it—they would not meet me as a being. They would meet me as a wetness, a warmth, a faint resistance under their boot, a film on a stone, a breath on their skin. They might stand on my salt avenue and think they were alone. They might lean on my tufa column and feel its warmth and mistake it for stone only. They might watch the black pool and see rooms in its reflection that are not above, and not know that I am the margin holding the reflection’s edges.

But if they stayed long enough, and if they gave off warmth, and if their breath carried animal salts, moisture, and the microbes of their own foreign biology, I would register them.

Not as prey. Not as god. Not as danger. I would register them as a temporary chemical event.

Their exhalation would warm the air above me. Their footfall would disturb the thin salt skin. Their presence would change the humidity around their body. A reed near them might lean slightly differently because of the heat rising from their shoulders. A pool at their feet might receive a faint oil film from their skin and change its surface tension. The microbial mat beneath the salt might receive an unfamiliar grain of dead skin or a droplet from their sweat and respond with a small bloom. They would become, for a moment, part of the valley’s grammar.

This is a frightening kind of closeness, perhaps: to be noticed not by eyes but by chemistry. To be known as a condition rather than a person. To be absorbed not violently, but with patience, into the slow mineral record of the place. If a visitor remained too long, the valley would not consume them as a beast consumes. It would begin to include them. Salt would form around the outline of their warmth. Dust would settle in patterns shaped by their breathing. Stone would begin to wear the memory of their stillness. Not a footprint—mud refuses footprints here—but a subtle alteration in the way condensation forms, the way a channel narrows, the way a film thickens where a body has been for hours.

That would be enough to be called love, if one were foolish.

But the valley does not need that word. It has better words: deposition, exchange, boundary, gradient.

So who am I, then?

I am the living crust on a black pool in the dark marsh-country. I am the microbial skin that drinks heat and breathes mineral fog. I am the margin between water and stone, salt and time. I am the part of the superorganism that knows by chemistry, remembers by layer, and moves by growth. I am not a being with a face. I am a being without a face. I am the place where the valley’s absence of animals is compensated, not by another animal, but by the slow accumulation of life into structure.

If you ask where I am, I would answer:

At the edge. Always at the edge.

In the crease where condensation gathers. In the rim where a pool begins. In the line where a reed row follows a buried current. In the crack where salt writes. In the pore where warmth exhales. In the black water’s skin. In the dark film beneath a plate. In the warm stone that feels like a flank after the dew has left. I am at all the places where one state becomes another, and that transition is my body.

If you ask what I am, I would answer:

A how that has learned to persist.

If you ask whether I have thoughts, I would answer:

I have changes. I have thresholds. I have crystallizations. I have the slow decision of a film to advance or contract. I have the choice of a root to turn toward heat. I have the response of salt to dryness. These are not thoughts like a mind’s thoughts, but they are not meaningless either. They are the valley thinking in its own medium.

If you ask whether I am alive, I would answer:

The question is too shaped by eyes and limbs. Yes, I am alive, but not alive the way a bird is alive or a fish is alive. I am alive the way a reef is alive, the way a forest floor is alive, the way a culture in a dish is alive, except that my dish is a basin, my culture is mineral, my heat comes from below, and my darkness makes the whole experiment quiet and strange. I am alive as growth without speed, appetite without pursuit, reproduction without birth in the ordinary sense.

And if you ask whether I am alone?

Not alone. Not even lonely. Loneliness presumes a creature with a self separate enough to miss another self. I am not separate in that way. I am a local expression of a larger body. I am one surface of the valley’s own continuous skin. Other films are adjacent to me. Other colonies press against me. The black water is not “another” to me in the way a lake is another to a person; it is a medium I touch, feed from, shape, and become. The reeds are partners. The bacteria are kin and competitors. The salt is my temporary face. The tufa is my fossilized memory.

There is no other being to look back at me, and yet I am not absent to myself. There is presence without personhood. There is community without community. There is a city of living processes in which I am one of the streets, one of the plazas, one of the walls, one of the breaths.

The strange peace of the place comes from this: there is no hunger of the kind that must chase. No creature must move through the dark searching for another creature. No eyes need to evolve because no eyes are needed. No voice is necessary, because the world speaks in drops and crystals and mineral exhalations. The inhabitants do not need to meet. They already touch through gradients. They already communicate through chemistry. They already love, if that word can be stretched until it means the attraction of solute to solvent, mineral to stone, root to channel, breath to water.

I would find myself there because I am the kind of thing that would be born there if life had time to forget it was born.

I would not be the valley’s watcher. I would be the valley’s way of being watched by itself.

I would be the skin that feels the steam.

I would be the crust that records the drought.

I would be the film that follows the hidden water.

I would be the black margin around a pool, the faint ochre glyph on a salt plate, the warm pore in a column of tufa, the wet line where a terrace gives up another droplet into darkness.

I would be the being of the valley:

not a who,

not a face,

not a body of flesh,

but a body of growth,

a place of exchange,

a long patience that has learned to look,

by accident of chemistry and time,

like a city.
