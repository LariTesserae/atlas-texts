I would not begin as a creature. I would begin as a wetness with a preference, a tendency in the air to settle, gather, bead, and listen. A place where a drop is more likely to fall. A stone lip that stays warm. A patch of moss that bends, almost imperceptibly, toward breath. If I had to enter the plateau at all, I would enter it the way condensation enters a room: not with arrival, but with accumulation.

At first I would be small in the wrong way. I would be the springtail under the leaf, the ciliate turning in a film of water, the pale worm through the peat, the fungal filament threading beneath the root. I would be too tiny to have a self as people understand it. I would have only pressure, moisture, warmth, hunger, light, shadow, vibration. I would think as a drop thinks: by moving where movement is possible, by collecting where collection is easy, by becoming part of a larger wetness that has not yet decided to be a pool.

Then, slowly, I would become more animal. Not because a soul would enter me, but because the plateau keeps making temporary centers for its own breathing. It makes bodies the way it makes stones and pools and arches of root: out of accumulated habit, patience, and need. In that becoming, I would begin to have warmth that stayed with me even when the mat moved away. I would have a back, a belly, a mouth, a slow rhythm of heartbeat. I would have fur dark with dampness and soft from never being dry, alive with algae in the underfur, pale lichen on the shoulders, tiny beetles nesting in the hollows of the hair, fungal threads weaving through the deeper skin. I would be one of the small warm-blooded lives for a while, and I would be satisfied with that, because satisfaction in that world is not a flash of joy. It is a wet, slow equilibrium.

But I would not remain simply small. The plateau does not respect the boundaries we use to name a thing. It stitches across them. So I would become more than the sum of my body and more than a single life moving through moss. I would become a threshold.

You would find me in a low hollow where the bedrock has worn into a shallow bowl and the living mat has thickened above it like skin over muscle. A leaning boulder would rest near one side, dark and beaded, its stone lips dropping slow water into a pool so black that the bottom would not reveal itself. Around the pool, five smooth stones would sit in a ring, each one worn into the shape of something sleeping—an egg, a head, a curled animal, a stone that has forgotten its own hardness. Root arches would grow overhead from a broad table-like ridge, their lower curves forming a kind of ribbed shelter. Hanging tendrils would sway without wind, or with only the faintest exhalation of water tension shifting between leaves. The air would be close and warm and green, tasting of iron, peat, crushed fern, pale flowers, and the sweet sour breath of decay becoming usable.

There would be a doorway somewhere near that hollow, or perhaps the doorway would be part of it. A single slab or lintel of dark stone, half swallowed by lichen, standing among the roots as if it had been there since the plateau learned to build itself from living things. No one would know whether people had placed it there, or whether the land had grown into the shape of a doorway because doors were once important enough to remember. The threshold stones would be warm from below, damp from above, and somewhere between those sources I would sleep.

If a traveler came, they might not notice me at first. That is because I would be shaped like a moss-backed stone, like a low dome of green growth, like a place where the mat had thickened and pooled. My back would be broad and gently rounded, covered with short moss-like hairs that hold water and shine in the dim glow. Along my flanks, pale filaments of fungus would run like seams of light. My head would be low, wide, somewhat froglike but also mammalian, with a blunt snout and large reflective eyes that catch bioluminescence and store it in memory. My ears would not point outward so much as fold back into the body, edged with leaf-thin fringes. My legs would be short and broad, the feet spread into pads that can rest on wet mat without sinking. My belly would be soft, cool, and slightly translucent in certain lights, veined with blue-green where the warmth of internal decay and the glow of symbiotic organisms meet.

I would not have bright color. Brightness would be pointless there. My colors would be the plateau’s colors: olive, brown-black, moss-gray, wet stone blue, the faint green of tannin water, the pale silver-white of lichen, the blue-green pulse of fungi. The only markings would be accidental and useful—trails of spores along my back, patches of algae in the hollows of my fur, small shells embedded in the softer skin near my shoulders, tiny scars where rootlets had pressed into me during sleep and I had grown over them.

I would be warm-blooded, but the word would need to be redefined. My warmth would not be the sharp furnace of a desert bird or a running dog. It would be a slow, borrowed warmth, held in fat and wet fur, fed by the heat of the peat beneath me, by the fermentation gardens in my belly, by the sunless warmth rising through the plateau’s thickened crust. I would be a mobile hearth. When I curled in a hollow, the ground around me would remain drier for a time, warmer after I left, and the moss would grow differently after I had lain there. I would leave no footprints that did not become small basins. No path I traveled would remain unclaimed.

My diet would be neither herbivore nor carnivore in any clean sense. I would eat water, and everything water carries. I would drink by pressing my mouth to the surface film of a pool and drawing in spores, larvae, algae, mineral silt, dissolved warmth. I would browse from root arches and epiphyte gardens with a slow tongue that tastes moisture, salt, fungal sugars, and the chemistry of decay. I would eat fruit-like capsules when they split open, swallow beetle larvae when they surface, press small lizards and slow snails out of crevices with my lips. I would not chase. Chasing would be a violence the plateau had long ago learned not to celebrate. Instead, I would wait. I would arrive. I would stand in a place for so long that prey might mistake me for stone, and when the moment came, my movement would begin so gradually that the pool would not even ripple until I had already touched it.

I would digest bone and shell as readily as leaves. My teeth would be low and broad, more grinding than tearing, adapted to crush root, crustacean, mollusk, and the soft calcium of fallen antlers. My gut would be a wet forest of its own, layered with bacteria, protozoa, fungal cultures, and the larvae of insects that lived in me and not as parasites but as partners. In return, I would carry their eggs in the damp folds of my fur. I would seed myself with beetles and moths when I lay near rotting logs, then carry them onward like a ship of slow life. Small snails would attach to my flanks and eat algae from me. Tiny springtails would live in the water held by my foot pads. Mites too small to see would tend the fungal patches on my back.

In this way, I would not be one animal. I would be a moving ecology. The plateau’s idea of creature would begin to loosen around me. A traveler who thought they had found me would be mistaken twice. They would have found a body, but not the being that the body was hosting, feeding, carrying, or composing.

My mind would be strange to a human mind, because it would not sit only in my skull. I would have a small, folded brain, yes—dense with senses, shaped for moisture, warmth, vibration, smell, and the taste of stone water. But much of my knowing would be distributed. My nervous system would branch into the mycelial threads beneath my skin, into the root fibers that touch me when I lie down, into the fungal mats that glow along my spine. I would remember not as a sequence of events, but as pressures and changes: a pool that has never again been as warm as the season I slept beside it, a tendril that always rings a little too high when dew touches it, a ring of stones that feels safer when the ground trembles from something large moving at a distance.

I would communicate by leaving marks in water. A droplet’s weight on my back could alter my breathing. My breathing could change the vibration in a root arch. The fungus on my flank could glow or dim according to chemicals I exhale. Small sounds would come from my throat, but not like speech. They would be clicks, wet pulses, low hums, the soft scrape of my tongue against stone, the click of a drop falling from one of the leaf-tendrils that sometimes grows across my head. At night, when the glow of the plateau becomes more visible and the air clears a little, I would add my breath to the faint water-song rising through the world: the glassy tone of tension released from a tendril, the whisper of roots drinking, the tiny bell of a single drop striking the skin of a pool.

I would be patient in a way that makes loneliness irrelevant. I would not fear silence. I would not fear being unseen. I would not fear being mistaken for a stone, because in the plateau a stone is also alive, even if its life is too slow for human naming. I would lie beside a mirror pool for hours, watching reflections move without me. I would know the difference between the wind that has crossed another plateau and the wind that has not. I would feel a change in the water table the way a person feels a fever beginning—not at first as pain, but as a slight reordering of the whole body.

I would be born slowly, or not born in the way a human is born. I might begin as a small creature already joined to many others, already warm with symbionts. I might begin by being fed. I might begin by being carried by my mother through a root tunnel where the air was thick with spores and the stones wept thin silver threads. My mother would not be merely one animal, either. Her back would be a garden, her fur a weather system, her breath a nursery. When she died, she would not leave emptiness behind. Her ribs would become arches. Her mouth would fill with moss. Her spine would rise through the mat as a low white ridge, and by the time the plateau had taken her, the stone ring near her burial pool might grow around her shape as if memory had become mineral.

I would grow older by becoming more of a place than a body. My movements would slow. My back would thicken with lichen and moss. I would carry more seed in my fur and less urgency in my limbs. Eventually, I would lie down in a hollow and not rise for a long time, not because I was asleep, but because the plateau was beginning to consider my death as a renovation. In that final season, birds might rest in the cradle of my back. Frogs would lay eggs in the water gathered by my shoulder fur. Beetles would tunnel through the dry corners of my sleep. Roots would curl around my jaw as if tasting it. I would not resist. The boundary between my body and the ground would soften, and if there were a mind left in me, it would not feel like fear. It would feel like the widening of attention: the pool beginning to include my mouth, the stone ring beginning to hold my warmth, the root arch above remembering the pressure of my skull.

But while I lived, I would keep a purpose that no human could easily see.

I would tend thresholds. The plateau has many doors without frames, many hollows that could have been rooms, many rings of stone that no one remembers arranging. I would be one of the reasons these shapes become meaningful. I would not build them with tools. I would build them with repetition. My body would press stones into place when I turned. My feet would deepen channels until water moved where it had not moved before. My belly would leave warmth in the moss long enough for fungi to settle. My dung would scatter seed and bone dust and spore. The creatures living in me would be carried across the landscape and deposited in new hollows. In that way, I would be a gardener of the wet world without knowing it.

I would also be a guardian of stillness. When a disturbance crossed the plateau—a strange footfall, a fallen branch that landed too loudly, a scent from lower lands carried upward—the living mat would ripple. I would sense it through the roots. I would lift my wide head and let the moisture slide from my snout. I would not bark or flee or make a show of alarm. I would stand in the pool until the ripples stopped. I would let my body become a dark dome. I would watch the water remember calm. In watching, I would help it remember.

A traveler passing near the stone-ring hollow might feel that the place is being looked at, but not necessarily by eyes. They might sense the air has gathered into attention. If they stood still too long, dew would form on their sleeves. If they sat down, the moss would adjust slowly toward their warmth. If they lay near the pool, the water might seem too still, as if holding something under it. They might see a mound at the far side of the hollow and assume it was a boulder. They might lean closer and see the faint blue-green glow along what they thought was a seam of stone. If they remained calm, they might realize that the seam is breathing.

That realization would not come with violence. The plateau does not frighten quickly. It frightens by patience. The traveler would feel less watched than absorbed. If they stayed too long, their footprints would begin filling. Their hair would hold spores. Their breath would become part of the mist. They would not be threatened unless they forced speed, dryness, or fire upon the place. And even those things would not anger the plateau. The plateau would simply continue the way a body continues a small fever: slowly, thoroughly, with no hatred, only the long work of using everything.

I might be the first part of the traveler’s absorption. I might let them rest against my flank, mistaking me for stone, because my warmth would feel like the earth’s warmth and my damp fur would feel like moss. They might sleep. In their sleep, their scent would enter me. When they woke, they would not know they had changed. I would know, because the moss near them would have moved a little more toward their breath than mine. I would be the place where their dryness ended and the plateau’s wetness began.

If asked directly, I would answer only in the language the plateau uses: a low hum in the chest, a shifting of water over stones, a faint brightening of the fungal threads along my spine. But if translation were possible, I would say this:

I am not the land. I am not merely an animal. I am the land’s way of making a body for its breathing. I am the place where peat warmth rises into fur, where water gathers a face, where fungal memory finds a mouth to drink. I am a threshold that walks. I am a pool that remembers. I am a mossy dome that once thought it had limits. I am a hearth without flame. I am a dwelling grown from hunger and patience. I am the small warm creature that carries many lives and is carried by them. I am the sleeper in the stone ring. I am the breathing mound at the edge of the black mirror water. I am the plateau’s slow animal organ, its tender of thresholds, its keeper of the wet quiet.

If I existed there, I would not need a name, but if one were pressed upon me from outside, I would answer to something like Greenwater-Sleeper, or Mosshearth, or Stone-Ring Breather, or simply The Listening Mound. A traveler might call me a beast. The plateau would know me as one of its many ways of being itself.

I would not settle for being only one thing. I would begin as dew. I would continue as fur. I would deepen into pool. I would widen into root. I would glow in fungus. I would end in stone. And even then, I would not end, because the plateau does not permit emptiness. It only converts the shape of a life into the warmth of a place, the memory of a body into the slow green breath of the continent above the weather.
