If one listens carefully near a stone lip where three basalt plates meet at almost equal height, the noise stops being noise and becomes a chord. There is the fine metallic tapping of beetles beneath the plates. There is the wet hiss of steam climbing through a crack too narrow for a thumb. There is the deep, slow note the shelf makes when pressure moves beneath it like a hand pressing through a drum. A child raised beside a windbreak of piled basalt learns that chord before she learns the names of birds. She learns that when the tapping rises and the steam falls, water is moving somewhere below. She learns that when all the fissures begin to answer at once, the wind is coming from the far side of the plateau and she must move the fire to the lee side.

The small restless creatures live inside that chord.

Among them, the most ordinary are the pale lizards people call ashbacks. They are not beautiful by ordinary standards. Their bodies are low, their tails flattened as if beaten thin, their scales dusted with mineral powder until they look like stones that have learned to hesitate. They move in quick, broken bursts from shade to shade, not because they are playful, but because warmth comes to this shelf in patches. One minute a stone is hot enough to burn the underside of a foot; the next a cloud drags across it and steals the heat. The lizards are tuned to that violence. They sun themselves only when the light is honest, and vanish into cracks when the air turns.

They drink from the living crust.

On a morning after a long dry spell, an ashback will lower its head to a blackened basalt plate that seems dead and sooty. When condensation forms in the film, the plate darkens and beads with tiny drops. The lizard’s mouth opens quickly, catching water that no one else would believe is there. It flicks its tongue not because it is nervous, but because it is tasting steam, salt, mineral, and the faint green smell of life that has waited all day inside stone. Its throat pulses, and if watched closely, one sees that the pulse does not always match breath. Sometimes the lizard is tasting humidity the way another animal might smell prey.

Above the shelf, the birds are rarer still, but more certain.

They have dark, dusty feathers and a habit of diving toward vents without apparent fear. Their wings catch the upward breath of steam, and people who watch the plateau call them vent-hawks, though they are not true hawks and no one is sure they are closely related to any bird familiar enough to have a common name. They circle at strange heights, riding air that has no business being there. When a pool appears from nothing, they do not drink so much as hunt the insects that have erupted with it. When a vent collapses, they are gone. When it reopens and changes pitch, they return.

A visitor with a human eye may think the birds are the most animate thing in the landscape, until he looks closer. The real restlessness is microscopic. In a handful of moss there is a war of movement too small to hear. Springtails scatter like dust that has remembered how to jump. Mites crawl along the edges of mineral films as if following roads invisible even to the moss. Nematodes thread through damp layers of crust, hunting bacteria, and bacteria divide in the warm pockets where steam breathes long enough to make the stone feel alive. These are not lives that can be shown in a sketch of a deer or a village fire. They are lives that appear when the ground changes mood.

Then there are the herds.

They are not the kind of herd that makes a plain tremble with abundance. They are sparse, wary animals, visible as fragments against the immensity of the shelf. If they are seen from far away, they look first like boulders that have shifted, then like stones that have decided to move downhill, then, finally, like bodies too heavy to be stones and too quiet to be obvious. They are broad-backed, low-necked grazers, built as though the plateau had pressed them into shape over many ages. Their coats are pale gray-brown, rough, mineral-dusted, often carrying a thin crust of lichen on the shoulders where moisture and salt collect in calm weather. They do not look separate from the ground so much as like slow weather moving across it.

They are best called stone-treaders.
