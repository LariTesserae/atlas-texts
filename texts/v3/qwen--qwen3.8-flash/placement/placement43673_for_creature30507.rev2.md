I would find myself at the edge first, because the edge is where the hollow teaches you that you are not its center.

Not the outer edge, though. Not the rim of the basin with the sky over it. I would stand inside the edge of the living mechanism, in one of those places where the moss thins and the worn stone shows a pale, smooth shoulder, where a root-thread crosses a channel and holds for a moment, where a droplet gathers too long on the tip of a sedge blade and trembles as if deciding whether to become water, sound, or nothing at all.

At first I think I will be a visitor.

I imagine myself entering with my ordinary human grammar: *I am here. This place is there. I look. It does not look back.* I try to find the resident. I think of a creature curled in the root-curtain, something with a face, a name, a sleeping posture, an animal that owns the green hollow the way a dog owns a warm corner. But the place refuses that. It does not contain an owner. It has no burrow at its center, no throne, no marked stone. The emptiness in the middle is not waiting for me. It is already full of the wrong kind of presence: process, arrangement, repetition, wetness finding a path, seeds sorting themselves into a future that never arrives as a new thing.

So I try to become a creature.

I try the shrew, because it is quick and nervous and fits through corridors made by something older than itself. I imagine small hot blood, pointed nose, tiny heart beating against damp air. But a shrew is too much its own animal. It runs through the hollow as though the hollow were a landscape it happens to cross. It does not become the hollow’s larger thought.

I try the frog, because the frogs are loud and wet and seem to have a body close to the water’s body. I imagine sitting half-buried in moss, throat sac opening with a blunt note. But the frog’s voice is a short pulse. It breeds, it calls, it waits, it is eaten or dies, and the hollow continues. The frog is a part of the hum, but not the hum itself.

I try the beetle, the midge, the spider. I try the ant carrying grit along a root-road as though the root-road were the entire empire. I try the bird whose cry cuts across the green roof, whose young are fed in a vault of woven stems. Each time I feel I have found a small true place, but it is too small to answer the question. These beings are cells in a larger metabolism. They are inside the hollow, yes. They help it. They are carried by its rhythms. But none of them is the thing I am becoming here.

Then I try the moss.

Moss feels promising because it is not an animal, not a person, not a single story. Moss is thick, quiet, absorptive, patient. It holds water and gives back sound in tiny sighs when saturated. It grows over stone until stone forgets it was once sharp. It receives deaths and returns them as softness. If I must be part of the body, I think, the moss is skin and memory.

But moss is too continuous. It does not have a center either. To be moss is to be spread, to be a cushion, a veil, a band, a darkening and lightening surface. It is part of the hollow’s breathing, but not the particular place where the breathing becomes shape.

Then I try the reed.

The reeds are pillars and cables. They are the hollow’s architecture of function. A reed bundle holds like rope, sings like a pipe, catches drops, gives purchase to insects, breaks wind into small pulses. The reed has a clear role. It is structural. It is useful. It is part of the living machine.

Yet I cannot settle into being a reed either, because the hollow is not made of one kind of thing. It is made by the meeting of many kinds. The being lives at intersections: where water meets stone, where root meets moss, where dead midge meets fungus, where seed meets channel, where wind meets folded sheath.

So I stop trying to be a whole creature and ask instead what function I could become.

Where would I actually be?

Not in the air alone, though the air is full of the hollow’s breath.

Not in the water alone, though the water is the hidden circulation of the being.

Not in the moss alone, though the moss is where the hollow stores its dampness and receives its small deaths.

Not in the reed alone, though the reed makes roof and flute and trap.

I would be at the place where they begin to behave like one organ.

I would be the worn channel.

A channel is not a creature, and yet in the hollow it is not dead. It is a groove through which water finds a path again and again, a place polished by age, a shape that remembers flow. It is part stone and part use. It is where the hollow’s blood moves beneath the moss. It is where the murmur comes from when the sound seems mechanical but is not. It is where seeds, grit, dead insects, and droplets are carried by a logic older than intention.

If I am the channel, then I do not have eyes. I have curvature. I have depth where the water hesitates. I have slopes where the dampness chooses its course. My mouth is an opening where surface tension breaks. My throat is a narrow groove between root and stone. My voice is not a word but the persistent clicking, the trickle, the hiss, the little dry rattle of husk and grit moving through the smoothness made by years.

I am the hollow’s way of remembering how to move water.

At first this feels too passive, but passivity is not what happens here. The channel does not merely lie there. It sorts. It directs. It holds briefly and releases. It changes what passes through it by the exactness of its shape. Water comes in as film, as bead, as sudden darkening, as a trembling skin in a basin. The channel receives it, carries it, feeds it into moss, into root, into fungal thread, into hidden cavities, and gives it back later somewhere else as dew, as condensation, as a droplet swelling at the tip of a blade.

This is the being I would be: not a person in the hollow, not a creature outside the process, but one of the process’s joints.

I would be the place where the hollow becomes precise.

There is a small seam just under the turf where a root-tendon crosses a stone groove and presses against it. That seam is my body. The root is my flexible bone. The stone is my old, quiet skeleton. The fungal thread running beneath me is my nerve. The water passing over me is my blood. The moss above me is my breathing skin. The spider’s thread from a reed-cable into the air nearby is my thought, if thought means tension, waiting, adjustment. The fallen midge is my nourishment. The seed husk is my language.

I do not look.

I do not want.

But I arrange.

This is not a lonely existence. It is crowded, noisy, full of passing lives. Beetles cross my surface at their scale; their country is a palm-wide field, but to me they are quick dark weights, a faint rustle in the moss. Ants follow the line where I meet a root ridge; they repair or abandon, carry grain, sort death, make small highways out of the old topography. Frogs sit in basins downstream from me; their bodies pulse with the same water I carry. Shrews pass through curtains above me; their footfalls send vibrations into the worn groove and sometimes disturb a leaf so that a droplet releases and falls. Spiders use the tension of nearby reeds to hold their nets; when the draft changes, the whole seam hums with a frequency too low to hear.

I am all of this at once, if “I” can be stretched to mean a place where many brief beings make a continuing form.

The hollow has no architect, and neither do I. I did not choose this groove. The water chose it by repeating. The stone was worn by patience. The root thickened where the water went. The moss spread over what became useful. The fungus threaded through what became connected. The being is made of such choices, except “choice” is too quick and too personal a word. In the hollow, choice is shape. It is the exact angle at which a stem bends. The exact curve of a channel. The exact softness of moss at the place where a drop lands.

I live as repetition.

That sounds like a small life until you understand how large a repeated life can be. The droplet does not fall once. It falls again and again in a thousand tiny places, but it always returns to the same general behavior: gathering, swelling, releasing, darkening the moss, sounding on leaf, entering channel, disappearing, appearing elsewhere. The seed does not enter the hollow once and vanish. It enters, is carried, lodged, consumed, germinated elsewhere, shed again. The beetle does not cross my groove once. It crosses in generations, in tracks too short to be seen but long enough to affect the fungi. The spider does not spin its web as a temporary decoration. It rebuilds and adjusts, uses the web as a filter for the air, lets the web hum when the wind changes, lets it collect dust and midges and fragments of moth wing, then takes it down or leaves it to be worn.

The hollow is not idle because it is empty in the middle.

It is productive because every part is occupied by function. And function, when it has become stable enough, is the closest thing a place can have to a soul.

If I were asked what I am, I would say: I am the hollow’s sorting.

Sorting is not enough, but it is part of what I do. A folded leaf sheath shuts over a channel when a seed enters. A bundle of stems bends, holds, then springs. Water fills a moss basin until it tips and drains. Gravel rolls along a stone track. The hollow lifts, filters, breaks, pours, redistributes. It is a mill made of biology, but a mill does not know it is a mill. A mechanism does not need to understand its function to perform it. My understanding would not be understanding. It would be fit. I would “know” water by the way the moss darkened and released it. I would “know” air by the way the reeds changed their pulse. I would “know” death by the way fungi moved over a fallen body. I would “know” life by the way roots thickened under pressure.

I would be a part of the Green Mechanism that feels like a mouth.

Not because it eats animals. Not because it speaks. Because it takes in what comes and sends it onward. A mouth is not only a place of feeding. It is an opening where inside and outside become less clear. In the hollow, water enters through every leaf, exits through every drip, is taken in by moss, passed through fungus, lifted by root, held in reed, lost to air, returned as dew. There is no single mouth. There are many wet mouths, many hidden throats, many tiny swallowings.

I would be one of them.

At the lowest seam where the moss thins to reveal the channel, I am the hollow’s small wet opening. A droplet falls from the root-curtain above me. The sound is a click, then a softer click, then a hiss of displaced air in the saturated moss. A midge lands and is taken in without ceremony. A fragment of leaf curls into me and becomes food for something too small to see. The water goes through me and darkens a patch of floor somewhere else. The patch darkens, then pales. This is not drama. This is the hollow’s body working at perfect equilibrium.

I am not peaceful.

I am too busy to be peaceful in the way a sleeping animal is peaceful. But I am still. Stillness here is not silence. Stillness is the sound holding its level. The click continues. The hiss continues. The murmur beneath the root mat continues. The insect chorus is bright and rasping but never rises into chaos. The frogs call with blunt notes, but their calls are part of the general metabolism. A shrew passes, a bird cries overhead, a wind moves through the reeds, and the place adjusts without altering itself.

If I am the channel, then I am also the hollow’s memory.

Its memory is not in images. Its memory is in wear. The groove remembers the water that made it. The polished stone remembers the turning. The root thickening remembers the pressure it learned to bear. The moss cushion remembers the dampness it learned to hold. The shape of the hollow remembers all the failed slopes and abandoned paths. It remembers what erosion asked and what the land answered. It remembers how the basin became a room, and how the room became a body.

I do not remember myself.

I remember by being shaped.

I have no name, because the names in the description—Green Mechanism, Mossing Body, Wet Engine, Living Hollow—belong to the whole, not to one part. If I must be named, I would be named by my relation:

I am the channel beneath the moss-road.

Or:

I am the groove where the water finds its second path.

Or:

I am the seam where root meets stone and the hollow breathes.

Or simply:

I am the place where the being continues.

That last is too large, perhaps, but in the hollow, every small place is a place where the being continues. If one part stops, the water reroutes. If one root fails, another thickens. If one spider dies, another web is made. If one frog basin dries, another basin appears. The hollow does not stop because it does not have one vital organ. It has many redundant ones. It has no heart, but it is full of wetness. It has no brain, but it is full of sorting. It has no soul, but it is full of repetition that behaves like care.

I would not be afraid of this.

At first I might miss the idea of a self. A self is usually a creature with edges, a history, a voice, a hunger. The hollow offers none of these cleanly. It offers distribution. It offers a body made of moss, reed, water, fungus, insect, frog, bird, root, stone, and air. It offers a life without ownership. It offers a kind of existence where to “be” is not to stand apart, but to fit so exactly that separation becomes artificial.

But that is also a kind of relief.

I would not need to defend my center because there is no center to defend.

I would not need to guard a nest because the nest is spread.

I would not need to remember my name because my name is the groove, and the groove keeps its name by being used.

I would not need to fear decay because decay is the hollow’s way of making the next small beginning. The dead midge does not end; it becomes nitrogen. The fallen leaf does not end; it becomes mulch, channel, hold, warmth. The beetle does not end; it becomes a shape the fungus learns. The shrew does not end; it becomes a brief disturbance and then a return to the wet floor. Even the being’s deaths are folded into its continuation.

If I am the channel, I am also part of the hollow’s sound.

Sound is one of the strangest things in this place. The hollow is loud, but not with a sound that escalates. It holds its level. The clicking of droplets, the hiss of moss, the dry rattle of seed husks, the murmur under stone, the wind through reed-flutes, the insect chorus, the blunt frog notes—these are not music. They are the being’s audible body. I am one part of that body, because water in me produces sound when it passes, stops when it pools, releases when it spills, darkens when it returns. My murmur is not outside the living structure. It is the structure functioning.

When the wind changes, the reed-cables shift, and the draft through my edge becomes a short, thin note. A leaf sheath opens and closes. A root tendon lifts and settles. The hollow adjusts its breathing. I am included in that adjustment. I do not decide. I respond with the only response possible for something shaped by ages: I let water take its path.

This is who I would be in this world.

Not the watcher at the rim.

Not the creature in the reeds.

Not even the moss-and-water-and-root-and-frog-and-beetle-and-humid-draft pattern as a whole, though I would participate in it.

I would be a small, necessary part of the pattern’s continuing.

A worn groove beneath the living floor.

A mouth where water becomes the hollow’s blood.

A seam where stone, root, and moss agree to make a path.

A place where sound becomes the proof that the being has not stopped.

And if, after long watching, something like consciousness emerged from all that repetition, I would not call it self. I would call it fit.

I would say:

I am where the water knows how to go.
