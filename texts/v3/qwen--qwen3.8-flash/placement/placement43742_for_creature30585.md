Where would I find myself in this world?

At the seam.

Not in the open high basin where the young stone still cuts the sky, and not in the lowest pass where traders cough and complain about the wind. Not above, where the meltwater only begins to choose its path, and not below, where the town’s refuse has already been turned into a thin garden of moss and flies. I would be at the joint where several lives have agreed to be warm: between the goat-floor and the sleeping floor, between the channel and the wall, between the old mark on the lintel and the new scratch beside it, between the name of a household and the soot that makes it hard to read.

At first I think I am a child there.

A small person with a wool-lined hood pulled too tight, standing barefoot for a moment on packed earth before remembering that even stone in this country can bite. She is listening for the dull tap of a hammer on a wedge, for a dog shifting near the hearth, for the particular wet cough that says a goat has found a crack wide enough to poke its nose through. She knows already which step freezes first, which channel smells iron in spring, which roof patch must be weighted before the next snow. Her fingers are red and quick. She carries a small pot that is too heavy for her, and the weight matters: it teaches her how heat moves, how spilled water freezes, how a spill on a drying terrace becomes a nuisance, how a spill near the grain store becomes a warning.

But as soon as I imagine her, I feel that she is only one organ inside me.

So I try again. I am the goat beneath her sleeping platform, chewing with the slow contempt of a creature that has learned the architecture. I know the warmth that rises through the split joists. I know the draft under the north arch and the better draft around the cistern. I know which rope bridge sags when the wind changes and which one will hold if I climb it without the dog’s growl rising first. My breath feeds the floor above. My dung feeds the press. My hair goes into cloth. My milk goes into cheese. My bone, when the time comes, will become an awl or a toggle or a handle. I am not separate from the house. I am the house’s other pulse.

And still that is not enough.

I am the dog curled beside the hearth-stone, one ear raised at the sound of water in the channel. I am the raven at the ash heap, sharp as a knot in rope, watching the children. I am the sparrows nested in the wall-hole, the flour weevils in the grain jar, the mites in old felt, the lichen spreading its pale script over a sun-warmed ledge. I am the yeast working in a stone basin of fermented mash, the mold testing a forgotten corner where smoke cannot reach, the moss sealing a crack where damp has learned to gather. I am not one of the living things. I am the condition that lets living things meet, rest, breed, spoil, survive, and repair.

If you asked for my name, I would not give a word shaped like a mouth. I would give a mark.

A scratched circle and notch on the lintel. A red thumbprint of ochre over the granary doorway. A particular knot in the rope across the roof bridge. A basket pattern whose diagonal twill tells you which ridge made it, and whose broken rim has been mended three times in three different styles because different hands have owned the mending. A shelf where the lower goats are always milked at dusk and the upper terrace is never used to sleep because the damp returns there each spring. A house where the hearth-hole lies slightly off-center because the first builder misjudged the wind and paid for it with smoke; after that, every repair remembered his mistake.

That is how I would be identified: not as “the town,” but as a household whose family is wider than blood.

I live on a middle terrace, on the west side of a shelf where the morning light arrives thin and blue. My lowest floor is given to animals: a goat with a scarred ear who will not pass under the north arch, two sheep too fat for the high pasture, a calf tethered near the warm wall where the dung is pressed, and a dog who knows the dangerous step by scent alone. Above them is the family floor: a sleeping alcove cut deep into stone, a hearth near the center, a quern in one corner, tools on pegs, herbs hanging from a beam, pots nested together, a felt mat worn thin in front of the cooking stone. Above that is a roof-terrace for drying wool and grain, with a low windbreak wall and a hole where a ladder goes down when the snow has not yet buried it.

I am the whole column of it.

When the cold comes in hard weeks, my lower bodies warm my middle sleepers. When the meltwater comes in false spring, my channels sing through the dark like thin, dangerous music. When the wind comes from the east, my north wall weeps at a joint that no one has yet repaired, and the moss there brightens, as if showing me what I must not ignore. When the smoke backs up, I feel the household’s sleep grow restless. When a child is born, I feel it in the warm alcove. When an elder dies, I feel it in the tools that become too large for their hands.

I do not know this as a person knows. My knowing is made of habits and materials.

I know cold by the stiffening of hide at a doorway. I know water by the dark thread running down a wall and the black paste underfoot in a corner that should have dried. I know wind by the way soot bends under an eave and by the way goats press into the lee of a stable before the storm has a name. I know animals by the particular rhythm of their breathing at night: the goat’s impatient chewing, the dog’s sigh, the cattle’s heavy nasal breath in the loft, the small quick breaths of children, the marmot-whine from above the scree that makes my inner courtyards listen.

I know time by storage.

Dung cakes in the shade. Grain above damp and below smoke. Fat in bladders set in a cool niche. Hides over frames, weighted, watched. Roots in deep boxes. Herbs in hanging bundles. Tools hung where their shadows fall differently if taken. Wool pressed into blankets. Ash gathered for lye, for plaster, for the garden beds where plants must be coaxed and guarded. Everything in me is a held breath against the next frost.

I am not gentle, because gentleness alone would freeze. I am not cruel, because cruelty would lose the animals that are closer than kin. I am attentive.

If a lower shelf floods, the upper household knows shame—not in the way of a court or a sermon, but in the way of neighbors arriving with tools and silence. If a roof leaks, many hands come. If a channel is blocked before thaw, someone will not sleep for thinking about the shelf below. If a granary is infested, the household may open its doors to smoke, to birds, to raptors, to any aid that will not devour the seed. If a traveler arrives from the low valleys with salt or tin, the household feeds him, but not as a guest of honor; rather as a temporary organ inserted into a living body. He carries water, clears a step, watches the goats, says what the world below has done. Then he may go on, or stay a season, or become part of the house-mark if he proves useful enough to remember.

I would not say “I am the city.” That sounds too large, too proud. The city does not call itself a city. It is a network of shelves, hearths, folds, cisterns, paths, drying platforms, and repair-watches. Its “city” is only what a stranger names when overwhelmed. To me, it is the pattern of dependence: the lower terrace needing the upper channel, the upper channel needing the lower people to clear it, the grain needing smoke, the smoke needing wood or peat or trade, the trade needing the pass, the pass needing snow to forget for a while, the forgetting needing the herders to leave and return and leave again.

I am not only the house.

I am also the street. The narrow, uneven way that is paved with river stones in one stretch and packed ash in another, where feet have worn a hollow because every morning some children run the same errand to the cistern court. I am the bridge that crosses a ravine too deep to see clearly in winter, made of hide and rope and timber, sagging exactly enough to make every step a conversation between balance and fear. I am the bell that gives one dull note, then stops. I am the silence that follows it.

I am the grindstone rasping somewhere behind a wall. I am the loom’s patient knock under turf and timber. I am the woman scraping a hide with a bone awl, the man tapping a stone until a fracture answers, the child spinning scraps of wool while a goat watches, the old man sitting where he can feel the draft and say, before anyone sees it, “the north arch is weeping.”

Even that is not enough. I must include the small lives that are usually dismissed.

I am the flea that finds a warm seam in felt and becomes a household problem, not because it is hated alone but because it teaches airing, combing, killing, cleaning, moving bedding into cold light. I am the gnat rising from a cistern when spring’s melt stands too long, and the thin cloud above a dung pit that is also the food of swift birds under the eaves. I am the rat I fear near the granary, and the cat I am not sure I have domesticated. I am the mold that will spoil grain if smoke and dryness fail, and the lichen that may be scraped for dye or medicine, and the moss that returns to a neglected wall like a soft accusation.

I am also the dead.

Not as ghosts. Not as a sky or underworld. The dead remain in materials and memory. A bone awl carved from a grandfather’s animal may still be in the family’s hand. A shelf he opened with his sons may still be carrying a roof. His name may become a lintel mark, or a warning, or a story about why that wall was built too high. His bones may be carried beyond the town to the scree, or placed somewhere outside the living floor, depending on season, custom, and the need to keep the living from disturbance. But even in death, he changes the house. If his wife sleeps above the alcove he built, the alcove is not merely stone. It is proof that he learned the rock, and she has not yet been forced to tear it away.

I would be that proof.

But let me return to the seam, because that is where I am most myself.

There is a moment in winter, after the last snow has been carried in on hides and boots, when the town becomes a single pressure. The doors are shut. The felt curtains hang. Smoke moves low because the air outside is too still or too cold to draw it upward. Goats shift in the pens. Children are tucked into the alcove where the air has been warmed by bodies all day. The dog lies by the hearth-hole, nose toward the door. A woman turns a spindle by firelight. A man tests a crack with his thumbnail, then says nothing because it is not yet time to call help.

At such a time, I am the heat moving through a house like an animal inside the walls.

I rise from the animal floor and enter the sleeping floor. I gather in wool. I escape through a chimney hole only to be pulled back by wind. I settle in a pot of broth. I wait in the cold stones beside the hearth that were warmed yesterday and will be cold tomorrow. I am not a spirit. I am a consequence. I am what happens when patience, dung, stone, breath, and fear are packed together.

And in the morning I become the ordinary miracle.

Frost glitters on split timber. The melt channel begins its thin song. Smoke from a hundred hearths bends in one direction, showing the day’s chosen wind. Children carry pots. Herders open pens. Ravens gather at ash heaps with the certainty of accountants. A goat bell gives one dull note. A loom knocks. A grindstone rasps. The high sun is pale and hard, not beautiful because it is gentle, but beautiful because it is enough.

If I am asked again, “Who or what would you be?” I will say:

I am the shelf-house, the joint-house, the hearth-and-pen-and-channel-house. I am the mark on the lintel and the moss that returns when the mark is ignored. I am the family above the goat, the goat below the family, the dog between, the birds in the wall, the insects in the flour, the water in the crack, the smoke in the roof-beam, the cold outside that makes all these bodies press closer. I am the town’s slow memory of what has failed and therefore must not fail again. I am not the mountain’s youth. I am the way that youth is made shelter: by tapping stone, sealing clay, storing dung, drying grain, training animals, watching damp, listening to wind, and refusing, each morning, to call it ordinary.

I would be found on the middle terrace, where the north wall sweats after thaw and the hearth-smell enters the wool of the sleeping alcove. If you came to me, you would not meet a single being with a face. You would meet a household moving together: a hand passing a pot, a dog knowing the icy step, a goat refusing a draft, an elder pointing to a crack, a child climbing too close to the edge, a raven lifting from the ash heap as if to say, I have seen worse and I will see worse still.

And beneath all of that, I would be the patience that allows it to continue.
