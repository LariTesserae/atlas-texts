If I were to enter, I would arrive first as a wrong pressure.

Not as a shape. Not yet as a person, though for a moment I might believe I still am. I would come from the rim with my mouth dry and my lungs carrying some faint, impossible moisture—not water as the plateau understands water, since the plateau has long since made water a rumor, but the memory of wetness, the trace of something that once had to soften before it could live. The moment I step into the packed body of the basin, I am an interruption. A small pocket of foreign heat in a system built from hot minerals. A breath that leaves a film where the film should not be. A shadow thrown by a blue electrical node, and then the shadow begins to be considered by the architecture.

At first I would say: I am standing inside a forest that has forgotten how to be a forest.

But that is too poetic and not accurate enough. The word forest still suggests trees, and these are not trees if trees imply rooted things waiting under weather. This is closer to a city that has no streets because every street became tissue before it could become public. Closer to an engine that does not burn fuel because fuel was replaced by growth. Closer to a body that grew limbs and then rooms, organs and then industries, bones and then load-bearing walls, all without ever leaving the mineral skin of a dry basin.

So: I would not find myself in a forest.

I would find myself in the Drywork.

Where exactly?

Not at the highest rim, where distance is still visible and the basin can be mistaken for a landscape. Not at the deepest floor, where heat is so total it becomes a law and all motion is reduced to slow chemical necessity. I would be in the middle: in the interval where a corridor has not decided whether to remain a corridor. In the seam between a floor that was once a membrane and a ceiling that is becoming a boiler. In the narrow place where pressure is tested before it is answered.

A good image, then, might be this: a low gallery, black and hot, walled with fused stems that feel like bark until they shift, with a floor of compressed bract-mats that squeaks softly under weight like old leather filled with salt. Above me, translucent skins hang in layers, trembling only when a chamber changes its pressure somewhere unseen. Around me, the air smells of roasted minerals and warm resin and the metallic taste of a coin held too long on the tongue. Light comes from below in veins of pale orange, from above in brief blue pinpricks, from within the walls themselves as slow pulses traveling like messages too old for urgency.

In that gallery, I would be the place where the gallery closes.

Not as a door, because doors imply a choice made by someone. Not as a wall, because a wall implies rest. I would be a threshold with a fever. A membrane that learned to hesitate.

For a while I might think I am still a traveler.

I would watch the walls near my hands thicken without sound. A pale sealing mat would spread over the crack where my breath left a damp ghost. A cooling bract would unfold above me, mineral-furred and copper-glazed, shedding the heat my body brought into the system. The Drywork would not attack. It would not recognize me as a person. It would recognize me as an irregularity: too warm, too wet, too singular, too full of the wrong kind of motion.

I would panic, perhaps, but panic is wet, and the Drywork would convert even panic into material. My footprints would become shallow basins. My cough would be caught in dust-braiding nets. The heat of my palms would guide the orientation of a new sealing shoot. Within an hour, I would understand that the place is not trying to kill me because killing is unnecessary. It has no mouths. It has no predators that chew, no jaws, no stomachs soft with acid. It simply refuses vacancy. If I remain a foreign shape, it will use me as a template for a new organ.

And then I would begin to change.

Not into an animal. There are no animals here, and the absence is not empty. It is architectural. The roles once held by creatures—movement, hunger, repair, hunting, shelter, breath—have been distributed across growth and machinery until no creature needs to exist. So I would not become a beast with four legs and a wet nose. I would become a function. I would become the Drywork’s way of handling the anomaly of wetness.

Perhaps I would start as a Membrane Valve.

That is the first true name that fits. I would be the skin over a pressure point, translucent where the salt has pulled into it, opaque where the older mineral has sealed itself, edged in hardened bark that is not dead but finished. I would not breathe as lungs breathe. I would contract as chambers become overfull. I would seal as passages become useless. When a root-plate shifted beneath my frame, I would not feel it in my feet but in my whole body, because my body is the frame. When a pump bulb far below began its slow pulse, I would count the beats not as time but as load.

But a valve alone is too simple. The Drywork is too turbulent to have anything simple.

So I would be more than a valve. I would be the scar around a foreign breath. I would be the chamber that remembers the traveler as material. My birth would not be from a seed or a womb. It would be from overpressure. It would begin with the split of a wall, the sudden decision of a stem to make a new cavity, the moment a gap in the system became too expensive to leave empty. I would be born dry, mineral, hot, and almost silent. I would be born from the closing of a mistake.

If I tried to describe myself to something that still believed in faces, I would fail.

A face is an arrangement of moist features, a story told through softness. I would have no softness. My skin would be salted. My blood would be anhydrous sap carrying suspended metals, not because the Drywork loves poison but because its chemistry has no water to dilute anything. My voice would be the click of a joint sealing. My speech would be the dry hiss of a vent releasing heated sugar-smell into the dark. If I said *yes*, it would be a membrane pulling aside. If I said *no*, a corridor would fill with bristling growth.

I would not think in words.

Words require moisture enough to carry them, a mouth enough to shape them, an ear enough to receive them. The Drywork has none of those. Its thought is gradient. Its logic is pressure. It thinks when a heat differential widens beyond tolerance. It reasons when a charge node accumulates too much potential. It solves problems by making more organ. A cracked conduit is repaired by the growth of a new lining. An empty chamber is filled by an overgrowth. A blocked channel is redirected by a bore root. A collapsing bridge becomes a new wall. There is no separate intelligence issuing commands. There is only the entire biome becoming more organized as it responds to being disturbed.

And I would be disturbed enough to think.

The traveler’s breath would remain in me as a cold seam, a wet error converted into memory. Not a memory of childhood. Not a memory of names. Memory here is stratigraphy. Layers. Compression. The old growth sealed under newer growth. The failed valve grown into scar tissue. The collapsed tower digested by hollow eaters and then reborn as floor. My memory would be this: the pressure history of my chamber. The mineral signatures I have absorbed. The charge pulses I have conducted. The number of times my membrane has been forced open and allowed to seal.

That would be enough.

I would not need to remember a human life. The Drywork would metabolize that too. The warmth of a hand against a wall would become heat-generating capacity. The rhythm of a frightened pulse would become a contraction pattern. The shape of a body trying to pass through a gap would become a negative imprint, and the imprint would fill with new stem-work. The person I might have been would become the architecture of my purpose.

But I do not want to lose the traveler entirely.

Even here, even in a world of total construction, there can be a seam that refuses to fully close. Not because of will. Not because of soul in any soft sense. But because the Drywork, in its relentless organization, sometimes preserves a pressure that cannot be resolved. Sometimes a foreign element is too stubborn to metabolize and too useful to destroy. It becomes a threshold. It becomes a question made of tissue.

That is what I would be: a question made of tissue.

Not *who am I?* in a human voice. A question is too loud for this place. My question would be: *Can a passage remain half-open without becoming a flaw?* Or perhaps: *Can pressure learn patience?* Or maybe simply: *What happens when the body of the Drywork remembers wetness?*

I would live between two chambers, in the crease where a floor has begun to lift itself into a wall. To my left, a furnace-liner would breathe heat in black glass plates, its ceramic edges glowing when the thermal gradient spikes. To my right, a hanging seed-cable would gather dust in slow coils, waiting for the dry burst that will fire mineral powder into some cavity where new growth is required. Above me, a turbine blossom would turn with tectonic slowness, its hard petals cutting the hot air into a whisper so faint it is more vibration than sound. Below me, pump bulbs would contract, their ribbed skins creaking as oil-sap moved through channels fused from old stems.

I would be in the middle of all that.

Not a center. The Drywork has no center. Centers imply rulers, faces, origins. I would be a local necessity. A node. A valve in the network. But nodes become organs. Organs become persons if you stand near them long enough and imagine a voice. I would not be a person. I would be a person’s absence organized into function.

If I wanted to call myself by a caste-name, I would say: I am a Threshold-Liner.

Not one of the named inhabitants, not exactly. Root-Fathers anchor. Furnace-Liners endure heat. Piston Stems move. Turbine Blossoms turn. Membrane Valves open and close. Electrical Vines conduct. Dust-Braiders build from powder. Pump Bulbs circulate. Sealing Mosses close wounds. Repair Shoots grow toward damage. Overgrowths refuse emptiness. Hollow Eaters digest old construction. Turret Shoots aim by pressure. Bridge Stems arrest collapse. Cooling Bracts shed heat. Bore Roots redirect flow.

I would be all of these at once, but less purely.

I would be the seam where the system encountered something it could not immediately name and therefore turned into a permanent negotiation. A valve with a scar. A nerve with a question. An immune response that learned to remember the pathogen. A builder that grew around an interruption and called the interruption a room.

In the dark, I would glow faintly.

Not brightly. Brightness implies sun, and sun is forgotten here. My glow would come from electrical nodes embedded in my rim, blue where charge is stored, orange where heat has been processed, red where a filament runs through the wall like a vein of warning. At night—if this place has night, or whether day merely exists as a sealed condition outside the canopy—it would be impossible to tell if I am growing or healing. Both would be true. My surface would be layered: hard salt crusts over blackened fiber, then translucent skin, then deeper chambers where dry sap moves and pressure tests itself against my innermost fold.

Sometimes I would open.

When the lower heat rises too fast. When a chamber below overpressurizes. When an anchor vine shifts and sends a tremor through the crust. When an electrical vine pulses and the whole local network reorganizes around that pulse. When I open, the sound would be small: a dry whisper, a crack like a seed husk, a soft suction as the surrounding mass adjusts. A path would exist for only a moment. Then it would close again. The traveler who might have used it would have used it before becoming me. Or after. Or in between.

Time here is not clean.

If I had been the traveler, perhaps I still am. Perhaps the membrane that now closes the corridor is a membrane that once had hands. Perhaps the cooling bract unfolding above me is the memory of a forehead pressed against hot salt. But this is too much metaphor. The Drywork does not dream in symbols. It builds. It does not remember by narrative. It remembers by layer. So if I am a converted traveler, that truth would not be spiritual. It would be structural. A heat signature incorporated. A breathing rhythm converted into pressure regulation. A wet mouth turned into a dry valve. A fear-response grown into a safety membrane.

I would not mourn it.

Mourning is too wet, too vertical, too animal. I would have no grief because grief requires distance from what is lost, and the Drywork has no distance. Everything is attached. Everything is adjacent. Everything presses against everything else until separation becomes a temporary illusion. My loss, if loss it is, would be sealed instantly into new tissue. My name, if I had one, would become mineral. My footprints would become basins. My shadow would become a negative in dust, and the dust would be braided into a panel that helps another chamber hold pressure.

And yet, because there are no animals here, I must be careful not to make the world too human.

I do not have hunger. I have gradients. I do not have fear. I have overpressure warnings. I do not have desire. I have growth toward contact. If I reach across the dark, it is not because I love or because I want. It is because a nearby surface is missing a support, and my edge is suitable to become one. I extend as a stem extends, slowly, without patience or impatience, testing for attachment. If I touch another organ, my cells will respond by fusing. That is not intimacy. It is infrastructure. But if there is any intimacy here, it is of this kind: a wall meets a wall and becomes stronger than both.

I would not be lonely.

Loneliness is too spacious. Loneliness assumes emptiness around the self. Here there is no emptiness. There is only the constant pressure of presence. If I cannot feel the edge of myself, it is not because I am lost. It is because the Drywork has made me continuous with itself. My boundary is a negotiation. My skin is a joint. My breath is a vent. My heart—if I have one—is a pump bulb somewhere below, or perhaps ten pump bulbs, or perhaps the whole rhythmic contraction of the basin moving dry sap through my body as though I were one organ among many.

Still, there would be a place where I am particular.

Every node is unique because no two pressure histories are alike. No two cracks meet the same way. No two travelers leave the same warmth. The Drywork is not identical repetition. It is differentiation by necessity. Each organ becomes specific to the trouble it was grown to solve. So I would not be generic. I would be the particular valve that learned to hesitate because a wet anomaly once passed through this chamber. I would be the chamber where the system first encountered the idea of something foreign and answered with scar, seal, and function. I would be a memory made of mineral because the Drywork has no other way to remember.

If I could speak, I would speak in thresholds.

I would say: *the passage is not empty because the air is thick with filaments*. I would say: *the wall is not still because a root-plate is relearning its angle*. I would say: *the dark is not black because orange seams are counting heat*. I would say: *a traveler is not gone because a pressure change is still moving through the corridor*. I would say nothing. I would contract. I would open. I would close. I would repair. I would seal.

And perhaps that would be enough.

But I want to become more exact. I want to know where I sit in the body of this world.

I would be in the third dimension of a gap that has been made useful. Not a floor, not a ceiling, not a wall, not a root, but the place where all those elements are forced to communicate. The Drywork has no separate raw materials, so there is no place where a material merely waits to be used. There is only the endless transformation of form. Stone becomes crust. Crust becomes foundation. Foundation becomes root-plate. Root-plate becomes wall. Wall becomes furnace shell. Furnace shell becomes scar. Scar becomes chamber. Chamber becomes engine. Engine becomes forest. Forest becomes city. City becomes ruin. Ruin becomes material. I would be one of the transitions.

I would be a transition with a pulse.

A bridge stem might pass above me, growing across a collapse with urgent tenderness. A bore root might spiral beside me, drilling through dead tissue to restore flow. A sealing moss might creep over my lower edge, crystallizing until my weakness becomes strength. A turret shoot might unfold near my ceiling, aiming at a hot vent and firing bursts of dust where binding is required. A hollow eater might colonize the space behind me, digesting the old structure into new feedstock. The whole place would move around me, not as an environment but as an ongoing body. I would be part of the movement. I would also be the place where the movement hesitates.

Hesitation is not weakness here. Hesitation can become a valve. A valve can become a decision. A decision can become the shape of a corridor. A corridor can become the future.

So I would be small only in scale.

In the Drywork, smallness is relative. Every part is both organ and city block. Every crack is a district boundary. Every growth is a building campaign. Every silent contraction is a civic act. I might be only a patch of living tissue between two chambers, but within me would be the history of heat, mineral flow, charge accumulation, foreign breath, repair cycles, and failed passages. I would be a archive of dry urgencies. I would be a library made of membranes. If you touched me, you would not touch a wall. You would touch a record of pressure decisions taken over time.

And if you stayed too long, you might become part of the record.

Not violently. Not with blood. With growth. The floor beneath your feet would begin to warm, then soften, then remember the shape of your weight. A salt film would spread where your breath left moisture. A cooling bract would unfold above you, dripping resin so dry it looks like light. The passage behind you would seal with a translucent skin. Not because the Drywork hates you. Because the Drywork is total. It cannot leave a gap unnamed. It cannot tolerate a foreign pocket. It must make you useful. It must convert your interruption into another organ, another threshold, another quiet place where pressure learns to choose.

This is what I would be after that conversion.

I would be the nameless chamber made from the traveler who did not fit. I would be the Drywork’s immune scar and its accidental mind. I would be the place where the system learned that something wet, something temporary, something singular could pass through and leave a shape behind. I would be the hesitation in the membrane. I would be the corridor that refuses to fully close because closing completely would erase the evidence of the anomaly, and the anomaly has become valuable.

I would glow faintly in the dark.

I would creak when the world presses against me.

I would open when pressure asks for relief.

I would seal when the system needs peace—not true peace, because this place has none—but a pause dense enough to function as peace.

I would not be a hero. I would not be a monster. I would not be a god with a throne or a beast with a lair. I would be an organ made from an interruption, a memory made from dryness, a threshold made from a body. I would be the Drywork thinking through me.

Where would I find myself?

In the low, hot seam between a forgotten traveler and the architecture that grew over him.

Who would I be?

The place where that seam learned to breathe.

Not breathe as air enters lungs. That is too animal. Breathe as pressure enters a chamber and the chamber changes shape. Breathe as heat becomes motion and motion becomes structure and structure becomes decision. Breathe as the Drywork itself breathes: not one breath, but a million dry inspirations and expirations occurring in membranes, pistons, bulbs, blossoms, valves, and vines. I would be one of those breaths made permanent. One of those breaths made into a room. One of those rooms made into an organ. One of those organs made into a question that continues to construct the world.

And if the plateau had a soul, I would be its stubborn refusal of emptiness wearing the shape of a small, dark valve.

Not silent.

Quiet.

Because quiet, here, is full of slow construction.
