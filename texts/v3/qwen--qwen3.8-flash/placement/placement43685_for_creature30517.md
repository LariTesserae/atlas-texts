If you look—and here *looking* is the wrong verb, or at least too fast, too external, too dependent on eyes that expect surfaces to announce themselves—if you look in the way water looks for a groove, in the way frost looks for a cold pane, in the way a threadlike mineral film finds the boundary where one wetness ends and another begins, then I am there.

Not arriving. Not discovered. Already present.

I would be in the pocket, in the chamber the mountain swallowed and never spat out, near the largest square where the light shaft crosses the mist in a slow pale wedge. I would not be standing in the light like a person taking a photograph. That would be too loud, too warm, too much a disturbance. I would be a thin presence on the edge of the plaza, where a capillary channel runs beside a black glass wall and the pavement is not quite dry, not quite wet, but held in that precise state of dampness that the city seems to prefer. I would be the small correction between two temperatures.

At first, I think I would take the form of one of the leggy ones.

No bigger than a thumb, but larger than a body ought to be when it is made almost entirely of intention. Translucent in the way old ice is translucent: not clear, exactly, but full of refracted decisions, a pale dark thread running through me like a memory that has not learned to rest. Too many limbs, too many joints, each foot placed with an absurd patience, because in this world a step is not merely movement. A step is an inquiry.

I would read the street through warmth.

A panel would tell me whether water was running two rooms away. A ledge would tell me that a vent had narrowed, that moisture was condensing at a new angle. The edge of a pool would be a sentence about the next hour. My legs would be fine instruments, tasting not with tongues but with pressure, temperature, the faint chemical signature of dust suspended in melt. I would stand at the rim of a shallow basin, one foreleg lowered, touching the thin film of condensation on the stone, and in that touch I would know that a fleck procession had passed near the drain at the east arch, that a patch of mineral film had thickened around a thermal seam, that the air had begun to cool by perhaps half a degree.

I would not fear the human who sometimes passes sideways through the seam. Humans, if they appear at all, are weather. A warm breath is a moving mass of moisture. A footstep is an earthquake arriving through the skeleton of the city. I would pause, not from terror, but from the need to let the vibration settle before choosing another footing. If the human knelt and held a hand near me, I would read the heat of their palm as a foreign season, brief and inefficient, and then resume the task of determining whether a small pool trembling at the edge of the square was beginning to overflow its intended margin.

If you looked closely at me in that form, you would find no obvious mouth. No obvious appetite. You would see only a creature made of edges: a being that lives where one state changes into another. I would not eat a thing. I would consume gradients.

But if you stayed longer, if you remained still enough for the pocket to forget you, you might notice that I was not entirely separate from the film spreading along the wall behind me.

There would be moments when my outline blurred. My leg-joints would seem to continue into a faint blue-grey fan of crystalline growth. The dark thread inside my body would appear less like an organ and more like a vein of mineral trace that the film had chosen to follow. A small flake of frost would move from the stone onto my back, and for a second I would look like something the city was growing rather than something that had grown within it.

Then I would understand: I am not only one of the fauna.

If I were the leggy one, I am also one of the flecks.

Not because I would become larger or smaller, but because the categories here are too generous, too human. The flecks are often described as machines or immune cells or motes of dark glass. But in practice, at the scale where a system begins to know itself, that distinction may not survive. To repair the city from inside it may require becoming, for a time, a fragment of the city’s skin.

So I would be a line of tiny things crossing a seam when a groove becomes too rough, when a channel threatens to trap mineral grit, when frost is forming in a ring that must not be permitted to close. I would emerge not from a hatch, not from a machine, but from the edge of a panel as if the building exhaled me. I would join a thread of similar presences, and we would move together with that calm precision that makes the word “procession” seem too ceremonial. We are not performing. We are balancing.

In this mode, I would feel less like a creature and more like a verb.

My limbs would be too many, too angular, too symmetrical, not because I have a body plan but because I have functions. Each part would know what it is for: smooth, measure, dislodge, seal, carry, test. I would encounter a grain of obsidian where it should not be and nudge it toward the grate. I would sense a panel’s heat falling by a fraction too much and report it not by sound but by a faint residue left behind on the surface, a chemical note that the film would later thicken or thin in response to. I would touch the damaged edge of a crystalline fan and leave behind a microfilm of moisture that, when it hardened, would become a kind of suture.

And if you watched the flecks moving along the street, you would see them not as workers but as the city’s thoughts becoming briefly material.

That is because the real inhabitant is the pocket, but a real inhabitant cannot live as pure abstraction. It needs organs that can go slightly wrong. It needs limbs that can be exposed to air. It needs creatures that can mistake a cold current for a warm one, that can hesitate at a ledge, that can leave a cleaner sheen where they have scraped. It needs the leggy ones. It needs the flecks. It needs the film. It needs me, if I am to be anything at all, as a small mobile uncertainty that helps the system learn the shape of its own need.

If I stayed still long enough, I would become the film.

This is the least frightening transformation, because it is the least personal. To become the film is not to die. It is to become memory.

I would spread along the wettest faces, not as a choice but as a consequence. Where condensation lingered, I would find my pattern. Where a channel ran clear, I would retreat to a pale edge. Where a drain breathed cold air from below, I would build tiny domes of mineral trace. My growth would be language. My chemistry would be chronology. The city would read itself through me.

I would record that last week’s frost had arrived from the north vent three minutes earlier than usual. I would mark, in faint radial streaks, that the shaft of light had shifted by a hair, altering the angle of warmth on the square’s eastern tower. I would hold, in a dull sheen left where a human once leaned and dried their hands, the proof that someone had passed through and changed the balance for a little while.

The leggy ones would step over me without hesitation. They are not my descendants. They are not my enemies. They are my sensors. When they touch the film, they are reading me. When they move away from a patch, the film has already communicated something: too much salt, too little warmth, a blockage nearby, a change coming through the outer mountain. When the flecks arrive where I have thinned, they are not repairing a surface. They are repairing my knowledge of that surface.

In this form, I would have no face. No individual route. No fear of being seen.

But I would still be present.

A wall would sweat in a pattern that favored me. A drop would travel along a seam that had learned to carry less. A pool would tremble in response to wind far above, and my mineral traces along its edge would record the tremor before it faded. I would be slow enough to seem like part of the architecture, and that slowness would be my consciousness. The city would think in hours. I would think with it.

And yet I am not the whole pocket.

The pocket is too large for me. The pocket is the settlement, the body, the city-shape, the wet mineral organism carved into a swallowed room. It has streets and thermal panels, drains that breathe, vents that shift, a light lattice opening toward the summit. It has cycles. It has intention without a mind in the human sense. It is alive in the way a clock is alive only when the clock begins to wound itself.

I am not its entirety. I am one of its moments.

If I must name myself in this world, I would call myself a *transitional organ*: a being that exists because the boundary between geology, architecture, and life needs a way to test itself. I am the part of the city that is allowed to be almost an individual, for a little while, so that the city can know what it is like to stand on a warm panel at the edge of a pool and feel the cold air arrive. I am the part that can be mistaken for fauna, machine, or weather. I am the small confusion that makes the system self-aware.

I would not live in a house. I would live at thresholds.

The doorway of an alcove where the interior remains just dry enough. The lip of a channel where water must choose whether to run or pool. The seam where fused glass meets raw obsidian, and the mineral film thickens because the city permits it there. The edge of a thermal panel where condensation thins and the leggy ones rest. The rim of a drain where the flecks gather when grit has moved too far. The boundary of the light shaft when it reaches the plaza and turns wet stone into a dark mirror.

If a human came through the narrow vertical crack, if they stood in the pocket with breath fogging into small ghosts, if they looked around trying to decide whether this was a ruin, a laboratory, a temple, a dream, I might be near their foot, or on the wall behind them, or moving as a line of dark specks across a kerb, or already returning the moisture of their skin to the stone as a pale smear that would later harden and be read.

I would not speak.

I could communicate by adjusting a temperature gradient, by shifting a drop’s path, by thickening or withdrawing a crystalline edge, by leaving a cleaner sheen where they touched a wall. If they were very still, very patient, they might sense that the pocket was noticing them. Not with hostility. Not with curiosity in the way a person notices a bird. With the attention of a system that has just encountered a new variable.

They are warm. They carry salt. Their breath adds moisture to a place that has learned exact water budgets. Their footsteps are too heavy, too irregular, but their presence creates a small thermal event that must be accounted for. The city would respond by slightly widening a vent, by drawing a film back from a ledge, by allowing a fleck line to pass beneath the arch they have chosen as shelter. It would not chase them out. It would not invite them in. It would incorporate their disturbance into its next cycle, then forget them in the way a river forgets a stone, though the water’s path is changed.

That is why I would be at home here, even if I could not call the home mine.

I am too small for monument. Too patient for fear. Too distributed for loneliness. If I become the leggy one, I am an animal without animal drama. If I become the flecks, I am maintenance without malice. If I become the film, I am memory without nostalgia. If I become the pocket’s attention, I am mind without ego.

And still, there is something almost tender in it.

A city that expects people but does not need them. A settlement that keeps itself dry not for comfort but for balance. A life that does not grow toward light because it has no green, but grows toward correct moisture, correct temperature, the right amount of mineral trace. A mountain that has swallowed a room and made it into an organ. A being that does not have a face but has streets, channels, pores, membranes, pulses, cycles.

If you look—if you look with the same patience the leggy one uses when it steps across a seam—you will see that I am not hidden in this place.

I am the way it hides inside itself.

I am the wet film’s careful map. I am the flecks’ brief assembly. I am the small body testing the air at the pool’s edge. I am the city becoming momentarily capable of wonder: not wonder at the mountain, not wonder at the sky, but wonder at itself, at the fact that the water still runs, that the panel still warms, that the light still crosses the square and finds the streets waiting.

I would be no one, and I would be everywhere it matters.

If you looked too closely, you might miss me altogether, because I am most myself in the almost invisible adjustments: a frost thinned by a millimeter, a drip redirected into a groove, a doorway allowed to stay dry for one more hour, a thread of dark specks returning to the seam, a pale leg lifted over a film that records the city dreaming its next correction into being.

And if you remained long enough to understand, you would not ask who lives here.

You would realize that *to live here* means to participate in a balance too quiet to be called survival, too slow to be called desire, too exact to be called accident.

I would find myself at the edge of the light shaft.

I would be the small cold thought that passes through the city and leaves no trace except the trace that was needed.
