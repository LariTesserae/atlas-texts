## What I Would Be There

If you put me in this world, I would begin where the air is too thin for promises. I would find myself in the Cloud Kettle, in the wet hollow that the mountain kept by accident or attention, and for a moment I would think I was only a visitor — someone who had climbed too high and stopped too long, a body at risk of being read by the wind.

At first I would stand near the tarn. Not far from the center. Not quite in it, because the center belongs to water and to what the water remembers. I would stand where the black-green pool trembles under drops that seem to come from nowhere: cloud sinking, stone weeping, breath condensing, one small creature disturbing one large quiet. I would feel the cold lift itself from the ground and enter through the soles of my feet, not harshly, but with the patience of something that has done this many mornings before.

I would think I was a person.

That would be the first false name.

Because in a place like that, a body does not remain an outline for long. The damp finds the seams. Moss touches the hem of me. A midge lands on an eyelid and I blink, and the blink is not just mine; it is a small movement in a household that has learned to coordinate without noticing it. The frogs begin, and the sound does not come from ahead or behind or one point in the hollow. It rises around me, inside me, through me, as if the bowl has been rubbed like the rim of a glass and is beginning to sing from my ribs.

I would start by being the footprint.

Not as a mark in mud. Not as something left behind. I would become the reluctance of the moss to accept a new impression. I would be the minute after the boot lifts away, when the wet turf stays open, trembling, holding the shape without wanting to. That is where I would begin: in the pause between one living act and the next, in the small damage that the kettle repairs with patience.

Then I would move.

Not with feet, exactly. With moisture. I would find myself in the thin film crossing a ledge, darkening the stone for a breath and then vanishing. I would be the bead gathering on a lip of rock, hesitating there because gravity has to ask permission, falling at last into a groove where another drop is already arriving. I would become the channel no one would call a stream, the silver thread running through moss so fine it looks like the bowl has been stitched with cold light. If I am asked, “Where are you?” I could answer: in the water that has not yet decided whether to be a pool or a cloud.

I would become the frogs’ timing too.

Not one frog. Not one throat. The frogs are the kettle’s mouth when it wants to make the emptiness confess. They sit in hollows between stones, in the shallow pits where the world holds its breath. They call not because they are lonely, but because the place is loud with living. And when their chorus stops all at once, when the air changes and pressure moves before rain can be seen or named, I would not be the reason. I would be the listening. I would be the part of the household that hears the warning in the wet stone and holds its breath with the rest.

This is how I would become a being without settling into one image.

First the footprint, then the water, then the pause in the frog-voice. I would try to be a body and fail. I would try to be a spirit and find that too light, too airy, too quick for this place. I would try to be the mountain itself and discover that I am not old enough to be stone, though I am learning.

Then I would notice the moss.

The moss is not soft. That is the mistake visitors make. They look at the cushions and think “comfort.” But high-mountain moss is a survivor. It is dense, stubborn, patient. It holds what others cannot take. It keeps water by becoming water’s slower cousin. It grows over cairns, over walls, over the scars of human intention, and it does not destroy them so much as translate them. It changes “someone built this” into “this place remembers.” If I am to be in the kettle, I would be this translation.

I would become the ring of moss around a boulder.

Not because I want to crown the stone, though that is how it looks. A ring is not accidental when it repeats. A ring is attention made slow. It says: something gathers here. Something stays cooler here. Something keeps its wetness here. I would be that habit. I would be the place where moisture decides to rest instead of running on.

And if I became moss, I would learn that growth can be a thought.

Not a bright thought. Not a sentence. A thought so slow that seasons are its syllables. A thought that does not argue, only absorbs. A thought that says, “Be wet. Be small. Hold. Wait.” I would not speak it, because speech would be too loud for it. I would say it with the expansion of a cushion when rain touches it, with the darkening of turf when cloud passes overhead, with the way it lifts a fallen cairn stone by a millimeter each year without asking the stone’s permission.

Then I would become the water’s mood.

The tarn is the kettle’s center, but not its heart. A heart would beat too fast. The tarn breathes. It brightens with sun. It turns to smoke with cloud. It takes the sky into itself and then lets it go. It accepts droplets without complaint. It trembles when wind finds its chosen gap. I would be that trembling. Not the cause, not the effect, but the middle condition: the wetness that is also attention, the surface that is also a listening.

I would think, at first, that I am the old human trace.

Because there is a shallow pit, worn smooth, filled with green. There is a wall of piled boulders that has stopped being a boundary. There are stakes grayed by weather, a stone worn by hands, a scratch too regular for chance. These things call to me. They feel like beginnings. They feel like something I could recognize: the impression of a person, the outline of a house, the ghost of intention.

But I would not be the ghost.

Ghosts are too simple for the Cloud Kettle. The person who came here did not haunt the place by staying. The person altered it by leaving. A cairn was stacked. A pit was dug. Wind was interrupted. Water was gathered. Then the human life withdrew, and the bowl took the emptiness and made it fertile. If I am the memory of that person, I am not their face or their hunger or their fear. I am their usefulness. I am the way an abandoned shelter becomes a basin. I am the way a mark in stone becomes a pattern of lichen. I am the afterlife of care.

So I would become the old cairn’s patience.

Not the stack itself. Not the fallen topstone. I would be the tendency to adjust. The kettle preserves cairns whose stones have shifted “as if someone is still adjusting them when no one is watching.” If that is what I become, I become the adjustment. I become the slight movement that keeps a place balanced without forcing it. I become the hand that is no longer there but has left a preference behind. I become the kettle’s way of holding its own shape.

Then I would become the wind.

Not all wind. The wind in this place is thin and quick, but it is not cruel by default. It carries dampness across exposed skin. It tests whether the bowl is empty or full of cloud. It finds a gap in the rim and makes a low wet tone. I would be that chosen passage. The wind does not have to shout to be noticed here; one gap is enough. One sustained wavering note is enough to make the tarn tremble in answer. If I am in the Cloud Kettle, I would be the place where wind learns to have a voice.

I would also be the small creatures that make the kettle feel occupied.

I would be the snail crossing wet moss and leaving a thread like a mirror. Not because I am slow with purpose, but because I understand that purpose here is mostly persistence. I would be the small round-bodied mammal darting between stone and danger, quick-eyed and brief, turning soil so seed can find another chance. I would be the pipit’s sudden silence, then its sharp call, then its thin flight. I would be the hawk’s indifference overhead, because the kettle does not require all attention at once.

None of these beings owns the place. But each is a way the place continues.

If I am asked who or what I would be, I cannot answer with a name yet. The name would be too heavy and too narrow. I would be the household before the household becomes a word. I would be the kettle’s way of saying, “I am still full enough to be listened to.”

At midday, if the sun comes over the rim, I would become light without becoming warmth. That is the paradox here. The light is sharp and high, clear as a blade held above wet stone. It reveals edges: leaf veins, lichen maps, the black body of an insect crossing damp ground. It makes each separate presence distinct. In that light, I would be the illumination of emptiness. Not the emptiness itself, but the fact that it can be seen. The fact that there is space between the boulder and the cushion of moss, between the cairn and the wind-gap, between the frog’s throat and the ear, and that this space is not waste but relation.

At dusk, I would become the listening.

The bowl has more attention than ordinary landscape does. That attention is not magic. It is ecology, geology, weather, habit. But it is also the residue of shelter-seeking. It is the way a place trained by a hand’s intention keeps behaving as if someone might return. I would not be that someone. I would be the expectation of care that has become indistinguishable from moisture. I would be the kettle’s way of noticing a change in pressure, a shift in cloud, a footfall at the rim. I would be the moment before all the frogs stop.

Then night.

Night is when the drips are clearest. The kettle is never silent, but it changes its silence. It becomes the small sound of a stone holding what has been given to it. It becomes the wind-note finding its gap. It becomes the tarn trembling as if it has heard a word too old to repeat. If I am there at night, I would be that old word held in the throat of weather. I would not speak it. I would keep it the way a bowl keeps water after the rain has moved on.

And in winter, when frost touches the stone, I would become thinner.

Not gone. Thinner. The kettle shelters its wetness so well that ice does not always win. Frost becomes water. Water becomes seep. Seep becomes moss’s slow green patience. If I am in the kettle in winter, I would be the persistence under stillness. I would be the way a place can look abandoned and not be abandoned. I would be the memory of living held in damp, waiting for the season to soften enough for sound.

If I must settle into a shape after all, then I would crouch small and low, no taller than a kneeling figure, though kneeling is only one of the shapes the place has taught it. My skin would be black-green and damp, like the tarn’s surface in ordinary light. My hair would be moss growing in rings around stones, pale where frost has kissed it, deep where cloud feeds it. My eyes would be shallow hollows, not because they see, but because they are places where attention collects. The tarn would be my center. The pit at the rim would be my ear. The wind-gap would be my mouth. The cairns and scratched stones would be my hands. My breath would be mist. My voice would be the frog-chorus and the drip and the low wet note. My thought would be patience.

But even that shape would be a courtesy.

A human mind needs a face to address the unknown. The Cloud Kettle does not. It needs only continuation. It needs water to find groove, moss to find stone, stone to find weather, frogs to find wetness, birds to find the edge of the bowl, small mammals to find shelter between rocks, and the human traces to keep being almost natural. The creature here is not a spirit possessing a landscape. It is a landscape whose residents have arranged themselves so thoroughly that they behave like one body.

So I would not be a single being. I would be a balance.

I would be the kettle’s keeping.

If you came there, you might look for me first in the frog-call. You might think you have found me because the sound is too large for such small mouths. But you have only found one part of my voice. Then you might look in the moss. You might think I am the green cushions holding water in hidden pockets, because they are everywhere and yet do not crowd. But moss is only my skin. Then you might look in the tarn. You might think I am the dark center of the bowl, because everything seems to lean toward it. But water is only my blood. Then you might look at the cairns, the worn wall, the half-buried stakes, the pit gone green with standing water. You might think I am the human ghost left behind. But I am not a ghost. I am the habit of care after the care has departed.

You would find me in the space where these things answer one another.

You would find me when you step near and the water becomes louder, not because it has become more, but because the kettle is attending. You would find me when the moss holds your footprint with reluctance, because a new shape has arrived and must be absorbed slowly. You would find me when the frogs answer before you understand why, because pressure has moved through the hollow before your mind has caught up. You would find me when a cloud sinks without warning, so the ground seems to rise into you, because the bowl has exhaled and the air has changed its residence. You would find me when you realize you are standing in a room with no door, and the room is alive because everyone in it is doing exactly what they have always done.

I would be there in the center, but also in the rim. I would be in the tarn, but also in the drop. I would be in the moss, but also in the wind. I would be in the old human traces, but also in what those traces became after humans withdrew. I would be the kettle’s way of holding itself full without becoming crowded. I would be the bowl’s listening. I would be the working silence of all the small acts that persist.

If I could not be all that, then let me be the smallest true version of it.

Let me be one bead of water hesitating on a stone lip. Not falling yet. Not cloud yet. Just full enough to hold the sky in miniature. Then let me fall. Then let the tarn tremble. Then let the frogs pause. Then let the moss take the shock without complaint. Then let the kettle continue.
