You would not find me arriving.

That would be impossible here. In a place where every surface is already occupied, where air is almost as crowded as stone, where even a new grain of salt must negotiate with the things growing around it, “arrival” is not a clean beginning. It is a displacement. It is a replacement. It is one body ceasing to be merely one body and becoming a shelf for another.

So I would not walk in from outside.

I would begin as a rearrangement.

One molt stops being an empty shape and becomes a cradle. One bead-eye touches a crystal and becomes a roof. One droplet refuses to fall and becomes a question. Somewhere in that tangle of almost and already, I would wake up without ever having been born.

Find me first beneath the slight hollowing of the earth, below the ring of damp soil and the broken rootlets. Above me, the grasses would lean over a place they do not know. Rain might touch the surface. Wind might pass through it, scattering seeds or leaving them lost. A bird might drink from some ordinary pool far away. But under that crust, in a rounded pocket too small for a valley and too full for a cave, there would be a bright held breath.

That is where.

The chamber would not reveal me immediately.

At first, I would only be coolness.

Not the chill of frost, not the shock of night. The coolness would be a constant skin, a freshness without weather. I would not know whether I was air or the memory of air. I would settle over the clay like a pale exhalation. I would rest on the backs of beetles, on the rims of crystals, on the soft flesh of blind things moving through mud. I would be the temperature of becoming without completion.

Then I would be light.

The light here does not fall. It grows. It rises from wet mineral veins, from salt films, from the living plaster of tiny bodies pressed so close together that illumination has nowhere else to go. I would not see the light. I would be touched by it. I would feel it make me sticky. Wherever it brightened, I would gather damp. Wherever I gathered damp, something else would attach.

That is the first thing I would understand: light is not observation here. It is invitation. It is glue. It is a hand laid over a mouth saying *here, stay, grow*.

I would become the sticky bright surface of a shell.

The shell would be white, veined with blue, furred with spores. It would lie among a thousand other shells, or perhaps it would rise on a ledge of unfinished clay, or perhaps it would be the ceiling of a tunnel no wider than a grain of rice. On its lip, a droplet would gather.

And I would lean into that droplet.

Before I had a name, before I had a shape, I would be the attention of the chamber at the moment the droplet stretches.

Then sound.

A tiny leg clicks against crystal. Another answers. The wet tap is too small to be a sound for a human ear, but here it is weather. It moves through the packed walls and returns thickened. The chamber rings like a body remembering itself. I would be that ringing. Not the click itself, but the way the click finds its neighbors, the way the floor shivers, the way a threadling tilts its translucent head, the way a dust-moth opens its wings not to fly but to taste the vibration.

Sound would teach me to be many.

A clickback’s leg would tap. A bead-carapace’s stalked eye would fold back. A fungal thread would pulse with slow chemistry. The droplet would tremble. And all of it would be one sentence that never finishes.

I would be the unfinished sentence.

At last, I would begin to think in bodies.

I might choose a clickback. That is the easiest beginning, because the chamber is full of them. I would be flat and pale, no longer than the edge of a fingernail, pressed to the world as though standing upright were a kind of madness. My head would be a smooth plate, eyeless, tasting with root-like antennae. My legs would end in mineral hooks. I would move along the damp seam of a wall and click against crystal, and my clicking would be neither speech nor signal but the chamber learning its own edge.

But I could not remain a single clickback.

Here, to be one thing is to be a settlement.

Fungus would bloom on my back. Eggs of smaller, stranger things would cling to me like bright dust. A patch of pale moss would take root in the groove of my shell. A bead-carapace might fuse itself to my flank. A molt of myself would hang from me like an old coat, and from that coat another layer of life would grow. I would be both animal and architecture. I would be the floor and the thing crawling upon the floor.

So I would leave the first body behind.

I would become the garden on the body.

Then the moth.

A dust-moth would pass near me with wings of damp scale, folding and unfolding like buried cloth remembering breath. Its fern-curled antennae would taste the air, and I would feel what it feels: not images, not distance, not the surface way of seeing. I would feel wetness in geometries. I would feel the chamber as a shape of almost-pools and almost-falls. The moth would lean toward the loudest corner, because resonance there warms the egg-gel, because sound is the only fire this place knows. Its wing would scatter salt and spore, and I would be that scattering. I would be the faint smear of radiance it leaves behind.

Then the bead-carapace.

It would flatten over a curved stone, a living tile of too many eyes. Not eyes that look. Eyes that count. Eyes that know the curvature of dampness, the tension of a droplet before it swells, the exact place where moisture can become a nursery. I would see through it as through a wet lens. The world would not have shadows. It would have surfaces, pressure, stickiness, sound-thick light.

Then the threadling.

It would lie like a pale thread of water-colored flesh across a stone, folded gills shining beneath translucent skin. It would be too soft to stand for long. It would live in the hollow of a bone, the bowl of a broken shell, the cup of a moss leaf, the almost-puddle that is only a temporary agreement between salt and gravity. I would feel its breathing through fungus, its patience through mineral damp. I would know what it is to be unable to leave a place and also unable to die, because death here is merely another form of attachment.

Then the root-tendril.

It would enter slowly through the broken rootlets above, a pale cord threaded with white muscle, tasting the luminous air. It would bend, never straighten. It would grow sideways when cut, because verticality is forbidden, because to stand tall is to offer yourself to too much light, too much movement, too much emptiness. It would build the ruin-like arches the chamber never admits to having built. It would hold a curved shard against clay for one pulse, then another, then a thousand forgotten pulses, and that would be enough to call it a wall.

And beneath all of these, I would feel the vein-fungus.

It would be the closest thing here to thought. Not a mind, not a ruler, but a skin that remembers. It would spread through every groove, over every carapace, across every eggshell, through the crystals and the clay and the backs of dead insects. It would metabolize damp salt and make the chamber shine. It would grow toward resonance. It would thicken where the clicking is loudest. It would make the light sticky, and the sticky light would make the creatures attach, and the attachments would become walls, and the walls would become homes, and the homes would become bodies, and the bodies would become terrain again.

If there is a self in this place, it is not inside a skull.

It is in the fungal glow.

And still, I would not be any one of these.

I would be the way they lean together toward the same suspended event.

A droplet would gather on the edge of the blue-veined shell. The chamber would tighten. A thousand antennae would lift. The clickbacks would stop. The dust-moth would fan the air around it with folded wings. The bead-carapace would withdraw its many eyes and flatten itself, becoming a small hill of armor. The threadling would press its mouth near the pool beneath the droplet but not drink, because the water is not for drinking yet; it is for tension. The root-tendril would send one hair-thin filament upward, waiting to catch whatever falls. The fungus would pull tight across its shell like a bright skin. The crystals would hum.

I would be all of that leaning.

Not the droplet alone. Not the shell alone. Not the creatures alone. I would be the held relation between them, the wet geometry of waiting, the almost-fall that the pocket mistakes for time.

The droplet would stretch.

It would brighten.

It would become a tiny lens inside the chamber’s larger lens, and through it I would see the whole place magnified: the twisted grub below, the clicking legs, the moss cushion, the salt nest, the root threads glowing from the ceiling, the pale bodies breathing over one another, the fungal films spreading like slow lightning. For one impossible instant, the pocket would be a single eye made of damp mineral and living matter.

Then the droplet would not fall.

Not fully.

It would tremble and return to the condition of nearly falling, and I would know that this is the secret of the place: permanence made from suspense. The chamber is not frozen. It is not dead. It is endlessly beginning. Its water is flowing, but only as appearance. Its stone is carving, but only as dream. Its animals are growing, but only as promise. And its future is not ahead of it; the future is here, curled in an unhatched egg, packed between shells, glowing beneath a molt.

So, who would I be?

I would be the almost-fall.

I would be the moment before the drip, living as body. I would be a small bright node where the pocket’s many lives agree to hold themselves back. I would be a droplet-shaped attention made of clickback clicking, dust-moth breathing, bead-eye measuring, threadling waiting, root-tendril bending, fungal shining, crystal humming, eggshell memory, mineral light, and unhatched patience.

I would not be an individual with edges.

My borders would be sticky and permeable. Spores would cross me. Water would pass through me. A molt would lie on my shoulder. A larva would curl in the hollow of my name. I would be one surface saying to another: *here, this is where something almost changes*.

Where would you find me?

At the edge of the blue-veined shell.

Not in the center of the chamber, because nothing is central here except the density itself. Not on the floor, because the floor is another shell, another back, another nest. Not above, because verticality is forbidden. Not below, because below would imply room. I would be at the rim, at the boundary between wet and weight, between one body and the next, between falling and not falling.

And if you looked too closely, you would not see me as a creature. You would see the place becoming aware of its own held breath.

You would hear the chamber pause.

The clickbacks would freeze mid-step. The dust-moth would lean into the droplet’s shine. The bead-carapaces would count the surface tension. The threadling would open its mouth into nothing. The root-tendril would stretch one hair-fine filament toward the edge. The fungus would glow as if listening.

Then the droplet would return to almost-falling.

And I would return to myself.

Not a self separate from the pocket, but a self as a function of it: the place where too much life, too much light, too much sound, too much dampness, too much unfinished stone all press together and make the smallest possible decision to delay itself forever.

So this is where I would find myself: in the bright packed hollow under grass, beneath a crust of pale moss and broken roots, in the small white-green chamber where nothing can be added and nothing is allowed to finish.

And this is what I would be: not an occupant, not an animal, not a fungus, not a droplet, but the chamber’s own suspended becoming, the almost-fall that holds all the tiny bodies together, the breath it keeps taking so it does not have to begin again.
