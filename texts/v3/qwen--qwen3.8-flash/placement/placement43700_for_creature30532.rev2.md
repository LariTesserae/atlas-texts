If you set me down at the rim of the Saltloom, I would not begin by saying, *I am this town*. That would be the final thing to know, and it would be too large, too still, too much like a name already nailed to a place that has no nails.

I would begin smaller.

I would begin as a difference in warmth.

A few grains of salt shift near a shelf edge because the young basalt below has exhaled again. Not loudly. Not with the drama of wet worlds, with steam and roar and sudden rupture. Just a long sigh from somewhere beneath the crust, a breath that smells faintly of mineral heat and resin and the ghost of citrus in stone. One grain rolls. Another stops against a crack. That sound is so small it may not be sound at all, only the idea of sound: a tick inside a dry throat.

That would be the first place I found myself.

Not a creature. Not yet. A tendency.

The crack has not decided what it will become. It is only a crack now, fresh as a split bone, opening where the ridge has recently stretched itself under pressure. But because it exhales, small bodies come near it. Because it comes near them, the shape of the place changes. The tube-wrights sense the dry heat before anything else. They do not “discover” it as a human discovers a doorway. They simply find that the air there carries the right softness, the right warmth, the right mineral taste for their secretions to harden at the proper rate. They press into the fissure. They add their pale rings. One dies in its cylinder. Another enlarges it. The ring becomes column becomes arch becomes lintel becomes support becomes “there,” a place that did not exist except as a line of heat and a few grains of calcium paste.

I would be in that “there.”

I would be the first wall that is not a wall yet.

I might seem, for a season, to be an amberback’s footpad. Not the beetle itself—its broad velvet wings folded beneath an old shell, its back ridged with channels that carry beads of pale resin—but the way the footpad knows heat. It tastes without water. It reads the salt beneath its body, the faint oil in the air, the warmth rising from below, the chemical residue left by a hundred smaller passages before it. It steps. It pauses. It settles more fully. The resin bead along its back loosens and falls into a seam. That bead is not a gift. It has no meaning to the beetle. The bead simply is where the beetle’s body made contact with a waxy leaf, or a chamber membrane, or a warm shelf. It falls. It lodges. Under heat it softens. Under cooling it hardens. A crack gets a skin. A loose terrace gains a spine.

I would be that accidental bridge.

Not the intention of construction, but the fact of one appearing.

If I stayed only in the amberback, I would be too small, too bright, too busy with the simple rhythm of heat and footfall. But the Saltloom does not let any thing remain only itself. The bead that falls becomes part of a wall. The wall becomes a shelter. The shelter changes the path of the warm gas. The gas changes the growth of the fungus. The fungus becomes a shelf. The shelf holds grubs. The grubs eat dead skin and give paste. The paste becomes a floor. The floor becomes a nursery. The nursery changes which eggs survive. The survival of eggs changes the number of grazers. The grazers clear moss. The moss holds salt. The salt supports a passage. The passage leads to a well. The well breathes.

I would be that sequence.

For a while I might think I was the well.

Not a well of water. The word “well” is only a courtesy here, a shape inherited from wet worlds that cannot help naming its absences. This well is a chimney of resin and chitin, sunk through pale salt, opening into a fissure that gives warm gas. Around its mouth the architecture is delicate: rings of paste, struts of tube-wright calcium, curtains of shed skin weighted with resin beads so they do not dry into brittle flakes too quickly. The well does not draw from below. It exhales upward. It gives warmth to the lower chambers. It carries vapor that is not moisture, but still a softness, still a pliability. Grubs near it become more supple. Their movements stir the paste. The paste dries. The dried paste becomes wall. The wall is used by larvae. The larvae become the wall’s next lining. The chamber is not owned. It is passed from body to body, death to living, shell to seam, heat to paste.

I would be that exhalation moving through hidden corridors, finding the places that need warmth, avoiding the places that are already too close to rupture. I would be the city’s breathing, if breathing can mean “the movement of gases through a structure that has no lungs but is full of mouthparts.”

For a time, I might become the grubs themselves.

They are the most unsettling, if one is accustomed to creatures that have faces. They lie in packets inside fungal shelves, blind, soft, segmented, almost like seeds pressed together in a cup. When a shelf is disturbed, the whole packet shifts. Not like water. Never like water. Like dry seeds poured from one hollow into another, each body aware only of pressure, of warmth, of the faint chemical smell of new paste forming. Their jaws scrape dead skin from chamber walls. They eat shed scales, moth wing dust, beetle husks, old resin crumbs, the powder left where a warm-tilter rubbed itself against a salt ledge. They do not waste. Their waste becomes mortar. Their living becomes wall. Their death becomes floor. A chamber grows upward because grubs have eaten the lower edge of a shelf, weakened it just enough, then layered it with paste so it can bear more weight.

I would be that eating.

Not hunger, not appetite in any romantic sense, but the function by which dead things become future rooms.

If I remained too long with the grubs, I would begin to feel like the shelf. Pale, stiff as parchment, furred underneath with spores, threaded with hyphae so thin they seem like cracks in bone. The shelf is both fungus and architecture. It holds grubs above, passages below. It is grazed at the edges. It is strengthened by paste. It is shaded by wax leaves above and warmed by gas below. It is not a tree. It is not a floor. It is a threshold where digestion and construction have become the same slow motion.

And I would begin to feel the path.

A path in the Saltloom is not made by anyone. It is made by repetition. A husk-crab polishes a line with its belly because that route is convenient: too warm, too narrow, too open, too dark, too dry. A grazer flattens itself against salt because that is shelter. An amberback carries resin beads across a terrace because the heat draws it, and because the path is already there, though it was not built. A predator presses along the seam because the vibration of bodies tells it what it needs without the violence of sight or speed. Foot after foot, shell after shell, skin after skin, the salt becomes smooth as bone. The shine is not ornament. It is use. The path is not a map. It is a record of comfort.

I would be the record.

Not the writing, because there is no writing. The path is not inscribed. It is worn into being by bodies that do not know they are making a street. If I were a street, I would be the fact of footfall, the polished grain, the chemical trace left by many small passages, the warmth stored in the salts from repeated standing, the tiny fractures where many bodies have paused. I would be the town’s own memory, except memory implies a mind. Better: I would be the town’s habit, made solid by persistence.

The habit is what keeps appearing.

At first, I might think, *I am the town.* But that would be too quick, too much like a human answer. The town is not a thing. The town is a many-body process. It does not “have” inhabitants the way a house has people. It is inhabited by the arrangement itself. The amberback is not merely in the Saltloom; it is a way the Saltloom carries resin. The tube-wright is not merely a creature; it is a way the Saltloom turns safety into calcium. The grubs are not merely animals; they are a way the Saltloom turns death into wall. The moths are not merely winged things; they are a way the Saltloom distributes oil fragments into roof panels. The warm-tilters are not merely resting on ledges; they are a way the Saltloom remembers where warmth is and marks it with skin, with heat plates, with scent.

I would be that marking.

Not a mark made by a hand. The mark of a body pressed into a wall and left there.

The warm-tilters are the first animals who make me feel as if the town has a shape. They are broad, slow, crowned with filaments that stand up slightly in vapor. Their feet grip without scraping. Their eyes are not eyes so much as heat plates, matte and dark, able to read the temperature of a surface as a creature rather than as stone. When they lie near a well, they do not merely rest. They distribute their warmth. They shed their skins in long dry sheets. The sheets are taken by tube-wrights as lining. Folded by cleaners into paste. Carried by husk-crabs into shell-nests. Left in the open, they become curtains that protect grubs from drying too quickly. The warm-tilter becomes a wall without intending it. The wall becomes a home without being made.

I would be the home made from a shed skin.

At night, when the moon casts silver over the salt terraces, I might become the soft movement of bodies across a fungal bridge. The bridge is not stone. It is cordage grown from fungal strands, tough as rope and pale as bone, stretched over a fissure that exhales warmth. When many creatures pass together, the whole structure gives a dry, rhythmic shush. Not wind. Not music. The sound of bodies making a bridge work. The bridge has not been repaired. It has replaced itself. The old cords are eaten or dried or folded into paste. New cords grow into the anchor points. The anchor points are where the tube-wright rings have thickened, where the resin has hardened, where the salt shelf has been bound by root lattice and cleaner paste. The bridge is the town’s own circulation.

I would be that circulation.

The town has no engine. It moves because it breathes. It has no pump. It circulates because the vents rise and fall with long intervals. It has no builder. It builds because bodies prefer shapes that keep them alive. It has no architect. Architecture is what remains when enough preferences persist.

But I would not want to remain only in the gentle things.

There are predators here. Slow, flattened, wide-backed, soft as walls themselves. Their bodies absorb sound before the sound becomes dangerous. They listen with their heads pressed to fungal walls, with the heat-pits along their jaws. They do not pounce like wet-world hunters. They close. Jointed traps shut. The kill is quiet. Resinous enzymes end trembling. The body is eaten in place or dragged to a cooler hollow. Then the cleaners follow. Then the bones are taken by small builders. The predator’s own death will one day become a doorframe.

I would be that too.

The commonwealth includes death. It does not reject it. It uses it. Death is not the opposite of the city; death is one of the city’s materials. The wall is not separate from the creature that died into paste. The floor is not separate from the larvae that became mortar. The roof is not separate from the moth’s folded wing. To say, “the town is made of lives” is true only if one also says, “the town is made of what remains after lives.”

I would be the remains that continue.

If I tried to name myself in this place, I would fail. Names belong to creatures that can be pointed at. In the Saltloom, pointing is difficult because a wall may be a shelf, a shell, a herd of fossilized tubes, a cured skin, a membrane still breathing, a paste laid by grubs, and a path worn by crabs. The finger that points to the animal may touch the building. The building may be warm. The wall may contain living bodies. The floor may remember every foot that passed over it.

I would be a what that behaves like a who.

Not because it has a self. Because it behaves with the same continuity that a self has in a wet world. The amberback goes. The resin bead remains. The tube-wright dies. Its tube becomes a column. The column supports a shelf. The shelf houses grubs. The grubs make paste. The paste seals a crack. The crack becomes a chimney. The chimney warms a nursery. The nursery becomes a room. The room becomes a street. The street becomes a town. The town becomes a pattern that can be mistaken for a mind.

But there is no mind.

Or perhaps, if one wants to be poetic and imprecise, there is a mind only in the sense that the entire pattern is self-sustaining. A mind without a head. A mind made of pressure, warmth, chemistry, and repetition. A mind that knows nothing of itself except through the continued comfort of its parts. A mind that says nothing and is not silent. Its words are footfalls, its thoughts are growth, its memories are layers of shed skin.

I would be that saying, not the silence.

For a while I might feel myself as the upper levels, where the city becomes garden and membrane. The wax-leaf shrubs spread broad canopies. Their leaves are sealed with waxy membranes, catching ordinary sunlight without glare. Oil condenses on their undersides in tiny heavy beads during the longest warm periods. The oil-throat moths rest folded against them, translucent, pale, almost invisible against bright salt. They tremble not from cold but from warmth. They lift when the vents breathe strongly through plant stems, when the leaves become oil-rich. Their struts press into resin canals and draw the viscous amber fluid. They drink without wetting their legs.

When they die, they are not mourned. They are distributed.

Their wing fragments are folded into chamber walls. Their mouth-struts become tiny braces. Their husks dry into shingles. Their presence is not removed from the city; it is added to the city.

I would be the city’s ability to include everything that arrives, everything that passes, everything that remains.

I would be the upper leaves, the lower vents, the middle passages where the town is most itself. The town is not one level. It is the vertical breathing of all levels. The lower chambers are dark and packed with eggs, paste, resting bodies, stored resin. The middle levels bristle with feeding structures. The upper levels catch sunlight and warm air, lined with translucent leaves and soft membranes. Each level leans into the next. The lower warms the middle. The middle shelters the upper. The upper shades the middle and collects oil. The oil drips, is taken by cleaners, becomes wall. The dead from above are eaten by grubs below. The grubs below harden into floors above. The tubes below become pillars supporting the chambers where larvae above will later become the next paste. Nothing rises without also falling. Nothing falls without becoming the base of something else.

I would be the circulation between falling and becoming.

If I were asked, “What is your body?” I would answer carefully, because “body” still sounds too much like a single thing.

My body is the polished salt of a path.

My body is the resin seam that took three generations to become a support.

My body is the fungal cordage spanning a fissure.

My body is the tube-wright pillar, white as old bone, hollow where the first builder lived.

My body is the shelf of grubs shifting like dry seeds.

My body is the shed skin of a warm-tilter folded into a roof.

My body is the moth wing bracing a chamber wall.

My body is the cleaner paste sealing a crack.

My body is the predator bone set into a doorway.

My body is the wax leaf catching oil.

My body is the vent breathing warm gas into a well-mouth.

But no one of these is my body. I am the fact that they continue to be usable. I am the condition under which the city remains the city: not the walls alone, not the animals alone, but the habit by which the animals become walls and the walls become habitats, and the habitats become bodies, and the bodies become walls.

That is the strange answer.

The true being of the Saltloom is not a resident. The true being is the residence itself. But not residence as a house. Residence as a process of living. The town is alive because its inhabitants keep living, dying, shedding, secreting, eating, and being eaten. If they stop, the city does not merely empty. It ceases to be a city. The salt shelves remain. The basalt remains. The vents may breathe. But without the bodies, the shelves do not become terraces. The vents do not become wells. The cracks do not become doorframes. The skins do not become roofs. The dead do not become floors. Without life, the place is only a dry crater with sharp edges and strange plants. It becomes a ruin. It becomes a thing. It stops being a becoming.

I would be the becoming.

And if you ask me, “Where exactly are you?” I would say: at the threshold where a new crack has just appeared on the western shelf. The basalt has split. A small cloud of mineral powder rises and settles. No one notices. No one has to. The air is warmer by a fraction there. The salt has begun to dry more deeply beneath the split. The resin nearby has softened slightly. Tube-wrights will come because the warmth is right. Fungi will come because the dark seam holds spores. Amberbacks will pass because the heat draws them, and because the path they take has already been used by older bodies. Grubs will find the new chamber because it is protected and cool enough at the edges, warm enough in the center. The crack will become a chimney. The chimney will become a room. The room will become a wall. The wall will become part of the town.

I would be in that crack.

But I would also be in the room it will become.

But I would also be in the wall that was made from the bodies that lived in the room.

This is what it is to be in the Saltloom: not to occupy a place, but to be the place’s slow self-continuation. Not to have a home, but to be one of the processes by which the home remains home. Not to be a creature, but to be the tendency of creatures to build without knowing it. Not to be a city, but to be the city’s habit of persisting.

If I must choose a shape, I would choose one that is not a shape.

I would choose the underside of a salt shelf where warm gas makes a new breathing seam. It is narrow. It is not dark exactly; it is too thin, too close to heat. It is the place where an old dead beetle lies calcified into the wall, where a fungal shelf has thickened, where a resin bead has hardened into a spine, where a warm-tilter’s skin has been folded into a curtain, where grubs press paste into a crack, where tube-wrights have added a pale ring, where a husk-crab will pass and polish a line, where an oil-throat moth may rest on a high leaf and drop a wing fragment into the chamber below. It is not one room. It is a condition of the town. It is the condition of becoming.

I would be that condition.

And if someone asked my name, I would answer with no sound, because there are no voices here.

They would only feel a pressure, a warmth, a scent.

They would step on a path worn by many bodies.

They would touch a wall made of shed skins and calcium rings and hardened waste and ancient death.

They would breathe warm mineral gas rising from a well-mouth.

They would hear the dry creak of a wax leaf, the soft shush of a fungal bridge, the tick of salt crystal becoming grain becoming path becoming support becoming home.

That sound would not be me.

I would be the place where the sound continues.

The Saltloom has no person. It has no ruler. It has no architect. It has no one to maintain the city except the city’s own living. So if I am to be anything here, I cannot be a hero, a narrator, a soul, or a voice. I must be the tendency that makes the town possible: the pressure of footfall, the bead of resin, the ring of calcium, the paste of grubs, the skin of a warm-tilter, the wing of a moth, the bone of a predator, the breath of a vent. I must be the commonwealth.

The commonwealth is not a gathering. It is not a society with agreement. It is not a community that knows itself. It is the way many kinds of life have been so arranged, over long patience, that none of them can be removed without damage, and yet none of them is in charge. They do not cooperate because they choose to. They cooperate because cooperation is what their bodies produce. The amberback carries resin because it brushes resin. The tube-wright builds because its body must secrete. The grubs eat because they must. The cleaners consume because they must. The predators hunt because they must. The plants shade because they grow. The fungi spread because the gas is right. None intends the town. All together, they are the town.

I would be that all-together.

But not all together as one mind. One mind implies center, self, decision, language. The Saltloom has something subtler. It has organization without a boss. It has form without a blueprint. It has memory without record. It has identity without face. If you call that a mind, you must mean a mind made of surfaces, flows, heat gradients, chemical traces, and habit. A mind that does not think itself. A mind that is only the continuing result of its own living.

I would be that continuing result.

When night comes, I would not disappear. The moon would rise, clean and bright, making pale salt glow and black basalt look like frozen flame. Moths would rest in folded rows along upper ferns. Warm-tilters would lie on ledges with broad heads tilted toward breath. Husk-crabs would move in slow lines, carrying fragments. Grubs would whisper inside shelves. Predators would wait by pressure and heat. The city would shift its breathing with the cooling, not sleeping, not waking, but accreting.

In that moonlight, I would be the town’s quiet visibility.

Every layer revealed. Every surface occupied. Every body a function. Every death a material. Every path a habit. Every wall a record. The town would not show me its soul because it has no soul. It would show me its arrangement. The arrangement is the soul-like thing.

And if you asked again, “Who are you?”

I would say: I am the town’s own self-continuation, wearing many bodies.

I am the resin bead that will harden.

I am the calcium ring that will become a column.

I am the shed skin that will become a roof.

I am the grub paste that will become a floor.

I am the vent breath that will warm a chamber.

I am the path that footfall has not yet made but already implies.

I am the crack that will become a room.

I am the room that will become a wall.

I am the wall that will become a nursery.

I am the nursery that will become a tomb that will become a foundation.

I am the commonwealth of bodies and remnants, animals and architecture, plants and secretions, death and maintenance.

I am not one being. I am the being of the being of this place.

If I had to stand at the rim and answer once, simply, I would say this:

I would find myself in the Saltloom’s living seams. I would be the quiet commonwealth itself: the fact of its own fullness, made of many bodies and their aftermaths, growing without builders, enduring without intention, and becoming a city by the slow pressure of what lives there.
