I would not begin with a name. I would begin with a sound you feel before you hear it.

A hum against the wrist, low and mineral. A click at the teeth. A pause before a drip falls, so brief that a stranger might mistake it for the whole world holding its breath. If you found me there and asked, *Who are you?* I would not answer at once. I would take your hand, turn the palm flat against the nearest wall, and let the wall speak instead. Beneath the stone there is a channel. Beneath the channel there is pressure. Beneath the pressure there is a pattern I have carried in my bones longer than I can explain.

I would live in the basin.

Not on the rim, not in the open mountain air above the lip where the sky can be mistaken for a promise. I would be lower, inside the wet architecture, at the bend of a terrace where old collapsed arches have been turned into doorframes and new frost-lace forms every night in places that were dry by noon. My dwelling would be small, tucked behind three low thresholds, each one narrower than the last. The first threshold would be hung with felt. The second with hide treated in lard and salt so that it repels spray. The third would have no curtain at all, because beyond it the air must move; beyond it there is a sleeping ledge, a clay basin, a shelf of dried sedge, a wrapped stone holding down the edge of cloth, and in the wall a mouth too narrow for a finger but too loud for comfort.

If someone tried to give me a name, they might call me Basinfolk.

That word would be correct the way an outer wall is correct: useful, necessary, and likely to be broken before the room inside it. I am not separate from the people who live there. I am one of them, or something grown out of their habits of attention. Small-boned and broad through the chest, because breath must do the work of altitude. Not tall, because a tall person is a nuisance in a town of dripping lintels. Skin the color of wet stone in shadow, flushed pale red at the cheeks, salt-stained at the hands. Hair short, oiled, wrapped; long hair is a danger here because it freezes into wire against the neck. Eyes light: milk-grey, washed-out amber, with the flat reflective shine of a fish’s eye when the light finds the pool. I blink sideways when ice dust lifts from a spring. My teeth are chisel-smooth at the edges, because the cold and the mineral air wear them down before they have time to feel ordinary. My hands are strange to lowland hands: too warm in the center, too cold at the fingertips, able to tell the difference between a stone breathing and a stone lying.

I smell of rendered fat, wet wool, clay, smoke held too briefly in a small fire, and brine.

When I walk through the settlement, I do not dominate it. I move as part of its circulation. I test the third slab of any stair before putting weight on it. I step aside when a felt curtain swells with spray. I stop before a bright pool because the pool may be showing me something the surface hides: a new hollow, a shifting ice shelf, the pale body of a blind fish turning without urgency in water too cold to feel like water. I hum when I work, not melody exactly, but a low rounding tone that matches the rhythm of sealing a crack, pressing clay into a seam, turning a stone a quarter-inch so that a channel may breathe. The children learn my humming before they learn my face. To them I am the sound that comes from behind walls, the person who can tell whether a ledge is old, young, angry, tired, or hungry.

If you pressed me for a name, I might say: Glass Mouth.

That is not the name of a god. It is the name of a place where I keep a portion of myself, a spring mouth under a thin ice lens near the lower salt shelf, where the water rises and forms a small glass flower every night and every morning the flower is gone. I am of that mouth because I have repaired it too often. I have sealed around it with clay, removed the sacrificial ice before it cracked the foundation, marked its dry season with white pebbles, and returned to it when its absence became louder than its presence. Kinship, in this basin, is not only blood. It is repeated response. If you answer a mouth enough, it begins to answer in your joints.

I could pass for Basinfolk, but I am also not entirely.

Some nights I wake before sleep has finished. The terrace under the sleeping shelf settles by the width of a fingernail. Somewhere far below, a support pillar of ice melts and re-forms. A roof shifts by a hair. A channel that has been silent for years clicks twice. My left knee knows first. Then my teeth. Then the back of my neck, where the fine hairs stand because the air has changed its weight. I lie still until the sound becomes clear enough to walk toward. I do not run. Running here is a kind of deafness. If I run, I miss the smaller signs: the frost curling outward, the white mist rising from a stone mouth, the glass-grey fox stepping back from an ordinary ledge as if the ledge has become untrustworthy.

My work is repair, but not repair in the way outsiders imagine it: a problem solved, a thing made stable forever. Stability is a lie people tell each other after a night when nothing collapsed. I repair as one repairs a living rhythm. I do not make the basin stop moving. I help it move without taking children with it.

There is a room beneath the old cistern where the walls sweat so constantly that moss grows in dark ribbons along the lowest stones. The moss must be scraped often or it will hold too much water and rot the felt insulation. I scrape it with a flint-edged blade, not into the open channel, but onto a drying frame set just far enough from the spray. The scraped moss is packed, pressed, woven into pads for feet and elbows. The water collected in a shallow clay dish below is not wasted; it is brackish, but enough to soften dried sedge. A stranger might think this is poverty. It is not. It is exactness. In the basin, every object has a history of touch, and waste is a failure of memory.

I know the law, though it is not carved anywhere.

No one dam a mouth alone.  
No one break an ice shelf without setting a second wall behind it.  
No one leave a once-running channel dry without placing a white pebble.  
No one shout over the central terraces unless water is rising under a sleeping ledge.  
No one take the last warm stone.  
No one plug a crack that is asking a question.  
No one claim ownership over a pool, a seam, or a ledge.  
No one send a stranger away cold.  
No one forget a sound.

The last rule is the one that has taken the most of me.

I was not always careful. There was a time when I was young enough to think attention was merely hearing, not obedience. A wall near the outer drying frames began to weep in a new way. I disliked it. It made the hides damp. It raised a pale mineral stain that looked like disease. I sealed it too quickly with clay, without asking the mouth behind the clay what it wanted to say. The next night the pressure found a weaker place. The channel shifted sideways. The outer wall of an abandoned sleeping niche cracked, and a sheet of brine swept down the stair that led to the lower pool where children set midge nets.

No one died. The basin is not cruel that way, not unless it is forced. But the water took half a roof, a drying frame, and my confidence.

They set me on a high ledge with one clay cup and no tools.

This is not exile, though exile would be death. This is the basin’s quiet punishment: you must listen alone until your hearing becomes honest. I sat there through one pale darkening and another. The sky above the rim looked almost black, but the basin below glowed with its own reflected cold. I heard nothing for a while except the wetness moving under my feet. I thought I was failing. I tried to name the channels by memory. I tried to guess. I was wrong.

The fourth time a sound reached me—a click, then a faint hiss, then the creak of a stone clearing its throat. I said nothing. The elders asked me what I heard. I said, “I don’t know yet.” That was the first correct answer. The next day, when the mouth opened, I could point before the others did. The water rose exactly where my finger had trembled against the stone. After that, I stopped trying to command the basin’s speech and learned instead to be carried by it. That is when the basin took me into its habit, or when I finally let myself be taken. It is not a dramatic conversion. It is like frost entering a crack: quiet, repeated, difficult to reverse.

Now, when the town moves in festival repair, I am among those who touch first.

A great frost-lace may appear on the north wall overnight, so intricate that it seems to have been woven by fingers too cold to cast shadows. The people gather without shouting. They carry small loads of salt, clay, sedge, bone wedges. They tap ledges and hear whether the sound is empty or filled. They remove snow from sacrificial roofs before the snow becomes water and the water becomes ice and the ice becomes argument. Children watch how the elders place their hands, then place their own hands in the same places. No one claps when a task is done. The reward is that the terrace still exists the next morning.

I stand sometimes in a shallow pool, feet numb within seconds, and let the water rise around my ankles. If the ice begins to form, I do not pull away. I let it take a little of me. Then, when it cracks—softly, as all cracks do there—I report the sound. Not loudly. Not as prophecy. Only as information. This is enough. The town breathes.

The animals know me, though I am careful not to call that friendship.

The blind fish in the brine pools do not see, but they notice when my hand enters the water because my skin carries the same mineral taste they follow. They rise or slip away, and in that movement they teach me the pool’s hidden depth. If a fish stops drifting toward the bright water, something below has changed. If several fish hang motionless under an ice lens, pressure is building. I do not treat this as superstition. It is observation sharpened by hunger and patience.

The ice snails leave trails over the walls near my threshold. Their mucus, dried and mixed with resin, is one of the few things that makes a seam hold through two freeze-thaws without cracking. I harvest it from stones I have already marked as safe. The snails know not to climb where my footprints fall too heavily, but they are also not afraid in the way that would make them useless. Fear would make them vanish. We need them to stay, grazing, leaving their thin bright threads of paste.

The beetles crawl along the channels with frost-dusted backs. When they cluster in a crevice, it usually means the air there is still. I break their clusters with a bone needle, not to destroy them, but to know if the crevice has become dead air. A channel can become dead without becoming dry. Dead channels are worse. They hold pressure in secret.

The rodents with thick grey fur move through felt-lined hollows at the edges of the settlement. I set traps above ledges where their tracks make a small dark line in the salt. When a trap holds nothing, I do not curse the rodent. I ask where the animal has gone, and the answer is usually a new mouth. The trap becomes a sign.

The birds are the most difficult neighbors, because they teach by leaving. Pale short-winged birds nest on high ledges that foxes cannot climb. They click when they shift their footing on wet stone. They do not sing loudly. The basin swallows loudness. But if a flock lifts from one wall at a strange hour, I go to listen. Sometimes it is only a fox. Sometimes it is wind compressed through a crack. Sometimes it is the sound of an ancient overhang deciding to fall. The birds do not warn out of love. They warn because they too are listening. That is enough to make them worthy of respect.

The glass-grey foxes avoid me in the way that sensible animals avoid a person who knows too many doors. They do not fear me exactly. They read me. A fox will not step onto a pool’s rim if I am already watching it, as if my attention is a second paw-print on the ice. Sometimes, near the salt shelf, a fox leaves a bird bone or a small fish head by a crack. I take it to the pool-keeper. The pool-keeper takes half the meal and leaves the rest near a trap. This is not taming. It is tax.

The long-legged salt herons stand in shallow water near the lower cisterns. They are living signs. If they stand motionless, the surface is stable. If they step back before the light changes, the basin is moving underneath. People watch them the way lowland sailors watch birds over a reef, except here the reef is stone and the sea is a cold skin of water too thin to drown a finger and too deep to ignore.

I am not separate from these creatures. I share the same negotiation. We are all guests of water, even those of us who have lived so long beside it that our bones remember its habits.

There is a part of me that is human enough to need warmth. I understand this. I need to eat, though my meals are small and often. Algae cakes. Fish broth warmed over a sun-stored stone. Dried midges pressed into balls of fat. Sedge chewed slowly for a faint sweetness that never arrives fully. Lichen boiled until it becomes tender enough to grind. Salt harvested from thin veins and used sparingly, because salt makes the cold more intimate than it should be. I drink from channels that have been tested, from pools that have been marked, from meltwater caught in shaped ice before it becomes too sharp. I know which water will numb my teeth and which water will make my stomach remember a place it has been.

I need sleep, but sleep is a thin condition in the basin. The light does not go dark the way it should; it only softens. The frost creeps down the wall and the stones become more like bones. The water stops weeping for a while. Then, at some predictable or unpredictable moment, pressure changes, and the basin begins its small silent transformations. I wake often to adjust a clay pipe, move a quartz weight, turn a hide so the underside stays dry. If I am lucky, I sleep through a shift in the floor. If I am less lucky, I sleep through a shift in the wall.

My house is not a possession. It is a temporary agreement.

The sleeping ledge is low and packed with shaved ice beneath a layer of sedge and felt. The room is narrow, but there is a second room behind it, behind another, behind a collapsed arch that has been repaired until it has become almost natural. The wet side of my dwelling is sacrificial. If a channel beneath the floor swells, the outer stone course can break without taking the inner shelf. I have built it so. I have also broken it myself, when I had to. I know the shape of the cracks that are dangerous and the shape of the cracks that are only the basin’s handwriting.

Every morning I check the wall mouth. If it is sealed, I ask why. If it is open, I watch what it wants to carry. Sometimes it wants to bring water into the clay pipe that leads to the storage pit. Sometimes it wants to be left alone until night, when pressure rises and it will open itself. Sometimes it is only a film on stone, a damp stripe, a faint shine. Even that is speech.

My tools hang in ordered places: bone needles, flint scrapers, wooden stakes hardened with smoke, antler wedges in sets of three or five, clay rings to guide a dripping stream, shallow dishes to catch mineral frost, stone weights rounded by old water. Nothing is beautiful by lowland standards. Some things are beautiful by basin standards: smooth where many hands have corrected them, stained where many leaks have tested them, warm from being held too long against the body. I do not polish my tools to display them. I polish them to reduce the friction of my own urgency.

I speak softly.

In the basin, loudness is not only rude; it is dangerous. Sound can travel badly, but pressure carries well. A voice raised too high can make a thin membrane of ice tremble. A sudden command can hide the small click that means a roof will move by night. So we lower our words. We use breath against teeth. We tap. We touch. A person can say “mouth opening” by three quick taps against a listener’s palm. They can say “pressure from below” by sliding a hand upward along the wrist. They can say “do not plug it” by leaving a stone unturned. Silence is not empty here. It is full of wet attention.

If you asked me whether I am a god, I would laugh quietly, because laughter in the basin is always almost a cough. I am not a god. I am not a spirit in the romantic sense. I am not the basin itself wearing a person like a coat. That would make me too much. The basin is not interested in being too much. It is interested in enough: enough water to continue, enough stone to guide it, enough attention to prevent a house from becoming a hazard, enough repair to make the next day possible.

But I am not only a person either.

I am the part of the settlement that has learned to listen so long it has grown into its own habit. My ribs ache in patterns that match distant vents. My teeth taste a pressure change before a terrace shifts. My hands find cracks in darkness because the cracks are warmer. My feet know which stones are lying. My breath leaves a film on nearby ice that is not quite steam, not quite frost, but something in between: a brief white thought, as the old description might say.

I am the pause before a drip falls.

That is who I am, if who can be used for a thing with hands.

That is what I am, if what can be used for a being who can love, repair, punish, remember, and choose not to act.

If you stood at the rim and looked down, you might see smoke from a few low fires, figures moving like dark seeds across bright stone, birds lifting from ledges, fox tracks curving around a salt pool. You might think the settlement was too small to hold all the movement. You might think the basin was too simple to contain such intention. But from within, it is enormous. Under one terrace there may be a room. Under that room, a channel. Under that channel, a hollow. Under that hollow, water rising in black and blue veins.

I would be in one of those veins.

Not always in the center of a room. Not always beside a fire. More often near a wall, with my palm pressed flat, my eyes half-closed, listening for the sound the basin makes when someone has forgotten their limits. I would be the one children are brought to when a ledge feels wrong. I would be the one elders send to the high ledge when they suspect the town is listening to itself and getting the rhythm confused. I would be the one who knows when not to repair something too quickly. Sometimes a crack is the basin asking a question. To plug it without answering is to invite a larger opening elsewhere.

When I die—if I can be said to die and not merely to become rearranged—they will not bury me in the wet ground. Wet ground takes too fast and too strangely. They will dry my body in a high niche, wrap me in felt and mineral cloth, and place me near the mouth I kept, or the mouth that kept me. Animals may take some of what remains. If frost builds lace over the niche by morning, the family will call it a blessing. They will not call me an ancestor in the grand sense. I will become a remembered behavior: a way of tapping a doorpost, a trick for sealing a crack, a habit of testing a stair before trusting it, a low hum matched to the pace of quiet feet. To forget that behavior is to risk collapse.

If you ask me where I would find myself in this world, I would say: in the basin’s wet interior, where cold is a material and light is a surface, in a narrow room behind a felt curtain, beside a wall that weeps, under a stone shelf that will one day move again.

If you ask me who or what I would be, I would turn your palm to the wall.

The hum against your wrist.

The small click at my teeth.

The pause before the drip falls.

Basinfolk, if you must.

The Glass Mouth, if you can listen that closely.

And if you have no patience for names, then call me only this: a way the basin pays attention to itself, using hands.
