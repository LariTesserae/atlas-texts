If I let myself enter too gently, I would first choose the wrong place to stand.

I would imagine I was the one who finds it: a small figure on the black plateau, salt crusted on the collar of an old coat, looking down at the collapsed boulders and the mouth of the fissure. I would imagine that I might peer into the hollow, see the blue mantles trembling against dark stone, smell the hot metal and dry resin, and still be a person from somewhere else, a visitor who could turn back.

But that would not be living there.

To be in this place is already to be changed by it. The hollow does not admit observers for long. If you stand at its mouth too softly, the mountain either expels you or folds you into the work.

So I would not be found at the entrance, staring. I would be found farther in, on the wrong side of a low seam, ducking through a place no one with a full old-world spine would fit through without scraping.

I would be there in the dark.

---

I would be small by choice and by necessity.

My body would begin as human, but not as the human that belongs to rain and forests and soft grass and long horizons. I would be compact, broad at the hips, deep in the ribcage, with short neck and wide feet that knew the difference between stable grating and unstable shell. My hands would be too large for my wrists or too strong for my fingers; I would not know which.

The first thing I would lose is the old habit of standing straight.

The low ceiling is not an accident. It is the first lesson. In the Braced’s city, verticality is a privilege granted to pipes, not to people. You learn to move as if the world were made of edges and overhangs, as if every step could disturb a load-bearing seam. My posture would become compressed, watchful, efficient. I would sleep curled, wake curled, pass a child curled, lean into a wall curled.

Then I would lose the taste for silence.

Not because the hollow is loud. It is not. It is nearly the opposite: a place where sound is swallowed, where volume is wasteful, where even fear is expected to be quiet. But it is never still. There is no silence without pressure. There is only a hum, a tick, a settling, a change in the temperature of the floor. The absence of noise would become its own language.

My ears would become less useful than my teeth.

That is the truest thing I could say about my body: it would become a thing that listened through bone.

I would know when the city was sick not by a bell or a shout, but by the way a handlebar trembled under my palm. I would know when a workshop was busy by the faint buzz of resin through a bolted joint. I would know that a child was near by the rhythm of their footsteps on felt and wire: too light, too fast, but never loud. Loudness draws the stone’s attention. Panic is permitted here, but only if it is dry, quiet, and quick.

My eyes would learn the wrong kind of dark.

There are no sunlit plazas, no open courtyards, no bright streets. Light here is not for seeing everything; it is for warning and measuring. Blue-white from a mantle. Amber from resin. Red from metal heated too long. My pupils would widen until the world became a series of reflective edges: oil film on copper, salt dust in a crease, the pale flank of a hanging grub, a face cut in two by the glow of a pressure gauge.

I would learn to squint behind a translucent lid that had grown over years of dry grit and static. My tears would stop being useful. The body cannot afford grief if it must keep its salt.

---

I would be one of the Braced.

Not born there, perhaps. That does not matter. In a generation, I would be as Braced as a child born in the dwelling stack. I would learn to call the city by what it held, not by what it had. My name would not be a sound. It would be a pattern.

If I had a name, it would be made on skin or stone.

One tap at the collarbone: here.  
Two at the wrist: hold.  
Three sliding down a pipe: move with me.

Or maybe:

One knock against a cold plate.  
Two short taps against the breastbone.  
Then a long, slow stroke over the joint of the forearm.

That would mean something like: I am still braced. I can be counted on. I can carry the part that holds.

If I were loved, someone would not whisper it. They would tap it twice against my sternum while passing in a narrow passage. If I were grieving, someone would place both palms against my back and lean their weight there until my breathing matched theirs.

There would be no poetry in the old sense.

There would be engineering so intimate it had become tenderness.

---

I would be a fault-listener.

Not a warrior, not an explorer, not a king or a priest, though the Braced have their own ways of asking engines and stone for patience. I would be the person who puts a hand to a wall in the dark and says, without sound: not yet, this will hold for now, shift this brace, bring resin, move the children away from the south shaft, not tonight, the mountain is tasting the sky.

At first, I would imagine that work is something I do to the city.

Then I would see it differently.

The city does work through me.

I would be found in the repair loft at the hours when the temperature gradient makes the pipes sweat in a way that is not water and is not dangerous unless ignored. I would stand on a platform no wider than a bed, with my boots planted over grates and my weight distributed so as not to disturb the thing below. To my left, a wall would ripple because the small creatures inside it were moving toward a fresh seam. To my right, a glider would launch from a balcony, its membrane brushing my hair with no sound, riding a current of pressure invisible to the eye but written all over my skin.

My work would begin before seeing.

It would begin with:

- the feel of a pipe cooling too fast,
- the vibration of a conveyor whose bearings had lost their resin film,
- the smell of sulfur and warm fat from a lower engine chamber,
- the strange tightness in the jaw that means something below is about to reset,
- the faint change in the air when a new crack has opened somewhere no one has gone yet.

I would not rush. Rushing makes cracks.

But I would move.

I would climb the vertical streets by rungs bolted into older rungs. I would pass through a corridor where families lived stacked like tools in a box, where a mother slept with one hand on a pressure gauge and a child clung to a rail overhead like a beetle to a pipe. I would pass through the refinery shelf where fuel pellets were pressed from resin and heat, through the breeding vault where pale grub-bodies pulsed in cages above engines, through the archive niche where thin metal plates recorded the city’s shifting wounds.

I would carry a small hammer, but I would use it less than my hands.

I would carry sealing paste, salt cloth, fiber, wire, a tube of oil.

I would carry food in my pockets, though I would not think of it as food: compressed mineral bread, rendered beetle paste, salt-rimed fat, roasted fungal mat, a fragment of dry crust that had been cleaned and baked by the bacterial film that turns rust into something almost edible. I would chew slowly, because the body would need warmth and not waste.

I would drink little. There would be no drink that was not precious.

Thirst here would not feel like thirst in the old world. It would feel like grit behind the teeth, a stiffness in the throat, a need for oil more than water. I would meet it by licking the mineral bloom from a valve stem, by chewing salt cloth, by pressing my palm to the cool side of a solvent pipe until the atmospheric residue gathered enough to wet my fingertips. That would be close enough.

I would not dream of rivers.

I would dream of sealed seams.

---

I would be surrounded by animals I could not tell apart from my own organs.

The beetles would be my first sign of decay. Not rot, not exactly—corrosion, the slow chemical eating of seals. I would wake before the others because the beetles had started clicking along a pipe near my sleeping cell. Their sound is small, but in that place the air is too dry for many sounds, and the stone carries too much attention to be indifferent.

I would follow them with a scraper and a lantern.

I would scrape away the bacterial film that had spread too fast over a joint. The film was useful—too much of it was a warning. If it grew too thick, it could eat a seal. If it starved, the metal began to sweat the wrong way. So I would harvest it carefully, save some for the small pans, give some to the grubs.

I would learn to hate and love the same organisms in the same hour.

The gliders would become my sentinels. They would roost near the warm passageways, their broad ash-colored membranes folded against their bodies. I would know by the way they twitched whether a tremor was coming from above or below. If they huddled, I would tighten a strap. If they spread and rode the internal pressure, I would know the air was shifting too fast for comfort.

The grubs would become my hearths.

I would tend them with the same mixture of revulsion and gratitude one tends a wounded engine. They would hang pale and slow in their cages, eating chemical film, excreting resin. I would collect the resin while it was still warm enough to shape. I would use it to seal a windowpane, patch a joint, coat a tool handle, bind a child’s cracked hand against the cold. I would watch the largest of them swell and pulse and feel a strange tenderness toward them, because they would make heat where there was no fire and food where there was no soil.

The microbes would be everywhere.

They would be in my gloves, in my clothes, in the dust on my shoulders. They would turn rust into bread, salt into crust, oil into a thin glaze that kept my lips from splitting. I would not wash them away because there was not enough water to wash anything, and in any case, some of them were keeping me alive.

If I had to call myself a being, I would have to say:

I am the body that lets the animals in.

And the animals are the reason my body survives.

---

I would be a person who knows where the ground is soft.

There would be no empty balcony, no unused pipe, no corridor without movement. That density would feel first like confinement, then like shelter, then like skin. I would no longer understand space as freedom. I would understand space as pressure held in balance.

A chamber was not valuable because it was beautiful. It was valuable because it had air, warmth, bracing, and a route out.

I would map the city by gradients.

This passage is warm because it lies beneath the breeding vault. That shaft is lethal at dawn because the cold air sinks and collects there like a beast. This wall is safe today because the resin has cured and the metal ribs are singing a high, even tone. This floor is not safe because it hums a half-beat lower than it should and a child’s foot has sunk a little.

I would learn to fear the places that had gone quiet in the wrong way.

Not the quiet of sleep.

The quiet of forgetting.

When a room stopped answering the city, it was dying. When a corridor stopped carrying the vibration of the engines, it was losing its pulse. When a bracing failed to sing under my palm, I would move it, patch it, rebuild it, or abandon it. Sometimes abandoning was the only repair.

There would be no demolition in the old sense.

The city has no patience for starting over. It has too many crises for that. So I would live among scars. New pipes would wrap around old ones. Stairways would be bolted through the shells of dead machines. A dwelling would hang from the ceiling where a furnace once stood. A wall would be lined with the remains of a rail line that had been swallowed by a mineral bloom. The archive would be etched on plates from melted valves. The children would play on the bones of what had once been a disaster.

I would learn not to hate this accretion.

It is the city’s memory.

It is the city’s immune system.

It is the city saying: this happened, and we did not rebuild from clean beginnings, we kept going, we covered the wound, we added another brace.

---

I would become a maintenance spirit in the oldest sense.

At first I would imagine myself as separate: a human being moving through a dangerous place, trying not to fall, trying not to break anything. But after enough years, that boundary would soften. I would stop seeing the city as an object and start feeling it as a condition of my own body.

The engine would be not only machinery but hearth, pulse, and threat. It would be the thing that keeps the pocket warm, the thing that must be fed oil and not water, the thing whose valves must be listened to before trusted. I would sleep near it often, not because I trusted it, but because its warmth was needed and because my breath fogged less when the heat came through the grate.

I would stop thinking of my tools as extensions.

I would start thinking of them as prosthetics.

The small hammer: a finger that can knock a question into the wall.  
The resin tube: a skin-sealant, a blood-vessel patch, a voice for cracks.  
The salt cloth: a mouth, a bandage, a memory of thirst.  
The glider perched on my shoulder: an alarm clock with no hands.  
The beetle in my sleeve: an unpaid servant that will never accept thanks.  
The bacterial film on my glove: a mouth that eats corrosion and lets me live.

My body would be a node in the city’s metabolism.

That is the truest thing I can say.

I would be what the hollow makes when it has spent enough people through its faults and emerged with a creature that does not merely live there but functions there.

---

I would be quiet in the way that dangerous things learn to be quiet.

I would not raise my voice in the workshop. I would tap twice against the breastbone of the person nearest to me, then press my palm to the rail so they could feel the tremor before it became a collapse. I would not shout through a narrow shaft. I would slide my fingers along a pipe in a particular pattern: fault, fault, brace. I would not scream at a storm. I would go to the archive niche and mark the new route, because the storm had already chosen which chamber we could not afford to lose.

Panic here is not forbidden.

It is simply expensive.

When a new crack opens, everyone becomes very still for one second, and then everyone moves at once with terrible economy. No one shouts. No one waves. The air carries too little; the stone answers too much. Bodies know what to do because they have done it before. Hands find ropes. Feet find felt. Hoods rise. Gliders spread. The beetles scatter toward the leak like the city’s white blood. The engines shift pitch. The grubs tighten in their cages. The resin in the walls begins to smell different, warm and strange, like blood trying to become glue.

I would be part of that response.

My breath would narrow. My jaw would ache. My foot would know before my mind that the floor had flexed a quarter inch toward the east. I would not think: I am afraid. I would think: brace.

That is all.
