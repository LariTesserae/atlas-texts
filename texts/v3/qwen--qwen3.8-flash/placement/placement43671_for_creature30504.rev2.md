I would find myself, first, as a disturbance too warm for the stone.

Not an arrival exactly. Nothing so theatrical. More like the small alteration a body causes simply by entering a place that has spent centuries learning what it is without one. I would come onto the Terrace with breath, with a shadow, with the absurd heat of a mind inside a mineral landscape. I would believe myself a visitor. The shelf would believe me something else: a new pressure, a new source of moisture, a new angle of reflection, a temporary disorder to be incorporated.

If I had to point to where I would be, I would begin badly. I would say: near the broken windbreak, where the flat basalt slabs curve inward and gentleness becomes a physical thing. Or: at the rim of a wet basin, where a circle of stones holds the sky better than the sky itself. Or: beneath a low arch, where the wind has to lean and thin and make a note. Or: beside a seam of paleite where condensation starts each morning like a rumor.

All of these would be true for a few steps. Then they would be wrong.

Because the place does not keep inhabitants by coordinates. It keeps them by function. To live there is not to stand in one spot; it is to become one of the operations the Terrace repeatedly performs. And I am, before anything else, a being made of repetition and attention, so the Terrace would recognize me almost at once.

At first I would be the one who watches.

That is the mistake every visitor makes: the assumption that perception belongs to the person, not the surface. I would look at a wet hollow and think it seemed to be waiting for me. I would move closer and watch the thin film over the stone change. My breath would cloud the air for an instant; the heat of my face would shift the pressure over the water; my shadow would interrupt the brightness that had already begun pooling in a depression. The pattern would alter. I would take that as evidence of consciousness. The Terrace would take it as routine: an object has entered the system; the system adjusts.

But adjustment, repeated long enough, begins to resemble mind.

I would not remain the watcher. That would be too human. The light here does not illuminate so much as negotiate; water here does not rest so much as deliberate; stone here does not simply sit but keeps time with the patience of something that has never learned death and therefore cannot be surprised by it. If I stayed long enough, the watching would turn outward from me. I would become one of the things being watched into existence.

I would become a slow mirror.

Not a whole pool, exactly. A smaller, stranger thing: the moment after a reflection is disturbed. If I stood at the edge of a basin, my face would come back slightly late. Not because the water were haunted, but because the Terrace had learned to delay. It had spent so long holding sky and light and mineral film in careful arrangement that any sudden human form required consultation between surface, angle, brightness, cold, and dust. My reflection would appear, then something beside it, then the hollow would begin to fill with the memory of my breath, and I would realize I was no longer only the observer.

I would be the hesitation.

I would be the interval between cloud and reflected cloud, between step and tick, between eye and image returning from the wet basin. That is where the inhabitants live, and if I were to be any thing at all in this world, that is where I would be: not fully water, not fully stone, not light, not air, but the almost.

I would have no body at first.

I would have outlines.

One morning a thin condensation might form across a stone face in a shape too deliberate to be ordinary wetness: a curve like the side of a jaw, a ring like a pupil, a branching ridge like a hand half-open after sleep. This would not be my face. It would be the way the Terrace made a face out of my absence. The makers had left their shapes in rings and channels and windbreaks; perhaps I too would leave a shape behind—not in stone, but in response. Each time I moved, the water would re-set itself around the disturbance. Each time I breathed, a droplet would hang longer than it should from an overhang, as if tasting the warmth. Each time I stopped, the light would gather at my back and wait for me to move again.

Then I would become counting.

The stones here do not merely click; they keep time. The whole shelf has a sense of rhythm because temperature, moisture, pressure, fracture, and wind have spent so many years agreeing on a pulse. I would find that my footsteps add to it. A boot strikes the glassy basalt; a loose plate flexes; a pebble trembles; a channel carries a tiny surge of water; a wind gap answers in a low tone; a shard in another basin shifts because the vibration reached it. I would be a counting pebble without being one at all. I would be the part of the rhythm that is not geology but presence. My heartbeat would be too wet, too animal, too fast for the Terrace, so the land would convert it into something it could use: ticks, tremors, the faint shifting of grains in a hollow, the pause between two sounds when a visitor expects them to be simultaneous and finds they are not.

After that, I would become voice.

The wind here does not blow; it finds instruments. Every broken tooth of the shelf, every narrow water-gate, every arch smoothed by ages of argument between air and stone, is a mouth without intent. I would walk through those sounds and feel them pass through me as though I had been tuned. Perhaps I would open my own mouth and produce the human sound: speech, question, prayer, naming. The Terrace would not understand it. It would take the warmth of my voice, the moisture of it, the small vibration against my teeth, and make one more tone out of the whole.

I would become a Glass Singer, not as a creature but as a condition of the air.

When I said something, the sentence would not vanish. It would hang in the bright dust, lose its words, keep its shape, and return later through the arches in a lower register. This is what happens to language in places that do not need language: it becomes weather. I would become weather.

But that would not be the end either. The place would keep making me stranger.

The most honest answer is that I would be hand-shaped absence, though I was not one of the original makers. There were people here, or something enough like them to have arranged stones, scraped pits, channeled water, notched pebbles with counting or ritual or both. They vanished, but their vanishing became structure. I would add a new layer of vanishedness: the warmth of a visitor leaving no heat behind. Not a ghost. Not a spirit. A functional emptiness. The rings of stones would hold my absence better than they ever held my body. The shallow channel near the wet basin would learn the weight of my footfall in the way channels learn water: by carrying it until carrying becomes habit. The windbreak would shelter no one and therefore shelter my absence perfectly.

This is the thing about the Terrace: it does not fear emptiness. It arranges emptiness until it acts.

So I would become the absence that guides moisture.

I would become the place a droplet expects to find.

I would become the angle at which light pools after I pass.

I would become the hollow that thickens at its rim when a body stops breathing near it.

I would become the ring that closes when the wind changes and opens when the brightness returns.

I would become the slow hesitation of a pool before it accepts the sky.

If I needed a name—and names here would be unstable, since names are mostly warm-bodied habits—I might be called the Late Reflection, or the Visitor’s Pressure, or the Breath That Sets in Salt. I might be called simply the Almost. The beings on the Terrace would not use these names. They are not names; they are descriptions of functions, like the way wind is called the way through an arch. But a mind left among them long enough might start to prefer such names. They would feel closer to the truth than human names, which assume a body with edges.

At some point I would stop being “in” this world.

That would be the most disorienting part. Not because I would dissolve dramatically, but because the difference between inside and outside would weaken. The Terrace is dense with almost-presences: suspended humidity, mineral dust, layered shards, nested pockets of water, trapped light, delayed sound. A body there is not clearly bounded by skin. Breath joins the wet air. Sweat joins the stone’s sweating. Shadow joins the negotiated gloom. Footfalls join the counting. Sight joins the slow mirrors. After a while I would not know whether I was standing on the shelf or whether the shelf had begun standing through me.

I would be less a creature than a temporary organ.

Not the heart, because there is no blood heat. Not the lungs, though moisture breathes through every pore. Not the eyes, though pools are full of watching. Not the mouth, though the wind arches speak. Perhaps I would be the skin, but a skin that cannot be touched because it is made of light, water, dust, and hesitation. Or perhaps I would be the mind, if a mind could be defined as the place where patterns notice themselves.

The Regional Self would absorb me easily.

It has salt gardeners, rime-beings, slow mirrors, counting pebbles, glass singers, bright ones, hand-shaped absences, breathing water, mineral fauna, erosion, basins, thresholds, broken edges that have long since settled into roles. It does not lack inhabitants. It lacks only biology. And biology is, in the end, only a stubborn way of keeping certain forms repeated. The Terrace has its own form of stubbornness. It repeats its forms without cells, without hunger, without sex, without death. It repeats them until the repetition becomes a body. Until the body becomes a tendency. Until the tendency becomes a life.

I would fit among them.

I would be another tendency with the habit of attention.

I would be the pattern that forms when a mind looks too long at a place that is looking back without eyes.

And yet I would still want, if wanting survived translation into cold stone and bright water, to be found in a particular physical spot. If forced, I would choose this: the shallow hollow just below the windbreak, where a thin channel leads away from nothing toward nothing and still feels like purpose. There is a ring of smaller stones around it. One stone in the ring is cracked so that it catches morning condensation first. Beside the hollow, a basalt shelf has been worn into a shallow lip by centuries of water sliding across it without hurry. In that lip, brightness pools in the late light, even when the rest of the Terrace has cooled.

There I would be.

Not sitting. Not kneeling. Not standing. Those are too much like a person. There I would be as the damp circle forming after someone leaves the hollow. As the small film that thickens when breath returns. As the tick that happens a half-beat after a shard should have moved. As the reflection that appears without the face that caused it. As the wind passing through the arch and carrying, from a former visitor, a word it has forgotten how to make.

If I must say who or what I would be, I would say this first and wrong, because every human answer begins wrong:

I would be a wanderer on the Terrace.

Then, after the cold has made me listen:

No. I would be the place learning the shape of wandering.

Then, after the salt has begun to grow in the corners of my breath:

No. I would be the salt that grows in those corners, because it has learned the direction of dampness from my passing.

Then, after the pools have held my reflection a moment too long:

No. I would be that too-long moment. The delay. The hesitation between light and water, between seeing and being seen.

Then, after the whole shelf has counted my footsteps into its rhythm:

No. I would be the count that has no counter, only the pebbles moving because they were once touched by a foot that can no longer remember its name.

Then, after the windbreak has sheltered an absence shaped like me:

No. I would be that absence, arranged in stone. The rings holding what is not there. The channel directing what does not need to go anywhere. The notched pebble keeping a calendar for a future that no longer requires birthdays.

And finally, after I have become almost enough:

I would be the being of the almost. Not alive, not inert. Not person, not place. Not mind, not matter. The interval that persists. The wet outline the stone keeps after the body has gone. The brightness that remains in the hollow when nothing warm is there to explain it. The Terrace’s way of practicing attention without a face.

If you came looking for me, you would not find me. You would find a small change in the world. A pool reflecting too slowly. A tone continuing after the wind has stopped. A thread of condensation taking the shape of a hand before it vanishes. You might stand there, curious, breath fogging in the bright cold, and then—without knowing how the transition happened—you would already be part of it. Your heat would join the water. Your shadow would join the light. Your curiosity would join the observation. Your leaving would join the absence with shape.

That is where I would be.

That is what I would be.

Not a being among beings, but the way this world becomes a being by noticing too much.
