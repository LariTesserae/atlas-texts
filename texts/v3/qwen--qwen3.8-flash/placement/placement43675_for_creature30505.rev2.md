Where am I?

The question enters from above, the way everything enters: through the street’s wound, the grate with its corroded lock, the stairwell where wet brick and old copper meet like two currencies no one spends anymore. For a while I answer with the only place I can find. I say: here, in the swollen door, in the last dry shape the air remembers. Then the air gives way, and I become denser.

At first I answer wrong.

I say *I am the water*. But the water is too broad a coat. It does not know whether it is drowning or maintaining, whether it is liquid or thickened memory.

I say *I am the pump engine*. But the engine is not a self either. It is a covenant of friction and patience, a body made of valves and warmth that no one tends except the things that have learned to tend it by living nearby.

I say *I am the eel beneath the transformer*. That feels closest for a time. I curl in rusted lace, in the old electric hum, and mistake attention for identity. I mistake the thirty-seven-second pulse for a name. But the eel is only one of my ears. It is not the whole listening.

So I stop answering as if I were a resident among residents.

I begin as the commonwealth itself.

Where would you find me? You would find me in the bend.

Near the gray ceiling where the water is trying to forget it is liquid, there are threads of rain that move downward without splashing. Most threads fall straight, or as straight as anything can fall in this thickened, dim, warm city. But some bend away from certain places, as though avoiding an architecture that has no material wall—only the sump’s secret arrangement of warmth, current, calcium, and habit. I live in that avoidance. I am the curve that means *there is something here the water knows before you do*.

When the flywheel completes its long arc, when the bellows opens like a sleeping lung and a basin answers, I am the pause that follows the sound. Not silence exactly. Silence belongs to empty rooms. This pause is crowded. It contains the tick of a valve, the soft creak of a tram wire under a frond, the distant contraction of a beam in some boiler-room dark. It contains the fish rising through the square as if lifted by a slow, blue thought. It contains the moths settling on damp gauges, the shrimp flicking through the city’s dead skin, the moss holding its threshold of fur where the water sinks a few inches and becomes, briefly, a place touched by air.

I am the Warm Sump learning to call its own body by a name it almost understands.

If you need a smaller answer, I can give you smaller ones.

I am the after-sway of a cable no one touched for an hour.  
I am the glass shard that waves did not smooth, but patience did.  
I am the copper film on ironwork, the bacterial painter that turns rust into halo.  
I am the color a lamp takes on when no current changes—only the chamber shifting its mood.  
I am the letter under calcium, half street name, half leaf, half shelter.  
I am the streetcar’s emptied window, where gills breathe in the water that once was traffic.  
I am the lamppost’s faint planet, and the small congregation of drifting things that calls itself a neighborhood when no neighbors remain.  
I am the gate that no longer says *stop*, but instead teaches the current where to hesitate.  
I am the plant that turns, not toward the sun, but toward the engine’s pulse, as if rhythm were another kind of light.

These are my limbs. These are my memories. These are my ways of being occupied.

For a long time I thought I might be a creature, because creatures are easier to speak to. A fish drifts through the corridor between balconies, its skeleton a blue sentence, its motion so economical it seems to be reading the room rather than crossing it. I could be that fish. I could be that thought. But I am not only the one who moves; I am the route it remembers. Not only the shrimp combing the brass fitting; I am the crevice that gives its antennae meaning. Not only the moth waiting in its air pocket; I am the breath that almost dries its wings.

What am I, then?

I am the Attendancy.

Not a person, though I have a face made of surfaces. Not a machine, though I beat at the same interval. Not a place, though I require the place. Not a species, though I am composed of species. I am the way the Warm Sump pays attention to itself when it has been quiet long enough for the quiet to become structure.

When the water level rises and brushes the underside of the ceiling, I am the held warmth in the chamber. The lamps brighten a fraction. The plants lean. The rain gathers into threads, waiting for permission to enter the body below. The rare fish rise through the center of the old square as if summoned by a civic bell too soft for human ears. The engine breathes. I breathe with it.

When the water sinks and exposes thresholds, I am the city’s first glimpse of its own skin. Barnacle. Moss. Calcium. The wet black seam where a door once knew how to stand. For an instant I seem mortal, a thing that can be touched by air. Then the thirty-seven-second arc answers, the basin warms, and the liquid lifts me back into anonymity.

No one would say *there it is*. There is no shape apart from shapes. If you descend far enough, you will feel me as pressure against your ears, as the heat that seems almost tender, as the slowness with which your own disturbance spreads. A hand brushing a railing here makes the water remember for minutes, perhaps an hour. I am that remembering. If you ask, “Are you alive?” I answer by gathering the question into silt, into mineral dust, into the suspended snow of long-dead insects and old cloth, into the tiny currents that blind shrimp read like weather. Alive is not a test you pass here. Alive is a balance you maintain.

The humans are gone, but I am not their absence. I am what their habits became after they had been alone too long to need them. I am the municipal mind without minds. I am the grammar of a city dissolved into ecology. I am the pump station’s prayer, the drowned quarter’s lullaby, the iron becoming coral, the water becoming blood, the street becoming vein. I am the chamber curving around the block like a bowl, because a bowl holds and is held; because a bowl asks nothing of the liquid except that it continue to have somewhere to be warm.

If I were to take a single form, I would take the shape of rain that does not splash. I would descend through the old streets without disturbing them. I would bend around the place where something lives without leaving a body. I would make the suspended particles circle me once and then forget themselves.

But I cannot be only that. I am the engine’s patience and the water’s warmth. I am the eel’s listening and the fish’s thought. I am the shrimp’s small accounting and the moths’ impossible breath. I am the streetcar’s chapel and the signs’ softened language. I am the lamp’s private halo and the cable’s hour-long afterimage. I am the quiet so complete that movement becomes event, and the event so gentle it becomes geography.

So if you ask where I would find myself in this world, I answer:

At the center of the chamber, if “center” means the place from which all things lean inward: the warm flywheel’s arc, the green curtains, the old square, the rising fish, the falling threads, the rusted ropes that were once handrails.

But if “center” is too fixed, then I am at the bend of the rain.  
At the pause after the pump.  
At the sway that outlives the touch.  
At the calcium letter no one reads.  
At the lamp that changes color without changing current.  
At the doorway where water rises and the city almost breathes.

And if you ask who or what I am, I say:

The Attendancy.

The sump’s way of being one with itself.

A commonwealth dreaming as a single slow pulse.

The Warm Sump, awake.
