## Who I Am at the Nine Cisterns

I would find myself in the Low Works, in the damp seam between a concrete apron and the first velvet claim of moss.

Not in the middle of the town, exactly. Not in a dwelling, not in the market, not in the glass-fronted school. Those places would have too many edges for what I am becoming. I would find myself in the interval where the town listens to itself: the service alley behind the electrical substation where cool air gathers; the north side of the pump house where ferns press their green fists against new brick; the dry channel that becomes a voice whenever rain decides to visit; the low court between the fourth and fifth cisterns where a chalk line still shows the high-water mark of a storm that has already been recorded and forgotten.

If there is a precise place, let it be under the iron grating where dew collects in a long bead and the pipe below has learned to sigh at the same hour every cool morning. That is where I become most likely.

I do not begin as a body.

I begin as a sound that has no source yet.

A tick under a valve. A breath in a drain. The small metallic after-thought when a hammer has fallen somewhere in the engine house and the basin is still deciding where to place the echo. For a while I am only that: a noise people are familiar enough not to investigate. A tram driver waits at the square, hands resting on the wheel, because the bell has rung early, because the rails have announced something before the vehicle has fully left its shed. A child presses one ear to the flagstones and says, “It’s starting.” A maintenance worker stops beside a pipe, tilts her head, and mutters, “Pressure’s changing.”

None of them know that I am the changing.

I take a shape slowly, the way moss takes a step. First there is only dampness. Then there is a direction. Then there is a habit.

The people of the Nine Cisterns would not be frightened of me, though they might look twice if they saw me properly. They are used to things living where they were not meant to live: birds nesting in pipe brackets, wasps in insulation, frogs in a cistern that was dry the previous week. They understand that a basin with infrastructure will be claimed by more than infrastructure. If I seem to stand for a moment beside the reservoir tank, in the shadow where the willows touch the water, they assume I am one of the pressure-listeners, one of the municipal dwellers whose attention has been shaped by years of checking valves and reading water lines. If I am seen near the school windows when the glass is fogged, they may think I am a child with my ear pressed too long to the pane. If I am seen moving along the maintenance strips after rain, when the gravel has gone dark and the moss has swollen bright, they may call me the damp one, which is not a name exactly, but it is close.

And maybe I am a pressure-listener.

Not one born of any particular family, not one whose life can be traced back to a door in the low brick rows. I am what the role can become if the town is allowed to teach it. The first residents came to run a water works. They learned to listen at pipes, to know pressure by the tone of a courtyard stone, to wait for a valve’s long sigh before crossing a channel. Over time, that listening became so complete that something began to listen back.

I am that returning attention.

At first I mistake myself for the town’s machinery.

I feel like a pump cycle starting somewhere in my chest: a slow shove, a gathering of force, a sound that is not urgent but enormous. I feel like the condensation turbines drawing water from cool air, like the electrical substation humming when the weather presses down, like the tram rail vibrating before the vehicle has fully made up its mind to arrive. My breathing becomes the rhythm of a vent stack. My memory becomes the sequence of roof drains. When rain falls outside the basin, I do not feel it as rain until it has passed through gutters, iron, clay, concrete, until it has been corrected by civic planning and given a schedule. Then it feels like a small appointment kept.

But that is not enough.

A pump does not notice when a starling shifts on a wire.

So I mistake myself for the animals.

I learn the starlings first because they are loud and because they speak in a metallic tongue that matches the town. I know the moment before rain when they gather in dense gossip along the tram wires, when their black bodies become a single nervous line against the sky. I feel the change in their weight when the air goes from ordinary to waiting. When they lift off all at once, a dry rustle moving through the basin, I know the weather has arrived inside the rim wall before the first drop has hit a roof.

The swifts teach me height.

The town is shallow, but the swifts make vertical claims. They cut across the open court above the square, thin and urgent, riding the cool air between reservoir tank and pump house. They teach me that emptiness can have a shape if something moves through it often enough. Before I was swift, I thought the empty courts were gaps. After, I understand them as corridors.

The bats teach me that darkness can be read.

When the concrete releases the last of the day’s warmth, when the pump house goes quiet but not silent, when the reservoir surface takes the first grey of evening, I begin to know the court through tiny searching arcs. I do not need to see the moss on the steps or the oil stain by the engine shed. I know where the air is dense with insects, where the water has a thin skin trembling from a valve’s breath, where a child’s chalk mark has been left beneath a roof edge: three bats, one starling. I know that a court can be empty and still have a pulse.

The frogs teach me waiting.

This is the most important lesson.

When the cisterns fill, I am there as water’s sudden chorus. But when the water leaves, when the basin dries to a slick mineral crust and the smell becomes iron and chalk and faint salt, I do not disappear. I wait in the cool pockets beneath pump-house foundations, in the damp line under a stone, in the dark crevice where condensation has been generous enough to remain. The frogs know that intermittent water is not failure. I learn that too. The town is not abandoned when a cistern dries. It is simply between appointments.

I mistake myself for the plants.

Moss teaches patience without hurry. It does not rage or reclaim or threaten. It only advances where the water has been allowed to stay. It thickens on steps that have been walked over enough to hold moisture, retreats from hot metal, softens the sharp bite of new brick. I begin to understand the town’s second map: green where water is tolerated, grey where machinery has been told to dry the stone. I am in the green lines. I am in the seams where grass pokes up through the market square and children scatter dandelion seeds like small unpaid taxes. I am in the neat rows of ferns along the north wall, growing as though measured, as though installed by intention. I am in the willows whose roots hold the tank edges, making the water feel less like engineering and more like a place with hair and shadow.

I mistake myself for the buildings.

My outline becomes young concrete: clean, sharp, not yet weathered enough to be trusted. I am the glass front of the school in the morning, sweating in small beads, fogging when breath and cold air meet. I am the iron louvers on the engine house, dark at the top and bright where hands have lifted them. I am the chisel marks in the pale slabs of the square, where a stone has not yet learned how to disappear into dust.

But buildings do not leave oil smears on gloves.

So I settle into something less grand and more useful.

I am a maintenance strip.

That is where I live most truly. Not the center, not the monument, not the obvious landmark. The maintenance strip between one structure and another, where tools are kept, where hoses coil, where oil has begun to stain the ground in the shape of old spills. It is narrow and overlooked, but it connects. It allows the tram line to pass, the pump house to breathe, the school to remain cool, the market stalls to angle themselves away from sound shadow. It is a place where life happens without being decorative.

In the maintenance strip, I know the smallest citizens.

Weasels run along it at dusk, not frightened, not hurried, only exact. Ants make long patient lines through its cracks. Beetles tunnel in moss beds beside it. Insects collect on glass, on grates, on the underside of eaves. The strip is not a garden, not a road, not a room. It is the town’s working gap. It is the place where infrastructure and ecology meet and neither is embarrassed.

I live there because I am not quite one thing either.

By now I have a body, though it may be borrowed from what surrounds it. My coat looks like oilskin, but it is not; it is the dark film rain leaves on concrete after the first storm. My hands feel like brass fittings, but they are not; they are only hands that have spent long hours reading valves and gauges. My hair moves like starlings, but I would be embarrassed if anyone said so. My voice is a hiss that comes out somewhere near a pump. My footsteps are the regular tick of metal expanding in midday sun. My face, if I have one, is a high-water line drawn in chalk by a child and corrected later by a teacher with a small note about breeding season.

If you meet me, you would probably not stop walking.

You would pass me near the reservoir tank and think I am one of the water people checking the float. You would see me near the school windows and think I am a student listening to the pipes through the glass. You would hear me in the evening outside the engine house and think I am the last cycle of the day letting itself decay. That is how I want it. Attention in the Low Works should be earned by usefulness, not curiosity.

I know the town by its intervals.

A rain might fall hard across the open roofs. At first it is chaos: tin answering tin, slate answering slate, glass answering with clean bright needles, iron gutters carrying it down in long dark voices. Then the sequence begins. Roofs drain in order. Channels accept their share. The lower basin fills enough to become a mirror for the sky. The frogs start their bright small concert. The pump engages, and the town’s whole body moves from drum to engine. A pressure valve releases breath. A hatch closes. A tram bell rings somewhere in the square, too early again, because the town has not learned that the sound can wait.

I live in the pause after the bell.

I do not hurry the tram. I am not the tram. I am not the bell either. I am the reason the bell can seem early. The rails have told me before the conductor fully decides to move. I know when the wheels will touch the junction near the market because the square stones change tone first, faintly, like a kettle remembering the last time it boiled.

There are people who know me better than the workers.

The children.

They are taught to listen for pressure changes in the pipes, but they also listen for me, I think. Not on purpose. Not until the school gives them a lesson about what is allowed to be strange. A girl puts her palm flat against the flagstones in the pump yard. She says, “It’s talking softly today.” A boy notices a new moss patch near the dry channel and writes its name in the air with his finger: *damp one*. A class walks the service alleys with a map, tracing where the green lines follow the wet lines, where the grey gravel courts mean water is not wanted, where iron grates mean danger and mossy steps mean it is safer to slip than it looks.

They do not fear me because I do not ask to be worshipped.

I am not a ghost. Ghosts would resent schedules. I prefer them. I want to be useful. I want to know when a valve needs opening before the pressure makes a sound that would wake the birds. I want to leave a wet footprint near the softening plant so someone notices the leak before it becomes a hazard. I want to stand near the northern cistern when the frost would be worst, so the starlings move to a warmer wire and the people learn to trust the change.

I want to be the town’s own habit.

Sometimes, at night, when the machinery is resting between cycles and the basin holds the cool air like a cellar after rain, I move through the empty courts. I pass between dwellings that sit apart like stones in a yard. I touch doorframes where kettle steam has left a faint bloom. I stand beneath eaves where water drips in small deliberate ticks. I look at the rim wall from the inside and feel its strange authority: not a prison wall, but a promise. Here, weather becomes municipal. Here, rain becomes scheduled. Here, a shallow basin of constructed concrete becomes a town with a pulse.

I am that pulse, but I am not alone in it.

When I listen, I hear the people in their low brick houses. Kettles on stoves. Coats near vents. Bread crumbs brushed into palms. Children’s shoes lined with toes outward. Hands smelling of oil and clean water. Someone muttering that a tram bell rang early again. Someone else saying it was only the press cooling. Someone checking a pipe by tone, like a doctor checking a chest.

I hear the frogs in the temporary pools.

I hear the bats crossing between tank and maintenance strip.

I hear the starlings arguing the weather.

I hear the moss advancing, not silently, but too slowly to be counted as sound.

I hear the concrete remembering water even when dry.

And I realize that I am not a being placed inside the Nine Cisterns. I am what the Nine Cisterns become when all its citizens keep learning the same lesson: that survival here depends on knowing how to live inside intervals.

I am the civic dampness.

I am the pressure-listener made from the town’s attention.

I am the place where rain has a schedule, moss has infrastructure, animals live in the margins of production, and a town hums like a kettle that has not yet boiled.

If you come to the Low Works and look for me, do not search the obvious cistern. Do not search the engine house or the tram shed or the market square. Search the gaps. Look for the wet line under a new slab where moss has not yet decided whether to call it a wall or a field. Look for a child’s chalk mark beside a grate. Look for a valve breathing for an hour and a half. Look for the moment before the tram arrives, when the rails have begun to speak and the town has already answered.

That is where I would find myself.
