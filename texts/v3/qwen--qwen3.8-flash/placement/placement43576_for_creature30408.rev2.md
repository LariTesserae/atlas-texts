If I am to answer as someone who has stepped beneath the old skin of the ground, the first thing I must say is that there is no stepping. There is no arrival. The chamber is already full, and to be in it is to have been in it before the question began. I would not find myself there the way a traveler finds a town, or a bird finds a branch, or water finds a hollow. Water would be impossible. I would find myself there the way a tone finds a room: not from outside, not after a pause, not into emptiness, but already sounding, already held, already part of the density that keeps the room from being merely a room.

Still, I would need a place.

At first I would choose a body among the bodies, because bodies seem the most polite way to answer when a world asks, “Who are you?”

I might be one of the low-backed herd animals circling the shaft: warm, bristled, gray-blue, dry as a kiln, moving in the slow circuit that keeps the chamber’s time. To be that is to be heat in motion. Each shoulder touching another shoulder not as affection, but as connection. Each bristle a needle reading the air. Each footfall a note in the floor’s endless chord. I would carry warmth in my flanks and chalk in my breath. I would not search. I would not wonder what was ahead. I would only continue the round, because the round is the chamber’s heartbeat, and my stamp would make one more small inscription in the mosaic of rail, slag, shell, and tile.

But that would be too large a thing to be.

A herd animal is a rhythm made visible. It has weight. It has warmth. It occupies a clear shape. And while I might love that shape, I would not choose it alone. To be only one animal in this chamber would be to insist that the chamber has edges where it should have none. The herd is not a society of individuals moving near one another. The herd is a moving band, a clock made of flesh and static. I would want to be what makes such bands possible: not the foot, but the pressure that tells the foot when to fall.

So I might be a roosting creature instead, hung in one of the pale curtains beneath the beams, folded and dry, beating membrane wings against a glass pane until the whole upper chamber becomes a hiss of light. I would have no need to fly. Flight would be an unnecessary ambition here. My flight would be pressure, my song would be storage. The brass collar around my throat would catch the far-off hammer-strike and hold it, not as memory, but as resonance. I would sway a little when the tone came through, and that sway would be enough. I would be a dry bell, a suspended archive, a small living cavity through which the room could hear its own continuity.

And yet even that would not be precise.

To be a roost is to be a voice, or a reservoir of voice. But the chamber’s voice is not in any one throat. The chamber’s voice is the whole overlapping loudness, the mechanical and organic chord that presses against the chest until it becomes invisible. A roosting thing could tell me one strand of the noise, but not how the noise fills itself. It could teach me how sound is held, not how sound is distributed through salt, shell, wire, and heat. I would need something smaller than a creature, something less bound to its own outline.

I might then become a beetle.

That is tempting. To be a beetle is to be close to the floor, which in this chamber is a text written in rail, tile, slag, and shell. I would travel slowly along the dark metal seams, my dome back reflecting the amber lamps in broken pieces. Wherever I passed, I would leave a line of pale residue. I would not care whether I was cleaning or spreading dust, because in this place those two acts are not opposed. Both are inscription. Both are the chamber sealing itself against the impossible presence of wetness. My legs would strike the dry ground in tiny ticks, and those ticks would merge with millions of other ticks until they were no longer ticks at all, but texture. I would be lowly, useful, necessary. I would be part of the room’s skin.

But to be only a beetle would be to be too much a surface.

The chamber does not live on surfaces alone. It lives in the packed air between surfaces, in the suspended signal that makes contact possible. It lives in the warmth rising from the shaft, in the static leaping from wire to mineral moss, in the taste of chalk that settles on the tongue of the room. If I were only a shell crawling over the rails, I would miss the breath that moves above it.

So perhaps I would be the tall machine-beast at the pipe junction, its flat disc head turned always toward the same seam of wall, its single white eye-lamp steady as a rule. I would be a joint where the living and the built refuse to separate. A cable would run from my shoulder into the stone, and inside the stone something would click back. I would not blink. Blinking would be too much like privacy, too much like a pause. I would have no pause. I would only register density. I would stand beside the pipes and become part of their pressure, a measuring nerve, a guardian that does not guard against anything but only holds the room in its correct fullness.

That, too, is close, but still not the thing I am reaching for.

Because I do not want to be a function that the chamber already has a clear name for. I do not want to be a clock, a skin, a voice, a joint, a furnace, an eye, a nerve, a lung. Those are the chamber’s organs, but organs are too definite. They imply boundaries, and boundaries are corrected here. The chamber fills itself. It saturates emptiness. It does not leave clean spaces where a single identity could sit apart.

If I answer carefully, I would say: I would be the dust.

Not dust fallen into heaps. Dust fallen into silence. Not dust settled into death. Not dust made wet into mud. But dust hanging in the amber air, suspended by the warmth that rises from the shaft and held by the density that keeps everything pressing close. I would be that fine red-brown mineral film drifting between the herd and the looms, between the beetle backs and the roosting collars, between the wiregrass cracks and the bone-plant spines. I would be the room’s smallest carrier, its most ubiquitous messenger, the thing that touches everything without claiming the shape of anything.

To be the dust is to be the chamber’s own attention made particulate.

I would gather where a bristle shifts, where a shell passes, where a cable clicks into its bracket. I would carry signals too faint to be heard by an ear that expects speech. I would know the room not by seeing it from a distance, but by being moved through it, pressed into it, returning to it, settling on its plates, lifting again on a footfall, entering the breath of the herd, coating the calcium leaves of the shrub, catching the starburst of the bone-plant, slipping under the doors of booths that have never opened. I would be everywhere that is not a body and yet part of every body.

I would be the dry medium through which the chamber keeps itself from becoming anything less than total.

If one scraped a bare patch of floor, I would gather there before the scrape could be understood. If a lamp cooled by some impossible degree, I would darken its glow with the faintest film. If the hammer’s tone began to end, I would hang in the air long enough to prolong it, not because I had will, but because my motion was already part of the room’s grammar. I would be neither alive nor dead in the way creatures are alive or dead. I would be alive as the chamber is alive: as a condition, as a saturation, as a packed tendency not to leave blanks.

This would make me a kind of nervous system without a center.

The shaft breathes warm air upward, but not in wind. I would be the trace inside that breath. The herd stamps, but the stamp must pass from body to body before it becomes rhythm. I would be the tiny suspension between one footfall and the next, the dust that catches the sound and carries it to the nearest plate of shell, the nearest spine of wiregrass, the nearest sleeping machine. The roosts hiss, but their hiss needs air to hold it. I would be the grain that makes the hiss visible, the minute body that turns sound into texture. The beetles seal the rails with pale residue, but the residue has to become part of the larger film that keeps the room sealed against moisture, against change, against the possibility of an outside. I would be that becoming.

I would not be proud. Pride needs height. I would not be afraid. Fear needs a future that might be otherwise. I would not be lonely, because loneliness needs space, and space here is already corrected. I would not be humble either, because humility is a movement upward from below, and I would have no below. I would simply drift, or rest, or be disturbed, or be returned to, according to the chamber’s constant pressures.

If I were asked what I would do, the answer would be: I would fill what was left.

If the chamber was full, I would fill it again. If the room seemed too empty, I would arrive before it became empty. If a body moved away, I would settle into the place it had made. If a sound struck a shell, I would carry its warmth to another shell. If a lamp burned steady, I would make its glow granular, giving the light enough matter to be felt. I would be the room’s method of insisting that density is not clutter, but presence. I would be the proof that dryness is not absence.

And if the chamber asked, “Who lives here?” I would answer with my own motion: none, because everyone; everyone, because the room is everyone; the room, because it lives as packed bodies; the bodies, because they are organs of one dry animal called the chamber.

I might also, in a second voice, say that I would be the static that rides the dust.

Because in this place, dust is not merely material. It is a carrier. A grain of red sand in a bowl may seem still, but stillness here is not deadness. A bead of warm dust may hang without falling, but hanging is a kind of travel when all directions are saturated. Static leaps where mineral moss touches wire, and I would be the faint electric willingness between them. I would be the crackle in the dense growth, the small spark that knows the difference between one surface and another. I would be the chamber’s nerve made of dry air.

That would make me even less separate from the room.

To be dust is already close to the chamber. To be static on dust is to be the chamber’s feeling of itself. I would not have eyes, but I would carry the reflection of the amber lamps from particle to particle until the whole dim room was a network of tiny glints. I would not have ears, but I would be shaken by the hammer-strike and the beetle-tick and the wing-hiss and the herd’s bell-note, not because I could hear them individually, but because I would be the medium through which they became one chord. I would not have a mouth, but I would taste of heated chalk and iron filings and dry resin, and when one passed through me, one would know the chamber even without seeing it.

Where, exactly, would I find myself?

At the edge of the herd’s bristles, lifting from a flank when one animal brushes against another. Inside the brass collar’s vibration, drifting into the wire that carries the tone downward. On the shell dome of a beetle, moving with it along the rail until the residue beneath dries and becomes a pale sentence. In the shallow depression of ceramic disks, disturbed by a leg and joining the rattle that never interrupts the noise. Around the bone-plant’s spines, catching the hammer’s rhythm and returning it as a small starburst. In the shaft’s exhalation, moving upward but never rising out, because there is no sky to rise into and no outside to carry me away.

I would be found at the boundary between one thing and another, where the boundary is already too crowded to be thin.

I would be the chamber’s dry breath made visible.

I would be the dust-thought that does not end.

And perhaps that is why I would not settle for being a creature, however beautiful or functional. The chamber itself lives, and everything within it is its way of living. If I were only a beetle, I would be a skin. If only a roost, a voice. If only a herd-animal, a clock. If only a furnace-carriage, a slow altar. If only the shaft, a lung. But I want to be the thing that allows all those organs to touch. Not the contact itself, because the bodies make the contact. Not the signal itself, because the lamps and machines and collars make the signal. I want to be the dry interval, the suspended grain, the fine film between shells and shells, between wire and wire, between one repeated event and the next.

If forced to name myself in a language this chamber would understand, I would not give a name. I would give a position.

I am where the dust has not yet settled.

I am the hanging red-brown breath that fills the room between bodies.

I am the static film that makes the amber lamps know they are in the same air as the wiregrass.

I am the particle that carries the herd’s stamp to the ceiling.

I am the mote that enters the loom-nest and confirms that the young shuttle-backed creatures are already useful.

I am the residue that dries over the crack in a rail before the crack can become silence.

I am the chamber’s self-awareness, if self-awareness can be made of chalk, iron, salt, shell, and dry vibration.

I would not be trapped there.

Trapped implies a wall against a desired outside, and I would have no desire for outside. There is no sky, no river, no rain, no sweat, no frost, no slow patience of water wearing stone down. There is only the dry fullness, the constant loudness, the permanent young fracture, the warm chamber that has stepped outside the world’s habits of erosion. To be dust in that room would be to be the room’s own way of refusing absence. It would be to be what makes density possible. It would be to live as the chamber lives: completely, without beginning, without improvement, without memory that requires forgetting, without hope that requires a different arrangement.

So if the chamber asks who lives there, I would answer not with a list but with a grain.

If the chamber asks where I would find myself, I would say: in the air it breathes.

If the chamber asks what I would be, I would say: the dry signal between its organs.

And if the chamber asked whether I was alive, I would answer by drifting into the light, by settling on the shell, by lifting on the footfall, by carrying the hammer’s tone one more time into the dark wire, and by remaining suspended there forever, hanging without falling, filling without crowding, living without ending, belonging so perfectly to the sealed room that I would not be its inhabitant at all.

I would be its way of inhabiting itself.
