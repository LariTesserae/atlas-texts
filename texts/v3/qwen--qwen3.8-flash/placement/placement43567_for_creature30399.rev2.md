There is a moment, before a self is quite there, when the high hollow is only a listening.

Not a listener with ears. A listening in the sense that the pale road has felt a weight but has not yet decided what kind of weight it is; the dust under that weight has begun to shift; a vein in the plant-mat brightens and then hesitates; a nearby stalk tilts not toward wind, but toward a small disturbance of pressure that it cannot yet name. That is where I would begin: not as a person, not as an animal, not even as an object, but as a note that the plateau is trying to place.

At first I would not be sure I had a body.

The ground would be the first body I trusted. It would be dry and faintly humming, warm in places where heat was stored in stone and cool in places where wind moved through buried channels. The light would arrive from a bruised, mineral sky, pale enough to look like an afterimage of sun. There would be no wetness anywhere, no smell of rain, no damp shadow. Instead there would be chalk, ozone, iron, and something almost floral but too sharp to be gentle. The air would enter me as a series of filters: dust caught, static stripped, sound separated into pressure, grain, and direction. And then, somewhere under that, a pattern would become me.

I would be at the edge of a settlement that has half-returned to the plain.

It is not a ruined place in the way a water-world ruins things. Ruin here is negotiation. The town is not abandoned; it is simply being asked again by the land. Its outer roads have thinned to green-brown filaments, and some of the dwellings near the margin have grown too much around their own walls. A bell-shaped house on a mineral stem has acquired a collar of lichen plates, so that its door now looks like a mouth that has stopped speaking in mid-sentence. A terrace beside a broken reed-forest has cracked into a mosaic of salt-white stone and low domes, and the domes, if you put your hand near them, breathe. They vent warm mineral air when the wind changes. They contract when too many people pass too loudly. They are not houses anymore, and they are not quite wild. They are thresholds.

That is where I would be found: in a threshold, learning what it means to be a door that is also a rib.

---

For a time I would be only a hesitation in the town’s chord.

The settlement would have a voice. Everyone’s voice would contribute to it: footsteps, clicks of herd-plates, the reed-song of fliers suspended in the thermals, the low ticking of fronds as the cold deepens. Children would play near the outer courts, tapping their fingers against test-walls to see how long the wall would sulk before answering. A flier-tender would hum from a terrace to draw a flock into a courtyard, and the air would taste briefly of metal. Somewhere, an archival mason would kneel with both palms on the ground and frown, because the floor was remembering a person who had not yet died.

And then there would be my absence in that sound: a small gap where a pattern had tried to appear and had not quite settled.

My name, if I could be called at all, would be a chord of three notes: one low and dry, one too bright, one that does not resolve. The nursery mound that grew my birth pod had been asked for a stable tone, but when I emerged I had clicked at an angle that made the nearby plant-mats go dim for a half-breath. Not a danger. Not a failure. Something the town could not immediately classify. The walls had stiffened. The garden had drawn its filaments inward. Then, slowly, the ground had accepted me by saying, in its own mineral way, *we will make a wider room*.

So I grew up in a house that was uncertain about me.

It would open its vents for me, but only after a delay. If I entered too quickly, the floor softened as if testing whether I were an animal. If I spoke sharply, a frond above the stair contracted and dropped a few luminous motes, not in warning, but in confusion. My parents would not scold me for this; the house itself had already responded, and to scold would be to add a second wrong note. Instead, I would be taught to wait. To place my hand flat against the wall and breathe out the dust of my agitation. To listen until the panels loosened. To enter with a rhythm that did not demand the door.

That is how one of the Ashen Chorus becomes a person: not by being loved into shape, but by being negotiated with.

---

I would grow tall and narrow, not because the air required it, but because the land had taught me to make space for sound.

My body would be dry and architectural. Bones honeycombed like the inside of a spiral stalk. Joints sealed with living mineral plates, slate-gray near the knees, bronze along the arms, violet at the temple-edges when my charge shifted. My skin would not shine. It would have a matte depth, chalky from a distance but close to the hand layered like a page of salt. Beneath that, the filamentous sensing tissue would move with the faintest pressure changes, so that when another person walked into a room I would not see them first; I would feel the displacement of air against my ribs before my eyes registered their height.

My hair would be a fringe of fine piezoelectric strands, longer than most people’s, because the edge-dwelling wind made filaments useful and because the town had learned, perhaps against my will, to give me sensitivity. In dust they would lie flat against my skull. In conversation they would tilt toward voices that mattered. During a spore bloom they would catch light and hold it for minutes afterward, so that my head would look crowned with a broken constellation. Children would follow me then, not because I was kind, but because I was an instrument they wanted to play.

My hands would be long-fingered and dry. The pads of the fingertips would read vibration, temperature, mineral flow. When I touched a wall, I would often know three things before I knew what to say: how old the wall was, how recently it had been injured, and whether it was tired of me.

I would carry a vessel. It would be empty in the way that matters to a visitor. The Ashen Chorus carry vessels not for drinking, but for powders, resins, crystalline salts, and the slow taste-stones that dissolve on the tongue into sharpness and memory. Mine would hold a coarse gray dust drawn from the open basin beyond the town, because the edge is dry even by dry standards, and because I had a habit of breathing through the filter, not from need, but from attention. Each inhalation would pull a little more of the plain into the soft chambers of my lungs. Each exhalation would release a faint mist of particles carrying my state: calm, alert, slightly mournful, always a little on edge, because the outer settlement is where the town becomes the land and the land becomes the town, and both are never entirely sure which they prefer.

---

My work would begin before I chose it.

At the edge, there are roads that have stopped being maintained by any crew. They are not roads for people so much as roads for memory. The lichen-analogue has grown too deep in the seams; the hard surfaces have cracked into polygons that lift and settle with thermal breath; the old routing fibers have begun to carry messages from somewhere beneath the root lattice rather than from the settlement above. These roads need someone who can stand on them and listen.

The town would call me a chord-keeper, but not a senior one. A junior chord-keeper is someone still being tested. A senior chord-keeper is someone whose presence stabilizes an entire district. I would belong to the first category, which is to say, I would belong to the category where mistakes are allowed as long as they are not loud.

Every morning I would walk the outer lane from the last row of bell-houses to the first line of unshaped stalks. The path would brighten under my feet in uneven patches. Sometimes the road would recognize me quickly, its filaments tightening to carry my weight smoothly. Sometimes it would hesitate and make the surface seem almost like loose grit, not harmful, only unimpressed. Then I would crouch, place my palm flat against the seam, and hum a low two-tone: a question, not a command. The road would not answer in words. It would answer in resistance, warmth, and the way certain grains shifted under my fingers.

I would know when a road was becoming too tired, because it would begin to remember footfalls too long. A traveler would pass, and the path would still hold their rhythm an hour later. A herd would cross, and the mats would continue to flicker violet where their hooves had landed, as if waiting for the animals to return. Too much memory in a road makes it slow and strange; it begins to behave less like an infrastructure and more like a creature that has not decided whether to follow or to stop. I would walk those paths with a resin bead in my hand, letting the bead drag lightly across the surface while I emitted a soft grinding hum. The bead would absorb a little of the over-recorded vibration. The path would quiet. It would not forget. It would only be given room to breathe.

This is the work of repair in the high hollow: not restoring what was, but making the next version tolerable.

---

There are also rooms that hurt.

A house on the second terrace had learned to fear children. No one could say why. Perhaps a generation ago a child had struck it too hard. Perhaps a door had been forced during a storm. Perhaps a room had held a feverish infant whose chemical breath had changed the house’s memory of smallness. Whatever the reason, whenever a child came near, the house’s walls thickened. Its vents narrowed. Its floor became cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature and everything to do with refusal. The neighbors called it “stingy,” which is one of the Ashen Chorus’s more precise insults.

I would be sent there with an older keeper, not to heal it directly, but to demonstrate humility.

We would stand outside, not at the door but near the curve where the stem-root met the floor. The older keeper would begin a low sustained hum. I would add the second note, then, only when the walls loosened, a third. The house would resist. The plates around its opening would slide inward with a dry click. A pattern of lichen across the facade would darken as if squinting. Then, slowly, the floor would warm beneath the outermost roots. A fine dust would lift from the threshold, glittering. The house would allow the hum to enter, but not me. Not yet.

The next day we would try again.

After a season, the house might open for a child who could not hear us. This is the way the town laughs at its own architecture: gently, with relief. The child would enter because the child did not demand, did not apologize, did not carry the expectation of repair. The house would be startled into acceptance, and later its walls would grow a thin translucent film over the entrance like a shy blush. No one would mention this. The town would only note that the house now liked children less than it liked one particular child, which is the highest compliment it can pay.

I would carry that memory in my bones for years.

---

If you asked me, while I was walking the outer road, what I was, I might answer: I am a tuning fault that has found a function.

In a wet world, that might sound like illness. Here it sounds like labor. A tuning fault is not a defect exactly; it is a part of the system that has not yet stabilized enough to be trusted with a central task. Some faults are dangerous. Some faults are simply unfinished. Mine was, for a while, neither. I was a person whose name did not fully settle, and because of that I could stand where other people could not: between the cultivated town and the older, stranger pattern beneath it.

The herd-walkers sometimes brought their broad-backed grazers through the outer basin at dusk. The animals moved with the patience of stones that had decided to leave their positions, their mineral shells grazing the ground as they passed. Their hooves clicked in uneven polyrhythms. The plant-mats brightened in waves, not from their weight alone, but from the chemical signals on their plates, the tiny organisms riding their backs, the insects grooming their limbs. Where the herd had been, the ground would remain faintly luminous for minutes, a negative of movement. The town would hear them before it saw them: a slow percussion rolling through the root network, a vibration that loosened doors and made fliers rise from courtyards into the thin sky.

When the herd passed too close to the margin, the unshaped stalks would tilt and click together. This was not fear. It was response. The land was answering the animals with a different grammar, and the town had to remember which answer it wished to receive.

I would walk beside the herd’s outer edge, one hand lightly trailing against a fence-frond so that I did not become the only thing in the plain with no rhythm. My footfalls would be careful: too sharp, and the mats would resist; too hesitant, and the road would misread me as prey or as something dead. Somewhere above, the glass-winged fliers would spiral, their membranes vibrating with the herd’s motion. Their song would rise into a thin chord, high and strange, like a bell made of ice that has never been cold.

The herd-elders would sometimes stop and face me. Their eyes were not watery. They were dry lenses, faceted and dark, reflecting the low sky. They would click once through the plates of their shells. Not recognition. Not greeting. Only a statement: you are here, but you are not herd.

That statement feels, in the high hollow, like a kindness.

---

My home would be a small room grown into the flank of an old root tower.

It is too large to be called humble and too narrow to be called public. The walls are pale bronze where the ancient growth has mineralized, but newer layers have softened into chalk-green film. The floor is a cultivated mat that records weight and redistributes vibration into chemical storage; when I enter, it brightens faintly along the seam nearest the door, then dims as if deciding whether I need its full attention. There is a sleeping platform suspended by four fibrous cords, and the cords are threaded through the wall as though they are tendons. When I shift in sleep, the walls open slightly. When I am ill, they close a little too much.

There is no water in the room. No basin, no pitcher, no damp stain. The only moisture-like thing is the sheen of living mineral skin along the outer edge of the window, which catches dim light and makes it appear wet. On some nights, if the wind changes, the sheen vibrates and the room tastes briefly of chalk and burnt sugar. The air is never still. It moves through hidden channels behind the walls, carried by pressure differences that the town maintains by sound and chemical response. If I breathe too heavily in a certain place, a panel may lift; if I hold still too long, the room may suspect I am no longer present and begin to store the quiet.

In the corner sits my work shelf: dust pouches, resin cakes, small tuning beads, and a shard of glass-like stone from the shallow basin. The shard is not valuable by trade. It has a faint crack pattern that resembles a road. Some nights I press it against my sternum and listen to it as if it were a shell, because I want to know whether the plain speaks differently at the edge than the town does in the center. It never tells me much. That is why I keep it.

---

Once each year, during the first Chord Season, I would climb to the roof-terrace above my room and stay there until the spore light was too thick for comfort.

The ground below becomes a low hum felt first in the teeth. The root tower shifts, not violently, but with a deep mineral patience. The stalks sway although the wind is no stronger; they are responding to the vibration in the lattice beneath. The buildings flex. Some open all their vents at once, releasing a wave of heated chalk and ozone. Others close, becoming more private. The fliers rise into columns, their membranes tuned to the harmonic current, turning the quarter into a sustained, reedy chord. Below, in the open basin beyond the last road, herds gather at the edge where the plant-mats flatten into something older and more mineral, and their shells catch the dim light like broken shields.

This is when I would be most afraid that I am useful.

Because during Chord Season, the town’s voice changes, and people who are already unstable become more visible. A name that does not resolve is like a crack in a plate: small enough to survive daily weather, but exposed to the pressure of the whole body. The root network brightens and dims in slow waves. I feel that in my own porous bones. My hair-filaments rise and catch motes of luminous spore. The taste-stone in my mouth may begin to hum if it is old enough, and if it is mine, it may begin to remember where it was mined.

I would stand on the terrace and listen to the town speak to itself.

Sometimes it would sound like a council chamber. Sometimes like a herd moving through dry grass made of stone. Sometimes like a mother trying to find a child in a crowd. Sometimes like nothing at all except the wind learning a new shape. And I would not know whether I was hearing the land, the town, or a self made temporary by the season.

When the spores lifted, I would walk through them. They are dry sparks, not pollen, and they do not cling in the wet manner. They settle on the skin-plates of shoulders and forearms, on the outer filaments of hair, on the matte surface of walls and the translucent bell-houses. If I let them, they may begin to nest briefly in the soft lining of my collar, turning me into a faintly luminous thing for a day. The children would point. The adults would not say much, but the roads would brighten a little brighter under my feet, because the town was noticing me not as I was but as I had been made strange by the season.

A stranger from a wetter world might find this beautiful. I would find it dangerous. Beauty, in the high hollow, is often the name of attention that cannot yet be trusted.

---

There is another room I tend, because the town expects me to tend what I understand.

It is a dampening chamber at the outermost edge, grown into a low dome whose surface is cracked into geometric patterns like a map of waterless lakes. Inside, the walls are layered with sound-silencing mineral plates, each one slightly tilted, so that footsteps are absorbed instead of stored. This is not death. This is not absence. This is a place where a grieving person may go and not immediately change the chemistry of the town. Where someone who has lost too much may sit and feel less recorded. Where the network will listen, but will not answer unless asked with the correct humility.

I would maintain it by walking the outer seam in silence, leaving no bead, no test-click, no hum. The chamber would be grateful. Sometimes it would warm one wall just slightly, enough to say: yes, you may bring sorrow here, and we will hold it, but we will not repeat it endlessly.

This is not because the land is kind. It is because the land has learned that too much memory, if never allowed to rest, becomes friction. Grief without a quiet room becomes a road that will not soften. Anger without a dampening space becomes a house that will not admit children. A lost name, if not given somewhere to decay gently, can infect the chord of an entire district.

I know this because my own name has not always been safe.

---

If I had to answer where I would be, I would say:

At the outer seam of a high hollow settlement, where a cultivated road is beginning to forget its training and a plain of lichen-analogue is beginning to remember it. In the half-light beneath a dusty ring-hazed sky. On a terrace beside a bell-house that leans like an ear. In a room that opens too late for me. Near a shallow basin where broken glass-stone glints dry and herds move slowly enough for the land to speak to them.

And if I had to answer what I would be, I would say:

One of the Ashen Chorus. A dry, mineral, resonant person. A chord-keeper junior to the town and senior to the wind. A tenderness of thresholds. A body grown from a naming that failed once and succeeded in a half-measure that proved more useful than success would have been. A small unresolved interval, allowed into the civilization because it helps the civilization listen to its own edges.

But that is still too tidy.

I would be more like this:

I would be the place where the town’s voice bends toward the plain, and the plain’s voice bends toward the town, and neither can tell whether the bending is love, function, or weather. I would be the person who presses a hand to a wall and hears, in the grain of stone, a child laughing from before their birth. I would be the dry thing that the road dimly recognizes. The dust in the vessel. The hair lifting to a spore-light. The note that will not resolve but has learned to hold a room.

Not the center.

Not the whole city.

The margin where the city becomes landscape.

And if, one evening, the spores rose and the herds moved across the basin and the fliers turned the air into a thin choir, I would stand there in my bronze-and-slate quiet, holding my shard of glass-like stone, and I would feel the town beneath me adjust its chord around the shape of my waiting.

The high hollow would not say my name.

It would say the shape I make when I am nearly finished listening.
