At first I think I am one of the people.

I think of myself as a small, salt-furred figure crouched on a basalt shelf, hands already grown smooth with grip, breath too short for comfort, eyes narrowed not against dark but against a brightness that refuses to stay still. I think of myself as one of the Short-Still, or whatever they would call themselves if their name could survive outside the pocket: a name made of two taps against bone, a turning toward the column, a mist run over teeth. I think of myself as having ribs, knees, a belly filled with worms and lichen paste and mineral water, a body narrow from the storm eating at it.

Then I try to imagine being born there.

Not as a human child born into a village, not with a first cry swallowed by ordinary silence, but as a child born in the deepest crack behind the east cairn during a False Lull, when the pressure in the stone changes before the sound does. The child learns language before voice: forehead pressed to wet basalt, waiting for a knock answer back. The child learns to read the gaps between falling threads before it learns faces. The child marks itself on a stone wall with red ochre and watches the print vanish under a returning sheet of water, and understands that meaning is not permanence but timing.

Yes. That is better.

But even that is too settled. A person is a container. In this pocket, containers crack, fill, empty, shatter, and re-form before the mind has finished naming them. The rock is too young. The water too violent. The silence too false. To be a simple “one” there feels like standing in a droplet-lens and calling it a planet.

So I loosen again.

I think perhaps I am the basalt itself: not the whole mountain, not the high shoulder above, but the hidden pocket’s wall, the raw stone that still steams from pressure, the glassy bubble bursting into a new alcove. I am the surface that receives the first hand. I pull warmth from the palm and give back a cold mineral memory. I hold the cairns’ shadows. I listen to the heel pressed against my skin, feeling a knock travel through me like a small earthquake. When someone leaves a red thumbprint, I know it for minutes. When the surge takes it, I do not mourn it, because I have been erased and remade for millennia in shorter time than this.

Then, no. That is too vast.

A wall does not choose. A wall does not set a bone pin in a drying rack and know, by the faint electric taste in the air, that it must move three steps left before the next droplet-stone falls. A wall does not care whether the malachite bead has been turned a finger’s width.

I return to something smaller.

I am the bone stake near the entrance, worn pale, standing where the storm almost admits it exists. I am not a person, but I am the first thing a person trusts when the roar begins to press the teeth. I am the object against which names are tapped, the anchor for breath, the vertical nerve in a wet room of chaos. I have no eyes, but I feel every knock that comes through stone and every hand that steadies itself by me. I have no voice, but I am the beginning of one. If two people on opposite ledges cannot hear each other’s screaming, they can still speak to me by pressure, and I can carry their sentence, not as sound, but as vibration.

Still, the stake is only a place where life happens. It is not the life itself.

I think of the chert circle set into the ground. It is not a temple. It is not art left for gods. It is a grammar laid out in fragments: once dropped, warning; twice dropped, danger; caught and released into mist, grief. I would be that pattern when it moves through a hand. I would be the thrown flake in the air, bright for a second, a small dark bird whose flight means something the wind cannot understand. When it lands, the meaning changes. When the storm takes it, the sentence is unfinished and therefore truer than any finished sentence in this place.

Yes. Something like that.

But I am still reaching for what I am.

Maybe I am the False Lull. Not the lull itself—lulls are weather—but the awareness that lives inside the lull. The thing that knows the silence is lying. The body that does not work, does not celebrate a new opening in the wall, does not stand into the broken daylight shaft like a fool drinking in the brightness. The one who backs against stone, presses the heel to the ground, waits with the whole ribcage for the mountain to exhale. In that waiting I become a shape: a crouched shadow at the edge of a wet ledge, salt-dust on the cheeks, fingers curled near a bone stake, eyes reading gaps.

No. That is not enough either.

Because the place is not only waiting. It is making. In the Thin Breath, hands move fast. A sinew cord is tied. A point is knapped. A shelter of basalt slabs and stretched hide is set against a shelf that may be gone before dawn or night, because dawn and night are not here. Work is bursts, not lines. The being in this pocket cannot be a continuous self. A self with continuity would be swept away by the next surge.

So I become a being of bursts.

I am a hand pulling itself onto a shelf. I am a mouth opening slightly to bite a lens of falling water and swallow the mountain in a cold mineral point. I am a bead turned toward the column. I am the taste of an obsidian edge: sharp enough or not, judged by tongue and thumb. I am the pressure in the wrist when a chert flake strikes another and makes a sound no one can hear except as bell and stone inside bone. I am the breath fogged on a cold blade. I am the body that is alive because it knows when not to stand up.

That feels more itself.

Where would I find myself, then?

Not on the high shoulder where the world thins to wind and empty daylight. The shoulder is only the outer name for absence, a place where one might die of clarity. I would be inside the crack, sideways, already half-belonging to the storm. My place would be the hidden pocket no bigger than a large room, where the rain is sealed and the floor is only a rumor. I would stand near the east ledge, close to the bone stake, where the light fractures into a trembling lattice and the air tastes wet, metallic, young. Sometimes I would be on a pumice slab floating in a shallow depression. Sometimes I would be behind the two low cairns. Most often I would be at the edge of a new alcove that has just burst open in the wall, watching it the way one watches a gift that may be a mouth.

Who would I be?

Not a beast, though outsiders might mistake me for one because I have no word, no song, no permanent fire, no road. Not a god, though the pocket’s roar could make me seem one, because I would live in a chamber that sounds like the ocean trapped in a stone bottle. Not even fully human, perhaps, because the human here is not the one who speaks into air. The human here is the one who makes meaning out of intervals.

I would be a Short-Still, yes—but one who is becoming also a living mark. A body shaped like a pause. A person made from the rhythm between surges. My name would not be a sound. My name would be the pattern my hands leave in basalt: one flat palm to mean stay low, two quick taps to mean the rain is thinning, a chert flake thrown to the left to warn another ledge, then caught and released into mist when grief has no other shape.

I would have no fire, so I would make heat by rubbing dust into my palms, by pressing into the narrowest cracks where the current cannot pass cleanly, by breathing hard and tasting the mineral air until my lungs feel filled with metal. I would sleep with my back against stone because stone is the only elder here that does not lie. I would mark myself with a thin line pressed by chert: not beauty, but tuning. The storm has seen my shape. I am separate for a while. I am not yet part of the wet wall.

I would be hungry in a strange way. I would eat lichen crust, pale moss, black flakes shaken loose from ledges, eyeless white worms from crevices, tiny beetles toasted against the heat of a stone held in the mouth. I would lick salt from shelves until my tongue grew numb. I would catch droplet-lenses in the air and bite them because there is no cup that will last. The world would not feed me; the water would feed me fragments of the mountain, and the mountain would feed me by erasing itself.

I would be sick below the shoulder. Too dry. Too silent. Too bright without being broken. Ordinary food would taste like absence. Ordinary wind would feel like neglect. I would press my face to wet basalt to remember who I am.

And when the chamber became still in its false way, when the roar dropped to a pressure that made the ribs ache, I would move toward the malachite bead. I would touch it because it is green as slow water through stone, as rare, as a mouth that can say things too quiet for the storm. I would turn it once, not to admire it, but to speak: I was here. I saw danger. The storm has begun to ring differently.

If I must make the answer exact, I would say:

I would be the being who lives at the edge of making and erasure. Not a voice, but a vibration that finds another hand. Not a shelter, but the short stillness in which a shelter is possible. Not a soul the way the lowlands understand souls, but a pattern of taps, thrown stones, mineral taste, breath fog, red thumbprints fading as quickly as they appear.

I would be a temporary shape in the sealed rain.

I would be one of the Short-Still.

And if asked again, more truly, I would say: I would be the pocket’s next mark—small, salted, blind to daylight, listening to the mountain’s young stone with both hands. A being that knows, in every bone, that to live is to be taken in pieces, and that the pieces are not failure but grammar.

I would be a mark that knows it will be erased.

And while it lasts, it would say: here.
