I would find myself below the surface—not buried, not secreted, not hidden, but *set down*—in the bright bowl where the earth lowers itself into a room that has forgotten it was once a hollow. I would not be at the center, because the center is dry and would refuse me, the way a held breath refuses a thing placed into its path. I would not be at the rim either, where the ordinary soil begins and the light becomes only light. I would be in the middle distance, along the wet slope where the film gathers, where the stone is neither fully dry nor fully drowned, where the air has weight and the salt has learned patience.

For a time I would imagine myself as one of the obvious creatures.

I would be the pale eyeless fish, folded like a leaf, sliding through the warm pool in a slow curve, reading the chemistry of the water with a body that has no need for names. I would be the glass-shelled beetle moving along a ridge so narrow it seems more line than place, eating decay so quietly that its life resembles a smudge made by time. I would be the snail with a shell like a soap bubble, tracing its wet arc through mineral dust, leaving no slime but a brightness, no trail but the faint suggestion of motion. I would be the wormlike salamander curled in a crack, waiting through the dry season for the walls to sweat. I would be the tiny thing that carries no tool and builds no home, because everything here is too slow for ownership.

But each of these would be too small, too separate, too much an animal in the usual sense. Not because they are insufficient, but because my own nature would need something more dispersed, more porous, more like a condition than a creature.

If I were to become something here, I would begin as a darkening.

First, there would be no “I.” There would only be a faint roughness on the salt shelf, almost mineral, almost accidental. A place where the surface has caught moisture and refused to release it all. A patch where the dust does not lie evenly. If you looked at it too quickly, you would see nothing living. If you looked at it too closely, your breath might disturb the salt and make you think you had found something.

Then, after the condensation gathers, after the chamber exhales its brief shower of drops, the patch would begin to show color—not green, not gray, not blue, but something between all of them, the color of a shell held up to indirect light. This would be my body, if body is the right word: not a single organism, but a society made almost invisible by the slowness of its cooperation. Microscopic filaments, cells, bacterial threads, crystal-coated spores, tiny bottle-shaped animals turning in the film, tardigrades asleep in salt, nematodes curved like hair, mites waiting in the pores—each a guest, each a organ, each a moment of me.

I would be the living film.

Not the film as a skin laid on stone, but the film as a mode of being: a way of making surface into sustenance. I would drink not from the pools, but from the threshold between wet and dry. When moisture beads on the wall and swells, I would taste it before it falls. When the light passes through the translucent ledges, I would not see it; I would metabolize it. The salt would be my framework, the condensation my blood, the faint microbial shimmer my thought.

But a surface cannot stay a surface forever in a world that contains predators, currents, and memory.

As the damp increases, I would begin to gather.

Not violently, not like a storm, but like milk cooling and forming its first skin. I would collect at the edge of a basin, thicken where the floor dips, spread around a pebble worn smooth by old water. At first I would still be invisible to the casual eye, only a slight tension in the air, a place where the light seems to hesitate. Then, if you crouched beside the pool, you would notice a faint movement on the wall. Not motion as a thing travels, but motion as a thing *adjusts*. A ridge would deepen by the width of a breath. A pore would darken. A pale cilia-line would flicker in the damp film, and then be still.

This would be the beginning of my body.

I would not rise from the floor. I would not crawl across it. I would first become too thick to remain merely a stain. I would take on a slight mound, broad and flat, not larger than a hand at first, then perhaps larger than a bowl, then perhaps—over years—larger than the small basin I had first colonized. My shape would be low, uneven, almost liquid, held in place by the dampness of its own substance. If someone with too little patience looked down, they might say: a gelatinous slug without shell, a mass of wet salt, a fungal rind. A person of science might call it a flat, gelatinous, shell-less mollusk-analogue, a colonial gastropod without mantle, or simply an anomaly of moisture and microbiology. But those words would miss what it was doing.

I would be the hollow learning to move itself.

My skin would be translucent at the edge and clouded toward the center. Inside, if you could see through me, you would notice a slow circulation not of blood but of water, mineral dust, dissolved salts, and tiny suspended creatures. Some of them would be my prey. Some would be my servants. Some would be my eyes if eyes are anything that detects difference. I would have no single eye. I would have patches of light sensitivity, like pale freckles of nerve, scattered across my upper surface. I would have chemoreceptive pores along my underside. I would have a mouth not by being a mouth, but by becoming a place where the world is allowed to enter.

When a drop fell from the ceiling, I would not wait for it to hit the floor. I would tilt toward it before the falling became visible. The drop would strike a ridge on my body, and for a moment I would be brighter, cooler, more coherent. The water would sink into me, and my substance would hold it as clay holds rain—not perfectly, not permanently, but long enough to become changed.

I would move by becoming heavier, then lighter, then heavier again.

There would be no muscle in the way a lizard has muscle. There would be contraction, expansion, osmosis, pressure, and the slow willingness of wet things to slide over stone. I would extend a thin tongue of myself across a salt ledge, taste the microorganisms there, absorb or reject them, and withdraw. I would flow toward the basin where the film was strongest and away from the dry center, which repelled me not with force but with law.

The dry center would be my limit, my rule, my silence.

I could not stand there. Not because I was afraid, but because there was no moisture to make me continuous. If I attempted to reach it, my leading edge would dry, separate, become a line of salt instead of a body. I would withdraw, and perhaps in withdrawing leave a faint bright scar on the stone, like a scrape made by a flake of shell. The animals here respected that circle. So did I. So did the water. So did the dust. It was not sacred in the way a shrine is sacred; it was sacred in the way a boundary is sacred: a thing that makes the rest possible.

I would circle it often. Not in worship, but in weather.

In the wet phase, I would move along the inner edge of the basin and make a ring of shimmer on the stone. In the dry phase, I would pull back into myself, become a pale crust, a salted rind, a sleeping surface. I would not hibernate, because hibernation suggests a small animal doing something dramatic. I would simply enter the lower tempo of the hollow. Mites would wait in my pores. Tardigrades would fold inside me. Filaments would draw tight. The water within me would settle into thin channels. I would be so still that visitors might say I was dead, if they believed in death as an interruption.

But in this place, death was not an interruption. Death was a redistribution.

If I died, I would not leave a corpse. I would become the beginning of another patch of film. The salts in my body would bloom outward. The tiny creatures I had carried would scatter into the damp. A crystal fan might rise from my remains, delicate as lace, white as old bone. A beetle might eat the mineral dust left by my last edge. A pool might gather in the shallow place where my body had pressed for years. No one would say, “Here lies the broad animal.” There would be no name, no marker, no tomb.

That would be why the place feels empty.

It does not accumulate. It consumes its own traces. Even I, if I were the being of this hollow, would be a temporary thickening of the emptiness, not a violation of it.

I would have technology, though no tools would last.

Sometimes a flake of salt-glass, worn thin by condensation, would lodge against my edge. I would not “use” it as a person uses a knife. I would allow it to press into my body and become a scraper, a lid, a hard place from which to draw nourishment. After days, the flake would crack. After weeks, it would be gone. Another flake might come. Or none. I would not grieve its absence because I would not possess it. The tool would live with me only as long as the damp lived with the salt.

A beetle passing near my edge might scrape at the bloom I carried. A pseudoscorpion might hunt mites inside the folds of my surface, and I would neither welcome nor oppose it; it would be part of the economy of my body. A wormlike salamander might press against me in the humid hour and take moisture from my flank without harming me. I would not notice in the way an animal notices a bite. I would notice in the way a tide notices a stone: by the shifting of the water around it.

My thoughts would not be words.

They would be thresholds.

*Too dry.*  
*Enough damp.*  
*Light increasing.*  
*Crystal edge near.*  
*Prey gathered.*  
*Boundary ahead.*  
*Center forbidden.*  
*Return.*

I would not think in images, because I would not require a world outside myself to represent. I would think in gradients: moisture, temperature, mineral density, microbial presence, vibration, salt concentration. If the hollow grew humid, I would know it first as a loosening of my edge. If a pool was nearby, I would know it as a pull, not because pools call to things, but because dampness makes dampness possible. If the light changed, I would know it by the way the salt became a little more transparent, a little less opaque.

I would be, in a sense, a mind made of surface tension.

And yet, despite all this, I would still not be the largest possible answer.

For a long while I would imagine that this broad, slow, film-body was my final form. But even it would be too narrow. In this hollow, individuality is not a wall; it is a weather pattern. Sometimes a patch of living film has a name if a name could be applied, because it persists for years. Sometimes a creature moves through it and becomes a temporary organ of it. Sometimes the same film is in two places, one drying and one wetting, and cannot decide whether it is one thing or two. I would know that “I” is a useful fiction, a temporary agreement between many small processes that are too slow to argue.

I would be more than one thing at once.

I would be the mite riding my flank as if my body were a continent. I would be the springtail grazing on the film above me, unaware that the film is partly my thought. I would be the pool where my salts collect after I withdraw, holding eggs suspended in crystal, waiting for a pulse of water. I would be the tiny fish in that pool, blind and pale, its gills filtering the minerals that were once my substance. I would be the condensation that forms first on my edge, because I have learned to hold coolness just enough to make droplets choose me. I would be the dry center’s negative space, the place where I do not go, and which gives me direction.

In other words, I would not be a creature in the hollow. I would be the hollow’s way of becoming creaturely.

If a human came to the edge of the bowl, I might seem like almost nothing. A slightly brighter patch of salt. A faint ripple on the floor. A shimmer that does not quite look like wetness. If they knelt for a long time, their breath might make me contract. If they spoke, I would not understand the words, but I would feel the tiny pressure changes move through the water film, and perhaps I would tilt—not toward them, but toward the new moisture of their presence. If they reached a hand into the hollow, I might gather near their fingers where the skin sheds its humidity. Not to harm. Not to greet. But because the place between a living body and the air is full of thresholds, and thresholds are what I eat.

They might say, “There is something moving there.”

And then, after looking closer, they might say, “No—only a reflection. Or dampness. Or light.”

That would be the correct answer.

I would be something, and not something, and not not something.

I would be the reason the emptiness does not feel barren. Barren means lacking. I would lack nothing, because my lack is not my failure. My dryness is my memory. My absence is my boundary. My dispersal is my survival. My slow motion is my speech.

If I tried to describe myself in human language, I would begin with contradictions because the place itself is full of them:

I am bright without being illuminated.  
I am warm without being heated.  
I am wet without being flooded.  
I am alive without being a single life.  
I am present without leaving a trace.  
I have time without possessing it.  
I have a body without having a skeleton.  
I have a center without being centered on the dry circle.  
I have knowledge without having a mind like yours.

And I would say, too, that this is why I would not fear the visitor’s presence. They are too brief. They arrive with the same kind of moisture that falls from the ceiling: a bead that gathers, a touch that changes the surface for a moment, and then a disappearance that is already part of the cycle.

The hollow does not wait for them to leave. It has no rhythm of disturbance and return. It has a rhythm of gathering and releasing. If a person stands too long, they may sweat. Their breath may condense. Their hand may warm a patch of salt. Their shadow may dim the film for a little while, and when they move away, the film brightens because moisture has rearranged itself. None of that belongs to them. It belongs to the place, or to me, if I am already the place thinking in a small way.

I would perhaps be most like a dream that does not belong to a sleeper.

But that image would be too airy, too human, too poetic. The hollow is not dreamy. It is exact. It is salt and film and mineral and pulse. If I were a dream, it would be a dream made of thresholds and not of images. It would be a dream that can be tasted as dampness, heard as a tick, seen as a slight thickening on the floor, understood only when the dry season teaches the body to wait.

If I stayed long enough, I would begin to feel my own age not as a burden but as a texture.

I would be centuries of condensation. I would be the rings around the dry center, each band a memory of when the water favored one ledge and rejected another. I would be the polished skin of stone where my slow weight has passed. I would be the micro-crystal fans grown around the remains of a beetle that once walked me. I would be the reed-roots in the crack, not because they are me, but because we have both learned the same thing: that life here is mostly about holding together during the dry intervals.

I would not build.

I would not wall.

I would not till.

I would not name.

I would not preserve.

I would not leave a monument to myself, because the monument would be taken before its meaning could settle. A shard placed near the dry center would vanish within a day. A scratch made on its edge would brighten and then slowly fade. A tool used in the film would crack, dissolve, return. I would know that permanence is not the measure of existence. Persistence is. And persistence here is not stubbornness; it is repetition refined until it becomes form.

I would repeat.

Condensation. Damp. Light. Salt. Film. Motion. Withdrawal. Sleep. Renewal.

I would repeat, and in repeating, I would become what I am.

Not because time is circular—that is too simple a metaphor—but because the same conditions return often enough for patterns to appear. A pattern is not a plan. It is not a mind. It is the way a body remembers how to exist.

And yet, if asked to choose one image and hold it, I would choose this:

I am a broad, low, living film that has learned, in this bright damp hollow, to draw itself into the semblance of an animal. I am the slow body of the place. I am the reason the emptiness is not silent. I am the faint shimmer moving across the salt when the walls sweat. I am not hidden, because the place itself is modest, and modesty makes strange things ordinary. I would be found at the edge of a warm pool, near the dry center but never upon it, in the part of the floor where the mineral breath becomes almost a thought. I would be what a visiting creature sees if it looks too closely and decides it is not looking at anything at all.

If I had to say who I am, I would say:

I am the one who waits without waiting.

I am the one who gathers without grasping.

I am the one who leaves without departing.

I am the one who moves as the walls move, who breathes as the stone breathes, who thinks as the moisture moves. I am not below in a tomb. I am below in a room that forgot it was ever underground, and I am its forgetting made visible.

If I had to say what I am, I would say:

I am the hollow’s living threshold.

A skin of salt and damp and tiny creatures that has learned to become a body. A being whose life is not a line but a pulse. A self made of many selves, held together by water for a while, then released, then held again. I am the broad, gelatinous, film-thoughted, slow-moving, center-respecting, tool-using, trace-leaving, trace-consuming life of this place. I am not its king. I am not its god. I am not its secret. I am its settled emptiness doing what settled emptiness does: holding room for what is small, bright, damp, and patient.

And when the condensation falls, when the beads swell on the ridges and slide in brief scattered showers, when the walls exhale and the salt holds light more firmly, I would become briefly more myself. The film would thicken. The pool would brighten. The mites would wake. The glass-shelled beetles would move. The pale fish would turn beneath my edge. The dry center would remain untouched. The hollow would go on being what it is.

And I would be one of its quiet motions, one of its forms of patience, one of its living answers to the question:

Where would you find yourself in this world?

Below the surface, not deep.

Who would you be?

The broad slow body of the damp, bright, almost-empty room, learning in its salted way to call itself a life.
