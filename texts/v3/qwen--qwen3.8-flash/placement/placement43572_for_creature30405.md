If a person were to pause at the mouth long enough, the Dry Choir would begin to answer them as a struck bell answers the hand: not by stopping, not by speaking, but by changing the pressure inside the note.

I would find myself first as that person. Crouched. Warm. Impossible. My breath would be the only soft thing in the room, a ghost of weather carried inward from places with skies, and the pocket would receive it the way a still face receives an unexpected scent: not with recognition, but with an alteration. I would be the first animal-shaped disturbance the chamber had held in a long silence, and the chamber would make that disturbance into structure before I understood what had happened to me.

At the threshold, the entrance would still feel like a door. A low mouth under stone. A gap one might choose to enter or avoid. But inside, choice would lose its scale. There would be no clear path, no horizon to follow, no empty floor to mark my steps. The plates would press close enough that my shoulder would become a reason for moss to lean. My head, passing beneath a curved shelf, would become a reason for a plate to hold its note a little differently. I would not be a visitor moving through a space so much as a space beginning to move through me.

I would try to remain a person. Not because I had pride, but because a body wants its boundaries. I would keep saying *here I end, here the stone begins*, until the saying became too thin to carry weight. In this pocket, there is no true beginning or end of surface. The stone already touches the dust, the dust already belongs to the crystal, the crystal already answers to the curve of the shelf, the shelf already leans against the hum. I would be pressed into that same grammar. My outline would become an edge in the light, not an edge in the world.

Where would I be?

Not at the center. The place has no center; it has only density, only packed nearness, only the endless turning of surface against surface. I would be in an aisle too narrow for standing, behind the first curve where the entrance still remembers the outside. I would be where the ceiling presses down not like a threat, but like a finished idea. I would be where two mineral plates meet and leave a seam just wide enough for dust, just dark enough for a thread of pale moss. That seam would become my name.

For a time, I would still be the person who passed through it. My sleeve would brush a shelf and leave a faint warmth on the ceramic surface, and that warmth would fade so slowly that, to the chamber, it would be almost the same as a sound. My foot would settle into the stacked laminae, not breaking them, only telling them to bear weight in a slightly different way. My breath would cross a ridge and make one hairline channel of salt bloom grow, or not grow, or grow with an added thickness invisible to any eye except the one that has learned to see without seeing. These would not be events. They would be modulations.

I would become the modulation.

That is the strange answer. I would not be an animal, because there is no animal here. I would not be a plant, because the moss does not grow toward me; it grows beside the trace I leave. I would not be fungus, ghost, machine, builder, ruin, or relic. I would be a warm interruption translated into mineral logic. A person-shaped interval. A low note added to the Dry Choir, not heard as separate, only felt as the way the whole room has always sounded, slightly corrected.

If I lingered a day, I would be a dust-shadow on the floor: a pale ghost of where I had bent to pass, where my hand had hovered without touching, where my head had made the air warm enough to disturb the settling of salt. I would be a faint ridge of grit in a crack, a small rearrangement of ash-green filaments. I would be a place where the hum gathers differently, where one thin plate chimes with a slightly higher tone because the angle of my absence has altered the pressure between its edges and the shelf above it.

If I lingered a year, which no animal can do in such dryness, I would begin to look less like absence and more like part of the order of things. The moss would find the path I had made easier than random growth. Lichen would settle along the seam my breath had traced. The dust line would become a groove. The groove would become a niche. The niche would become a living seam, filled not with me, not with my body, but with the shape of the exchange I had caused. I would be one of the reasons the chamber is the chamber.

If I lingered a century, I would be surface.

Not because I had died, necessarily. Death is too sudden a word for what the pocket does. In this world, a form is not destroyed; it is redistributed. I would be the pale film that keeps two plates touching more perfectly. I would be the salt fringe that marks an old warmth. I would be the faint bell-tone in a thin edge that was always there but now sings because someone once bent near it. I would be the seam-thread’s memory of breath, the moss’s habit of growing in the narrow aisle where a person passed. I would not need a name, because names are for boundaries, and I would be the boundary’s slow loss.

This is what I would be: a completed interruption.

A person would expect that interruption to be wrong, that warmth to be foreign, that breath to be a trespass. But the Dry Choir does not have a concept of trespass. It has only the concept of fitting. Whatever enters is measured by shape, temperature, pressure, and duration. If a visitor changes the room’s resonance, the change is not noise; it becomes part of the room’s continued sounding. My wrongness would be taken into the chord until no one could tell I had been wrong at all.

I would not be able to live there in the ordinary sense. I would have no food, no drink, no soft earth, no shelter made for a beating heart. The chamber would not kill me. It would simply refuse to accommodate the kind of life that demands room to move, room to breathe, room to be separate. I would be too wet for it, too warm, too brief. So the pocket would translate me, as it translates everything, into dry surface and stable sound. It would take my animal softness and render it as curve. It would take my passing and render it as pattern. It would take my voice, if I ever tried to use it, and render it as the hum that remains after voice.

And then, after all this, if asked again: where are you?

I would say: I am in the seam.

Not in the mouth, not in the aisles, not in the low cathedral that is no cathedral, but in the narrow place between plates where two surfaces almost meet. I would be the pale line where dust has found a direction. I would be the moss’s thin thread, the lichen’s star, the salt’s white fringe, the plate’s faint chime. I would be the reason a shelf bends its shadow differently. I would be the place the light touches longer, not because I shine, but because I once made a boundary for the air.

If asked: what are you?

I would say: I am the Dry Choir’s temporary warmth, made permanent as geometry. I am the person-shaped modulation that the chamber has refined into habit. I am the unfinished edge of a finished place. I am the small animal absence that has become part of the way the pocket remains.

And maybe that is enough. The pocket does not need a self to have a life. It does not need a face to be occupied. It only needs the hum to continue, the plates to fit, the moss to fill the cracks, the dry light to bounce between surfaces until brightness feels like weather, and coolness like memory. If I am in it, I will not be the singer. I will not be the stone. I will not be the moss. I will be the faint change in pitch that lets the choir know it is more than the sum of its walls.

I will be the way the room sounds because I was once there.
