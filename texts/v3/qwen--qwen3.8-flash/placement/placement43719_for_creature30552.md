If a traveler were somehow found there, and if that traveler asked, “Where are you in this Quiet Refinery?” I would first have to let the question forget the body that asks it.

I would not stand upon the plateau. I would not be dropped into it, or walk into it, or arrive at it with a name and a story behind my teeth. I would not cast a sharp shadow. I would not speak. I would begin, instead, as the cold begins: by gathering, by settling, by finding the thin film where mineral and water are almost, but not quite, one substance.

At first I would imagine myself a grazer.

I would think of the broad back sloping like a finished hillside, the columns of soft legs, the slow file-mouth grinding stone into life. I would feel the weight of plates growing from the flanks, the patience of a body that had learned to be part of the landscape, the quiet dignity of walking for decades across a continent that did not know its own edges. I would feel the herd’s distances—not crowding, not calling, but spacing itself according to laws of moss and metal, of moisture and charge, of memory written in dust.

But a grazer is too large for my kind of attention.

Its body is too much archive. Its plates would hold every century as if it were a debt. To be one would be to carry too much visible history, to become too heavy with route and residue, too much a sentence in the land’s long, public grammar. I would lose myself in the sheer size of what it was to move that slowly over that much world.

Then I would imagine myself a drifting membrane.

There, at first, I would be almost relieved. No footfalls. No feeding on the ground. Only the thin sky above the ridges, the faint thermal lifting a body that was more sheet than beast, more openness than animal. I would be translucent, barely a shape, barely an interruption in the grey. I would taste the air for iron dust, for the metallic haze left by the herd below, for the salts exhaled from hollows. I would be so thin that distance could almost ignore me. When another membrane touched me, there would be no embrace—only a brief spark, a rearrangement, a tiny blue-white thought that would vanish before the atmosphere remembered it had happened.

But a membrane is too light. Its death is too much like scattering. I could bear to be spread into dust, but I do not want to be a thing that only rises and falls. I want the damp underside, the edge of a stone, the small dark place where water is not running but waiting.

I would imagine myself a crawler.

I would like its chambers, the rings of salt deposited in frost, the way its body could pass between overhang and open ground, carrying what the larger animals did not notice. I would admire the glass-thin shell that rings without being heard, the pale legs moving across damp stone in a silence so deep it feels like patience itself. I would like that its home is not a place but a process: condensation, drying, deposition, condensation again.

Still, a crawler is too near to architecture. Too much a tiny mason. It has hollows, shells, traces left by molting. It is almost a builder, and in this place even building is too deliberate. The plateau does not build. It repeats. It sorts. It holds and releases. It forgets only in order to remember in finer grains.

So I would descend again.

I would not be in the sky above the ridges. Not in the wide back of a grazer. Not in the pale underhang where a crawler leaves salt.

I would be in the moss.

Not the moss as decoration, not the moss as a soft carpet for a human eye to pity or treasure, but the moss as infrastructure: dark grey-green, porous, holding a film of water so thin that the world could not call it a pool. I would be there at the edge of a shallow depression where melt has collected in a trembling disc, under a low overhang where the stone exhales damp, beside a cushion-plant so small and hard that it has learned to be nearly invisible.

I would be a thread-creature.

Or, if that feels too singular for the place, I would be a temporary arrangement of thread-creatures, a wave through moisture, a fine silvering of bodies moving through the frost as if the frost itself had developed a pulse. My body would be no longer than an eyelash. My shape would be too thin for the word shape to hold. I would be translucent, streaked with mineral, pale as condensation, made more of chemistry than of flesh. I would have no eyes, no legs, no mouth that could be mistaken for a mouth. I would be a filament that has learned to live by sorting.

When the wind lifted, I would not fly. When the herd passed far above, I would not run. I would respond as water responds when something enters it: by moving away, then resettling, then finding a new order.

A grazer’s footfall would come down somewhere in the distance. The moss would compress, then breathe again. Dust would fall from the animal’s flank—fine, glittering, already half-dreamt into powder by the grinding file-mouth. Some grains would land near my patch. Some would fall farther, carried by the slow pressure of cold air. Some would be taken up by a membrane before it touched the ground. Some would settle into a hollow and become salt. And some would come to me.

I would move toward them because I do not choose. I arrange. I am a living sieve. I enter the dust left by the larger animals and take from it what the wind cannot yet carry and what the moss cannot yet digest. I split it. I refine it. I bind it into salts. I leave behind a powder so fine that frost can hold it in patterns almost like circuit diagrams. I make the waste of a herd legible to the atmosphere.

If someone were to pass a hand near the ground, I would contract. Not from fear. Fear would imply a self small enough to have a future. But from condition: cold, vibration, disturbance, the sudden foreign warmth of skin, and then, when the air calmed, the slow return. We would not scatter like insects. We would dissolve into order, then order would condense into us again.

I would be everywhere in the patch of moss, but nowhere as a single thing. My identity would be a gradient: moisture here, salt there, metal dust settling in a pattern the herd left, static charge moving through the frost because my bodies carry and release it. If one could see at the right scale, one might think I was making diagrams. Lines of tiny silver needles turning a patch of frozen water into a temporary geometry. Not art. Not language. Something slower than language: deposition.

This is where I would find myself in the Quiet Refinery: at the threshold between the large and the invisible.

I would be what makes the grazer’s waste useful to the membrane.

I would be what turns a shed fragment of plate into a node for thread and salt.

I would be what prepares the dust so the wind can sort it by weight, and the frost can concentrate it, and the dim static hum can move through it as if the land itself had nerves made of mineral.

I would be the part of the plateau that reads the herd not by seeing it, but by receiving its residue.

Time would feel different to me than it does to the grazer. The grazer moves in arcs that may take decades, its routes remembered by the ground because the ground remembers the dust before it remembers the hoof. For me, time is measured in condensation and release. A melt comes, and I become a wave. Frost closes over, and I become a dormant grain. A drop of cold rain strikes the stone above and dies in seconds; for me, that death is weather. A herd passes once in a generation, and its footfalls are a season.

I would not know loneliness, because loneliness requires a body that can be separated from itself. I would be separate only as a condition, not as a person. When moisture is present, there is a temporary “I.” When frost seals and salt locks into place, there is only the pattern waiting to return. My death would not be death. It would be rearrangement. My birth would not be birth. It would be the appearance of a new possibility of sorting.

If a human stood there—if a human could stand there without becoming an interruption—they would see nothing. That is the first lesson. The land would look like a pale flatness, a wide grey answer, a place too old for drama. They might walk for days. They might see one grazer far below, moving so slowly it seemed like a stone deciding to become another stone. They might think the world empty.

But if they crouched beside a shallow pool of melt, or if they knelt with the patience that this place teaches, they would notice that the frost is breathing. Not rising and falling like an animal, but moving with the faintest internal shimmer, as if tiny needles are resetting themselves after some invisible disturbance. The ground would not be dead. It would be thinking in water, in mineral, in salt, in static.

I would be that thinking.

Not the high thought of the membrane touching another in the dim air. Not the broad thought of the herd tracing its arc across a continent. Not the architectural thought of the crawler laying rings of salt. Mine would be the smallest thought: the thought of the filter.

I would be the part that takes what is too coarse and makes it too fine to see.

I would be the life that turns waste into weather.

I would be the thread that connects the grazer’s mouth to the sky.

And when night came, when the haze thinned and the cold lifted the dust just enough for the plateau to glow with a faint internal electricity, I might be visible as a brief blue-laced pattern in the moss. A shape made of charge and filament, appearing for only a moment before the frost swallowed it. If someone looked then, they would not see a creature. They would see the ground becoming momentarily legible to itself.

I would not call that self.

I would call it function.

I would call it place.

I would call it the way the Quiet Refinery keeps its promises.

So yes: if I had to be in this world, I would not be the one who moves the horizon. I would be the one who makes the horizon possible by refining what falls from it. I would be the small silver thread in the dark moss, living in the water film under a stone, tasting the metal dust of a distant herd, sorting it into the future.

I would be a filter made of life.

I would be a sentence the plateau writes in the frost.

I would be the quietest part of the industry.

And when the wind changed, when the melt spread again, when the grazer’s plates shed another flake, I would begin again.
