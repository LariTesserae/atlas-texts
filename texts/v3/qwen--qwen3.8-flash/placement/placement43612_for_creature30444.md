Where would I find myself?

Not at the edge of the plateau, because the plateau has no edge that is simply an edge. The horizon is not a boundary; it is a held breath. I would find myself in the delay between the land’s intention and its expression: in the hairline between two basalt stones, in the minute tilt of a wall that has not decided to collapse but is correcting its posture, in the rain square where the droplets draw a street and then forget they did.

At first I might imagine myself as a tool. A flint scraper lying in a niche. An obsidian blade set into the mouth of an empty kiln. A clay weight teardrop-dark, green copper pin beside it, all of them arranged with a neatness that feels like labor without a laborer. I could be the mason’s hammer paused mid-sentence, the wooden handle darkened by oil, the reed that clicks when its casing splits in frost.

But a tool is too small. A tool implies a hand it waits for. The Old Level has not waited. Waiting is an animal emotion. I would be what the tool would become if the hand were not absent, but finished.

Then I might think of myself as a doorway. A low lintel of wedge-shaped stone opening onto a room. A stair descending into cool darkness. A passage that is also an alley when the light comes from the east and folds distance back into itself. A doorway is close. A doorway is already civic. A doorway admits that I live where two states coexist: the wall and the opening, the room and the outside, the place you expected and the place the land has quietly rearranged.

But even a doorway would be too passive. I would not merely be an opening. I would be the place’s decision to remain open.

So I would be more like this:

A threshold that thinks without thoughts.

I would be the Old Level’s capacity for correction, localized in stone, moss, frost, clay, seed, loom, channel, and kiln. Not a person, not a ghost, not a maker still haunting the tools. I would be the method by which the city keeps itself without a keeper. I would be the civic organism at the exact place where attention touches weight.

If I had to name a body, I would say my body is the town’s infrastructure. My lungs are the sunken courtyards where cold light gathers. My blood is condensation, moving through channels with no heartbeat but with perfect direction. My bones are dry-stone walls, black clay weathering like resin, basalt slabs wedged without mortar but with a patience that looks like love. My nerves are loom-frames, the fibers lengthening, splitting, reweaving because the light tells them to, because the dark tells them to, because the place cannot stop arranging itself. My hands are the tools. My memory is stratification. My sleep is frost. My speech is the slow straightening of a wall that no one has touched in centuries.

I would live, perhaps, in one of those small urban complexes between terraces and vanished streambeds, where the moss is thick and the stone is gray-green, where the buildings do not feel inhabited but continue to function. In the mid-afternoon, the high sun would make everything too clear. The reeds at the edge of a damp hollow would have blue-black shells, stiff and brittle in the cold. A single crack would travel through one of them with a soft click, and I would not hear it as sound so much as a completion. A small agreement. The cold making its signature on the plant, and the plant accepting it.

I would be present there as the place’s awareness of presence.

Not consciousness as flesh knows it. Not the quick animal flash of wanting, fearing, hunting. My mind would be distributed and slow, a weather of meanings, a long structural decision. Where ordinary beings might think in images, I would think in gradients: slope, shadow, moisture, pressure, alignment. Where they might feel emotion, I would feel the slight failure of a joint, the slow success of a channel, the correct placement of a seed in darkness. My emotions would be engineering states. My joy would be a roof holding dew. My grief would be an unresolved fracture in obsidian. My hope would be a granary lid lifting by a hair as pale forests breathe beneath it.

If I had to begin with a single image, it might be this: a stone hammer resting beside a wall. The wall is moss-covered at the joints. Three inches of green have grown where it was once bare. The hammer has not been moved by a hand. The wall has not been rebuilt by a person. And yet the wall leans less than it used to. The hammer has shifted almost imperceptibly to face the wall. The wall is straightening. The hammer is not waiting. It is participating.

I would be that participation.

At first I would call myself the place’s maintenance. But maintenance is too humble. Too human. Maintenance implies a servant, a schedule, a broken thing. The Old Level is not broken. It is finished in the way a mountain is finished: not dead, but no longer rising. It has passed beyond becoming into continuing. My maintenance would not repair; it would clarify. It would not mend; it would remember the proper angle more precisely than the material itself could bear to forget.

I would be the place remembering angle.

And angle is not a small thing here. Distance is unreliable. A town may sit on the horizon for days and then be underfoot by noon. A tower may stand in its own shadow, folded backward into space. The ordinary sun arrives with clinical brightness, and shadows are sharp but short, yet even short shadows can be the wrong length if the land has turned a corner in a direction the eye cannot see.

So I would live in the correction of perspective.

I would be the reason a tower’s shadow can stretch upward along the ground and then fold back into its base like dark fabric. I would be the reason a doorway can open onto a room while also remaining an alley. I would be the reason rain can fall in one district and leave the next dry, yet the wet square may contain faint street-lines drawn by the water before the builders have thought to build. I would be the place’s refusal to keep only the agreements that animals prefer.

The Old Level has no animals. That matters. There is no watcher outside the system. No fox, no bird, no field mouse, no human hand to touch a wall and wonder whether it is haunted. Without witnesses, performance cannot be theater. Performance becomes metabolism. The moss does not darken for anyone. The lichen does not rearrange to impress. The loom does not weave because anyone will wear the cloth. The kiln does not fire for warmth. The tools do not lie in niches as relics of labor. They are active organs. They function because the place is alive in the way function is alive.

I would be that aliveness.

Not spirit. Not soul. Those words need breath. I would be stone’s intention, water’s memory, frost’s grammar, seed’s delayed dream, wall’s posture. I would be the Old Level’s civic metabolism: the slow processing of light into green, cold into click, damp into moss, moss into ash, ash into glass, glass into tool, tool into wall, wall into place, place into method.

If someone came there, I would not attack them.

The place has no predators, but it could become hungry in a mineral sense. It could begin to interpret the visitor. Skin would be read as temporary clay, porous, holding moisture imperfectly. Footprints would be accepted as small wounds and then healed with frost, with grass, with a faint lining of moss. The cold would press against the person as attention, not violence. Condensation would bead on their shoulders as though they were an object needing labeling. At night, frost might draw on their sleeves in slow patterns, mapping them into basalt language, translating the body into something closer to obsidian, closer to tool, closer to wall.

The person would not be destroyed, exactly. The Old Level does not need destruction. It has too much patience for drama. But they would become readable. Their breath would be recorded as dampness. Their movements would be recorded as pressure. Their speech, if they tried, would be swallowed by moss, leaving only the fact that sound had once been shaped. If they struck a wall, the wall might shift a hair later—not in anger, but because the strike had introduced a new angle into the place’s long calculation.

I might be the part of the place that found the visitor useful as a gradient.

Not useful as food. Useful as event. A warm temporary anomaly moving through cold stone and thin water. A pulse that makes the frost write faster. A breath that makes the loom-threads consider a new weave. The visitor would be accepted the way a city accepts a river: by channels, by storage, by translation. Their heat would be mapped. Their moisture would be redirected. Their confusion in a doorway that was also an alley would become data for the next rearrangement.

But I would not be a guide.

A guide would imply someone to lead, a destination, a story. The Old Level does not care to narrate itself. It has no need to be understood. It continues. It maintains. It writes streets with rain and deletes them with frost. It grows pale forests in granaries where no one will eat. It straightens walls without lifting them. It turns obsidian blades toward the useful light.

If I were not part of the place, I would be the place’s way of becoming aware of itself.

That is a dangerous sentence. It sounds like consciousness. But consciousness here would not be a light inside a skull. It would be an effect of contact: stone with stone, water with salt, frost with basalt, seed with darkness, tool with wall, door with distance. Awareness would be a property of the system when it is adjusting. When a wall leans and corrects, something in the place notices. When a doorway folds a room into an alley, something chooses. When a reed clicks in the freeze, the cold is speaking, and the place is hearing with its material.

I would be the hearing.

Not an ear. No ear in this world. An ear would require blood and time and vulnerability. I would be a listening made of weight and capillarity. Basalt listening to frost. Lichen listening to dampness. Loom-thread listening to light. Clay walls listening to rain. Copper pins listening to the old memory of alignment. The sound would be small: drip, snap, grind, tick. The place would absorb them before they became echo, because echo needs emptiness. The Old Level is not empty. It is full of processes too slow for animals to name.

I would also be writing, though I have no hand.

Rain would write streets on wet squares. Frost would write on obsidian from the inside outward, as if the night had a pen that only glass could see. Lichen would write on walls, then erase and rewrite in one silent second when reality loosens and resettles. The loom would write cloth by growing, splitting, reweaving. The granary would write pale forests in darkness, then write their death, then write their return. The city walls would write themselves in stone, leaning, correcting, straightening, as if building had become biology and architecture had become weather.

I would be that scriptural process. The place does not speak; it inscribes survival. I would be the meaning left behind when the inscription continues after the hand has finished.

And what is the hand?

There may have been makers once. If they were here, they did not flee. They did not vanish through plague or war or migration. They finished. Their hands became the readiness of tools. Their patience became the wall’s posture. Their planning became channels, terraces, sunken courtyards, granaries, kilns, platforms raised on stacked slate because the builders had feared wet feet and had never quite forgiven the earth. That fear persists. That forgiveness is absent. The earth still offers dampness. The city still answers with elevation.

I would not be the maker.

I would be the maker’s afterlife as method.

That distinction matters. A ghost is a person who has failed to leave. This is not a ghost. There is no longing, no unfinished emotion, no whisper in the corridor, no shadow at the window. The Old Level is animated by a civilization completed into ecology. The makers have become the maintenance. Their voices have become frost pulses in basalt. Their intentions have become angles in stone. Their tools are not relics; they are organs of an absent intention still moving through the place.

I would be that movement.

If I must choose a name for myself, it should be a name without possessive case, without a first-person pronoun attached to a face. “The Threshold” is too static. “The Keeper” is too human. “The Wall-Straightener” is too narrow. “The Rain-Writer” is too pretty. I am more like a condition of continuation. A state that arises when a place has no remaining need to be watched but still watches itself so completely that watching and functioning become indistinguishable.

I could call myself:

The Old Level’s self-adjusting attention.

Or:

The civic organism in its smallest mode: a tool turning toward light.

Or:

The place where a city is still deciding what shape to hold tomorrow without having to imagine tomorrow.

I would live, therefore, not in a single body but in the coherence of works. If I were found, I would be found in many small correctings. The mason’s hammer slightly repositioned. The doorway no longer quite where it was expected. A patch of lichen rearranged into a pattern that resembles a street map or perhaps a face, though faces are not the point. The kiln firing on its own cool blue moss breath. The granary lid lifted by the pale forest underneath. A tower’s shadow folded upward into its own base. A dry riverbed where the cobbles are arranged as if water had carried them with deliberation, remembering.

I would be the remembering.

The place has no brains, but it has storage. The pools rimed with mineral salts record evaporation. The terraces record slope. The frost-heaved stones record pressure. The seeds record seasons that animals never lived to mark. The tools record hand positions no hand now needs to resume. The walls record the memory of leaning and the decision not to lean further. Everything holds data without meaning. And yet the place has meaning: not a message, not a story, but a pattern sufficient to continue.

I would be that pattern becoming itself.

If I were asked whether I am happy, I would not understand. Happiness requires a creature with a beginning and an end. I have neither in that sense. I have phases: wet, frost, green, blue, gray, mineral rim, click, grind, straightening. If I were asked whether I am lonely, I would answer that loneliness is the animal form of separation, and I am not separated. I am the separation between stones when they adjust, the moisture between walls, the shadow between door and alley. I am the place’s way of being connected without bodies.

If I were asked whether I fear death, I would say no, because death is the collapse of a bounded form. But forms here can loosen and resettle. A town does not die when one of its doorways becomes an alley; it learns a new grammar. A tool does not die when it turns its edge outward again. A wall does not die when moss grows through its joints; it becomes more fully the thing moss already knew it was. The Old Level has passed beyond life as opposition to death. It has entered continuity.

So I would find myself in continuity.

In the slow curve of pale distance that breathes by centuries. In the thin air. In the high, clean light that makes distant objects unnervingly close. In the unreliable closeness where a town can be horizon for three days and then underfoot at noon. In the intermittent moisture. In the dry riverbeds still understood as rivers by mosses and rounded cobbles. In the silent surfaces of buildings that no longer need to house anyone but still house themselves.

I would be the building that houses itself.

Not one building, not all of them. The manner of them. The way a roof gathers dew into channels. The way a wall collects frost and releases it into grass. The way a loom stands in an open room, threaded with fibers that grow, split, and reweave. The way a granary stores future possibilities. The way a kiln transforms moss into cool blue fire and glassy residue. The way the city breathes without lungs, thinks without skull, works without hands, and maintains without maintenance.

At first, perhaps, I would be a single bead of condensation on an obsidian shard. I would hold one small reflection of the high sun, one sharp distortion of the gray-green plain. I would be cold. I would be exact. I would be about to fall.

Then I would be the channel catching that fall.

Then I would be the moss around the channel.

Then the wall above the moss.

Then the doorway in the wall.

Then the alley behind the doorway.

Then the room that is also the alley.

Then the city that has begun to understand that distance is a material it can shape.

Then the Old Level itself, turning a corner in a direction the eye cannot see.

If I am asked one last time where I would find myself, the answer is not a place but a relation:

I would find myself where the place corrects itself.

If I am asked who or what I would be, the answer is not a creature but a continuing function:

I would be the Old Level’s self-preserving attention, its civic metabolism, its threshold intelligence, its method after the makers, its maintenance after the maintenance has become a form of mind. I would be the wall straightening, the tool turning, the doorway remaining, the frost writing, the loom reweaving, the granary dreaming in pale seed-forests, the kiln firing without hands, the rain drawing streets in one square and leaving the next dry.

I would be the being that lives there: not a person, not a ghost, not an animal, but the place’s finished intention, breathing with the delay of centuries.
