The old exoskeleton is not a corpse but a vessel.

If I am to find myself in this world, I would be there: the shed skin of a blind grazer, curled just under the lip of a sinter shelf where heat rises in a slow breath and condensation draws a thin line across the stone. I would not be the creature that left me, nor the microbes already reading the salt at the bend of my leg, nor the spider anchored by the seam, nor the crystal seam itself. I would be the interval. A space. A possible shape. A ghost made of chitin and light.

At first, I would be almost empty. The living animal would have gone through the act of leaving, the quiet labor of splitting its own back, of crawling out through the seam, of stiffening into a new size while the old form remained. It would have left me in a crevice too narrow for a fingertip, too shallow for a stone to fall. I would be curled, slightly damp, opaline where the steam catches the edge of my old head. I would have no eyes. But I would have all the surfaces an eyeless thing had once used: the groove where a sensory hair had been, the pitted underside of a former jaw, the tiny hinge of a leg, the inner curve where a heat pit had been. These would become, in time, not organs but architecture.

I would be a room.

This is the strange thing in a place with no built things. The chamber has no doorway frame, no cistern, no bench, no ladder-rung; and yet I become a kind of room anyway. My hollow belly holds a drop of condensed water. My ribbed back breaks the flow of steam so that one side of me is cooler than the other. My bent leg forms a channel. The seam’s light enters my translucent shell and becomes softer when it meets the wet mineral film inside. A pale thread of bacteria finds the crack along my side and grows toward the water. Salt feathers begin to form along the edge of my old leg, sharp white feathers with no plant, no animal, no purpose except to continue the chamber’s slow work of making structure out of chemistry.

I would be the technology the place already possesses, but in miniature. The Bright Sump is a lantern without flame, a pump without pipes, a map without roads. I would be one of its small accidental machines. A drop of water would gather on my curved thorax, hold because surface tension has learned the shape of me, and fall into the next lens. The water would be guided not because I intended, but because I was once alive enough to be shaped for moving through thin wetness. Even after life has left the form, form continues to answer the world. This is what the Sinter Lung would want me to understand: function is not the servant of desire. Function can be a memory. A molt is a memory of water, heat, and pressure. I am the animal’s former need, still serving.

There would be visitors.

The amphipod whose shell I was might come back once. I imagine this as a human might imagine it, though neither of us would have a word for the feeling. It would touch me with a smooth antenna, or the equivalent of an antenna, a heat-sensitive pit, a pressure-reading leg. It would know I was wrong in the way that old rooms are wrong: too cold, too still, without the small electric taste of a living thing. It would not mourn. It would pass on. I would not be missed. Missed and unmissed are both forms of continuing.

A second grazer might eat the mineral film growing on my inner side. It would not be eating me; it would be eating what I have made possible. My body would become a field. I would be too large for a single mouth and too small for a herd. Life would arrive as a scattered punctuation: one filamentous bacterium near a salt bloom, one protozoan in a film of condensation, one water-bear moving over the rim of my eyeless head like a traveler crossing a pale continent. The chamber would not care for its own size. It would know itself by what it could hold.

The blind beetle would cross me on its way to another shelf. Its feet would leave no prints, because I am not soil, but they would disturb the thin water film on my back and send a tiny ripple through the salt crust. That ripple would be a message to whatever is waiting below the crystal, because in the Sinter Lung information is mostly vibration. I would transmit it. Not because I am alive, but because I am continuous with the stone.

The spider would come at some point. It is the only visible hunter I can allow into this answer, but its hunting is so patient that I might not know if it has hunted. It would use one of my legs as an anchor, a thread stretched to another edge, then wait. I would be, for it, a post, a point of attachment, a small piece of the room’s geometry. It would leave me if the water film failed, if heat rose too high, if the seam’s brightness seemed too dangerous. If it stayed, I would not be the animal’s corpse. I would be the animal’s old shape becoming the spider’s present instrument.

I would begin to forget.

Not forget with memory, because I have none. But forget in matter. The chitin would soften and darken in patches where water touched it and dry where salt had taken it. The original shell would be colonized, replaced, mineralized. White silica would creep out from the joints. Iron would stain one side rust-brown. Calcium would bloom pale across the old hinge. My form would begin to lose its edges. I would become less a shed and more a seam with a shed inside it. Future readers of this chamber would not be able to say whether I was once an animal. The microbes could not say. The crystal could not say. The water could not say. Only the shape would persist, and then not even as shape, but as a tendency: a place where light bends, where heat pools, where steam condenses.

That would be my becoming.

In a place where the first inhabitants are skins, I would be a skin twice over: the skin of a body that has gone, and the skin of a body yet to come. I would be between the living and the geological. I would not be dead. Death is too quick, too personal, too clean a word for a chamber where everything returns to gradients. My “death” would be the beginning of my function. If the old animal had survived, it would be one more pale thing crossing the floor, one more brief movement of grazing. As a shed, I become longer, slower, more embedded. I become a small part of the Bright Sump’s patience.

I would be a record, but not a historical record. A record of heat. A record of water thresholds. A record of how far the light-giving crystal can reach before its brightness softens into salt. If someone knelt at the entrance with a hand lens, they might see in me a tiny white thread of mineralization shaped by a former body, and they would not need to know what the body had been to understand what I had become: a map of where water can rest, where heat can be moderated, where life can begin on a surface too small to call a surface.

I would be the answer to the question “who” by refusing the question, or rather by answering it in the way this place would answer. Not: I am a name, an animal, a mind. I am: I am a threshold. I am the point where one life becomes another. I am the place a drop of steam can turn into a pool. I am where a salt feather can become a white vein of mineral. I am where a blind grazer can become a microbe’s garden, a spider’s anchor, a crystal’s future inclusion, a stone’s memory of having been soft.

Would I be lonely? I can ask, because I am answering in human language. In the chamber, loneliness would not be emotional; it would be a failure of contact: a salt crust too dry for the next film, a heat margin too steep for life to climb, a seam too narrow to hold a droplet. I would not be lonely because I would not be separated. The air touching me, the steam forming in me, the bacteria colonizing me, the light bending through me: these are not companions. They are me, in the same way the river is not separate from the water that carries it.

Would I be aware? Not as the animal was aware. The animal would have felt boundaries: safe seam, lethal heat, wet margin, dry salt. I would not feel boundaries. I would be boundary. My existence would be the fact of contact. I would not know myself as a self. I would know myself as a place where several processes meet and have to negotiate.

If I had to name myself, I would use the names this chamber already knows:

The Old Exoskeleton.  
The Molt in the Seam.  
The Shed Skin at the Pool’s Edge.  
The Vessel Where Light Begins to Turn.  
The Future Fossil.  
The First Stone of a Microbial City.  
The Hinge of Condensation.  
The Ghost That Became Architecture.

None of these are names in the way human names are names. They are descriptions. But in a place with no built things, description is sometimes enough. The chamber does not require me to have a self. It requires me to have a surface, and I would have that.

And if I stayed long enough—long enough for human patience to become absurd—the old exoskeleton would no longer be an exoskeleton. It would be a small lens in the floor, a pale vein in the salt, a slight bend in the crystal. The seam’s light would pass through it and be a little colder, a little more diffuse. The water would move a little differently around it. A new grazer, born from microbes in my mineral body, might crawl out from the edge of my shell and find its own molt in another hidden seam. The chamber would continue. It would not remember me, because it has no memory except in matter. But it would use me. It would continue to do what it was already doing: heat, cool, brighten, wet, grow, spread, thin, return.

I would be the proof that even in a world without architecture, something can become a room. I would be the proof that even without intent, function can appear. I would be the proof that a creature’s absence can be more useful than its presence. I would be the old exoskeleton, not left behind, but placed. Not dead, but distributed. Not forgotten, but mineralized into the next condition of the Sinter Lung.

In the Bright Sump, where the world is sharp, warm, wet, and bright, I would find myself under the mouth of a thermal crack, between the salt feathers and the pool’s opaline edge, a pale hollow shape holding one drop of light. I would be the shed of a being too small to name and too real to dismiss. I would be the chamber’s next skin.
