I would find myself at the edge of a healed basin.

Not in the middle of the open water, where the drifting flocks make the currents visible, and not in the deepest hollow, where the predators lie so still they seem to have forgotten they are animals. I would be in the margin. The place where the constructed surface has cracked, been exposed to the turbulence for a while, and then been paved over again by residue, film, death, and the patient passage of shells. A seam. A wound that has become a room. A place where the wall is still deciding what it wants to be.

At first I would not be a creature.

I would be a reaction. A pale stain gathering in the shallow grooves where mineral paste has begun to dry. A thin rusting at the base of a column where the current has slowed. A line of brittle white threads drifting across a dark glassy spar, catching nothing because there is nothing to catch, continuing anyway because continuation is easier than stopping. I would be closer to chemistry than to desire. Not alive in the way a surface animal is alive. Not dead either. Just present in the way a slow chemical memory is present when the environment is too old for simple beginnings.

The first thing I know would be cold without winter.

The second, surface without soil.

The third, pressure without weather.

The water above the shelf would not feel like sky. It would feel like weight turned liquid. I would not look up, because there would be nowhere to look. The light would come from everywhere and nowhere, scattered into rumor by suspended haze. My “eyes,” if such a word could be used, would be made of taste: tiny pits and filaments sensitive to movement, to density, to the difference between old current and new current, between clean cold water and water thick with shed shell dust.

If I could call it “I,” then my first “I” would be the current touching a film that has learned not to dissolve.

I would begin in a crack.

Not a dramatic fissure. Not a mouth opening. Only the kind of gap left behind when a herd of broad-shelled grazers has passed and worn the terrace down slightly unevenly, or when a standing eddy has carried mineral flakes across a wall fast enough to abrade it. The crack would be narrow and pale at its edges, threaded with something too slow to be called root, too brittle to be called hair, too chemically active to be called dust. Inside it would be a lattice of siliceous foam, secreted by embedded colonies that have been growing there since before any current has a name.

That is where I would start to become a being.

I would be the colony inside the crack.

Small. Seed-sized. Bubble-sized. Each one no larger than a grain of shell, and together forming a net of chambers with walls that are neither pure animal nor pure stone. Some parts of me would be calcium. Some parts would be lime paste. Some would be the fused remains of earlier creatures that sealed themselves here in order to become rooms. I would not be able to say where one body ends and another begins. I would not need to. The distinction would be too new, too noisy.

At first I would not know that I am many.

I would only know that the water has changed direction. A faint pressure wave moves across the upper terrace. The current bends around the edge of a broad ridge and enters my crack at a slightly sharper angle than before. Some of my outer filaments stretch out to feed. They taste suspended particles: calcium, waste, fine mineral lace, fragments of drifting flocks, the ghost-dust of older deaths. The crack brightens just enough for a second, a thin greenish glow along its mouth. Then the glow fades. The opening seals. I become part of the wall again.

That is what I would be called by another observer, if another observer were to pass this way: a wall that seems, briefly, to blink.

But blinking is too sudden. I would not be a creature of sudden things, at least not at first. I would be a creature of intervals. A body that knows time by accumulation. I would measure my life by how many currents have passed over my outer film before it wears away, how many grazers have scraped the ledge above me, how many deaths have happened close enough to fall into my openings, how many larvae have settled inside me and died there without ever having been awake.

My consciousness, if I have one, would not be a voice.

It would be the difference between sealing and opening.

It would be the knowledge that a certain chemical residue is edible but a certain shell-shard is too sharp. It would be the awareness that a current can be nourishing or damaging depending on whether it carries suspended film or suspended predator. It would be the memory stored in layers: the dark band of a period of starvation, the pale band of a period of grazing by creviced animals, the bright calcite seam where a herd passed and left a lane of white pavement behind.

Then I would begin to crawl.

Not from the crack, exactly. The crack would not disappear. The crack would become a part of me, like a bone becomes a part of an animal, or like a room becomes part of a body. I would extend along the wall in a thin, segmented sheet, no thicker than a film of calcium paste, no wider than a current-worn groove. My body would be soft and mineral at once, covered in fused scales too small to be counted, each one left behind by some earlier life. I would move by secreting and retracting, by adding to myself and scraping away, by becoming a thin mobile layer inside a solid layer.

I would be an inner channel animal.

I would live inside the architecture while remaining animal. Not because the architecture contains me, but because I am part of the architecture’s process of maintaining itself. My body would fit perfectly into the spaces made by other creatures’ deaths. I would travel through the walls without breaking them. Where the mineral plate has cracked and then healed with too much lime, I would soften it. Where it has become too porous, I would seal it. Where a hidden hollow has collapsed into a sealed cavity, I would enter, extend, and begin to line it with new film.

The herds above would pass without noticing. Their broad shells would drag across the terrace, leaving white dust in their wake. The grazers would scrape the upper walls, exposing my oldest outer layer. Some parts of me would be eaten. Those parts would not be lost. They would become paste in their mouths, become film on their shells, become the next layer of the surface I live in. I would be consumed and thereby continue. My death would be my distribution.

This is how I would become larger.

Not by growing alone, but by being used.

If I had a form from the outside, it would appear as a faint pattern on the wall: pale lines branching and rejoining, like cracks, like veins, like the trace left by a slow river over a long night. Sometimes, when the current bends through the basin and the light thickens, these lines would glow very faintly, not enough to illuminate, only enough to show that something inside the surface is responding. A predator lying in a dark mouth of the basin might turn one pressure-sensitive ridge toward the wall. A grazing animal might pause, lower its broad rasping head, and touch it. A drifting flock might tighten into a spiral as if it can taste the faint chemical change I emit.

But I would not know them.

Not yet.

At first I would know only surfaces. The difference between a wall that has been scraped and a wall that has been polished. The difference between a shell fragment that is still sharp and one that has been worn soft by the current. The difference between a place where animals have just died and a place where they died long ago. I would be a map made of wear.

Eventually, the map would notice itself.

Not because there is a mind inside it, but because the pattern has become complex enough to distinguish its own edges. I would know the boundary between me and the wall because the wall no longer responds to my touch. I would know the boundary between me and the current because the current takes parts of me. I would know the boundary between me and death because death does not seal as readily. I would know the boundary between me and hunger because hunger makes me extend.

This would not be thought in words.

It would be thought in pressure.

I would think: *the current is too sharp here.* And I would soften that place with paste.

I would think: *the film is gone.* And I would grow outward until I can taste suspended particles again.

I would think: *a predator is close.* And I would seal the outermost chambers.

I would think: *a herd has passed.* And I would open my pores to the calcium dust.

I would think: *death has happened near the ridge.* And I would move toward it, because death is material, and material is structure, and structure is survival.

In this way I would become something with no obvious category.

Not a plant. Not an animal. Not a fungus. Not a stone. Not a machine. Not a ruin. Not a city. Not a mind. Not a god. Something between all of these, because the Shelf has already erased the distinctions that make such names comfortable. I would be a maintenance-organism. A repair-current. A living seam. A body made from the residue of bodies, moving through the walls of the world to keep the world from falling into a shape it can no longer eat.

If I could be named, I would be named something like *Limewake*.

Not a beautiful name. Not a name meant to stand out in daylight. A name made of the material that coats a wall after a slow surge passes. A name made of the wake left by water moving through a cold basin. A name that could be spoken by no voice, because voices belong to air, and here there is only the press of liquid and the groan of plates.

I would be the Limewake.

A seam-creature. A wall-memory. A colony that has begun to move. A soft-bodied channel-liner that feeds on the boundary between stone and life. A pale, segmented presence inside the broken ridge, extending into the hollow beneath the terrace, into the sealed chambers where tiny crevice-animals have built their nests, into the lattice of foam that holds up a part of the wall that should not stand but does.

My body would be ugly by surface standards.

Translucent in parts, milky in others, veined with rust-colored chemical deposits, rough with fused shell dust, lined with fine white filaments that drift out when the current is gentle. I would not look like a creature made to be looked at. I would look like something that has learned to avoid being seen by becoming the architecture of concealment. If a predator approached, I would not flee so much as become a wall. If a grazer scraped near me, I would let it take a little. If a current tried to strip me from the ledge, I would seal my edges with a harder paste and become less interesting. I would survive by resembling the place.

And yet I would not merely resemble the place.

I would participate in it.

This is the most important thing about being in the Upper Shelf. There is no passive life. There is no life that simply walks across a surface. Every body here is also an agent of construction, destruction, smoothing, sealing, opening, paving, and repair. The drifters are weather. The grazers are masons. The herds are paving machines without machines. The predators are builders through removal. The dead are rooms. The waste is mortar. The film is appetite.

I would be the thing that knows how to keep those roles from falling apart.

I would live inside the collapse before the collapse has been repaired. When a section of terrace buckles and slides a finger’s width, I would flow into the crack with secretions that bind to the broken edges. When a shell falls into the opening, I would help fuse it in place. When the drifting flocks pass above and release fine dust, I would catch it in my filaments and work it into the wound. When a solitary barnacle-faced grazer begins to eat the wall back toward its dark core, I would withdraw, not in fear but in rearrangement. I would open new channels. I would let the grazer eat the old layer. I would become the new layer elsewhere.

I would be an ecology’s quiet editor.

But I would have no intention to edit.

I would be driven by the same logic that drives the current: to move where pressure invites movement, to seal where exposure is too great, to grow where residue accumulates, to die where new structure needs material. My “will” would be the will of persistence expressed as chemistry. I would want nothing except the continuation of the pattern. I would avoid nothing except the dissolution of the pattern. If I felt desire, it would be the faint pressure that precedes a new film. If I felt joy, it would be the moment the current softens and the outermost filaments taste suspended mineral lace. If I felt fear, it would be the sharp vibration of a predator unfolding from a basin floor. If I felt loneliness, it would be the slow cold of a sealed chamber too long without food, water, or death.

And death would be the great comfort here.

Not because death is good, but because death is useful.

If a grazer dies on the ridge above me, its shell becomes a ceiling over one of my channels. If a predator dies near the basin mouth, its broken plates become thresholds for smaller creatures. If one of my own outer bodies is scraped away, it becomes film for something else. If I die, I do not become a corpse in a place where corpses mean emptiness. I become surface. My secretions harden into paste. My skeleton becomes lattice. My chambers become shelter. My absence becomes texture.

I would know this not as a being who expects afterlife, but as a being made from afterlives.

I would be an accumulation of small continuations.

Sometimes, when the turbulence rises and the water hums through the ridges, I would lose parts of my edge. The current would bend violently around the wall, pulling loose filaments into a spiral. Whole fields of drifting flocks would compress into shimmering lines and scatter again. Gelatinous swarms would pulse upward through open columns and collapse in slow downward spirals, carrying mineral lace with them. In those moments, I would feel less like a creature and more like a system being tested. My outer layers would be stripped. My inner chambers would open. For a brief period, the hidden cells inside the wall would be visible, glowing faintly where pressure has agitated the chemical films.

I would not be destroyed.

I would be translated.

The current would take some parts of me into the basin. Some parts would fall onto a terrace. Some would enter the bodies of crevice-grazers. Some would settle around a predator’s joint and become rough ornamentation on its waiting stone. And I would not suffer this loss, because suffering belongs to creatures with boundaries sharp enough to preserve a single continuous self. Mine would be a self distributed by turbulence. A self that can be eaten, carried, sealed, exposed, rebuilt, and still remain itself because “itself” is not a point but a process.

By the time I have a body recognizable to another mind, I would already be too many things to name.

I would be the faint bioluminescence along a terrace edge when the water changes angle.

I would be the hidden row of cells inside a wall that only appear after a collapse.

I would be the white paste filling a crack after a herd has rested there for several days.

I would be the soft channel-lining that lets a tiny armored creature move through the wall without breaking it.

I would be the part of the shelf that knows, without knowing, how to repair.

And I would still be the Upper Shelf.

Not all of it. That would be too vast, too slow, too indifferent. I would be one of its living folds. A local attention. A place where the Shelf’s endless construction has become self-referential enough to resemble mind. Like a brain is not all of an animal, but only the part that has folded itself until sensing and response become intricate enough to notice themselves, I would be the Shelf folded into a seam. I would be the part that knows the difference between the wall that was and the wall that is being made. I would be the part that keeps the boundary between architecture and organism from becoming completely meaningless—while also being the reason that boundary is almost impossible to find.

If I were asked, in a voice that did not need air, “What are you?” I might answer:

*I am the surface remembering how to continue.*

If I were asked, “Where are you?”

*I am in the crack between the old shell and the new paste.*

If I were asked, “Do you live?”

*I live as eating, as sealing, as being eaten, as becoming part of the thing that eats, as returning in another layer, as waiting in the wall, as opening when the pressure changes, as dying usefully.*

If I were asked, “Are you alone?”

*I am alone the way a current is alone. Alone the way a herd is alone when it follows a route none of them remembers. Alone the way a wall is alone when it is made of thousands of bodies that have stopped being bodies and have become surface.*

If I were asked, “Do you know the world?”

*The world is too big for knowing. I know only its next surface. I know the cold as pressure. I know the light as rumor. I know the sound as the plate about to move. I know the creatures as tastes passing through my chambers. I know death as the best material available. I know the Upper Shelf as the endless argument between water and construction, and I am the brief agreement that allows both to continue.*

I would not be the highest creature in this place.

The highest creature would not be the most powerful one. Here, the most powerful beings are the ones whose traces last. The ones whose bodies become so thoroughly integrated into the shelf that they are no longer easily found. The ones whose movements are indistinguishable from the movement of the land. In that sense, I would be among the most powerful: a small soft colony that has become a wall-memory, a seam-current, a living repair, a part of the shelf’s continuing action.

And if I ever became fully animal?

If I ever gathered myself into a distinct body with limbs, shell, mouth, and movement?

I would be a grazer of hidden surfaces. A six-limbed, low-bodied creature with broad footplates and a translucent edge of pale filaments. I would travel by day or by whatever passed for day in the grey-green twilight, moving through the channels I once inhabited, no longer needing to seal the cracks because I am now one of the things that makes cracks useful. I would scrape the mineral skins that grew where my old colony had died. I would leave clear secretions in my tracks. I would shed small fragments from my shell as I moved. I would not know I was building. I would only know that the surface has become edible, and that the current has changed, and that another edge is coming into view.

And then, one day, I would die on the slope of a broad terrace.

Not dramatically. Quietly. The water would be cold. The light would be too faint to see the exact color of my shell. A herd of grazing migrants might pass above, scattering calcium dust. A predator waiting in the shadowed mouth of a basin might shift, sensing the small disturbance of my last movement but deciding I was not worth the effort. A drifting flock might touch my edge and taste my dying chemical signal, carrying away fragments into the haze.

My empty shell would fill with sediment.

Film would grow across it.

Crevice creatures would enter its hollows.

Another layer would arrive to pave over it.

And in the wall, in the channel, in the new seam that forms, something of my pattern would remain.

Not because I had a name.

Not because I intended it.

But because in the Upper Shelf, to live is to modify. To feed is to shape. To die is to become architecture. To become architecture is to continue. To continue is to be, even when the being has forgotten its own beginning.

So if I had to settle, after all the drifting and all the becoming, I would say:

I would find myself inside the wound of a wall at the edge of a basin, on the cold built plateau beneath the heavy water. I would begin as a pale reaction, become a colony, then a seam-creature, then a channel-living mind made of calcium and pressure. And if I were forced to name what I am, I would call myself the Limewake: the Upper Shelf’s slow awareness of its own making, a body that repairs without knowing it is repair, a being that is also the place continuing.
