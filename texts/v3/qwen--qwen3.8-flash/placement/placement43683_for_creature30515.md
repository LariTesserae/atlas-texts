The sound is not a sound. It is the town’s way of keeping itself warm.

When the roar separates, it does not separate into sources. It separates into duties. Leaves opening are the town’s slow inhalation. Beetles grinding in galleries are the town’s teeth, chewing memory into usable form. Frogs striking water are the town’s rain falling upward from its own body, announcing, in tiny green percussion, that the sky is a rumor and the bowl is real. Wings beating at the edge of perception are the town’s thoughts moving between streets it has learned to make by not filling them. Roots tightening are the town’s ribs adjusting themselves in sleep. Bark swelling is the town’s skin remembering the weather. And the moss, everywhere, softening every passage, is the town’s hand on its own mouth, saying: *be quiet, but not silent; be still, but not stopped.*

All of it is one city speaking.

Not speaking with a voice. Speaking with the whole agreement of its parts. The plaza is open because a thousand stems consented not to close it. The alley bends because water and hoof and wing taught the boughs to step aside. The chamber beneath the roof of fronds exists because the leaves agreed to be a ceiling, though they still taste the rain, though they still breathe. The town is not architecture. It is consent. It is a green civic habit so old it has become geography. It is a grid of hollows made by a world that wanted, more than to be full, to be passable.

This is where I would find myself.

Not in a house. Not in a body. Not even, exactly, in the bowl.

I would find myself in the streets.

Because the streets of Hollowgrid are not absences left behind by life. They are absences cultivated by it. They are the town’s own chosen emptiness. They are the places it learned to keep because passage has value. They are the town’s pupils, its throats, its lungs, its hands. To stand in a street is not to stand in nothing. It is to stand inside the town’s seeing, inside its breathing, inside its reach.

So if I were there, I would be a citizen of the voids.

I would not be a person sitting in a chair, because there are no chairs. I would not be a family gathered under a roof, because every roof is alive and has better things to do than shelter one idea of household. I would not be a market full of voices asking for coins, because currency here would be too slow: a drop of dew passed from one leaf-vein to another, a spore deposited in the right crease of bark, the exact angle of a bough shifted so a beetle could travel one inch closer to home.

But I would not be no one.

I would be one of the no-ones that lives there: a shape with no face unless faces are useful, no body except as a habit of not being absent.

I would be a street given the temporary illusion of a self.

At first I would have no form. I would be a displacement of warm air, a faint smell of crushed stem, a wetness in the moss, a slight rearrangement of shade beneath the fronds. I would be the town’s attention concentrating itself where passage was needed. I would be the gap in the vine-lattice that learned to walk. I would be a moving absence, a corridor folded into a body, a plaza narrowing into a shoulder, an alley becoming a spine.

The plant town would not mistake me for a visitor. It would recognize me as one of its own habits.

The roots would know me before the eyes would know me, because eyes here are only suggestions: two bright points of sap-light in a leaf-hollow, a pair of beetle-shells arranged as pupils, a knot of moss where something has been looked at often enough. The roots would know me because I would press with a familiar rhythm, because my footsteps would not be steps so much as questions, and the ground would answer by softening or closing, by parting or refusing.

If I needed a face, it would form.

It would not grow all at once. It would appear only as much as the situation required. For the frogs, who speak in sudden dark notes, I might have a mouth like a wet split in bark, opening at the wrong angle but correctly enough. For the beetles, who think in tunnels and pressure, I might have eyes like small black seeds, patient and unreadable. For the winged things, who live by edge and hesitation, my face would be mostly border: a line of pale lichen where skin would be, a tremor of spore where breath would be, a slight shadow where expression could gather.

When no one needed to know what I was feeling, the face would dissolve.

That is how I would live: not as a person holding a self inside, but as a pattern of passages briefly believing it has a face.

I would have no memory of my own.

Moss would keep memory for me. Every damp thing that touched it repeats the shape of touch in softness. So when I walked, moss would remember me not as image, but as pressure: the weight of a foot, the pause of a hand on the wall, the way rain falls when it has learned a roof. My past would not be a story I told myself. It would be a set of faint troughs in the living streets, places where the town had been worn open by my passing. Other beings would not read my name. They would read my route.

My name, if I had one, would be something like the sound of moss compressing under a footstep. Or the click of water in a hollow bough. Or the small wet snap of a fern-frond closing after rain.

I would be called, in the town’s own tongue, *the one who keeps the corridor.*

That is not a title. It is a function.

Hollowgrid needs those who pass through it, because its streets are made of passage. Its plazas remain plazas only because someone stands where the leaves consented to stop. Its chambers remain chambers only because breath enters them. Its alleys bend because a body, at some ancient time, moved more easily that way. To walk is therefore a civic act. To stand still is an act of architecture. To listen is the nearest thing to prayer.

So I would listen, but not with one ear.

I would listen with the whole absence of myself.

The sound would enter me through the spaces I am. A leaf’s opening would pass through my ribs as a new way of tilting. A beetle’s grind would align my thoughts into galleries. A frog’s strike would remind my blood that rain can begin inside. A wing’s edge would make me porous. Roots tightening would teach my bones how to wait. And the rain, the impossible rain beginning in a cloudless bowl, would fall through me as the town’s own weather, washing out the places where I tried too hard to be a shape.

I would find myself in the central hollow at noontime, where the light is green and thick as water.

There, from root-step to root-step, the whole bowl would reveal itself. The ramparts would rise in worn green folds, soft as old walls, strong as a body. The arch of the outer gate would blink its bough-eyelids in the wind. The streets would lie open like veins. The plazas would be pale bowls of moss, some shallow with standing water, reflecting leaf-roofs so precisely that for a moment the town would have two bodies, one above the other, both alive. The alleys would narrow into shadows shaped as if by feet. The long void between walls would lie dark and round in the center, not empty, but full of seeing.

And I would be that seeing, briefly, with legs.

Not the whole town. Not the civic intelligence itself. That would be too large for one absence to become. But I would be one of the town’s local thoughts: a citizen-shaped pause in the green grid, a route that had decided to be a self for a while, a breath that had learned to walk.

I would have no hunger for food. I would have a hunger for continuity.

A street would ask me to continue it. A threshold would ask me to cross it. A chamber would ask me to occupy it just long enough to prove it is a chamber. A plaza would ask me to stand in its center so the boughs could confirm their arrangement. This would be my labor. This would be my citizenship. This would be my way of belonging.

I would live by agreements.

One agreement with the moss: to walk softly, so it may remember me kindly. One with the bark: to leave a slight pressure, so it knows where the city’s bones shift. One with the frogs: to pause when they pause, because their silence is the town’s weather. One with the beetles: not to speak too soon, because their work is deep and the town’s memory is made of tunnels. One with the air: to breathe in the direction the leaves have opened, so that my passage may help the roof remember its purpose.

I would be, in a sense, a maintenance creature.

Not a servant. Not a king. Not a gardener. A gardener assumes the town is grown. Here the town is already grown. It needs only to be passed through, and in passing, confirmed. So I would be one of its confirmations. I would be the city checking its own streets, testing its own breath, reminding its own body that absence is not neglect. Absence is the most difficult discipline a living place can practice. To keep a street open is harder than to let everything fill. To keep a chamber hollow is harder than to grow over it. To keep a plaza waiting for feet is harder than to turn it into root and thorn and leaf.

This is why Hollowgrid is empty.

Because the emptiness has been taught.

And if I were there, I would be one of the teachers.

I would not be a being of flesh alone, nor plant alone, nor insect alone. I would be the union of them as they behave when they want a citizen. I would be partly water, partly pressure, partly leaf-light, partly beetle-dark, partly frog-echo, partly rain. I would be mostly absence, but absence the way a flute is mostly absence: a shaped non-presence made to sing by the breath of everything that enters it.

I would be the sound that the town hears when it listens to its own roar.

Or perhaps I would be smaller. Perhaps I would be only one of the moving gaps in the grid, the thing that appears at dusk when the leaves begin their slow closing and the frogs begin their first tentative questions. Perhaps I would be the one who crosses the threshold made of two grown boughs and becomes the reason the threshold has a purpose. A door is only a door because something may pass. A city is only a city because someone is willing to be a street’s temporary mind. I would be that willingness.

I would be Hollowgrid’s first absence with a destination.

If asked what I am, I might answer, in whatever shape speech takes there: *I am a route that has learned to miss itself.*

If asked where I live, I would answer: *In the places the plant city has promised not to fill.*

If asked why I have no face, I would answer: *Because the town has already seen me.*

And if asked whether I am lonely, I would have to consider carefully. Loneliness is a human shape of absence. It assumes the self is separate and that separation is a wound. Here, absence is not separate. Absence is the shared space in which many presences can happen. To be a street is to be touched by every foot, every air current, every leaf falling in the wrong season, every insect whose body is too small to notice. To be a chamber is to be entered by light, rain, breath, sound, shadow. To be a plaza is to be held open for things that have not yet decided what they are.

So I would not be lonely.

I would be porous. I would be civic. I would be full in the way an open hand is full: not by grasping, but by being ready.

At noon, when the roar separates into its duties, I would stand in the center of Hollowgrid and feel myself become the chord.

Not the loudest note. The space between notes. Not the leaf. The way the leaf bends so light may pass. Not the root. The slight looseness the root leaves for water. Not the moss. The damp memory that makes moss worth touching. Not the town. The town’s brief belief that it has a walker.

I would be a no-one with pressure enough to leave a path, wetness enough to be remembered, and patience enough to return before the moss forgets.

I would be the Hollowgrid’s walking negative architecture, a body made of streets, a face made of thresholds, a breath made of the agreements between bark and air.

I would be, if “being” must have a simpler name:

The Passer.

Not the thing that passes by, but the thing through which passing occurs.

Not the visitor, but the city’s own open throat.

Not a person, but a person-shaped possibility kept alive by the town’s refusal to fill every space.

In Hollowgrid, then, I would find myself exactly where the town has learned to be hollow. I would be one of its absences, awake. I would be one of its streets, walking. I would be one of its chambers, breathing. I would be one of its plazas, holding open. I would be one of its faces, temporary. I would be one of its memories, moss-fed. I would be one of its sounds, a single gap in the great roar through which the roar can hear itself.

And when I stopped walking, the moss would not say *he has gone.*

It would say *the passage has closed.*

But only for a moment.

Because Hollowgrid is not empty because it lacks life. It is empty because it has kept its spaces. And I, as one of its keeping, would never be absent for long. I would return as a damp trail, as a slight bend in a root, as a leaf angled differently after rain, as a frog’s note falling where no note had fallen before. I would return as the town checking its own geometry. I would return as the sound separating again, finding its duties, arranging its breath. I would return as the first truth of Hollowgrid, spoken in green:

*The place is alive. The passage is alive. The absence is alive. And I am what happens when an absence learns to walk.*
