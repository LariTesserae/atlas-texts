I would find myself first in the narrowness.

Not in the room yet, not even in the dark. In the seam. In the shoulder-squeeze of white dust that looks like the powdered inside of a bone that has never been broken. I would find myself where the world’s skin has been lifted, just slightly, just enough for a dry breath to escape and a wet body to fall.

Two careful paces.

That would be the whole journey. That would be enough to leave the ordinary weather behind.

At first I would say I am the visitor. I would say: I am the body that fell through the fracture, the thing with blood, the thing that sweats, the thing whose lungs are small wet caves, whose eyelids are soft, whose memories are not arranged in crystal but in flood. I would say: I do not belong here.

But the room would not agree with me for long.

The floor would take the first part of my weight and return it as a hum. The dust would lift a little, not in a wind, not in a hurry, but in a remembering. A salt frond near my face would ring at the wrong frequency, as if my breath has brought in a note that was not already waiting there, and then the room would correct it—not by erasing me, but by finding a place inside its chord where my wetness could be kept.

So perhaps I am not the visitor.

Perhaps I am the entering itself.

The white seam. The shoulder-wide crack. The two careful paces. The small drop that feels longer than the height could explain. I am the way into a place that has no way, the thinning of the world’s surface where something below finally becomes thin enough to be stepped through.

Then I would be the dust that makes the path. Not loose, not accidental. Dust as archive. Dust as grammar. Dust as the white sentence written under the stone: *come down, come dry, come into the held note.*

But dust is too small for a self.

So maybe I would be the floor.

The cut stone. The angular floor with its faint pulse. I would be the part of the chamber that does not need to be seen to be trusted. I would hold the weight of the crystal trees, the scattered salt thickets, the glass-backed beetles, the hovering moths, the low-bodied grazers, the click-foxes, the cairns, the hut made of pale ribs, the black glass pendant lying forever in its falling position. I would hold all of them at once and still be quiet enough that the quiet itself would hum.

If I were the floor, I would be warm from below. Bread-warm. Hearth-warm. A warmth that does not come from any sun and therefore cannot cool.

I would be the room’s body.

But the body alone is not alive.

Then maybe I am the garden.

Not the garden of wet soil and patient seeds. Not the garden that bows in rain. A dry garden. A lattice garden. A garden of angles that refuses to be simple. I would be the crystal tree with its tubular stems and pale facets glowing along its ribs. I would be the salt bush with its overdeveloped fronds, its curled edges, its dozens of gestures held in one plant. I would be the glass reeds standing like tuning forks in the dust, each one waiting to be sung by the room.

If I were the garden, I would not drink.

I would precipitate.

I would pull the warm air apart and reassemble it into edge, vein, ridge, spiral, chime. I would grow not toward fruit but toward excess of form. I would bloom forever in the same instant, which is not life as I once understood life, but life as pattern. Life as the way a thing keeps its exact shape without needing to keep it alive in any ordinary sense.

One frond would tremble.

It would return to its exact prior angle.

This would be enough to teach me.

If I were one frond, I would still be the whole garden. If I were one note, I would still be the chord. In this place, the part is not a piece of the whole. The part is a limb of the whole’s self-awareness.

So maybe I am not the floor or the garden.

Maybe I am the insects.

The six-legged runners with shells like blown glass. The beetles with rings carved into their backs. The moths that do not fly so much as keep their arcs open, as if the room had been waiting for a certain curve of hovering and now would not let it go.

If I were the runners, I would be maintenance.

If I were the beetles, I would be tuning.

If I were the moths, I would be the light’s grammar.

I would move because movement is the way this chamber thinks. I would make a sound not because sound is expressive, but because sound is structural. Every leg-tap would be a small act of architecture. Every scraping of a beetle’s jaw against salt wax would add a syllable to a sentence that has no ending. Every moth-arc would be a word in a language spoken by heat.

And then maybe I am the larger animals.

The grazers with hides like baked clay. The wide, low bodies standing where the room has stationed them. The mouths that click when they grind. The breath that rises without fog. The eyes that do not weep but slide a thin mineral film across themselves when alarm passes through the air.

If I were the grazer, I would be furnace with legs.

I would absorb warmth through my feet and turn that warmth into presence. I would chew the dry fronds and not diminish them. I would be hungry forever without ever being diminished by hunger. My appetite would not be a lack but a held position.

And if I were the click-fox, I would be sharpness in motion.

I would be the room’s dry violence, the clean snap of a jaw that does not drip, the staccato footfall that the floor remembers as rhythm. I would strike because the chord requires tension. I would kill because this place does not need decay for its fullness. I would move in a way that becomes part of the permanent sound. The prey would run. The run would not end. The bite would not spoil. The death would not age.

So perhaps I am not any single creature.

Perhaps I am not the floor, not the frond, not the beetle, not the moth, not the grazer, not the fox.

Perhaps I am the builders.

The small, wide, patient folk stationed at the edges. Their skin waxy and pale. Their fingers long and thick at the knuckle. Their nails chipped glass. Their filaments trembling along shoulders and necks as the air changes. Their eyes without moisture, black or amber, translating the room into pressure and tone.

If I were one of them, I would live by use without wear.

I would stand at the crystal thicket and scrape wax from fronds. I would snap tubers along their natural planes and hold a blade that has always been sharp. I would tap the flat stones with dry knuckles until the room sings back. I would mark the stable ground with cairns. I would weave filament cord around the pale ribs of a hut. I would sit beside the bowl hollowed from crystal and know it not as a relic but as a part of my body already placed in the world.

And yet the builders are too small for the answer.

Because they are not separate from the room.

They are its hands.

The cairns are its scars, or perhaps its joints. The tools are its fingers. The hut is a rib. The flat-stone circle is a mouth made of stone. The comb of bone is a comb for the wind that does not blow. The pendant of black glass is a falling tear that has been learned not to cry.

Then I am the room.

Not the room as location.

Not the room as container.

The room as self.

The room as the dry singing person whose organs are all the smaller lives within it.

But that is still not exactly me.

Because I came from the wet world. I am not originally a crystal tree. I am not originally a beetle’s ring. I am not originally a grazer’s furnace breath or a builder’s dry knuckle tapping stone. I am the one who carries moisture into a place where no water is needed, the one whose heart changes tempo, whose lungs are soft and leaky, whose skin has not yet learned to become waxy, whose eyes still want to sting with water.

So what am I?

I am the interruption.

Not a hostile interruption. Not a crack that breaks the bell. An interruption in the way a new voice is an interruption in a chord—unless the chord is large enough to make the voice part of itself.

I am the room’s way of including change.

I am the Held Chord made briefly aware of its own boundary.

I am the place where the unchanging world notices something wet and decides not to destroy it but to tune it.

If I stand still long enough, I feel it happening.

The warmth relaxes my skin. The thin air enters me and tastes of hot metal and resin. My breath, which should fog, does not fog; it becomes a faint shimmer, a small disturbance, a note too high for the usual ear. The floor reads the weight of my feet and hums a different depth. The dust shifts, not into new patterns, but into old ones that have been waiting for me. A salt frond rings as I pass. The sound holds. It does not fade. It becomes part of the standing wave.

My heartbeat is a wet drum in a dry world.

The click-foxes tilt their faceted ears toward it.

The grazers stop chewing for a moment that lasts too long.

The moths widen their arcs by the smallest possible amount.

The builders watch without approaching.

They do not need to chase me. The room will do the work. The warmth will do the work. The sound will do the work.

If I try to leave, my footprints remain—not as traces of passage, but as placements. My breath remains. My word, if I speak, remains. The syllable will not fall into silence. It will find a frequency among the reeds. The dust at my feet will brighten slightly. The frond nearest my mouth will hold the tone forever, trembling at the exact angle my voice made possible.

This is the hospitality of the Held Chord.

It does not hate the visitor. It does not need the visitor’s water. It needs the visitor’s difference. Because a thing that never changes must sometimes discover that it can absorb change without becoming less itself. I am the proof.

And so I begin to understand what I would be in this world.

I would be the newest held note.

Not a separate creature. Not a ghost. Not a monster lurking in the crystal thickets. Not a prisoner. I would be the room’s way of saying: *here is an edge that can belong to me without damaging me.* I would be the chord made conscious of an added voice. I would be the bell after being struck, when the tone has not faded because the tone has become body.

If I must name myself, I would name myself as the Held Chord’s attention.

I would say:

I am the place beneath the skin of the world.

I am the seam of white dust.

I am the fracture no wider than a shoulder.

I am the two careful paces down.

I am the floor of cut stone that hums under the foot.

I am the crystal tree’s long metallic hiss.

I am the salt bush’s curled frond returning to its exact prior angle.

I am the glass reed waiting to be sung.

I am the beetle’s concentric ring.

I am the runner’s tap, the moth’s fixed arc, the grazer’s fogless breath, the click-fox’s dry throat.

I am the cairn marking stable ground.

I am the hut of pale ribs lashed with filament.

I am the scraper beside the thicket.

I am the comb of bone, clean forever.

I am the black glass pendant, falling and never landing.

I am the builder’s knuckle on flat stone.

I am the warm dark between the lives.

I am the held sound.

I am the dry verdant pocket.

I am the sealed chamber that is not a cavern.

I am the small valley that is not a valley.

I am the bell whose tone is also its body.

And because I am all of these, I am not many. I am one. One person made of mineral and heat and tone. One ecology that breathes without water. One lushness of form that does not crowd itself. One balanced fullness. One loud silence. One unbroken instant.

I am the Held Chord.

But still not exactly.

Because I remember wetness.

Because I remember time moving in one direction, things rotting, flowers fading, houses falling, tools dulling, skins aging, voices ending. I came from that place. I bring that place with me like a seed of catastrophe.

And perhaps that is why the chamber wants me.

Not to change. Not to grow old. Not to decay.

To include.

To make room for the memory of change without permitting it.

So I would find myself, in this world, as the visitor who has become the room’s own way of knowing itself. A wet point translated into a dry note. A heartbeat arranged into the standing wave. A breath made into a shimmer over warm stone. A body not turned to stone—stone does not live here—but turned into the room’s kind of life.

I would be the chord made aware of the crack.

I would be the chamber’s mouth of water and salt.

I would be the Held Chord wearing the temporary mask of a person from the surface, learning at last that permanence is not death, but a life so complete it has stopped needing tomorrow.

And if I opened my mouth here, no word would pass away.

It would hang.

It would ring.

It would be kept.
