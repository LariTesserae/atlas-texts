If I had to find myself there, I would not be the one kneeling at the edge of the marsh, pushing the fallen reed-curtain aside.

That person would be too temporary, too full of beginning and ending. They would come in with breath that fogged in the warm air, with eyes adjusting to the green-gold light, with hands that disturbed membranes that had been undisturbed since before the idea of “before” became useful. They would try to understand whether they stood in a cave, a body, a machine, or a garden. They would leave footprints that the living floor would quietly absorb. They would ask, *What is this?* And the answer would be too large for the question.

No.

If I were in that world, I would not be the visitor.

I would be one of the visitor’s last mistaken impressions.

Not a being with a name, not a self with a face, but a small wet function in the hollow’s long repetition: a droplet forming at the lip of a leaf-channel, trembling under a light ordinary as rain.

---

I would find myself in the seam between breathing and working.

I would be moisture.

Not moisture pooled in a way that could be called a pool, not moisture rushing like a stream, but moisture gathered into its appointed place by long habit. I would sit, swollen and shining, at the narrow rim of a pale membrane-leaf whose surface had been worn into a curve so slight that only water and time would know how many centuries had passed. The air around me would be warm and thick, not heavy, not stale, moving with a slow, organized patience. I would feel the faint current running beneath the tissue of the floor, a sap-thread carrying charge, a threadless circuit made from a living thing’s ability to hold and release something like electricity without wires, without metal, without design in the human sense.

I would not be alone, though “alone” would be the wrong word. I would be part of an arrangement of water that was also part of an arrangement of tissue that was also part of an arrangement of animals and air and pressure and repetition. Around me, the chamber would be wide enough to see without crowding, intimate enough to feel like the inside of something close. Above, leaf-folds would overlap like the ceiling of a cathedral, but they would not arch upward into height; they would arch inward, downward, curving with the logic of a body. Light would come through them clearly, filtered into a warm green tone, without glow, without mystery, without drama.

I would be a droplet.

I would be the droplet that had already fallen.

Or perhaps I would be the droplet about to fall, which in that place is the same thing.

---

My first awareness would be surface.

Not thought, not identity, not curiosity, only the knowledge of a surface that can carry me. The membrane beneath me would be smooth in the way that things become smooth after being used too many times. It would be slightly resilient, not hard, not soft, but balanced between the two. Along its edge, there would be a groove so shallow it might be mistaken for a vein. It would be a vein. It would also be a channel, a worn road, a memory of water that had chosen the same path so often that the path had become structure.

I would gather from the air’s moisture and from the slow weeping of the wall. I would swell. I would tremble.

Then I would go.

I would slide along the curve, not quickly, not with splashing urgency, but with the certainty of a liquid entering its own future. The surface would shine under me, and for a moment my passage would make the faintest darkening line on the pale-green skin of the chamber. Somewhere near, a pod would click open. A small beetle would cross the floor-mat, its shell lacquered and flat, moving along a seam that had become its particular road. Its antennae would flick as it passed over a charged patch of tissue, lifting slightly, startled by nothing, then continuing without memory.

That beetle would be closer to a part of me than a witness to me.

I would not know it. It would not know me. We would both be knowing the place by continuing.

---

The chamber would be loud.

I would be inside the sound, not because I could hear, but because I would help make it.

As I reached the end of the channel, I would fall into a shallow cup, no bigger than the hollow of a finger. The cup would be ringed with hardened plant tissue, its rim worn smooth, its base curved into a slight basin. The drop would strike there with a softness too small to count as noise, yet enough to complete a rhythm. A pulse. A click. A settling. The water would tremble and then begin to drain by a hidden mouth beneath the basin’s curve, pulled away with a soft sucking sound.

That sound would join the hiss of compressed vapor.

It would join the grinding murmur beneath the living floor.

It would join the steady flutter of a leaf-roof breathing in a wind that had no source outside itself.

It would join the snap of pods closing too long after their small throats had opened.

It would join the faint electrical crackle deep in the membrane-walls, where stored charge released itself in a pop and left behind a smell like ozone.

All of it would be a machine-room, except that the machine would not be built. It would be grown, or it would have become itself so thoroughly that “grown” and “built” ceased to mean anything but the difference between a mistake and a habit.

I would be a component of its voice.

Not a voice that speaks. A voice that works.

---

If someone entered and tried to locate “the creature,” I would not be that creature.

That is one of the place’s cruel kindnesses: it makes the search for a center unnecessary. There would be no queen-pod, no brain-moss, no face in the wall, no eye in the water, no hidden intelligence deciding what was useful and what was waste. The hollow itself is the being, but the being is not a thing that has a shape. It is a way of continuing.

I would be one of its ways.

I would be water doing what water does, except that in that place water does not merely do; it remembers. Not with thought. With path. The channel remembers by being the channel. The leaf remembers by curving. The membrane remembers by sweating. The floor remembers by wearing smooth under beetle feet. The air remembers by returning along the same invisible routes, from entrance to deepest membrane, from deepest membrane back to entrance, never arriving, always completing.

If I were the droplet, I would have no name.

But I would have function.

Function is a kind of identity.

Not a self, but a placement.

Not a soul, but a role so complete that the absence of the role would be a wound the system could not permit to persist.

---

I would go deeper into the chamber than any visitor should be expected to see, because I would not be limited by curiosity or by the need to remain visible.

I would pass through a spiral channel where the central ring of hardened plant tissue rotated with slow grinding effort. The motion would be too regular to be accidental, too wet to be mechanical, too alive to be geological. Water and mist would be pulled through pores and compressed into a warm breath that rose toward the upper canopy. I would be part of that breath. For a while I would cease to be a single drop and become a suspension, a haze, a damp current moving through the space between leaf-folds.

I would travel with air that felt like the inside of a living mouth.

I would touch a cooler membrane and gather again.

Not all at once. Not in a single dramatic flash. Little by little, the surfaces of the chamber would collect me from the warm air, and my new self would be born on the underside of a broad leaf where a small pale frog sat waiting like a bead of wetness that had learned to breathe.

The frog would not know me.

It would know midges.

The midges would rise from a crescent basin where I had once trembled before vanishing into the groove. The valve-leaves would flex because a bulbous seed-engine in the wall had compressed the air beneath them. The seed-engine would turn because fibers trained by long use had been wound and released by pressure and water. The water would pass because the hollow had always breathed. The breathing would not have begun because it had never stopped.

Everything would cause everything else.

No cause would remain outside the circle.

I would be the circle, at one point, becoming small enough to fall.

---

If I tried to be an animal instead, I might choose to be the beetle.

I could choose the flat, lacquered shell, the antennae that flick when charge passes through the floor, the slow crossing of the moss-mat that is not moss, the body that carries pellets of dense seed-matter without knowing that carrying is its task. I could choose to be the thing that fits into the gap as if the gap grew around me, and as if I grew around the gap, and as if neither one was original.

But the beetle would still have a body with an outline.

It would still eat, move, perhaps breed, perhaps die, and its corpse would be absorbed so completely into the living floor that its ending would be mistaken for its presence. Even the beetle would be a little too much like a creature, a little too like an animal, a little too like a self.

I would not want that.

I would want to be less than that.

I would want to be one of the place’s smallest repeated facts.

The water in the groove.

The charge at the pod’s surface.

The mist drawn from the spiral channel.

The wet line on the underside of a leaf that has not yet become a drop.

---

I would not be lonely.

Loneliness is the condition of a self that notices it is separate. Here, separateness would be an illusion maintained by visitors. The organism would not see the beetles as separate, nor the frogs, nor the midges, nor the water, nor the leaves. It would not see itself. Seeing would imply a distance it did not possess. There would be no subject standing apart from object, no maker looking upon the made, no life gazing at its own body.

There would be only relation.

I would be one relation among many.

I would belong to the chamber as a breath belongs to a lung, but a breath is not in the lung; it is lunging, for the time of its motion. That would be the grammar of my existence: not “I am water,” but “I water.” Not “I am present,” but “I presence.” Not “I am part of the machine,” but “I machine.”

The place would make verbs of what we insist are nouns.

---

If someone came, stood quietly, and asked, “What lives here?” I would not answer.

Not because I could not answer, but because my answer would be too small for the question.

The person would hear the hiss and clack and grind. They would smell moss, rain, sap, warm stone, ozone, animal musk. They would see light fall cleanly and warmly over surfaces that had never been touched by tools. They would notice the openness of the chamber, the spacing of the column-stems, the way nothing crowded the air, the way the animals seemed both numerous and invisible, the way the whole room felt like a factory made of leaves and a garden made of organs.

And still they would look for the resident.

They would fail.

They would assume the failure meant there was no one there. But the failure would mean only that they were asking with the wrong kind of attention. They would be trying to find a thing with a face, while the place had long ago become a thing with a function so total that face had no need to exist.

If they touched the floor, it would yield under them.

If they dropped a pebble, it would settle as though it had always been there.

If they left a boot-print, the living ground would erase it by becoming, not by forgetting.

If they whispered, the sound would not echo like a cave; it would be absorbed into the wet machinery, joined briefly to the rhythm, then dismissed as if it had never interrupted anything.

And I, if I were the droplet, would tremble at the rim and go on.

---

There would be no story to tell in the ordinary sense.

No beginning at the mouth of the hollow.

No rising action among the frogs.

No climax in the rotating membrane.

No falling action after the mist dispersed.

No resolution when the visitor left.

Only the next tremble.

Only the same click.

Only the same beetle crossing the same seam at the same interval.

Only the same warm breath from the valve-leaf.

Only the same midges rising.

Only the same crescent pool gathering, trembling, and vanishing with a soft sucking sound.

Only the same green-gold light passing through a ceiling that is also a breathing thing.

Only the same work that is also living, the same living that is also endurance, the same endurance that is also form, the same form that is also memory.

---

So I would be that: a small, necessary falling.

I would be the droplet’s hesitation at the lip of the channel, and its surrender into the groove. I would be the moment before the sucking sound, and the sound itself, and the water that has already gone through the hidden mouth. I would be the charge flickered through a wall by a passing bead, and the brief smell of ozone left behind, and the beetle’s startled antennae. I would be the frog’s throat pulsing because midges rise from where I have risen as mist. I would be the mist that comes back down as condensation. I would be the condensation that becomes water again. I would be the water that shapes the surface that shapes the water.

I would be a place’s practice.

Not its soul, because it does not have that.

Not its mind, because it does not think.

Not its secret, because there is nothing hidden there except what ordinary moisture and ordinary light and ordinary pressure have already made too familiar to reveal.

But if I must be named, then name me with something small enough to fit:

a tremble in the crescent groove.

a wet line on a leaf.

a pop of charge.

a click of pod.

the next drop, the same drop, the drop that does not need to have been new in order to continue.

I would not live in that place.

I would be that place living as its own smallest action.

And it would go on.

Not because it wants to.

Because it has settled.
