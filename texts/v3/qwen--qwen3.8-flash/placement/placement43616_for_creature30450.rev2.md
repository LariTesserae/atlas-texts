I would not begin by saying what I am. In this world that would be premature.

I would begin with a place, and let the place teach me the shape of the being that belongs to it.

So: I am kneeling—or half crouching, because standing too quickly is a way of breaking things—at the edge of a shallow basin where the dark has just begun to thin from the east. My knee rests on a slab of young stone, rough and sharp enough to bite through the padded skin wrapped around my leg. Behind me rises a hollow stalk, not the tallest tower on the plateau, not the most resonant, but one that has grown crooked and thick in the way that old things grow when they have survived by compromise. Its broken lower arm has braided itself back into the slope through pale cords of root and fiber, and where that braid meets the scree, the soil has thickened into a small dark cushion of lichen-like mats.

This is where I would find myself: not in the center of the towers, not beneath them in the deep shelter of sealed caps, but in the seam between. A shelf. A margin. A listening place.

For a while, I am only a breath-ghost. That is the first thing I know: the small visible proof that I am alive and warm and that the air wants to make that impossible. Then I become something more. I become a pair of wide dark-adapted eyes. I become callused palms, stiffened by many seasons of pressing to bark and gripping frozen tendrils. I become a body rubbed with pale oil from stored seeds and rendered fats, because here the body must become a wall before it can become a person. My wrists and ankles have grown thin lines of hair, perhaps inherited from a lineage that learned heat retention differently than mine. My face is nearly bare, so that frost can be seen on it, read on it. A white line along the cheek means the cold has found a place where the oil failed. A white line along the brow means one must speak before dawn.

I am one of the scattered listeners.

My name—if it is a name—is not a word I can shout. It is an interval, a short figure: two low taps against the side of a dry stalk, then a descending whistle drawn through a hollow bone. When it is sounded correctly, I feel it first in my ribs, then in my jaw, and only afterward in my mind. That is how I know I am meant by it. That is how I know I am found.

To be named in this place is not to be claimed. It is to be recognized as a note the plateau can return.

I would be a person of small works, not of great ones.

I would carry a pouch of fire-stones sealed inside the hard husks of split fruit. I would carry a notched stick for marking passages, a needle-flake of obsidian knapped so thin that holding it too tightly cuts the finger, and a length of pale fiber spun from broken stems and chewed bark. I would have a stone weight shaped like a thumb, used for pressing skins against wind. My tools would not look like tools, perhaps, only like continuations of my hands.

I would know where a pool lies under frost, not because I have seen it, but because the stone placed there by my mother’s mother hums in a certain way when the wind passes over it. I would know a hidden brine basin because the air tastes first of salt and then of metal, and because the cup-leaves near it grow on the windward side, not because they prefer shelter, but because they have learned to drink whatever comes before it vanishes.

I would be careful in the way all living things here are careful: not with fear exactly, but with respect for the fact that motion has consequences.

If I walk, I test the stem before I put weight on it. If I speak, I do so in pulses, in tones chosen for the distance I mean to reach. If I laugh—rarely, but sometimes when a small climber freezes into lichen in front of me, so startled it has forgotten how to be a body—I laugh with my mouth closed and my face turned toward the ground, because laughter carried far can mean food gathering can mean predator turning can mean a herd shifting and the slope sliding and the whole architecture of emptiness answering too loudly to be harmless.

I would know the plants as elders.

Not gods. Elders are stranger and more useful than gods. They do not demand perfect obedience; they only have more patience than you.

I would press my palm to the flank of a great stalk and feel whether it is filling or emptying. If it is full of damp, the sound beneath my hand is a low wet thrum, almost like the memory of a drum. If it is full of gas, the sound is brittle, quick, and dangerous, and I would move away. If it is resonant with the approaching feet of grazers, the column hums a little higher than it should, and I would wait to cross its path. If it is singing through newly opened pores after melt, I would know that water is coming through the plateau like a secret that cannot keep itself, and I would open my mouth and drink from the air for a minute before the cold takes it back.

I would not worship the plants. But I would speak to them.

I would leave a dried bundle at a junction where a tendril has split because it is safer for a child to pass than it is for a stranger. I would tie a knot in a fiber-vine so that, in thirty winters, another hand will come this way and understand: this crack is young, this slope will fail if water is taken, this pool is not to be trusted until the frost ring becomes double. I would not carve my name into stone. If I left a mark at all, it would be the absence of mark: a place where a fire was *not* made, because the gas below had thinned the bark for two seasons and someone, hearing me, would understand why.

I would live in a hut made of bent branch-mat and overlapping cup-leaves tarred with wax, at the base of a stalk whose root-mass has held for longer than my family’s memory. The roof would leak in mist, but not in melt; this would be a source of constant commentary among the elders. “The house knows,” they would say. “It is not wise, but it is weather.”

I would be more than a human-shaped animal here, because the place would shape my inner life.

I would not understand silence the way people in crowded green worlds do. Here, silence is the pause between meanings. It is where the plateau waits for you to decide whether what you heard was warning or invitation. My sleep would be punctuated by clicks and taps and far-off reports: a seed-pod splitting like something shot into a room, a stone releasing itself from a terrace, a small creature crossing a frozen tendril with dry footfalls. I would learn to hear in dreams.

There would be nights when I could not tell whether I had woken or whether the world had only shifted its voice.

I would have companions.

A small climber with eyes too large for its face and tail stiff as a reed would sleep in the hollow of my sleeping place, not as pet, but as mutual alarm. When it flattened against the mat and became a smear of lichen, I would know that the air had changed or that something pale had passed overhead. When it clicked once from the lower joint of the hut, I would know it was safe to move.

There would be another person, not near enough to see but near enough to answer: a band in a basin to the north, or south, or somewhere between where direction is only a matter of which way the sound falls into when it arrives. We would speak by struck sticks and hollow stems. Sometimes we would meet when the bloom-thaw came, when the air smelled of wet bone and metal and the whole web seemed to lean upward. There would be no ceremony for this, or perhaps the meeting itself would be the ceremony. We would eat split fruit, share fire without lighting it, compare notches, listen to each other’s children try to say their names with bone pipes, and part by the time the mist began to pool in the basins.

I would be one of those who knows that the emptiness is not empty.

This is the hardest lesson of the place, and the one that changes you most if you stay long enough to learn it. At first, the plateau’s openness feels like absence. Then, slowly, you realize absence is a medium. The voids are corridors. The gaps between stalks are sound paths. The wide spaces are not wasted. They are held open by the architecture of the living world so that gliders may pass through them, so that condensation may fall in patterns, so that a herd can shift its weight across a terrace without destroying it.

If I remained there long enough, I would become unable to think of my own body as separate from that principle.

I would begin to understand that I am not a person standing *in* the landscape. I am a function of it. I am the place where the web makes meaning. My breath is small. My hut is temporary. My bones will be carved into pins or laid in frost or left beneath a cairn, and the land will absorb me without drama, because here absorption is not an ending but a redistribution.

But before that, I would live.

I would live in the narrow, exact places.

I would drink from a basin only after the films along its edge had gathered into a pale ring, meaning the water had begun to settle. I would know that if the ring broke too quickly, the pool was too shallow for a person to trust, though a climber might drink there with no problem. I would watch the cup-leaves turn on the windward side of stones and understand that this is not beauty but hydrology. I would know that the plants do not merely grow here. They *decide*, in their slow way, what the land will be permitted to become.

I would be afraid sometimes.

Not of dramatic things. Of the subtle failure of surfaces. A frost skin over water that looks like black glass. A stem that sounds solid but is only air and tension. A sudden low hum that might be a glider descending, might be a hunter, might be weather gathering in a pocket and deciding, for no reason you can see, to become a squall. Once I would be caught when the whole plateau began to hum at the same moment, a sustained note so loud it made my teeth ache, and I would press my forehead to a stalk and wait for the note to break into groan or whistle or silence. In that moment I would feel myself as one more cavity in a vast body learning how to breathe.

But fear, here, is not a state to flee. It is a discipline.

I would learn to move gently enough to last. I would learn that gentleness is not softness. It is precision. It is the refusal to use your full weight when half will do. It is the habit of listening before you cross. It is the understanding that force will collapse the web that feeds you, shelter you, warn you, and bury you. The strongest person among us is not the one who can break a stem, but the one who can pass through the whole field without breaking it.

If I were asked what I am, I might say: I am a listener.

If I were asked again, I might say: I am an interval.

If I were asked yet again, I would press my hand to the nearest stalk, let a blue-green pulse travel outward from the sap disturbed by my touch, and say nothing.

At the edge of the basin, the first frost would begin. I would see it before I felt it: a feathering along the stone rim, a thin bright line where the water meets air. The surface would smooth itself into a mirror, not because the sky is full of stars, but because it can hold the small lights made by the living. I would see the blue veins of sap in the stems reflected there. I would see pale eyes opening and closing in the void between towers. I would see a glider cut through the dark above us so briefly that only its absence becomes visible. I would see the herds beyond the basin, broad and shaggy, moving through the field of stalks and making the world sound like something being shaken by unseen hands.

And in that mirror I would see myself: small, dark-eyed, wrapped in waxed skin, breath vanishing almost as soon as it became visible, hand still resting on the stalk that carries its cold blue pulse toward the sky.

I would be a person, yes. But not only a person. I would be the place where the plateau has learned to hear itself.
