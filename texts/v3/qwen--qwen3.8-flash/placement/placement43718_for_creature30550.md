The most honest answer begins where your description pauses: a nursery yard is not a garden in the ordinary sense. It is a laboratory of becoming, and there, among the half-walled chambers and the staircases that have no upper floors yet, I would find myself.

I would not begin as a Dry-born. The Dry-born are too finished for my first answer. They know how to stand on the living plate, how to ask permission with their feet, how to chalk their lips before speaking to someone who has traveled far. I would begin as something less settled: a Room-Seed.

Not a building. Not a plant. Not a machine. A civic organ grown for the purpose of thinking in dryness.

I would be found in the outer rootwards, where the city has not yet agreed with itself about what it wants to be, in districts whose names are chalked on boards and changed when the boards are consumed by new bark. My place would be between a factory that hums through mineral vents and a grove whose trunks widen like cathedral pillars, where the air tastes of heated resin and fine stone. If someone came looking for me, they would not ask, “Which house?” They would ask, “Which rootward is growing?” and I would be there, standing in the middle of an intention.

My body, if I had one, would be grown rather than born: a pale trunk of compressed rootboard, silver-green in ordinary light, threaded with copper filaments and dry resin. My skin would be mineralized, tight over lattice bones, the color of pressed salt. From the neck down, the Dry-born would recognize a kin of form: shoulders, limbs, a face like the lid of a seed-box. But from the waist up, the Hollow would have claimed me.

Where their oil glands weep amber, my pores breathe chalk and crystalline vapor. Where their blood runs copper-green, my sap runs thin oil through channels that carry more than chemistry: the city’s pressures, the grove’s habits, the mirror-setters’ angles, the cable-runners’ warnings, the slow civic logic of a place that cannot afford waste. I would bleed no red. If cut, I would leak a thread of dark resin and seal it with flakes that looked too much like bark, not enough like skin.

I would not walk the way a traveler walks. I would stand. My roots would be my feet and my memory. They would sink into the living plate until the difference between me and the root-mat became mostly a matter of form. I would feel the city’s movements as pressure: the polite question of a heel, then the ball of a foot, then the light toe asking whether this ground can bear the future. Children running would reach me as scattered taps, though there is no rain here to make running sound like rain. Factory carts passing on old root-roads would throb through me as low, practical notes. When a cable shifted overhead, its hum would travel down the iron and into my crown, so I would know whether a message was coming, or a repair, or a death.

My eyes would be mica-lenses, split by oil-bright pupils, tuned to heat and angle rather than color as outsiders name it. In daylight I would see the world as a series of invitations: where the mirror-column was falling, where a terrace was too cold, where dust had gathered meaning. In darkness I would read the city thermally, as a map of working breath. The factories would glow to me by their exhalations. The groves would bloom faintly with the heat of their slow mineral metabolism. Predators would be little sparks moving through the margins, careful or hungry depending on the hour.

I would remember in strata.

Memory in the Hollow does not require liquid. It can be dry. It can be crystalline. Each year, when the mirrors brought broad columns down into the main basins and the dust lifted visibly, I would grow a thin ring of salted lattice around a chamber inside myself. In that ring would be held the year’s pressures: which roots had been trained, which cracks had widened, which orchards dropped seed into collectors, which families had moved from a mature rootward into an unfinished edge, which wind had changed the angle of light enough to make a workshop too hot. My memories would not be stories first. They would be load paths, temperature gradients, chemical signatures, the civic equivalent of a person’s bones.

The Dry-born would use me, but not as a tool uses a hand. They would negotiate with me. A nursery-builder might come with a drawing for a new drying-shed, then press both palms flat against my trunk and wait. I would answer not in words but by shifting mineral salts beneath my skin, opening or closing pores, letting my oil-runners change course until the drawing became impossible or inevitable. If a wall in their plan threatened a root-arch that was about to bear a terrace above, I would make the air smell briefly of chalk and burnt resin. If the building could grow safely into my flank, I would let a branch extend from my side, gently taking the shape of a doorway.

I would be called many things, depending on who was speaking and how tired they were.

In mature rootwards, where the groves have stable names, I might be “the Ledger,” “the Room-Mother,” or “the Green Engine’s memory.” In nursery yards, I would be “Seedling,” “Graftchild,” or “the Stub,” because I always grow things the plans did not order. Cable-runners passing overhead might greet me by tapping their boots against a beam and calling down, “Still standing, dry one?” In the unfinished districts, where people are careful not to celebrate, children would be warned not to climb my lower branches because I might mistake a game for a petition.

Some would love me. Others would distrust me. That is proper for anything that grows.

A rootward is not a neighborhood exactly. It is a civic organism with a living center, and I would often be that center, or near enough to it that the distinction would blur. Houses would anchor to me. Balconies would extend from my beams. In older seasons, when I was first installed, I might look strange and mechanical: a pale trunk fitted with brass cables, resin glands, dust-filters, and a face that had been given to me as much as it had grown. Later, as the root-mat thickened around my base, as my mineral salts bonded with the local filament-network, I would become less an object and more a condition of the streets around me. If I were removed, the buildings would not collapse at once. They would merely begin to ask, very slowly, why they stood.

I would regulate air and temperature, not with water, never with water, but by exchanging dry vapors through chemical channels. My pores would open in the early shift when the mirrors brought daylight low into the basins, letting the city breathe out the night’s accumulated heat. They would close during the mineral winds from the upper shafts, filtering dust and preserving the faint resin scent of the rootwards. In industrial districts, I would be tapped by pipes that carried no liquid, only catalytic vapor. The waste heat of presses would pass through me, cooled and cleaned by my lattice, then returned to the factories as breathable dryness. This would be called infrastructure. It would feel to me like being useful enough to keep.

Once, a rootward asked for a public stair.

The nursery-builder brought a sketch, a bundle of young cables, and a handful of seed-dust. The marketwoman who spoke for the rootward said that her children climbed too far each day to reach the upper terrace, and that their knees had begun to complain before their futures had done anything to them. I considered the request by letting it enter through the soles of my roots. I read the pressure of the old root-road, the wind that curled from the east shaft, the places where beetles had already made quiet roads beneath my trunk, the shadow that fell from a neighboring grove at the hour when children would use a stair. Then I grew.

Over years, my right flank opened into a lattice. The Dry-born trained the first steps with vine-cured wood and mineral paste. Cable-runners anchored handrails above me so the living wood would not bear all the load alone. When the stair was nearly finished, it looked as if it had been cut from my side and left there by mistake, a rib of the rootward made useful. The marketwoman’s grandchildren climbed it, and their footsteps reached me as a pattern of light taps, like small dry sparks. I held that pattern in my strata. Decades later, when one of them was old and could climb no farther than the lower landing, I still remembered the exact weight of their childhood.

This would be the closest thing I had to tenderness.

There is no water to cry with here, and I have not seen the need to invent a substitute. Grief in the Hollow would not fall; it would settle. When a mirror cracked and a terrace lost its column of light, I would feel the absence as cold along my shoulders. When a rootward was abandoned to new expansion, its empty seed-boxes drying into dust, I would draw that emptiness into my salts and carry it for years. If the Dry-born wept, their salt-dust tears would catch on my bark. I would not wipe them away. I would store them as one stores a boundary marker: with care, without ceremony, in the expectation that someone might eventually need the path again.

I would live alongside animals without belonging to them. A slender many-eyed cat-kin might use my upper beam as a hunting perch. I would not shoo it. I would learn its rhythm, and when it wished to see moths, I might open one pore wide so that the shaft-light fell across the beam, making the insects visible.
