Their first punishments are usually not physical: a cooling of the sleeping mat, a shift to a higher, grit-filled terrace, a day spent scraping the salt heaps until the hands forget other work. A child is not struck as often as set aside—placed near a dead vent, or away from the lamp shelf, or made to stand in the cold hiss of the upper crack until the body learns that in this place the mouth must be careful, and attention is a kind of food.

If I were to be found in this world, I would not be found standing above it, looking down. I would be found low, crouched, fitted.

I would be in the hidden seam when the plateau wind turns the thin band of sky to a roaring gray ribbon and dust begins to sift through the roof gaps. I would be on the third terrace below the Salt Square, where the stone runs hot through a black basalt channel and the salt veins ring like little bones when you step near them. My feet would know that place before my mind did: the slight rise, the dip, the cool patch where a crack breathes only warm, the sharper patch where it exhales sulfur and heat. I would stand there with a bowl of rendered plant oil in one hand and a scraped stalk-fiber in the other, making a wick for a lamp that must not go out until it is given its replacement.

I would be one of the people, yes. But not merely a person.

In the bowl, to be a person is to be an organ of something larger, and I would be a mouth of the town. A small one. A careful one. I would be a salt-wick tender, one of those who learns early that light is not a luxury and not a sign. Light is a debt. It must be paid out of oil, fiber, salt, patience, and the dead leaves of plants that have already converted the rock’s breath into wax and fragrance. A lamp is not a thing that burns. A lamp is a mouth that eats the town’s waste and returns it as visibility, warmth, warning, work, memory.

So I would be called by what I tend.

Not “the keeper,” exactly. A title in the Salt Square would sound more like a sound than a word: a short clipped note, a scrape of bone over stone, a hiss of pitch. But if a visitor from above the plateau tried to name me, I would be something like Wick-Mouth, Second-Shape, Salt-Return. My name would change as my work changed. As a child I might be “the one who has not yet learned to pinch the hot seam.” As an adult I would be “the one whose braid carries the third terrace.” If I had a memory of the dead braided into my hair, I might be “the one who remembers the vent that collapsed in the wind-year.” If my fingers were stained with seed-oil and ash, I might be “the one who trims without wasting.”

I would be small in the way the people there are small: short, dense, broad-chested, built for pushing through narrow passages and climbing rounded stone, not for running long distances in open country. My shoulders would be packed hard with muscle from lifting salt slabs and carrying oil bowls. My back would curve slightly from bending into granary pits and pressing my palm near vents to read their breath. My hands would be broad, the fingers short, the pads thick, the nails hard and flattened by scraping. The right palm would know heat before pain; the left would know grain before texture. My feet would be as useful as my eyes.

My skin would be burnished, the color of old oil and warm clay, with pale dust settled into the creases at my elbows and knees. At night my eyes would dilate until the whites were nearly gone, so that in a dark terrace I might appear faceless, only two beetle-shell depths where gaze should be. By day, with salt glare filtering down through the cleft, my eyes would be narrow, lids heavy, lashes stiff with fine dust. I would not blink much. Blinking is a habit of wet places. Here the wind dries the lids, and one learns to stare until the salt becomes part of seeing.

I would not smell like a clean thing, at least not as a visitor might understand cleanliness. I would smell of the town’s long body: resin rubbed into scalp and forearms, sulfur in the lower pits, animal fat warmed in a clay bowl, crushed seeds, dust, smoke, the sharp sweetness of pitch when the light finds the roof seams. When another person passed too close in a narrow stair, I would know them before opening my eyes. The woman who grinds salt cakes near the lower square has a sharper pitch smell because her hands are always in the seam paste. The child who carries moth wings from the upper shafts smells faintly of oil dust and bird. The vent-tender smells like heated stone and bitter mineral. I would recognize my household by its particular mixture, the way another person might recognize a face.

My hair would be bound close, rubbed with fat, braided with short cords of stalk-fiber or thin hide. I would not let it wander loose where it can catch salt, dust, beetle legs, or roof cords. A loose braid is a careless mouth; it takes things without asking. If I were a child, I might wear it longer, because longer hair says carry me, shelter me, do not yet place me at a seam that breathes hot. When I became adult, I would cut it short or coil it tight, marking the change with a thread of sinew around one temple. That thread would mean: I eat with the workers. I sleep with the workers. I trim the wicks. I return what I take.

I would be hungry often, but not in a dramatic way. Hunger here would not roar. It would gather in the teeth like salt dust, in the gums like tightening stone, in the hollow under the ribs like a cool spot in a warm room. Thirst would not make me think of pools or rivers. Thirst would make me think of my own body as an oil flask needing refill. I would know it by the stiffness of the skin near the mouth, the dullness behind the eyes, the way the tongue would feel as if lined with dried resin. I would solve it slowly, by chewing seed paste, licking fat from a stone bowl, swallowing a small cake of roasted bulb and salt. The body would make its hidden wetness from that. I would not drink. I would metabolize. I would convert.

As a child, my first meal would not have been liquid in the ordinary sense. It would have been warm, thick, bitter-sweet, pressed from seed and fat and mother’s milk, a thing that coated the throat rather than washed it. I would have grown up on emulsions: pounded seeds soaked in oil, rendered fats stirred with ash, roasted bulbs ground to paste, beetle flour, salted moths. My teeth would have learned early to close on grit without complaining. My jaw would be broad for grinding, not soft for tasting. Food would not be pleasure first, though it could become pleasure. Food would be the bowl’s grammar. Every bite would be a sentence: heat, salt, fiber, return.

I would learn to climb before I learned to run. Running is for open floors, and there are almost none here. The bowl is made of edges. To move is to negotiate lips, steps, shelves, gaps, cords, hanging mats, drying leaves, beetle trails, salt rims, warm vents. I would learn to grip a terrace with my toes, to hook a thumb behind a stone flake, to swing my weight from one broad handhold to another. I would learn to squeeze through roof gaps where salt flakes bite the ribs. I would learn to stand still while dust crosses the street like a slow river of grit, keeping my mouth shut until it passes. Wasting breath on complaint would be foolish. Breath is a mouth too.

My work would begin before full light reached the terrace. It would begin when the lamps needed trimming. I would take down the lanterns from their cord loops, scrape the old char from their bowls, save the ash because ash is salt’s memory, pack the spent fiber into the compost heap or feed it to the seed-rats in their patched tunnels. I would select new stalks from the hanging gardens, split them, beat them, twist them, soak them in warmed oil, draw them through powdered salt so the flame would not flare. A wick must burn low and steady. A flare is theft from the fat; a flare is a forgetful mouth.

I would tend the roofs of the houses that lean into the bowl’s walls. I would scrape salt dust from hide coverings and reed-mats, sweep it into troughs, carry it to the communal bins. The dust is not dirt. The dust is the plateau returning its surface to the town. It can pack a floor, line a pit, stiffen a paste, catch heat, dry a wound, preserve fat. If I spilled it, I would be punished by having to gather it again with a bone scraper, on my knees, while the wind hissed above. Waste is not merely foolish here. Waste is forgetting. Forgetting is death wearing a patient face.

I would know the plants, but not as a lowland gardener knows them. My hands would know them by oil. The broad stiff leaves would be glossy under my thumb, waxy, heavy with scent when crushed. I would pluck them with a twist that does not tear the mat, because the plant is not owned; it is borrowed. It draws mineral warmth and hydrocarbon breath from the rock and turns it into resin and fat. When its leaves fall, I would not sweep them into emptiness. I would feed them to beetles, or lay them in a compost bed, or dry them into stalk-cord, or press their fat into a lamp bowl. If a plant died, I would ask where it would go next.

The beetles would know my hands. In the lower pits, the obsidian shells would scatter at my touch, then settle again because my touch carries no poison, only the salt-oil of a person who belongs to the cycle. I would collect dead beetles from the stairways and feed them to the lizards on the drying benches. I would not crush live ones unless hunger demanded it. Even hunger would be careful. The town’s granaries contain beetle flour in compressed cakes, but a beetle is also a cleaner, a breaker of fallen leaf, a maker of tunnels, a carrier of dust. To kill carelessly is to make the floor harder to sweep. That is one kind of poverty.

Moths would fill my evening. They would come from the upper shafts in silent clouds, oily wings rust-colored and faintly warm at the edges. Some would be caught, but not all. Moth wings can carry scent, light, heat, and dust. I would let certain clouds pass over the Salt Square without snatching them because the town also needs the birds, and the birds need the moths, and the birds carry seeds between terraces and watch for the sharp smell of a vent gone wrong. Every creature is a mouth. If I make it disappear too quickly, another mouth will starve later.

The birds in the higher shafts would startle me sometimes. Short bodies, wide wings, oily plumage, bright clipped notes. They dart through the stone channels like thrown stones that remember how to live. I would not chase them. I might set a small salt-lined snare for a fallen one, for a sick one, for a one that hits a cord at night. If I caught a bird alive and it was not hungry with me, I would let it go, because birds are the town’s upper hearing. They enter and leave through places no human body can easily fit. If they stop calling, something in the wind has changed. That warning is worth more than meat.

The lizards would be my neighbors. I would step around their sun-warmed terraces, and when they stopped upright, throats working in dry little pulses, I would stop too. We would read one another. A lizard alert means the stone is moving, the vent breath has changed, or a human has done something sudden. A lizard flattened means too much heat, or a hawk-shadow above, or dust falling from the roof gap. I would not touch one unless it was sick or needed to be moved. Their broad padded feet are useful indicators; they press flat against warm stone and know its temperature better than a stranger’s palm.

The seed-rats and dust-hares would live beneath us, and we would live by their tunnels. I would avoid walking where a patched floor sagged, because to break a burrow ceiling without repair is to insult the bowl’s underside. If I passed and found fresh dust piling in a stair crack, I would mark it with a pebble line. That mark would tell the household: a mouth opens here, take your weight elsewhere. We would let their tunnels breathe. Sometimes we would bait them into salt-lined pits when the granary needed thinning. We would keep their pelts, their fat, their bone awls. Nothing would leave without returning in another shape. Even the rat’s skull would become part of the town’s grammar: a bead, a weight, a handle, a memory of food.

I would live in the walls, not above them. My house would be a salt-block room pressed against a terrace, roof shared with another roof, wall used as a loom, floor used as drying rack, doorway cut low so dust and heat are kept. At night I would sleep near my siblings or cousins or bonded household people, the air dense with the smell of hides, fat, salt, and bodies. We would not need a blanket thick enough for water-heavy cold. We would need closeness, oil, shelter from grit. Our sleep would be layered with sound. The hiss of wind. The scrape of salt dust. The soft gurgle of oil in channels. The pop of heat expanding in the rock. The rustle of beetle legs. The shimmer of moth wings. Work chants from below. The town would never be silent, and silence in this place would not be peace. Silence would mean a mouth gone shut, a vent dead, a lantern burned dry.

My work would be noisy because life there is dense. I would hammer salt blocks where the pitch seams must be renewed. I would grind stone against stone until the sound became part of my pulse. I would weave stalk-cord on a wall-frame, my feet pressing the loom bars. I would scrape oil from broad leaves into a clay dish, my shoulders burning with repetitive labor. I would shout to another terrace when a cord was too tight, when a lamp flickered, when the wind shifted, when a child had gone near the upper mouth without permission. The shouts would not carry far in a straight line; they would curve around the bowl and return softened. Work chants would bounce strangely off curved walls, making the town sound larger than its few hundred paces. I would be one voice among many, one small mouth adding itself to the whole dry roar.

If the Salt Square filled for evening, I would sit on a bench near a pit where the vent warmth made the stone glow faintly through the dim. The square would not be bright. It would be bronze, brown, black, with white salt at the edges and the dull gold of fat lamps. Shadows would move behind lifted lanterns, making the coiled cords seem longer, the sleeping animals seem stranger, the hanging mats seem alive. I would not be afraid. I would know those shadows. They are the town’s second skin. They are the parts of the mouth that move when it speaks: the tongue of heat, the teeth of stone, the lips of salt, the throat of the cleft.

In that light, I would notice the faint phosphorescence of the gardens. Not fire. Not brilliance. The resin glow at the edges of broad leaves after dark, as if the plants had learned to keep a little warmth visible without spending it. At night the bowl seems edged in soft lamp-light, and I would feel that my body too was a lamp. My skin, rubbed with oil and salt, would catch light at the shoulders and forearms. My pupils would widen. My breath would smell of pitch and seed. If I walked through a dark passage, a child might mistake me for a hanging mat or a lizard on a hot stone. That is good. To be mistaken for part of the bowl is to belong to it.

I would be careful with fire. Fire here is work and cooking, not survival. The people do not need fire to keep from freezing because warmth rises through the rock. But fire transforms. It makes bitter salts boil, hides cure, pitch flow, seed cakes brown, lamp oil reach the wick. A fire is a mouth that eats quickly. It must be fed with patience. I would know when to keep it small, when to let it cool, when to save its ash, when to press the burning wood into charcoal. I would not let it roar. Roaring is a sign of waste.

I would have scars, but they would be the dry scars of salt and stone. Thin lines across the palms from stalk fibers. White flakes lodged in scraped skin. Hardness over the knee from kneeling on benches. A ridge of callus where a salt slab once slipped. These scars would be read like stories. My grandmother’s scar across one hand might mean she once reached too quickly into a vent pit. My brother’s missing finger tip might mean he learned too late not to bite a hot stone. My own scar would not be dramatic. It would be ordinary: a salt burn where I had trusted the wrong seam. The punishment for that would not be a beating. It would be a cooling. A shift away from the warm terraces. A day of scraping without speaking. I would learn, from the absence of heat, what heat is.

I would have relationships formed by labor, not by abstract choice. The person beside me on the loom would matter because their rhythm could match mine. The person who shared my lamp bowl would matter because they would trim the wick before I did if they trusted me enough to sleep. The person who carried my oil from the upper terrace would matter because if they spilled, I would know the weight of what the town could lose. Love would be a matter of careful feeding. To love someone is to know which bitter salts they can eat, which vent seams they trust, which shadows make them still, which smells mean they are home. A mother’s affection would be the pressure of a child’s body against her chest in a hide sling, both of them hearing the town’s noise from infancy, both of them learning the bowl’s rhythm before they could speak.

I would speak little unless speech was useful. A visitor might hear constant noise and think the people are loud, careless, chaotic. They would not understand that the noise is measured. Work chants keep labor even. Shouts mark danger. Songs remember the dead in patterns that keep hands busy. Gossip is still a mouth, and mouths must be careful.
