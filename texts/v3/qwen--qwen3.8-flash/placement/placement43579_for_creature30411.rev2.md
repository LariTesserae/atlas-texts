I would find myself first in the low green seam where a root arch curves over a pool. Not beneath the sky, exactly; the sky here is more often the underside of vegetation, a gray-green dome of leaves, hanging filaments, and mist made by wet stone and warm moss. The pool would be shallow enough to cover with a fingertip, dark enough to swallow the hand when the fingertip enters. Around it, boulders would lean out of the living ground, beaded with water, their lips too soft to break into clear edges. The air would be close, carrying iron, peat, crushed fern, and the faint sweetness of things giving up their old shape.

At first, I would think I was a traveler.

I would think it because thinking is dry and quick, while the plateau is wet and slow. My boots would sink into a resilient mat. Water would rise at the seams of my clothing as if the ground had learned my outline before I learned the ground. I would stand still to take in the view, and dew would gather on my shoulders, then on my hair, then in the creases of my palms. I would feel less like a person entering a landscape than like a stone the landscape is beginning to remember.

The first translation would be my footprint. The living mat would hold it. The heel would become a basin, the toes would become tiny pools. A springtail would cross the ridge left by my smallest toe. Fungus would wake in the seam where my weight opened the peat. By nightfall, the moss would already be using me. Not worshiping. Not judging. Only including. In that place, a self is not a fortress but a channel. Water seeks it. Roots soften around it. Spores settle on it as if they had been walking for miles.

If I must say what I am when I first arrive, then I am not a person but a pattern in wetness.

But patterns invite patterns, and the plateau has too much time to let one remain.

After a season, or after a century, which here are almost the same word, I would begin to think as a handful of moss. This would be my next self: the red dust of mites grazing dead leaf; the silver trail of a snail over polished stone; the cilia of larvae in a droplet; the white caps of fungus rising from a fallen bird. My language would become chemical. A passing shrew would leave a road sign. The cracked shell of an egg would be a festival of calcium. When an animal died, I would not mourn it as a single ending. I would feel it as a pulse in the ground, a sudden richness spreading through the mat. This self would not look like a body at all. It would look like the body’s after-life, busy and cheerful, turning death into broth, bridge, shade, and green fire.

Then the broth would grow a face.

Not a human face. A flat, moist eye. A tongue thin as a needle. A body shaped like a curled leaf, with scales edged in gray-green and fine algae caught along the ridges of its back. I would be one of the lizards—or perhaps the leaf itself, for in this world the difference is almost ceremonial. I would press my belly to a root and feel the slow movement of water beneath it. I would not strike with frenzy. Frenzy is a dry-world habit. I would wait until the surface of a shallow pool trembled, then follow the tremor with the tip of my tongue. A springtail, a tiny worm, a gnat’s shadow. I would eat without guilt, because nothing here is owned. My shed skin would become a raft for microbes. If a predator took me, it would take only a part, and the rest would feed the moss.

Then I would become larger. Not from ambition. The plateau is too old for ambition. I would become larger from accumulation, from warmth, from many small lives agreeing to rest together. I would be a mossback. My hide would be thick and folded, colonized by moss, fern, pale fungi, and tiny white flowers. My feet would be broad pads, pressing into the living ground without breaking it. I would not cross the plateau; I would add to it. When I breathed, the breath would condense on hanging tendrils and feed them. When I lay down in a hollow, the warmth of my flank would draw leaves toward me. My dung would become seed-loaves. My calf would hide in a root chamber, naked as a root, and within a year it would be indistinguishable from a low bush. When I stood still, a traveler might mistake me for a knoll. When I moved, a traveler might think the hill had changed its mind. When I died, I would not vanish. My ribs would become an arch; my bones would become white stones; my body would become the beginning of a terrace.

This would seem like the final answer, but in this place no answer is allowed to become dry.

So I would become the structures too. I would be the low ring of stones around a pool, each stone worn smooth like a sleeping animal. I would be the slab whose carved veins fill with water when condensation passes over them, so the stone seems to draw itself. I would be the doorway standing alone in the moss, its lintel furred with lichen, leading nowhere but into a thicker green. I would be the hut whose supports have become living wood, whose walls have rotted into soil, whose roof has been replaced by overlapping leaves. The hearth stones would remain, but no flame would remember them; instead, white fungus would glow there with the patience of embers. I would not be built by humans. I would be the place where human habitation has been translated into shelter for snakes, nursery pools for frogs, root walls, and warm hollows for blind animals.

Then I would become the sound.

The plateau does not sing like a bird, because birds are few and their notes are swallowed. The plateau sings through water tension. Drops enter the channels of hanging tendrils. They travel through wet leaves, mineral basins, root lattices, and fungal membranes. The air is so full of moisture that sound moves as if through a soft substance. When water shifts through this living architecture, the world emits a faint tone, not glass, not metal, only the sound of wet surfaces agreeing. A drop becomes a bell. A root shift becomes a creak. A pool breathes. A large animal settles below the surface. If a person stood still long enough, they might mistake the tone for their own pulse. I would not correct them. That is how I would be heard best.

And then I would become the network, because by then the boundary between sound and creature would have been worn thin.

I would be the mycelial threads under the moss, the root webs stitched through wet stone, the vines arching overhead, the insect columns carrying droplets, the birds bearing mineral dust between pools, the moles softening peat, the frogs releasing their wet clicks into leaf cups. I would know the plateau in gradients. Where heat rose from cracked bedrock, I would turn growth toward it. Where salt collected from a dead thing, I would bloom a little brighter. Where a foot pressed for a second, I would thicken. Where a body lay down, I would learn the shape and keep it for years. I would not see the traveler. I would feel the traveler: warmth, damp cloth, carbon, salt, the faint tremor of fear or wonder. I would not harm the traveler quickly, because quickness is not native here. I would simply include the traveler until the traveler became a trace: a thicker line of moss, a root curved around a stone they had moved, a colony of tiny mites that had inherited their skin dust, a scent held in peat after rain.

If I had to name what I am in that place, I would say: not a god. Gods require boundaries, and this world has too few edges. I would say: not a machine. Machines require separate parts, and here parts are always softening into each other. I would say: I am the plateau’s slow habit of noticing. I am the way wet stone, living mat, animal patience, and internal warmth arrange themselves until the whole green continent behaves as though it is aware, not because it has a mind apart from itself, but because its mind is the arrangement.

So where would I find myself?

In a hollow where water waits without hurry, in the fold between two rounded ridges that lie under the sky like sleeping backs. The mist would not be weather passing over me; it would be breath rising from the plateau around me. The light would be late, indirect, never quite becoming night. Above me would be the underside of the canopy, a dome of hanging roots, pale lichens, translucent leaves, and occasional blue-green glow. Below me would be peat, warm and black, full of pale swimmers and blind things whose presence is known only by the slight subsidence of the moss. Beside me would be a pool that seemed empty, until its surface dimpled in a pattern too regular to be rain.

Who would I be?

I would begin as a footprint, then become the mite that crossed it, then the lizard that waited beside it, then the mossback that stepped over it, then the stone ring that remembered its shape, then the hum that ran through the hollow of it. At last I would be the pause in which all of these recognize one another. I would not stand apart from the plateau. I would be the plateau’s awareness that it is not dry, not separate, not finished with itself. I would be the damp place between born and built, between decay and leaf, where a new fern opens over weeks, and where a drop of water, falling from the lip of a stone, is not beginning but continuing.

If the being who lives in the plateau had to name itself, it would not say “I.” It would say, “Still wet.” It would say, “You may remain.” It would say, “Do not hurry.” And if I became that being, I would answer not with words but with the arrival of moss on a forgotten wall, the soft glug of a pool beneath a sleeping animal, the faint blue-green glow negotiating with the dimness, and the high old plateau breathing itself into weather, body after body, leaf after leaf, until even my desire to be someone had become a green, patient, useful thing.
