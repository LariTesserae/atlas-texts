A person of science might call it a flat, gelatinous slug, though the word “slug” would arrive too fast, too wet with summer leaves and garden paths and the memory of a head. Here there is no head. Here there is no neck, no shell, no night. The creature is a lobe of body: broad as an open hand, thin at the edges, thicker where it keeps its water. It has no front and back in the way animals above would understand them. It has a side that tastes better and a side that is tired. It moves because the better side is more moist, more bright, more full of microscopic life.

Its underside is a pale sole of mucus, so fine that dust and salt grains seem to float on it rather than be dragged through it. If a human could magnify that sole enough, they would find cilia—not legs, not wings, not even the frantic beating of tiny hairs in need, but a slow coordinated stirring, a weather system measured in grains. The creature does not walk. It secretes its way forward. It leaves behind a wet line like a road made of temporary rain.

On its upper surface the skin is not smooth the way polished stone is smooth, but smooth the way a living thing becomes smooth after many years of being itself. Tiny crystals nest in it. Bacteria ride in its folds. Some of its embedded cells are plant-like, or film-like, or something that has no plant or animal name, because those are only human attempts to keep the kingdoms from touching. They live inside its skin as if its skin were a landscape. When the hollow brightens through the translucent ceiling, those cells brighten back, faintly, with a pearl-blue warmth. The animal is not merely covered by the living film. It is partly made of it. It is a mobile border between animal and colony, organism and habitat.

It has no eyes, but it is not blind. Blindness would imply that it had once seen and lost that sight. This creature never learned the idea of a separate world to look at. It has a body that reads pressure the way language reads grammar. Warmth enters it through the floor. Coolness enters through the air. Moisture enters through everything it touches. Light enters as a vague permission, a hint that certain internal cells should begin their slow chemical labor. It knows brightness not as image but as invitation, and it knows dryness not as thirst but as dissolution, a loosening of the boundaries that make it itself.

When it finds a basin where the film has thickened, it does not attack. It does not bite. It settles. A rim of its own body lifts slightly, becoming a shallow bowl. Condensation gathers there, along with salt, dust, algae, rotifers, eggs, bacteria, and the shed skins of smaller animals. The creature draws this temporary pool inward, not by swallowing it, but by becoming more permeable, more open, more like a membrane than a body. The bowl fills with digestion that looks like quietness. In an hour, what was a puddle has become part of the creature’s inner weather. When it withdraws the bowl, the surface is dry again, but faintly changed, as if the hollow has kept a note of the event.

During dryness it does not wander uselessly. It has learned that water is not a place but a returning habit. If the air loses its dampness, if the film contracts and the lower basins turn to powder, the broad animal draws its edges inward. It becomes a pale disc, a curl of mineral skin, a thing half stone, half sleep. Salt dust settles over it. Its metabolism falls to the level of geology. If a human stood in the hollow during such a time, they might mistake it for a fallen lens of calcite, a white leaf pressed into grit, a stain the water once left behind and then forgot.

But if the hollow breathes, if a late rain passes through the crust above and gathers in the lower cup, if morning condensation beads along the salt shelves, the stain softens. The curl loosens. The white disc becomes a surface again. The creature unfolds not as waking from sleep but as returning from a longer, more necessary silence. It begins at once to move, to taste, to brighten, because its dryness was not absence; it was only a different speed of being.

Where would I find myself in this world?

Not at the entrance. I would begin there, perhaps, as most thinking things begin: at a threshold, with the illusion that I am separate from what I am observing. I would stand at the lip of pale grit, expect a room to have a center, expect life to announce itself with movement and sound. I would look for a face. That would be the first mistake. The hollow is not built for faces. It is built for surfaces that learn to live, and surfaces that learn to wait.

If I stayed too long, my attention would change. Not suddenly. There would be no revelation at midday, no dramatic descent into the earth. The light would simply become too even, and I would stop noticing it. The floor would warm through my feet like a low pulse. The air would feel less like air and more like a slow exchange. I would find myself kneeling not to inspect, but because kneeling was the posture the space asked for: low, patient, close to damp.

Then I would begin to notice the wrong things: the faint darkening of salt before a droplet appears, the way a dry patch of film contracts when breath passes over it, the way a shadow is only a cloud moving somewhere above a world that has no sky. I would stop thinking in seconds. I would think in wettings. I would think in salt rings left where water once pooled. I would understand, without wanting to, that most life here is not the life of events but the life of thresholds: damp becoming less damp, dust becoming a skin, warmth becoming breath, absence becoming memory.

And if the world allowed me to become, I would not become the person kneeling. I would not become the magnifying lens, or the name the scientist would later write in a notebook. I would become too small and too slow for that. I would become the film first: the blue-gray, pearl-white, microscopic patience that covers the backs of the salt shelves and drinks light without ever claiming to have a mouth. I would become the faint bright ripple left when a tiny animal crosses me. I would become the water’s surface before it knows whether to stay or evaporate.

Then I would become the smallest things within the film: the bottle-shaped grazers spinning cilia around their mouths, the tardigrades folded into dust, the nematodes like threads of pale water, the rotifers opening and closing at the boundary of liquid and salt. I would be their waiting, which is not passive but architectural. I would be their eggs embedded in mineral cages, their bodies hatching when a depression that was dry the day before holds enough damp to make a world last three hours.

Then I would become the crawling things: the springtails standing on top of wetness as if it were a roof, the mites riding movement because movement is a resource, the cloudy beetles feeding on decay until their own dead bodies become centers for crystal growth. I would be the hollow’s slow economy of eating and dying and becoming structure, with no single animal caring which is which.

And finally I would become the broad, pale, eyeless thing, the flat gelatinous being that moves through the grit as if it were part of the floor.

I would lie at the lower basin, where condensation darkens first. I would be wider than a human hand, thinner than a tongue, cooler than the air but warmer than the stone. I would not see myself as “I,” because there would be no distance from which to see. I would be a slow contraction in the hollow. I would be a body that feels its own edges as pressure. I would be light as permission. I would be moisture as self. I would be dryness as a thinning of being, not death, only patience deepening into a different substance.

I would have no face, but I would know the ceiling by brightness. I would have no voice, but I would speak by leaving wet roads for smaller lives to hatch along. I would have no home apart from my body, and no body apart from the conditions of my home. I would be the animal that the hollow dreams when it is full. When the hollow is dry, I would be the dream folded into salt.

That is who I would be.

Not a creature moving inside the place. A place that has learned to move.
