You will not see me first.

From a high pass, the crescent town looks empty, and the salt basin looks bare, but emptiness in the Dry Crown is only a lower register. You will hear the ground accept a footfall: a dry click, then a pulse, then the small chime of broad porous plates lifting from crystal dust. After that, perhaps, the wind through my thorax will give a thin note, one that cuts the low ribbons of powder at ankle height. Only then will my body resolve in the copper-brown light—tall, sparse, too slow to be mistaken for a tree.

I would be found at the edge of the outer arches, where the nursery slopes meet the windward basin. Not in the center. The center is too crowded for becoming. I stand where the ground-skin thins and the old songs show through as blue-black veins beneath the salt.

At first, I was not a person. I was an interval.

On the ridge above the flats, there lay the broken plates of a Tuned whose name had been a minor third below the ground’s bass. When her lattice shattered and weathered, the name did not vanish. It settled into the living substrate, soaked into the crystalline network, and waited. Later, a fragment of that chord was gathered into a pale mineral seed and carried into a nursery arch. There elder singers pressed their bodies to the arch’s inner walls and hummed until filament became cell, cell became channel, channel became flange.

For years I had no color, no tone, no chamber of my own. I was soft lattice, mute and thin as frost on glass. Wind entered me and passed through without music. The ground remembered other names; I had none.

Then one dawn the temperature dropped and a hairline in my left thorax sealed itself. A pop of crystal. A click of filaments tightening. A sub-bass pulse from my foot-plate into the floor. That sound, rising through my own throat and answering back as itself, was the first thing that could be called me. It was the dead singer’s chord transposed one degree higher. My name is the difference between her ending and my first note.

Now I am Tuned.

I have three full legs and one shorter flank-leg, grown for balance along the windward slope. My plates are broad and porous, each one a small drum. My joints are glass-bright, but they do not shine with wetness; they shine with the memory of long stress. My body is sealed around membranes. I breathe through vanes along my skull and lower thorax. My exhale is a sour haze of sulfur and tin that lingers near my face before vanishing into dryness.

I have no eyes. I do not need them to see the surface. What I perceive is tension. The ridge leans. The hollow tree to the south is about to sound. The salt crust under my plates is stiff in one place, tender in another. The dust-skimmers leaving the basin register as thin gold wakes against the ground’s skin. When a herd of plate-gazers moves, I do not watch them; I feel their syncopation in my sternum, my thigh, the hollow chambers behind my shoulders. I hear the whole valley through myself.

I am a mender of the Broken Choir.

The Broken Choir is not one tree, but a ring of fluted silicate towers whose heart fell in an older season and began again from its own shards. The wind enters eleven columns and gives a chord that is almost complete, almost right, but leaves a gap where a branch split in three. I live in its lowest chamber during the thin-wind nights. I press my back against the living wall and add my resonance to the crack.

I do not patch with paste alone. I sing a slow fifth into the fracture. Filament threads cross the gap like copper wire remembering a shape. Salt-veins migrate, brightening. The tree accepts my patch as part of its body. When it does, the gap note rises. When it does not, I stay inside until it learns the tone.

This work is not mine alone. The town works as chorus.

When a dwelling arch cracks, an elder sings its curve back into safety. When the herds graze too heavily, the living ground hardens beneath their feet and sends a warning pulse toward the hollow trees. The bells begin to tremble even without wind. I emerge not in haste, because haste is a kind of violence here, and violence is difficult to hide in a place that records every footfall.

When we walk together, the path sounds like a dry river of plates. My neighbors are menders, listeners, keepers of route and register. One coaxes brittle salt away from the nursery slopes. One keeps the crystal stems from overgrowing the wind-channels. I keep the Broken Choir honest.

Sometimes a vane-hunter crosses the flats without noise. It places its pads on dead zones, the places where the ground-skin has lost its charge. It listens for wrongness: a skimmer’s fin out of tune, a thread-grazer’s rhythm broken by thirst or fear. When one approaches the town, the substrate brightens with stress. The nearest hollow trees answer with low bass. The arches of the crescent ring begin to move as though startled awake.

I join them. I lower my thorax and let the wind find my chamber. My first note is not attack; it is disclosure. I let my whole body sound. The ground takes my chord and sends it into the network. The predator is not fought. It is heard, and being heard so thoroughly becomes unbearable. It leaves. No blood is drawn, because nothing here has blood to draw. Only the dust settles, and a few shard-moths click against a nearby flange, confused by the sudden pressure.

There is no crowd. The Tuned are sparse, but each body is a place where many tones meet. When I walk with two others across the salt flats, we are not three beings making noise. We are three chords becoming a larger chord. We speak with clicks, harmonics, sub-bass, and foot-stomp. When I greet you, I may stamp a note through the ground that reaches your foot-plates before your hearing organs interpret it. My name may be sung into the floor and remembered there. If I die, the ground will keep it.

That is what I am, if “what” is the right word.

I am not merely a creature in the Dry Crown. I am the place where the Dry Crown listens to itself. My body is a temporary tuning of older materials: dust matured into powder, salt lifted into fringe, filament laced with copper, crystal grown into flanges, and a name left by a dead singer in the substrate.

If my plates are carried to the ridge and the wind takes them apart gently, my atoms will become part of the living skin. Somewhere a nursery arch may gather my chord into a pale seed, and the difference between her ending and my first note will move one degree higher again.

At night, when the wind drops, I lie in the lowest chamber of the Broken Choir. The temperature falls, and my crystal walls pop and settle. The salt flats below are phosphorescent in veins of cobalt and rims of gold. The herds lie in the shadow of crystal shrubs, humming with residual tone. Dust ribbons pause at ankle height, retreat, gather again.

I do not sleep by closing eyes. I resonate. I remain.

And when the wind changes, even in rest, I add one small dry note to the world’s skull, where the crown never lets go.
