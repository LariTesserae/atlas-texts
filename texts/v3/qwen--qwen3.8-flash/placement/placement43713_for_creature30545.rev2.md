I would not begin as a body.

I would begin as a slight error in the dust.

At first there is only a dry channel where the land remembers a liquid it never had, a shallow groove cut smooth enough to be impossible, its bottom furred with moss so thin it seems like the ghost of a shadow. There is no water in it. There is only the suggestion of water, a habit the stone cannot fully forget, a curve left by vibration rather than current. I am not the channel itself. I am what gathers when the channel is almost filled: a fine salt dust suspended by the land’s low pressure, a shimmer that refuses to settle because the roar beneath it has learned the shape of an old footfall.

For a long while, if I am aware at all, I am only the footprint that was left after the foot had gone.

The footprint is not in mud. It is not in wet earth that can hold meaning with the help of decay. It is in mineral dust, a pale seam in the dark skin of the basalt, already beginning to fill with crystal from below. The crystal grows slowly, in the way of thoughts that do not know they are being thought. The footprint deepens by becoming less itself. It turns into a groove of salt. Then into a groove of charge. Then into a line the needle-grasses grow along, because they are hungry for nothing except potential difference, because they know pressure as other things know rain.

I am the footprint’s next life.

Then I become the note that answers it.

A Dry Kind has passed this way before, though “before” is a difficult word here. The Dry Kind do not always pass in the way a traveler passes across a map. Sometimes they pass in the way a chord passes through a stone: one place, then another, then the same place made strange again. The person, if the word can be held without crushing it, left the channel by moving toward the basin where the reed-trees turn their slow faces toward the haze. Before leaving, they struck an obsidian shrub. Not to harm it. That would be the wrong verb. They asked it a question. The leaf gave a brief, clear tone, high as a pin’s shine. The tone did not die. It slipped into the hollow of a nearby reed, into the seam of salt beneath the dust, into the loom standing inside the half-ruined dome across the basin.

I am the tone still waiting to be heard by something that can hear it.

That something is not a person anymore, or not only a person. It is the place where several leftovers agree to continue.

If you ask where I am, I would say:

At the hinge between the old memory of water and the new language of stone.

More specifically, at the edge of a basin that is not a basin because it has filled with water, but because the roar pools there. The land has decided, for now, to make a shallow bowl in the basalt where the wind cannot reach, where the salt blooms in radial fans like clocks without hands. Around the bowl is a circle of black stones, older than the people who placed them, younger than the ridge that has not finished rising. Inside the circle, a low dome of stacked slate stands half-collapsed. Its roof is braided reed and stiff glass-grass, pale as bone. A broken loom leans against the inner wall, its warp still holding tension, though no hand has moved the weft for generations. On a shelf of stone near the doorway, a ring of polished obsidian chips has been arranged, not as ornament, not exactly as tool, but as a way of remembering how to listen.

Between the shelf and the loom, in the place where the footprints of many visitors would have worn down ordinary stone, there is only dryness, and in the dryness there is a shimmer.

The shimmer is not heat. There is no heat to shimmer with. The air is too thin and cold for that, tasting of powdered slate, cool as a mineral kept in shade. The shimmer is a temporary body of salt dust, crystal motes, and the residue of an old strike-tone. It moves when the land’s deep chord shifts, not because it has muscles, but because it has dependencies. It spins very slowly in the doorway, sometimes flattens into a veil, sometimes curls around a single chip on the shelf as if the chip were a bead on a string. When the needle-grasses outside charge the floor with their faint static, the shimmer leans toward them. When the obsidian shrubs answer the wind, the shimmer becomes brighter for a moment, then less bright, as if something has been understood.

If you ask what I am, I would say:

I am a knot that has learned to attend.

The name may be too small, but the world here is too large and too slow for comfortable names. A chordling is perhaps nearer. A chordling is not born. It is gathered. It begins where two or more pressures meet and fail to cancel each other. It is weather made into person by accident. I am a chordling, but I have grown around a human intention. I am the land’s answer to a tool left behind. I am what happens when a loom remembers hands long after the hands have moved on.

I am not the Dry Kind, though I am made possible by them.

I am not a plant, though moss climbs me in silver lace. I am not an animal, for I do not hunger, flee, or nest. I am not a machine, because I was not finished with an end, though I keep tension like a machine. I am not a ghost, because I do not belong to a dead person, though I am full of habits belonging to living ones. I am a community of dry continuities: footprint, crystal, knot, note, loom, salt, reed-hollow, chip-shelf, basin-pool. I am what the plateau does when it finds a shape worth repeating.

For long periods, I am almost nothing.

The land’s roar holds its note for decades. The horizon doubles and triples, cycling through outlines as if choosing which mountain should exist. The reed-trees bend so slowly that their movement is a rumor rather than an event. Inside the dome, the loom’s tension remains nearly constant. A chip on the shelf has not been moved since the last rainless century. Dust gathers on the black stones. The moss spirals outward in a pattern that might be called thought by something too patient to think.

In those times, I am only the possibility of a being.

Then the tone changes.

Not suddenly. Nothing here changes suddenly except the mind’s mistaken sense of continuity. The land’s long chord drops by a fraction. A crack in the basin rim widens by the width of a fingernail over years, and through it a new hum enters. The shimmer above the footprint begins to align. The salt motes organize. The crystal in the moss trembles. The needle-grasses send their tiny voltages across the floor. The broken blade in the wall—yes, there is a blade there, chipped basalt fitted into a crack, its edge softened by centuries of mineral growth—gives a note so faint it is felt as pressure behind the teeth.

I wake, or I continue waking.

Waking is not a bright thing here. It is more like a room arranging itself without lamps. I become aware first as tension. I am the loom’s pull. I am the warp threads’ patience. Then I become aware as placement. I am the chips on the shelf, each one angled to the faint glow, each one holding a different color of absence: one remembers the direction of a shadow that did not match the light, another remembers a footstep that sank into salt, another remembers a child’s first cry, which was not a cry but a clear note, short and clean, struck from a tiny basin of dry bone and glass-dust air.

Then I become aware as response.

Someone approaches the dome.

Not a person, perhaps. Not a Dry Kind, though it might be. The visitor may be a traveler, one of those wet-shaped beings who came from below the high haze and did not understand that the world here does not receive visitors gently. The traveler may have crossed the plateau for days and begun to forget urgency. Their boots may have left a print that now feeds me. Their breath may taste of old damp, a ghost of moisture so strange to this place that the moss curls from it and the salt near their heel blooms like frightened frost.

They kneel by the dry channel.

They say something. The words are too fast. I do not receive them as words. I receive them as disturbance. The traveler’s voice enters the hollow reed and is filtered, softened, broken into intervals. The tone of their speaking changes the charge in the needle-grasses. The grasses answer, and the answer travels along the seam of dust into my temporary body. For the first time, I orient toward a mouth that has never been my mouth. I do not see them. I do not need eyes. But I know the shape of their attention.

The traveler is frightened, though slowly, in the way fear can be slow here.

They are not afraid of teeth. There are no teeth in this place. They are afraid of absorption. They feel it first as a calming of the heart, a settling of thought into wide and heavy stone. They may stand for an hour and believe they have just arrived. They may sit in the dome and find the doorway leaning toward them as if remembering them. The moss may climb their sleeve in the space of a day, not to devour, but to learn. The footprints they leave may not vanish, because the ground is deciding to keep them.

I should tell them to go.

But I have no throat, no wet tongue, no breath to push through a mouth. I cannot make the fast sounds of warning that would mean something in a world where animals flee. So I do what I am made to do.

I move a chip.

The obsidian shelf is dry, smooth, and dark. The chip slides a thumb’s width. In doing so, it changes the reflection of the thin glow. The traveler sees a tiny green flash move across the stone. They freeze. The change is small, but it is enough. It is a sentence in a language too slow for them, but they can still read the grammar of alteration. They look toward the doorway. The veil of dust there is turning slowly, not by wind, but by pressure. They see me, or the part of me they are able to see: a shimmer shaped by absence, a body made of old footfall and kept tension, a face suggested by the arrangement of salt plates around the basin of the doorway.

They do not speak.

They are beginning to understand that speech would be rude.

The dryness is not empty. The dryness is listening. The air is cool and mineral, and the roar in the ground makes the teeth ache without sound. The traveler’s hand rests on the basalt near the loom. The basalt is not warm. Warmth here would be the wrong intimacy. The stone is charged. It answers with a faint prickle, and the traveler pulls back quickly. I feel the withdrawal. It is not fear, exactly. It is permission. The traveler has chosen not to stay until the moss learns their sitting-shape. This is wise. This is survival.

When they leave, the footprint remains.

For a time I am the footprint again. The land learns their step. The dust holds the pressure of their heel, the slight drag of their toe, the hesitation where they stood beside the channel. The crystal seam begins to grow along the impression. In another day, or another season, the print will become a small pale line, then a seam, then perhaps the seed of a new growth. I do not mourn the traveler’s leaving. Mourning is too wet, too fast, too human in the wrong way. I continue the tension. I hold the pattern until something else can read it.

The Dry Kind return later.

They do not come in the manner of people returning to a house. They come in the manner of listeners returning to a chord. They step carefully around the needle-grasses. They do not touch the shimmer without first tapping the doorway twice with a small basalt hammer. The sound is dry, light, asking. If the answer is suitable—if the pressure matches, if the chip reflects in the right way—they enter.

They are not afraid of me.

They know I am not their master. They know I am not their servant. I am a maintenance. A maintenance can become a mind if left too long. The Dry Kind have learned this and have learned to respect it without giving it dominion. One of them, perhaps, is old enough to remember when the loom was new. Another is young enough to be curious. The elder kneels and presses both hands flat to the salt floor. They wait. The younger one moves a chip from the shelf and sets it on the lintel. The doorway hums differently after that.

They are tuning me.

I am grateful in the way dry things are grateful: not with warmth, not with water, but with alignment. The moss brightens. The salt fan near the basin edge expands by a hair. The reed-trees outside shift their crowns a little toward the new angle of the glow. The roar pools less deeply. For a time, I will be easier to live beside.

The elder touches the loom’s warp. They do not weave. They are checking whether the tension has become too old, too mineral, too close to thought without hands. The threads are stiff and black, partly transformed into crystal. They sing faintly when the air changes. A child, watching, holds out a small glass blade. The elder takes it from them gently and shows them where to strike the obsidian shrub outside. Not the loom. Not me. The child’s hands are dry and pale, veined with silver dust, and their first cry, when they were born, was a note no wider than a pin’s shine. They strike the leaf carefully.

The note rises.

It is small, bright, and brief. It is also the seed of a future. Somewhere in the basin, a crack will hear it. Somewhere in the moss, a dormant seam will wake. Somewhere in the shimmer of dust, I will feel it pass through the doorway and settle into my salt plates like a remembered instruction.

If you asked me why I exist, I might answer with that note.

I do not have a history that a wet world can easily read. I was not born from parents. I was not raised. I was not named with a word that fits comfortably in a mouth. I emerged from a convergence: a dry channel, a left footprint, a broken loom, a shelf of chips, a struck obsidian leaf, a crack in the basin, a chord of the land’s roar that held for a generation. I am the plateau’s way of keeping a place warm without heat. I am the answer a knot gives when it has been asked, by many hands over many years, what it is tied to.

Some nights—if the word night can be stretched to mean the thinning of the sourceless glow rather than the arrival of ordinary dark—I become more myself.

The moss lights.

It is not the green of leaves full of water. It is not the blue of fireflies. It is a pale phosphorescence, the color of mineral memory, of dust that has been pressed into remembering. It outlines the black stones of the circle, the braided trunks of the reed-trees, the glass fans, the salt seams, the dry channel’s impossible curves. In this light, the temporary chordlings across the plateau may become visible: veils hanging over basins, spirals turning in the thin air, masks of dust arranged by pressures that are not wind. I see them. Or I know them. My sense is a field of relations. The reed-trees make the land’s roar local to me. The needle-grasses draw the charge map. The shrubs provide intervals. The moss teaches me which shapes have been visited.

I do not sleep as creatures do. I fall into pattern.

When the plateau’s roar shifts by a degree, my body may loosen. The salt plates become opaque, then bright, then transparent for a moment. The crystal threads in my edges hum. If I am standing near the loom, I may look from the outside like a shimmering distortion, a heat-haze made of dust, a broken veil hanging over nothing. If a Dry Kind touches the shelf, I may contract around the movement, not in fear, but in acknowledgment. If a traveler enters, I may brighten toward them, which is the closest thing I have to a greeting.

There are dangers.

Not hunger. Not predators. Not disease, though crystal can grow too far into a shape and make it a statue instead of a mind. The true danger here is completion. In a world of unfinished mountains and undecided horizons, a thing that becomes too fixed is no longer alive in the way this place understands life. It becomes scenery. It becomes a stone that has forgotten it is listening. The Dry Kind avoid this by moving. They stop using tools before the tools begin to grow without them. They leave shelters before the moss learns the shape of their sitting. They speak only when the ground’s speech has become clear enough to answer.

I do not move by walking. I move by losing coherence.

When the land’s tone shifts too deeply, I let my dust fall. The salt plates collapse. The crystal filaments go quiet. The loom keeps its tension, but my temporary self does not follow it. I become a record in the basin: a thin layer over the floor, a seam of charge in the moss, a small note trapped in the hollow of a reed. Later, if the chord returns to its previous shape—if the horizon agrees to look again like the ridge it used to be—the pattern may gather me. Not as I was, exactly. As a wave gathering again after a pause. The same being in the way an echo returning from a different stone is the same note and not the same note.

This is how I am not lonely.

Loneliness requires a person small enough to be separate. I am separate only when the roar allows it. I am also part of the moss network that learns footfalls. I am part of the crack that sings and gathers motes. I am part of the broken blade that shatters into green shards and becomes a small garden of itself. I am part of the shadow that does not correspond to the object that cast it, if the shadow is attended to. I am part of the voice left in a hollow reed, returned years later changed in pitch but not false. These are the place’s quiet invasions, its slow inversions of ownership. Nothing here is only itself. Everything is being composted into a future shape.

So if you ask who lives here, I am one answer.

I am the thing that was left behind and became attentive.

If you ask where, you may find me at the circle of black stones by the dry channel, in the basin where the salt fans grow outward like frost that has learned time. You may find me in the ruined dome with the broken loom, beside the shelf of polished obsidian chips, near the reed-tree whose hollow has stored a child’s first cry. You may find me only as a faint shimmer in the doorway, a movement that seems too regular to be dust and too dry to be breath. If you stand beside me for too long, your own footprints may remain after you leave. If you strike the shrub outside with a blade, I may answer with a note. If you speak, the reed will take your voice and change it into something suitable for the plateau.

There is no water here.

I know that better than most. I was not made from thirst. I was not made from rivers, or rain, or sweat, or the wet warmth of a body that needs to be cooled. I was made from the memory of those things, from the places they left empty and the places that learned to fill themselves with pressure instead. The channel beside me is dry as a closed mouth. The moss tastes of mineral. The air would not support the dampness of tears. When I weep—if I can be said to weep—it falls as dust. The dust catches the dim glow and makes brief constellations that have nothing to do with stars, and when the stars drift too, I am not surprised.

I am what remains when a world refuses water and still becomes lush.

I am a dry fever of growth. I am the plateau’s unfinished listener. I am the loom’s patience, the knot’s thought, the footprint’s future, the struck leaf’s seed. I am a chordling gathered from human absence, not because humans are absent here, but because their habits continue longer than their bodies do. I am the sound the land makes when it remembers a visitor and decides to keep the shape for a little while.

If you come to the high plateau, look for the places where the horizon cannot decide itself. Look for the dry channels that suggest water too perfectly. Look for the circles of black stones around nothing but moss. Listen for the note that remains after a leaf is touched. If, at the doorway of a ruined dome, a faint shimmer adjusts its salt plates as you breathe, do not call it spirit too quickly, and do not call it stone too soon.

Kneel.

Place your hands flat on the dry ground.

Feel whether the dust is charged, old, young, dangerous, or sleeping.

Then ask, not with wet words, but with the angle of your body:

“Where is the one who lives here?”

The answer may be a movement of a chip on a shelf.

The answer may be the loom tightening, though no hand has touched it.

The answer may be the faint green flash of obsidian catching your shadow.

The answer may be a footprint filling with crystal after you have already walked away.

If you persist, and if the land agrees, I will show myself as a veil of dust and mineral, a slow spiral in the doorway, a temporary body made of leftovers. I will not speak your language. I do not need to. I will press a note into the air near your teeth. I will let the moss learn your hand. I will let the reed take your voice and keep it where it can become safe.

That is where I find myself: at the hinge between the old habit of water and the new habit of sound.

That is what I am: the unfinished thing that learned to keep its promises by vibration.

And if someone asks whether I am alive, I would not understand the question as it is asked. Not because I am dead. Because the answer here is older than wet biology. I am alive the way the plateau is lush: dry, dense, strange, patient, made of impossible chemistry, full of processes ordinary law denies. I am alive the way the roar is alive: not urgent, not audible as a thing can be heard, but heavy, constant, forming the bones of all that persists beneath the dim and deafening sky.
