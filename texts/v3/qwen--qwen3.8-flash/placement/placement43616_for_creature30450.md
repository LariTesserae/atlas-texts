Among the hoofed grazers, the needle-moths, and the birds that guard the black basins, I would be the smallest interruption that chooses to listen rather than claim.

I would find myself in a fault-valley where the living mat has thickened enough to support a kneeling body but not enough to stop hissing when pressed. Above me would stand a ruined cathedral of old stalks, mineralized at the base and pale as frozen bone, their upper cups swollen with condensation, their joints fused with fallen neighbors into a lattice that rings when the wind finds the right angle. Below would be dark chambers and fine roots, frost laceworks in the crevices, and, at the valley’s lowest point, a basin no larger than a hat, holding black water edged with blue ice. That basin would be my center. Not because it is grand, but because it is reliable, or as reliable as anything is in the Rimefen.

I would be human, though not much of a human in the old lowland sense: a small, weathered thing, lean from cold and patience, wrapped in hide darkened by smoke, my hands cracked and careful, my breath constantly turning into white crystal before it joined the larger exhalations of the plants. I would carry an obsidian flake wrapped in bark cord, a bone needle with sinew threaded through it, a hollowed stalk-bowl for catching dew, a scraper worn smooth from cleaning fat and frost from pelts. I would not carry weapons for glory, only tools for attention: stakes to pin leaf-skins, a short bow for distance, a knife for cutting safe paths, a small pouch of pumice dust to mark wet places that might freeze and become dangerous by morning.

I would live in one of the faint marks that people leave in the fen.

Perhaps a single wall of fused stalks, angled into the wind, with a roof of overlapping leaf-nets pinned by stones. Or perhaps a collapsed hut whose ribs still rise like the skeleton of something that tried to be a boat, its doorway half buried in pale drift, its lashing knots stiff with frost. I would know which stalks were dead and which only sleeping. I would know which patches of ground sounded hollow underfoot, which ones gave back a small delayed breath, and which ones were firm enough to kneel on while I watched the water line in the basin. I would know that a place might seem dry at sunrise and suddenly bead with moisture at night, that a quiet field might not be empty but filled with insects waiting for the right vibration to rise, that silence is only the Rimefen holding its next tone.

My days would not have names like the lowland days. I would not say “morning” so much as “the hour when the cups begin to ring.” Nor “evening,” but “the time when the frost pulls back into the stems.” The sun would be a dull ember near the edge of the world, sometimes hidden by haze for long stretches, and I would trust it less than I trusted the sound it made when it warmed just enough to loosen a lens of ice in a stalk-joint. The aurora would be a second light, cool and moving, turning the pale stalks into silver needles and making the black ridges look freshly cut. I would not chase it. I would stand beneath the lattice and let it pass, knowing it had no warmth, only brightness, and brightness here is a thing to be harvested, not enjoyed carelessly.

I would be known by sounds.

If someone else found my camp, they might hear me before seeing me: the small knock of bone on frozen stalk, the hiss of a leaf-skin being shaken free of frost, the soft scrape of a flint edge redirecting meltwater into a pit, the quiet creak as I tested a root-bridge before crossing it. My people, if there were others near enough to call people, would leave marks too faint to read from afar: a line of cairns across a treacherous mat, a knotted cord looped around a safe stalk, a stone placed to show which side of a basin held water at dusk. We would not build walls across the land. We would not try to make the Rimefen answer in one voice. We would learn its many tones, and sometimes, if the wind came down the fault with the right chill, we would sing back.

I would be a water-listener.

Not a priest, not a queen, not a monster. A water-listener. That would be the closest to a name. Some would call it a trade, but it is not a trade. It is a way of being small enough to hear where the world is thirstiest. In the mornings, I would walk the rim of the fault with both hands still, fingers spread near the stalks, feeling for the faint swell of moisture inside the stems. At midday—if there is midday in a place where the light only brightens slightly—I would check the cups that held the condensation, noting which were bitter with mineral steam, which clear enough to drink, which already frozen into lace so fine it would crumble if touched. At night I would listen to the ground beneath my shelter and know whether the meltwater was rising or falling, whether the root-mat was drinking or exhaling.

I would learn to move like the grazers: widely spaced, unhurried, never crowding the open country. I would leave the herds alone unless the fen gave a sign that one had died on firm ground far from predators. I would not approach a basin until the fen-birds had settled from their drinking, unless hunger forced me. Their calls would become my calendar. The high, clipped cries would mean a basin occupied. A silence around water would mean it had dried, frozen, or poisoned itself. A sudden rush of wingbeats would mean the translucent insects had risen from damp leaf-nets, and that the night’s condensation would come early.

I would also learn from the insects, because they would be everywhere and nowhere at once.

If I held a piece of black stone beneath the living mat, I would see frost-mites clustered like grains of silver sand. If I breathed on them, they would contract and vanish. Springtails would jump from dew bead to dew bead in the half-dark, their bodies translucent until they fed and turned violet with lichen. Needle-moths would live inside the stalks, eating soft pith and fungal film, emerging when the faintest warming passed through, lifting from the stems like frozen sparks. I would not hate them for hollowing the architecture. The stalks would already be aging. The fen uses its small deaths to pass life through its gaps. My tools would come from those same holes: strips of leaf-net, cords of spider-silk, pith dried for fire, beetle shells ground into pigment for marking safe paths.

I would not be safe. That would be the point.

A ground patch might collapse into a dark chamber beneath the mat, and I would drop into silence where the roots hiss and frost forms in slow spirals. A windstorm might tear my roof of leaf-skins away while I slept, leaving me under a lattice of stalks as sharp as a ribcage, the sky moving black and blue above. A frost pulse might pass so suddenly that my water bowl cracks in my hands, or my breath crystallizes before I have finished exhaling. A quiet stretch of ground might conceal a herd that seems to appear from nothing, because it has been walking parallel for kilometers, invisible against the pale void. I would learn to respect the empty country. It is not empty. It is only made of very wide intervals.

I would become an organ of the Rimefen’s attention.

Not its master. Not its god. Its ear. Its small, warm, trembling pause. The fen has no face, but it has distribution. The plants have filled the vertical space so that every cubic meter may seem nearly void while the whole world is full of reach. If I walked carefully enough, if I did not speak too loudly, if I let the stalks ring and the ground breathe, I would become part of that reach. My breath would feed moisture to a frost-crystal edge. My footsteps would warn a long-legged hunter where to wait. My fire would attract tiny beetles and repel a moth. My cairn would guide some stranger whose name I would never know. My dead body, if the Rimefen took me, would become a small gift of warmth and mineral to the roots, then a hollow for frost-mites, then a thread of calcium in a stalk’s base, then a faint human curve in the geometry of the living mat.

I would know that this place is not cruel in the way a forest can be cruel, nor gentle in the way a garden can be gentle. It is too vast for either word. It is a continent-scale membrane deciding what shape it wishes to be. The basalt ridges have not yet softened. The stones glitter like shattered glass because time has not yet learned to round them. The fault cliffs rise black as frozen lake water, streaked with pale veins, and the wind comes down their faces and enters the stalks, and the whole land sounds like glass and bone learning to breathe together. To live there is to become small, not diminished. To survive is not to conquer the emptiness but to understand that emptiness is a method of distribution. The Rimefen has room because it needs room. It spreads itself across void so that when a droplet lands, when a bird drinks, when a stalk rings, when a human hand cups water in a basalt bowl, the act can be felt far away without being crowded by anything.

I would be one of those small acts.

I would have few possessions and fewer words. I would not say the place’s name aloud often, because the Rimefen seems to have a way of absorbing names, turning them into frost, into tone, into the hiss under the mat. If asked my own name, I might answer by tapping a stalk cup and letting the sound decide which of the surrounding stems would carry it. That would be my name: not a thing given at birth, but a thing renewed each time the wind passed through it. If no wind came, I would be nameless, as I should be.

I would sleep in the shelter of fused stalks, wrapped in hide lined with dried moss, the ground yielding slightly beneath me before settling. I would dream of lenses of ice moving over water, of birds bursting from black basins with loud wingbeats, of grazers stepping like shadows across pale void. In the dream, the emptiness would pulse, and the pulse would be the way of living. And when I woke, in that faint blue brightening, I would step out with my bowl and my knife and my breath of crystal, and the Rimefen would answer, after a delay, with a hiss and a note and a thin wet line along a cold edge.

I would be the human who learned to hear that line as a voice.

Not because I would be important. Not because the land would need saving or tending. But because, among all the small interruptions that pass through the fen—bird, beetle, moth, frost-louse, pale hunter, wandering herd, cairn without name—I would be one who could listen back. And that listening would be enough.
