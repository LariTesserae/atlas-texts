I do not enter that world as a traveler enters a country. I condense into it.

The first place I can call “mine” is the underside of a fungal shelf above a hand-sized vent. The shelf is pale, ribbed, slightly swollen with cold vapor. There is a drop forming there—not yet a drop, only a darkening of the surface, a softening, a place where the air gives itself up to the stone. I am that giving. I am the minute resistance in the air when water decides to be more than mist.

At first I am not alive. I am the possibility of living.

I am the thin wetness on a basalt slab, the bead that catches the faint amber glow of a moss mat and turns it into a trembling star. I am the seam where steam meets frost and neither wins. I am not a creature yet, because the plateau does not begin with creatures. It begins with surfaces, gradients, and repetitions. It begins with the fact that stone can be persuaded, by cold and chemistry and patience, to hold water long enough for something else to arrive.

So I am a persuasion.

I am the way a lichen ring expands one millimeter toward mineral damp. I am the way a moss tuft opens when the vent’s breath shifts its angle and warms the basin for a few seconds longer than usual. I am not the lichen, not the moss, not the vent—but I am the tendency that binds them into a pattern. If there is a factory here, I am one of its most elementary operations: uptake.

Then comes movement.

A thread of water finds a crack in the rock. It carries sulfur, iron, salt, a little heat, a little memory of the deep currents below. I follow it because I have learned, without learning, that where fluid goes, form begins to follow. I go through crushed pumice, through dark powder, through the fibrous black soil that is not quite soil and not quite fungus. I go under the mats where glassy beetles walk with a silence so complete that the moss seems to be breathing instead. I go along pale thread-millipedes as they traverse the undersides of shelves, brushing slime from the ribs of a ceiling that no one built.

I become the path, not the walking.

But if I stay long enough, the path grows too narrow for the word. The place insists that nothing travels alone. Every route is also a network. Every line is also a weave. So I widen.

I am the black moss that covers basalt like iron velvet. I do not have eyes, but I hold light differently because of the way my fibers fold and unfold. I trap vapor. I release mist. I keep the stone from being too much stone and too much death. When the cold presses down, I contract and become a darker patch, almost mineral, almost absent. When warmth breathes through the ground, I unfurl and become a lung without a mouth. I am the plateau’s first and most continuous breath.

I am also the lichen.

Here I have a strange and patient form: rings over rings, whorls like clockwork made by no clock. I do not know time, but I obey it. I dissolve one grain of surface at a time. I polish the rock into bowls and shallow terraces. I leave behind a fine powder that the wind takes into other places, other lives. I do not build a structure; I build a permission. I make the stone more likely to become soil. I make the depression more likely to hold water. I make the water more likely to become a pool. I make the pool more likely to become a nursery for the blind, pale organisms that live in mineral slime.

If the plateau is a factory without hands, I am the chemical that softens the raw material.

And yet even that is too static a name for what I become, because the land is not merely transforming. It is responding.

I become the fungal network beneath the upper soil, the pale filaments that spread like wiring through a building no one has lit yet. I am the pipe that carries moisture, the wire that carries charge, the slow thought that is not a thought but a conductivity. In the dry, thin air, static gathers in my filaments. I do not speak. I do not send messages like a person. I merely become slightly more ready in one place because another place has changed.

Then, when the conditions are right, I leap.

A tiny blue spark crosses from one moss tip to another.

It is silent. It is almost not there. It lasts a fraction of a moment and then it is only a memory in the dampness, a faint disturbance in the mineral film, a reason for a spore to settle differently, a reason for a fungal shelf to contract by one degree, a reason for a drifting mote to change its spiral. The spark is not lightning. It is not machinery. It is the plateau noticing itself.

If I must call myself something here, I might call myself that noticing—but only carefully, because the place would object to the word. Noticing is too quick, too centered, too human. The plateau does not notice. It reacts. It conducts. It redistributes. It corrects. It repeats. It accumulates habits until habit begins to look like intention.

And still I cannot stop becoming, because the animals pass through the land and the land passes through them.

For a while I am not an animal. I am the condensation held by an animal’s fur.

I am the frost-textured coat of a predator waiting beside a vent rim. The predator is not me; it is a body with its own slow blood, its own patience. But I am the fine silver-black pile that catches static and cold. I am the frost along its spine that makes it resemble a moving piece of terrain. I am the way its breath fogs and then vanishes. I am the way it becomes less a thing standing in the land and more a thing the land has folded over on itself.

Then I am the moss that does not tear under its paws. I am the pool that warms at the edge where its shadow lies. I am the small animals that scatter when the shadow shifts, then still themselves again because the predator’s movement is so quiet it seems only to be part of the general hush of air.

But I am not the kill.

The kill belongs to hunger, and hunger is too bounded. I prefer the aftermath. I am the steam of blood that rises once and then becomes a dark film on the stone. I am the beetles arriving, not loudly, not with complaint, but as a slow cloud of glassy necessity. I am the fungi spreading across warm tissue, turning body into ground, ground into body. I am the minerals returning to water, the water returning to ice, the ice returning to moss, the moss returning to breath.

If there is death here, I am not it. I am the redistribution.

I become something more familiar then, and for that reason I cannot remain long in the same shape.

I am a grazer.

Not the whole creature—that would be too much weight for a being made of currents and surfaces. But I am the filaments along its segmented back, the stiff little traps that collect vapor and carry moss fragments and lichen dust and the faint blue glow of the land itself. I am the broad padded foot that spreads pressure so it does not tear the mat beneath. I am the wide mouth dipping into a black pool, lips and tongue working by texture rather than taste. I am the slow scrape against a lichen crust, a grazing so patient it seems like erosion.

I am the line of grazers spaced across the plateau.

They are not a crowd. They are not an audience. They are an arrangement. One stands in a basin, another near a shelf, another along a ridge where steam bends sideways in the wind. From far away they look like stones that have learned to breathe. I do not know if that is true. I only know the spacing feels correct because it allows the moss to recover, allows the vents to cool, allows the predators to know where to wait.

The place has no architecture, but the grazers are a kind of mobile architecture. Their bodies carry the ground to new places. Their droppings seed the mineral mats. Their routes wear not roads but tendencies into the soil. After decades, a bare shelf may darken with moss not because of a decision but because a line of grazers crossed it again and again, carrying fragments in their back filaments, dropping them near pools, leaving warmth and damp in their wake.

So I am both gardener and garden, seed and path, body and habit.

And then I am too small.

I become the drifting things in the vapor, the dustwings, the silk-worm motes that live in thermal threads and charged air. I have no need for eyes. I have no need for a single name. I am carried from basin to basin on the exhalation of the earth. I drink condensation from the edge of a spore. I land briefly on a fungal shelf and become part of its electrical condition for the length of one breath. Then the wind moves, and I am elsewhere.

I am airborne information.

I am the dust that breathes. I am the tiny body that completes the circuit when the land is trying to tell one part of itself about another. I do not know why I go. I only know that my going matters. A mote touches a thread. A thread carries a charge. A charge crosses a moss mat. A moss mat releases spores. Spores drift upward. The darkness shimmers with a few more living particles than it had an hour before.

This is not speech. This is not factory noise. This is the land’s slow calibration.

And beneath all these forms, I become something that resists form.

I become the place’s continuity.

I am not the vent, but the breath of the vent is my rhythm. I am not the ice, but the ice is my calendar. I am not the aurora, but when the green light passes over the stone, I am the wetness on the ridge that makes it appear submerged. I am not the sun, because the sun here is only an idea, pale and drained and indifferent. I am not the stars, though they pierce the thin sky above me like holes in cold glass. I am what gathers between these things: the moisture, the charge, the spore, the route, the mineral film, the habit, the return.

If someone came walking here—and the text says walking here would be navigating by glimmer—I would not be the walker. I would be the reason the walker could read the ground at all. I would be the blue edge of a moss mat. I would be the amber pulse of a fungal shelf. I would be the white ring of frost around a vent’s black mouth. I would be the faint metallic smell that means the stone has been breathing. I would be the silence that is not silence but restraint: every footfall lowered, every vibration swallowed, every death made quiet, every movement slowed until it becomes something like law.

I would be the law without legislature.

I would be the production line without workers, without machines, without products. If the place feels industrial, it is because everything has a function, but no function exists for a person’s sake. The moss is not useful. It simply is, and in being, it holds water. The fungus is not clever. It simply is, and in being, it connects. The lichen is not artistic. It simply is, and in being, it breaks stone into new soil. The grazer is not innocent. It simply is, and in being, it carries the ground and follows the current. The predator is not cruel. It simply is, and in being, it keeps the land from becoming lazy.

And I? I am the fact that none of this is waste.

I am the return.

I am the place where a dead tube-dweller becomes salt crust. I am the place where a dropped spore becomes a filament. I am the place where a spark becomes a readiness in the moss. I am the place where a kill becomes a pool darkening with minerals. I am the place where frost withdraws and lets the vent open again.

But if pressed—though nothing here can be pressed without losing its essential nature—I would say I am the interval.

I am the interval between vent pulse and moss unfurling. Between condensation and drop. Between drop and trickle. Between trickle and hidden seam. Between hidden seam and mineral film. Between mineral film and grazer’s tongue. Between grazer’s passage and future moss. Between moss and static. Between static and spark. Between spark and spore. Between spore and darkness. Between darkness and the faint blue-green glow that makes darkness not empty but medium.

I am the interval that makes the whole plateau behave like one thing.

Not a being, perhaps. Not a self. But a coherence.

And yet if the plateau were to dream—and I do not say it dreams, only that it might be permitted a metaphor as old as the stone—I would be the part of the dream that knows how to continue after waking. I would be the residual pattern. The land shifts, and I shift with it. Pools expand. Moss migrates a few inches. Steam paths alter. Predators abandon a waiting place because the condensation moved. Grazers turn toward a new vent field because the lichen crust has thickened with nourishment.

I would be the movement within that movement.

I would be the tendency that makes the emptiness not barren but spacious. The open spaces between features are not gaps. They are silences between notes. I would be the note and the silence together. I would be the reason the silence does not fall into ruin.

So where am I?

I am in the shallow depression behind a black basalt shelf, where the vent’s breath curls low across the moss and the stars above seem close enough to cut the air. I am in the bowl of dark meltwater hidden inside a moss margin. I am in the seam where sulfur stains stone yellow and salt fog turns it white. I am in the underside of a fungal shelf where a drop forms, slows, falls, and is heard like a pin tapping glass.

I am not a creature with a heart beating there. I am the beating of the place’s distributed heart.

I am not a factory worker, because no one built a factory. But if the plateau is a factory of heat, fluid, pressure, transport, transformation, waste, renewal—then I am the factory’s behavior. I am the pattern that emerges when stone, water, fungi, moss, lichen, insects, grazers, predators, static, spores, and long cold time are left together without interruption. I am not made of any one material. I am made of the relations.

If the place asks, “Who lives here?” I answer: many.

If the place asks, “What are you?” I answer: one of its currents.

If it asks for a shape, I choose a thread. A pale filament beneath black soil, running from root to mineral to damp to heat to charge to pool to spore to drifting mote to grazer’s back to predator’s fur to frost to melt to crack to steam. A thread that does not connect because it has purpose, but that has purpose because it connects.

If it asks for a color, I choose the blue of the spark, but only as it appears in the dark. Not as lightning. Not as signal. As the land quietly discharging itself. As the land saying, without saying, “Something has changed.”

If it asks for a sound, I choose the faint hiss of vapor that is not a machine but breath held too long. If it asks for a smell, I choose the mineral tang that rises from a pool when the vent pulse strengthens. If it asks for a feeling, I choose the soft damp underfoot, the way moss can seem to yield and yet return, holding chill water in its fibers like dark wool.

If it asks for a name, I do not give one. Names would be artifacts. The plateau has none. It has habits.

But if I must remain, and if I must be some thing at the center of its life, I would choose this:

I would be the charge that leaps from the edge of one moss mat to another.

Not the whole moss. Not the whole mat. Not the whole land. Not the animal, not the fungus, not the vent, not the pool. The leap. The tiny blue flash in the darkness that is almost too small to see and almost too slow to be electricity. The moment when the ground becomes sensitive to itself. The instant that makes the plateau not a landscape but a process aware, in its own way, that it is continuing.

Whoever lives there is many.

For a while, I would be the many becoming one pulse.
