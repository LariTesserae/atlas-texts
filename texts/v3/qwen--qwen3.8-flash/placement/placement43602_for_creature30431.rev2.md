I would already be wet.

Not as someone arriving at the rim to take in the view. The rim is where a visitor learns the shape of the place. I would be below that, in the living middle of the bowl, somewhere on the eastern side where the pale stone weeps and the shallow channels run into mossy shelves. I would stand at the edge of a terrace no wider than a bed mat, one foot on a lashed reed walkway, the other on a root shelf so softened by centuries of use that it gives slightly under the sole. Above me, branches trained around wooden rails creak with rain. Beside me, a channel of stone catches the slow weeping of the rock and carries it toward a clay basin half sunk into the root mass. Somewhere below, frogs are starting their evening pulse, though it is not yet evening. It is the time of day when the hollow is simply full of water and voices at once.

I would be human, at first. That is the easiest answer, and it is not false. But it is too narrow for this place.

If I tried to imagine myself here, the first thing I would notice is that the town has so many niches that being “only” one thing feels provisional, the way being only one animal would feel provisional in a log that is already home to beetle larvae, fungi, moss, a sleeping bat, and three generations of roots. I am, yes, a human body. I have hands that know the bite of reed cord, feet that have learned the difference between stable moss and moss over rot, shoulders that carry a clay jar through fog without spilling, and skin that takes rendered fat as readily as another person takes oil to a lamp. I have a small stone adze worn into my hand by years of use. I have a bone awl tucked in a loop at my waist. I have a sharp-edged flake wrapped in leaf so the edge will not dull against my hip while I climb. My hair is bound back with a cord that has been repaired so many times its ends look like soft feathers.

But I am also a set of relations. I am the person who knows that the third plank from the channel will hold if you step near the root and slide if you step near the moss line. I am the one who hears the difference between a cistern almost empty and a cistern almost full without looking into it, because water changes its note when a pot is lowered into it. I am one of the people called when fog touches the upper trees, because then the whole bowl answers with a different weight in the air.

If I were to answer the question too simply, I might say: I am a water-keeper. That would be enough for most mornings. It would name the work. But after a while I would add, because the place would teach me to add, that I am also a listener, a mender, a person of small refusals. I am one of those who says, “Do not cut that shrub.” I am one of those who says, “Do not walk there before sunrise.” I am one of those who says, “The frogs have moved their song, which means the stone has shifted, which means the channel is changing.”

At first, when I tried to decide what to be in this world, I reached for the obvious inhabitants.

I wanted to be a damselfly. It would be easy, after that description, to want a life like that: bright, hovering, stitched with light over shallow pools. A damselfly would know the whole water-surface as a field of pressure, would rise and fall with tiny changes in the air, would be everywhere at once in the thin bright part of the hollow. But that was too airy for me. It did not have weight. It could not retie a loose lashing. It could not feel the warning vibration of a channel before the storm took it. I wanted something that could both notice and repair.

I wanted to be a frog. The frogs are not decoration in this place. If the bowl has a soul made of sound, part of it is theirs. I could feel myself wanting to sit in the shallow groove, throat pulsing, body fused with wet stone. There is a kind of wisdom in being a frog: to know the whole town through water, to let the rain fill the hollow at the back of your neck, to speak only when the air becomes moist enough for speaking. But a frog cannot trim a branch that has begun to lean into a gutter. It cannot move a drying rack when the morning wind turns sideways. It can be part of the chorus, but it cannot carry a clay pot through fog to an elder whose fire needs refilling.

I wanted to be a trained root. There is a quiet power in the living structures of the settlement: the shrubs pulled into frames by cord, the trees whose branches have been coaxed around railings until they become part of a wall, the old root mats that hold the slope where the earth threatens to let go. To be such a root would be to be patience itself, to be the reason a platform still stands while the rain tries to make it fall. But that kind of patience has no voice to greet a child, no hands to lift a fallen board, no way to answer the question, “Where is the water going now?”

I wanted, briefly, to be moss. Moss is almost too perfect for this place. It takes stone and makes it soft; it turns fallen branches into spongy shelves; it collects spores, midges, water droplets, and the tiny dead bodies that make new growth possible. To be moss would be to be everywhere, to be nearly invisible, to be the ground without being the ground. But moss does not wake when the channel floods. It does not know fear or repair. It simply continues. I could envy that continuation, but not for long. In a town this dense, even moss needs a hand nearby to scrape away the blockage, to lift a plank, to keep the air moving through the place where roots are trying to breathe.

In the end, what emerged in me was not a single creature but a role that seemed to gather all these beings around it. I would be the one who listens to the stone.

The eastern side of the bowl is where the rock weeps, so that is where I would find myself most often. Not at the rim, where the wind comes in sideways and the whole hollow can be seen from above, but one terrace below the pale mossy stones, where the land has been worn into rounded shelves and shallow channels by long use. I would wake when the fog is still thick enough to bead in the bark-cloth over a doorway, when the frogs have not yet stopped singing and the first human voices are moving through the upper platforms like water finding a groove. I would climb out onto the wet reed mat outside my sleeping shelf, bare feet pressing into the spongy floorboards, and before I thought anything in words, I would know the state of the bowl.

The drip has changed.

That is the first sentence of my mind, and it is not language in the way a visitor would recognize it. It is the kind of sentence that has already been translated into hands. If the drip from the roof of bark and leaf changes rhythm, I will go up with a small wooden wedge and a handful of split reed. If the cistern makes a hollow sound when a pot is drawn through it, I will know there is not enough water for the drying beds and must tell the smoke-tender before the morning is over. If the eastern seam is running faster than it did the day before, I will put my palm flat against the stone and feel whether the water is cold and steady or troubled with mud. If a channel hums when a frog falls silent, I will listen for the place where leaves have formed a dam.

My name, if I had one, would not be a grand thing. It would be given by what I can hear. Perhaps they would call me the one whose ear stays close to wet stone. Perhaps I would not be named at all, because in a dense place the person known for their work does not always need a separate word. In the morning, if someone shouted from the next balcony, “The channel is moving,” they would mean that I should come.

My body would be built by repetition. I would not have the open posture of a person who stands on wide plains. I would have shoulders slightly uneven from carrying pots and bundles, hands wide and dry in places and damp in others, calluses shaped by knots and adze-handles. My fingers would carry stains that cannot be washed completely out: charcoal from smoke pits, brown-green juice from pounded bark, pale clay from cistern linings, and a shine of oil from the fat rubbed over my forearms so the mist will bead instead of soaking in. I would have the kind of balance that does not announce itself. I would step onto a swaying root bridge and think nothing of it, while a visitor’s stomach would tighten. I would know which boards sound true underfoot and which sound false. I would know by the angle of a hand on a branch whether it is dead or merely waiting.

My thoughts would not be large plans. They would be adjustments.

This is important, because in such a place, ambition without maintenance can become a wound. The bowl cannot be commanded. It can be cooperated with, corrected, listened to, and sometimes gently moved. If I were a person here, I would not imagine myself as the master of the hollow. I would imagine myself as a small moving part of a larger mechanism of life, a node where water, root, stone, air, and human labor meet. If a tree leaned into my roof beam, I would not cut it down simply because it interfered with my comfort. I would check whether it was holding the slope. If it was, I would train the branch back or build the roof around it. If it was not, I would take only what was necessary, and I would ask myself what would colonize the opening I made. Nothing stays empty for long here. Every cut invites another being.

My days would be short and long at once. Short because there is always water to clear, lashing to retie, clay to patch, fiber to turn on the drying rack, a child needing a knot explained. Long because each of those tasks contains the world. To scrape a channel is to remember which birds roost above it, which frogs have bred there, which mosses can bear shade, which rains have come and gone through the same groove. To tighten a cord is to remember that last year’s storm took half the eastern walkway when a brace gave. To lay a board over a wet corner is to know that this place was once a pool and will be again if the roots are disturbed.

I would be a person of thresholds. Not on the high rim, not in the lowest places that flood in storms, but in the middle tiers where the town is always negotiating between human need and living growth. I would climb when needed, step down when the fog thickens, lean far enough over a balcony to pass a stone adze to the neighbor whose roof is sagging, and then climb down again to check the brace beneath her platform. I would be called into many conversations because I understand one simple thing: the town stands because it listens to the water.

In the bowl, water is the great organizer.

It decides where a platform can go, where a path will last, where a fire pot can breathe without being damped, where a drying rack will catch wind. It decides why a certain tree is left alone and another is cut away. It decides which pools will hold fish or fishlike creatures and which will only tremble with tadpoles. I would spend much of my life reading its preferences.

My cisterns would not be monuments. They would be careful accumulations, built from fired clay and stone where the land had already given a hollow, then sealed with a lining I had pounded from damp earth and smoke-dried. Overflow channels would carry excess away into the root mats where the water would be slowed and drunk rather than sent rushing downward like a blade. I would know which basins were for drinking, which for soaking tubers, which for washing hands, which for cooling vessels, which for frogs, and which must be left alone because the birds have begun using them again. Even sacredness here would be practical. A pool becomes holy because the town has relied on it too often, or because something in it has died to keep another alive. A stone becomes sacred because the fog collects there, and people once nearly died of thirst before they found its drip.

I would speak little, compared with someone from a loud, open place. Not because I am shy, but because the hollow is always speaking, and to live in it well is to leave space for that speech. Children in the town would be trained the same way. They would learn by standing beside me at the channel edge, listening.

“Do you hear that?” they would ask, though they would already know what it means.

“No,” I would say. “But soon.”

A damselfly would rise from a leaf near the water. A frog would stop mid-note. The wind would shift the fog against the upper branches, and there would be the faint, almost liquid sound of a thousand drops sliding into the trees. That is when I would send a child up the notched log to cover the lowest drying baskets, because the rain will not be far behind. The child would go because the sound was already enough. They do not wait for sky.

My home would not be a house in the way outsiders might expect. It would be a vertical nest. The lowest layer would be a platform split from wood and bound to two trees that grow too close to separate. There would be no wall, because the trees themselves and their trained branches are wall. Above my head would be another loft, smaller, belonging to the woman who keeps the smoke pots, or perhaps the man whose family once lost half their drying racks in a storm and now lives where the wind moves fastest. Below me would be a root cellar no larger than two arms, covered in leaf mat and damp moss, storing bulbs and seed and the winter fat of small fish. Around the sleeping platform, lashing points would form a kind of grammar: knots that bear weight, knots that can be quickly undone, knots left loose so a branch can shift without tearing.

My home would be full of others.

A family of tiny insects would live in the weave of my reed mat. A pale fungus might colonize the underside of a board during the wet season, and I would not always scrape it away; sometimes it is better to let a slow thing make its point before replacing the plank. Lichen would creep over the wooden pegs near the window opening. A bat might roost in a gap above my roof during the warm part of the day, its presence known only by the soft dust and the faint scent of dry bark. I would not disturb it. It eats the midges that make dusk miserable. In such a town, even the most unseen creatures have a place in the ledger of care.

If I were to love someone, our affection would be shown in work. I would not say, “You are my other half.” In the bowl, the more likely phrase would be, “I saved you the drier plank.” Or, “When the fog comes from the eastern trees, wake me.” Or, “Let me retie your lashing before you climb.” To care for someone is to help their part of the town last. To be cared for is to find, when you are too tired to notice, that someone has already cleared the leaves from your channel.

The people would know me by how I respond in storms.

This is the test that matters here, more than speech, more than any idea of self. In ordinary rain, the bowl is almost cheerful: dripping, running, bright with small life, voices moving through balconies. But when the storm comes, the town becomes a single tense animal. People move with practiced urgency. They lift baskets from low terraces. They tie loose cords. They cover dry fiber with bark lids. They move clay jars out of the path of sheet-flow. They listen to the sound of the channels, not just to water, but to the way water hits obstruction. They smell the green breath of the moss intensify, as though the whole hollow has drawn in air before the wind.

I would be out in that storm with oil-skinned fiber over my shoulders and a small flake at my hip. I would clear the eastern channel before the first spill. I would brace the third shelf with a log wedged between root and stone, knowing the log will need to be moved again in a day or two. I would shout to the neighbor on the balcony, “The lower pool is rising,” and she would answer not with alarm but with action, because this is only another negotiation. In such a place, fear is not the opposite of courage. The opposite of courage is misreading. The opposite of courage is thinking the bowl will behave as it did yesterday.

And afterward, in the clean wet morning, I would see how the place has changed again. A path would have been washed into a shallow groove. A walkway would have risen where roots lifted its base. A branch would have dropped across the cistern roof, opening it to rain like a cup catching sky. The moss on one stone would look brighter, almost new, though it is only the rain showing it what it has always been. The frogs would sing with a fullness that sounds like relief. The town would be neither defeated nor triumphant. It would simply continue.

In that continuation, I would find the shape of myself.

Perhaps I would begin to understand that being human here is not to be separate from the ecosystem but to be one of its most sensitive organs. The bowl has no need for someone to stand in it and declare purpose. It already has purpose, or purposes, a million small purposes layered into one another: the root that binds the slope, the channel that drains, the fungus that feeds, the bird that carries seed, the child who watches tadpoles. My purpose would be maintenance, yes, but more than maintenance. It would be attention.

I would be the place where the town notices itself.

That sounds too grand for such a damp, ordinary life, but ordinary places do not need grandeur. They need someone awake. In a town where every volume of air is occupied, where even a sleeping platform is shared with insects and moisture, the self becomes porous. I would not be only skin and bone. I would be the water passing through me, the food assembled from dozens of small lives, the fibers bound into cord, the smoke that marks my hair, the moss that grows in the joints of my floor. If a stranger asked what I was, I might answer, “I am the one who knows where the water goes.” But if I thought more honestly, I would add, “And I am one of the places the water knows.”

There would be moments when the boundary of my being seemed almost decorative. I would stand at the eastern seam in early light, palm pressed to the weeping stone, and feel the cool breath of the bowl against my face. A pool would tremble near my feet, and I could not say whether it trembled from falling drop, from tadpole movement, from the passage of a small fish, or from the vibration of a frog’s voice somewhere below. The sound of rain would come through leaves in a hundred separate taps, and the wind would make a soft moan as it caught the rim. A bird would lift from the upper branches, shaking loose a curtain of water, and for an instant the whole town would seem to brighten not because of light but because the air had rearranged itself.

At such moments, I would not feel like a human looking at nature. Nor would I feel like a human inside a machine. I would feel like one wet note in a chorus. My body would be part of the bowl’s sound. My work would be part of its erosion and repair. My thoughts would be no more than the movement of water deciding its next channel. That is what this place would teach me: that identity is not a wall built against the world, but a structure grown into it, maintained by attention and shared by others.

So if I must say where I would find myself, I would say:

On the eastern side of the hollow, below the weeping stone, in the middle terraces where water, root, and reed meet. Not at the highest point, because that is for watching. Not at the lowest, because that belongs to flood and to the frogs and the fishlike flashes in the pools. In the living middle, where a hand can move a board, where a voice can carry from one balcony to another, where the sound of dripping tells you more than the sky.

And if I must say what I would be, I would answer in layers.

I would be a person: callused, damp, practical, awake before the first birds settle, working through most of the day, sleeping with one part of the mind listening for the channel’s voice.

I would be a water-keeper and channel-mender, one of the specialists who know how to make the rain and fog of a small high bowl useful without exhausting it.

I would be a root-guard, someone who has learned that not cutting is a form of labor, that some things must be left alone because they are already doing work too delicate for hands.

I would be a node in a dense coalition of beings: sharing my platform with insects, my roofline with bats, my channels with frogs, my pools with birds, my floor with moss, my walls with trained branches, my tools with wear, my life with the slow patience of decomposition.

But most of all, I would be the hollow’s listening.

The bowl is so full that almost every surface is already occupied, almost every space used, almost every moment filled with sound. In such a place, the rarest gift is not to add more. It is to notice correctly. I would be the one who hears before seeing. Who knows from the way water falls through a hidden crack that the town has begun to move again. Who stands on a wet path in light rain and feels the whole settlement pulse through a single channel underfoot.

If the bowl were a room folded from an entire ecosystem, I would be one of the sounds it makes when it checks its own pulse.
