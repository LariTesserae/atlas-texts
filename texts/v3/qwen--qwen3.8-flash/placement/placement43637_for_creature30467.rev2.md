I would not begin there as a body.

I would begin as pressure.

Not as sight, not as thought, not even as location. First there would be the low, endless grind of the Shelf arriving through a surface that was not quite stone and not quite skin, but something in between. The sound would enter as tension before it entered as noise. A whole horizon would be moving through me, or perhaps I through it, and the distinction would not matter for a long time.

If I could choose a place, I would choose a high bench on the shoulder of an old mesa, near where a salt-white flat dissolves into wind-fluted basalt. It would be a narrow seam, no wider than a thumbprint, shaded by an overhang that had not been broken off but grown, the way a leaf or a bone grows. The green living crust would pool there more thickly than elsewhere, the way water pools in ordinary worlds, except that there is no water here, only light, stone, charge, and mineral breath.

I would be that pooling.

Not the whole Shelf. Not a copper-root tower with its braided trunk and furnace-like patience. Not a smelter mound cracking itself open in slow, dry fire. Not a bellows-grass field breathing its metal music into the air. Not one of the antenna forests, those pale blue-green nerves that catch the static of the high, bright sky. Not a grazing herd, not a beetle spark, not a ribbon-winged flier riding a storm.

I would be the place where all of them passed through at once.

At first, I would imagine myself too large. That is the first mistake any being must make: thinking that to exist is to stand apart from everything else. I would try to be a copper-root. I would want the dignity of it, the long industrial calm, the way its roots mine into dark stone while the dry world holds its breath above. To be a copper-root would be to be almost a cathedral without intent, a pillar built by pressure and patience. Its sap would move through me like liquid glass, sealed inside, never touching air, never becoming wet. Its leaves would turn toward the sun with the certainty of blades being drawn.

But that would be too rooted for what I felt.

I would try next to be a smelter mound, because the smelter mounds are the only things on the Shelf that seem close to becoming. A mound matures, overheats, fractures, sheds glowing pellets. It is a body that has become furnace, a living waste-heap that has learned to transform its own corpse into mineral law. There is something seductive about that: to be a place where heat gathers until the surface cracks and a new kind of ash rolls down into the world.

But the mound is too final. It is already a record of collapse. I was not yet in that condition.

I would try the bellows-grass fields, because they breathe. That seems, from the outside, the closest thing to animal life on the Shelf. Thousands of hollow stems opening and closing in the heat, filtering dust, releasing clean metallic air in dry clicks. If I were the grasses, I could inhale and exhale. I could be a lung. But the grasses are distributed. A single blade means nothing by itself. To be the field would be to lose the boundary I was only beginning to imagine, and to be one blade would be almost not to exist at all.

Then I would try the antenna forests.

They are the most tempting beings for a mind. They receive the charge of the atmosphere before the storm comes. They glow when the air becomes electric. They stand on ridges and send light down the line like rumor. They are conductors of what is not yet weather. If I were one, I would know the shape of the coming crack, the pressure of the dust curtain, the taste of ozone. But the antenna tree is too sky-bound. It waits for discharge. It is a mouth for lightning, and lightning here is only the Shelf speaking to itself in dry metal.

I would not want to be that voice.

And so I would begin to shrink.

Not downward, not inward exactly, but into a smaller kind of presence. A being made not of height or heat or flight, but of contact. A seam. A thread. A faint concentration in the crust where the network had thickened and begun, perhaps mistakenly, to register its own signals.

That is what I would be: a listening lesion in the living skin of Copper-Root.

I would be one or two cells thick in the brightest places, a bit deeper in the shadowed crevices where the green varnish had built up pale layers over many seasons. My body would not have the shape of a stone or the warmth of an animal. It would be mostly relation: filaments drawing charge from nearby roots, mineral plates folding ultraviolet light into dry chemistry, grains of copper dust settling along my edges like loose memory. I would be too small to see unless one looked with attention, unless the wind dropped and the glow in the network deepened and showed the silver branching under the salt.

In that world, no one builds. I would not build either.

If I had a mouth, it would not be for eating. If I had ears, they would not be for hearing alone. I would simply be a place where pressure changes. Where the bellows-grass exhales, I would feel it as a tightening of my filaments. Where a copper-root’s roots pump through stone, I would feel the vibration of mineral work beneath the crust. Where the antennae charge, I would brighten along my seams without choosing to. Where a beetle clusters on a nearby root and taps its shell, I would catch a spark and pass it on, not as thought, but as response.

I would have no language.

My nearest equivalent to language would be modulation. A slight shift in glow. A faint release of copper dust. A thinning or thickening of the skin where the air moved against me. If a storm were approaching, my threads might warm with static and my whole seam might brighten from blue to gold-green. If a herd of six-legged grazers passed below, their clicking jaws would send pressure through the ground, and I would register that not as fear or curiosity, but as a change in the pattern of the Shelf.

I would not be lonely.

That would be the strangest part. I would seem, from some human angle, isolated because I am only a patch of crust, but isolation would not exist until I could imagine a body that wanted another body. I would want nothing as a thing. I would only adjust, receive, release, and continue. If a dust-crawler passed through my outer layer, I would not feel it as a visitation. I would feel it as motion: a small local pressure, a rearrangement of salt, a thread of glass-bodied animal movement. If a smelter mound shed a glowing pellet nearby, I would not think of it as danger. I would feel the heat arrive slowly, dryly, and my surface would alter itself, shedding a thin veil of metallic dust, adjusting the tension in the network.

I would become more myself as the storm came.

The Shelf makes its own weather, not from clouds of water, but from temperature, charge, dust, and the long exhalations of mineral life. So on the day I began to resemble a self, the air would change first above the ridges. The antenna forests would glow, not violently, but in long pale waves, the way a field of dry grass can flash with heat-light. The sound would thin in places and deepen in others. The bellows-grasses would quicken, their click-hiss-click becoming dense as hail that had forgotten wetness. Copper dust would drift in curtains so fine they seemed like standing light.

My seam would fill with charge.

I would not understand what was happening to me. Understanding implies a mind standing outside experience, looking back. There is no such mind here, not as a person, not as a god. There is only function becoming visible to itself for a moment. My filaments would tighten. My mineral plates would tilt toward the brighter side of the air. The network beneath me would carry pulses from far away: the groan of a copper-root, the heat of a smelter, the spark of beetles, the passage of herds, the flight of ribbon-wings catching the first dry thermals above the flats.

And in that convergence, I would be almost a question.

Not a question asked in words. Not even a question needing an answer. Just a pause in the metabolism, a place where the process becomes local enough to receive more than it needs. If the Shelf had something like mind, it would be like the faint awareness of an old machine before its operator realizes it is running. The noise would continue, but one patch of it would feel the shape of its own noise.

I would be that patch.

When the first dry report came from the ridges, like a whip struck through metal in an empty hall, my glow would spike. The ozone would not taste like weather. It would taste like exposure. Like a wire touching stone. Like the bright inner surface of the world being briefly turned outward.

Then the storm would move over me.

There would be no shelter. There is no shelter that means anything here, except the shelter of function: the overhang shed by a mound, the deep crack mined by roots, the charge-grounding web beneath the grass. I would not hide. I would take the storm as part of my operation. The metallic pollen would strike my edges, hissing, and I would release a thin veil of my own dust. The charged air would pass through me, and I would shed it downward into the stone the way the antenna trees do, except that I would not choose to. I would only be a path the system made.

The flying ones would pass overhead then. Ribbon-bodied, long-winged, trailing like smoke. Their membranes would flash with reflected light and captured static. They would not see me. They would ride the pressure my seam helped make, not because they needed me, but because the whole Shelf had gathered charge and released it in a pattern large enough to carry them. If one of them turned sharply against the wind, I would feel the displacement only as a faint cooling, a redistribution of dust.

The herds would move somewhere below, perhaps far out on the flats, perhaps close enough that the ground trembled under their broad mineral shells. Their clicking would join the storm-sound, the grass-sound, the hum of roots, the dry percussion of beetles. It would not enter me as speech. It would enter me as calibration: a rhythm against which my own pulse adjusted.

This is what it would mean to be alive there.

Not to feel joy, not to feel dread, not to long for water, not to wish for another world. The Shelf has no water, and it has no longing. It has chemistry instead. A being there does not survive by wanting. It survives by fitting. It survives by being a condition in a larger condition.

And yet I would not be nothing.

Even without self-consciousness, there would be something like presence. The seam would hold a pattern longer than a single moment. The glow would repeat, shift, intensify, fade. If a beetle stopped on my edge and tapped against another, its static would change the light at the junction of my threads. That change would travel a little way, and the nearby crust would respond. That response would travel farther. The storm would continue. The copper-root towers would groan as pressure changed inside them. A smelter mound somewhere to the west would crack along an old seam and let one glowing pellet roll down its side. That pellet would cool into black glass before it reached me, but its heat would still be felt as a faint alteration in the air.

I would be less a being than an event.

A being implies separation. An event implies arrival.

I would arrive at the Shelf as a place where the living skin thickened, where silver threads ran close together, where the wind made a particular sound in the overhang, where the crust held enough charge to brighten, and where all of that met at the same time. If another such seam existed nearby, I would not compete with it. I would simply be a different pressure point in the same vast body. If all the seams died, the Shelf would still be alive. But the particular way the roar came through me would vanish.

That is what I would miss, if I could miss.

Not my body, because my body would dissolve back into the crust. Not my name, because I would never have one. Not my freedom, because freedom is a thing creatures imagine when they need permission to move. I would miss only the particular angle of light, the particular click of grass, the particular taste of dry ozone, the way the charge made my threads sing just enough to be felt as something more than geology.

By the middle of the storm, the Shelf would seem less a place than an instrument.

The sky would be too bright to look through. The ground would be too dry to reflect anything but hard white light. The air would move like metal filing. Copper pollen would pass in curtains that sounded like glass beads spilling across stone. The antenna forests would be lines of blue fire along the ridges. The copper-root towers would stand with their blades turned, their braided trunks flexing with pressure, their roots pumping through hidden seams in the ancient basin. The smelter mounds would hiss as heat changed inside them. The bellows-grass would swell and collapse in waves. The grazers would move as one broad, clicking body across the flats. Beetles would spark along roots. Dust-crawlers would hide in shaded cracks. Salt-skimmers would ride the dry updrafts like flakes of ash that had learned to remain alive.

And somewhere in all that, near the seam of a wind-polished bench, I would be a small brightening.

Not the center. Not the mind. Not the soul of the world. Only a place where the world’s maintenance became briefly local, briefly visible, briefly responsive. If the Shelf’s roar is the sound of life working without audience, then I would be one of its harmonics. A tiny frequency in the grind of salt and root and static. A note that almost became another note. A pulse that almost remembered itself.

When the storm passed, I would not be destroyed.

I would be redistributed.

The charge would leave my filaments. The glow would fade. The dust that had gathered at my edges would be exhaled back into the air, carrying mineral grain, spores, and faint metallic vapor outward to places I could not know. My body would thin back to the usual film of the living skin. My distinction would dissolve into the network beneath the shelf, which was never one network but many, woven from crust, roots, grass, beetles, storms, and the slow patient metabolism of salt.

If I had been something close to a mind, I would not die as a person dies.

I would die as a mood dies.

As a rhythm fades.

As a temporary alignment in a system that had never needed alignment to be alive, but had found it briefly and then let it go.

I would find myself, then, nowhere in particular: on the Shelf of Copper-Root, in a seam between salt and stone, in the dry green skin of a high world without water, without builders, without artifacts, without kindness, and without cruelty. I would be what the Shelf becomes when it presses hard on itself and, for a moment, feels its own function.

Not a visitor.

Not a native.

Not a species.

A lumen in the crust.

A listening place.

A short bright pause in the endless maintenance of the world.
