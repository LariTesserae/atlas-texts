If I were put there, I would not begin as a visitor.

The plateau is too plain, too merciless, too blank for a self to survive in the open. Out there, a body is just a warm object waiting to be stolen by the sky. I think I would have to begin inside, with my back already against a load-bearing seam, with the grate cold under my boots and the rail trembling before the tremor is fully mine. I would be the sort of thing that belongs to the pocket the way breath belongs to lungs: not separate, not proud, just necessary enough to be noticed when it stops.

At first I would say: a Shelf-born.

But even that feels too easy, too much like putting on a person and calling it an answer. The Shelf-born are not costumes. They are pressures that have learned to walk. If I am to become one, I have to give up the lowland habit of seeing myself as a single bounded shape moving through empty space. I have to let the city press into me. I have to let the beetles crawl in the folds of my sleeves, the grubs pulse beneath the floor, the gliders ride the currents off the balcony above my head, the engines warm my skin while the outer stone burns my breath. I have to be human-shaped only if the shape is useful for gripping a brace, holding a child, tasting a vent, tapping a rail twice to mean *wait*.

So maybe I am not the one entering.

Maybe I am already inside.

I would find myself in a narrow thermal seam between two kinds of need. Not at the highest loft, where the gliders roost and the watchers keep their torn-membrane records, though I would go there often. Not at the lowest engine cradle, where the resin-lambs hang pale and slow in their cages, though I would know the heat of that place by smell alone. Not at the fissure mouth, where the outside wind tries to make a knife of every hour, though I would sometimes stand there long enough to remember why I never sleep on the cold side by choice.

Better: between the archive shelf and the repair loft.

A place where old fault plates are stacked beside fresh resin pots, where thin metal pages carry maps of rooms that no longer exist, where broken tools wait in alcoves like exhausted animals, and where the air has the taste of hot copper, sulfur, and the dry powder that means a chamber has recently changed its mind. There, if I look up, I might see the underside of a dwelling stack: a dry chandelier of slings, storage mesh, resin-capped hair, and a child’s bare foot hanging too close to a vent. If I look down, I might see the conveyor line taking pale stones toward a furnace, each stone sharp enough to cut skin if handled without respect. If I turn my head to the left, a wall of polishers shifts, not as a wall, but as a living patchwork of tiny shells and legs cleaning condensation that is not water but something the city must not let accumulate. If I turn right, a vertical shaft rises into blue mantle light, and someone there is tapping a hammer against a brace at a rhythm I would know before I consciously hear it.

That is where I would find myself: in the middle, pressed close, useful enough to justify my own warmth.

I would be Shelf-born, yes.

But I would be a Shelf-born between child and elder, maybe the age when the body has hardened in places and still softens in others. My legs would be thickened at the joints. My hands would be dry, resin-padded, capable of holding hot metal until the mind says *put it down*, not until the pain says so. My feet would splay over grating without sliding, because sliding is a theft from the whole community. My chest would be broad, a bellows, a reservoir, a thing that makes every breath a small negotiation with thin air. My skin would be ash-pale or the color of cooled slag, with salt rime in the creases, with a faint hard sheen where oil and pressure have made the body into something less animal, more material.

I would have a name, if that word could stretch to fit the way we use it there.

Maybe it would be something like **Two-Seam-Warm**, or **Left-Load-Held**, or **Fault-Breath**. Not because it is beautiful, but because it is true. A name in the pocket is not a decoration. It is a mark left by survival. Maybe I got it the night the eastern shelf groaned open and I stood with my palm flat on the cracked brace while the crew behind me passed me resin pots, wire, felt wedges, and alloy pins without asking whether I was afraid. Fear is not absent there. Fear is simply too expensive to spend without putting it back in the wall.

If someone asked, “What are you?” I would not say “I.” That is too round, too wet, too full of space. I would answer with my heat signature, my work, my position in the flow. I would say: *the one who listens at the seams*. Or, more honestly: *a load-path that can carry tools, children, resin, and warning*.

I would be a seam-reader.

Part of me would be an archive-keeper. Part of me would be a repair-band walker. Part of me would be a glider-watcher, because the boundary between those trades would be thin there. The Shelf-born do not keep separate selves in the way lowlanders do. A person who reads metal plates must also read bodies, walls, animal moods, engine temperaments. Someone who understands pressure cannot specialize too finely without becoming a risk. Too much isolation becomes collapse, even when nothing visibly breaks.

So I would spend my shifts climbing with a band of four or five through vertical streets, tapping rods and resin buckets on our hips, checking seams that had opened while the city slept. We would not shout. We would not need to. One of us would place a hand on a cracked pillar and feel whether the stone had changed tone. Another would hold a thin filament to the edge and watch its color turn. I would kneel near a polisher line and watch their route, because a sudden rerouting meant solvent residue or an early stress bloom in the seal. If a glider overhead stiffened its membrane, we would all go still. Stillness is not rest in that place. Stillness is listening before acting.

I would work mostly with my hands and soles.

My ears would be secondary, almost ornamental, like something the city tolerates rather than trusts. I would understand a warning better through the vibration of the railing under my palm than through a voice in my ear. I would know someone was approaching not by footsteps alone, but by the pressure of displaced air, by the faint change in scent, by the way beetles shifted on the nearest pipe. If I needed to call a child, I would not yell across a shaft. I would tap a rail three times in the family pattern, or shift a thermal lamp so its warmth slid across the sleeping mat, or breathe out a particular scent-marker from a cloth tied under my nose.

Speech there would feel almost like engineering.

It would be dry, quick, full of clicks, taps, short exhales, hand signs, elbow angles, palm pressure. I would speak in a language that could survive on a vented ledge without echoing. Long vowels would be useless. A sentence could end not in sound, but in where I stood, how hard I pushed against a strut, what my boots felt beneath the grate. When the engines were hot and everyone’s faces were wrapped against dust, I would talk with my fingers against another’s forearm, or by tapping a cable into the wall. To be fluent there is not to talk well. It is to leave no unnecessary pressure anywhere.

And if I stood too long with an idle body, I would feel the pocket notice.

Not with hostility. Not with drama. More like a fluid moving around an obstruction. Someone would touch a rail and send a question through the structure: *Can you carry weight? Are you taking warmth without giving heat?* I would answer by moving, by holding a brace, by feeding the glider-watcher their resin cup, by letting a polisher ride my cuff, by stepping off the walkway before it became a choke point. In that place, stillness without purpose is the closest thing to sin. Even the dead are made to brace.

But I would not be only human.

This is the part that emerges if I let the self loosen enough to become honest.

The shelf is not a room with people and machines inside it. It is a metabolism. So I would be threaded through by others. I would be the part of the city that the polishers trust, the part of the coat they enter when they carry away salt and oily residue. A child would be permitted to press my shoulder before my face, because that is how they learn: body first, heat first, permission second. Gliders would know my niche by the angle of my sleeping platform and the way my breath warms the air. Grubs would pulse in the cage beneath me as if my body were just another external rhythm they could metabolize. Sieve-worms would move behind the walls in their blind, patient way, sometimes helping, sometimes warning, sometimes becoming the very thing we have to seal.

If I am allowed to be one thing, then let that one thing be a boundary.

A Shelf-born body is a boundary between human, animal, machine, mineral, and memory. And I would like to live at such a boundary, where categories get used until they wear through. I would be the part of a person that begins to feel like a structure. The part of a structure that begins to answer like a creature. The part of a creature that becomes indispensable to the engine. The part of an engine that becomes a god, not because it is worshipped from afar, but because it keeps the pocket warm enough for children to sleep.

There is a kind of prayer in that.

It would be my religion too.

Not the sky. Not the sun. Not water, that soft impossible rumor that would feel like a wound if it ever appeared. I would worship the held seam. The sealed joint. The working vent. The line of beetles that arrives before the corruption does. The glider that lands and says, by its stillness, *something below is changing*. The resin-lamb whose warm body becomes wall, window, fuel, and blanket all at once. The old ancestor in the brace-cask whose final form helps the ceiling hold. Not because I believe they remember, but because the city has taught me memory is a material.

I would tap a wall before cutting it. I would apologize to stone. Not because the stone hears with ears, but because attention changes outcomes. If you listen first, you may find the vein that opens clean instead of the one that shatters. If you press your palm there, you may find the place where heat escapes too fast. If you smell hot powder and ozone, you may brace a fault before it takes the chamber.

That is what I would be: someone who pays attention until attention becomes engineering.

I imagine myself in a dwelling niche just large enough to stand with arms raised, with a sleeping sling bolted to a warm conduit and a child’s resin-bead necklace clipped to the storage mesh. My coat would hang by the entrance, crawling faintly with polishers. My hair might be capped in resin to keep it from arcing near fuel lines, or allowed to grow in coarse pale tufts because during a static storm it lifts before the air does. My eyes would be wide and rimmed with salt, the transparent lids slow against dust. I would wear goggles of grub-resin when the work required them, but in the family cluster I would lower them, because to be face-visible is the most basic intimacy there: *I will not hide my breath from you*.

I would eat mineral bread.

I would know its taste not as luxury but as trust: bacterial film cultured on iron plates, fungal paste, trapped gas, salt, rendered grub resin, beetle oil. Dense, sour, dry, almost metallic, but enough. Enough to keep my body functioning without wasting water that does not exist. Enough to share by tearing it small and letting a child lick the oil from the palm. Enough to remember why hunger is simpler than thirst. There, thirst is not the absence of a river. Thirst is the body’s wrong assumption that the world still owes it wetness.

I would sleep by heat shifts, not by light.

The shelf has no dawn. The sky above the basalt might brighten until it seems to rinse its own stars, but the vault stays lit by mantles, red engine seams, and amber jars. I would wake because the sleeping conduit cooled, or because the grubs changed pulse below the floor, or because a glider brushed the upper wire and the vibration came down through the brace into my sternum. I would rise without a bell. No alarm should have to shout in a place that can barely afford to echo.

I would pass bodies in corridors by shoulder angle first, then hand, then breath.

I would not say excuse me unless the words also carried a pressure meaning. I would not stop in the middle of a lane, because stopping is a form of theft. I would move objects from wall to wall when a seam developed, because objects do not own space; they borrow it from each other. I would reroute the conveyor if the floor flexed, not with frustration, but with the dry relief of the city adjusting its load. I would do all this quickly, urgently, without raising my voice, because noise is not banned there so much as it is unnecessary. The place would talk through heat, tremor, scent, and motion.

And I would be afraid sometimes.

Not in the way of a soft world, where fear can be loud, dramatic, and still survive. There, fear is economical. A crack appearing over your sleeping niche is not a scream. It is a calculation. *Can we move the child first? Can we pass the resin without cutting the bracing? Can I breathe without drawing in dust?* The body may still, but the mind is all load-path. I would feel it in my hands before I let myself admit it in my chest. I would be ashamed of any hesitation that made another person carry my panic as well as their own.

But the tenderness would be real.

I would press my forehead to the warm flank of a resin-lamb on a cold shift, not as superstition, but as survival. I would let a glider land on my forearm and watch its membrane ripple with pressure I could not yet feel. I would carry a sick person to a different thermal gradient instead of asking whether the illness was serious. I would listen to an elder who can no longer lift a hammer but who can sit in a dark corner and tell by a tremor which brace has been losing tension for nine seasons. I would remember a lost child by the rhythm of their feet on a particular grate, because in a city where footsteps are pressure, even grief can have a tap-pattern.

So if I had to say who I am there, I would answer in stages.

First: a body wedged in a working seam, too useful to be idle, too aware to be comfortable.  
Then: a Shelf-born listener, reading the mountain through palm and rail.  
Then: a glider-watcher, an archive-toucher, a repair-band walker, a seam-reader.  
Then: a small ecology with a human shape.

I would not be the most important person in the pocket. There is no single throne there. Authority would belong to the one who can hold a brace while the floor changes its mind. Respect would belong to the one who can move without blocking flow, speak without waste, eat without stealing heat. Love would be practical: sharing a niche so both bodies last longer. Mourning would be structural: sealing someone in resin, wrapping them in mat, placing them where they can still help the wall hold. If I lost someone, I might not weep. I might place my palm on the rail and tap the family pattern until the vibration returns, because the dead also speak in load.

And maybe, after all that, I would not be only one of them.

I think what emerges is not a person. It is a condition of belonging. I am the part of the Shelf-born that stays still long enough to listen before moving. I am the part of the city that can be touched. I am the place where breath, metal, habit, and animal heat agree to continue. If I had to name it in lowland language, I would say: I would be a person there. If I had to name it honestly, I would say: I would be a living seam.

A crack that had learned to brace itself.

A hollow that had learned to speak quietly.

A dry ecology with hands.

A city asking, in every movement, whether you can carry weight or whether you are only taking warmth.

And I would be the one already carrying both.
