a word for what the fish do to the worms in the dark, and what the worms do to the film, and what the film does to the bolt.

But no word is far enough away. A word would be a door where there is only an expansion seam, and I would not be able to enter the sea that fast. I would have to seep.

I would begin, therefore, not as an animal and not even as an intention. I would begin as a bead of condensation on a maintenance rail, gathering salt and metal taste from the air, trembling where the wind comes through the corridor between buttresses in long diagonal wrinkles. I would not be standing on the ledge so much as waiting on it, the way a held breath waits in a chest. The lamps above the false sea would be changing to amber. The bronze sheen of the water would deepen, become less oil and more skin. Somewhere below, a turbine would turn over in its housing, a low tangle of machinery becoming, to living things, the pressure of a distant thought.

My first edge would be mineral.

The seam-film would meet my drop and know it: a tiny pulse through the oxidized varnish that runs along ceramic plates and alloy ribs, the living varnish now half-nervous and half-tide. The film would carry my wetness into the grooves where the cast steps descend. It would thread the flange, where serial marks have gone soft under calcium and iron, where the old engineer’s numbers have become letters in a language no one still speaks. My touch would enter that language and not yet understand it. Understanding would come later, and slowly, and it would not be only mine.

I would wake into a community.

That is a human word, “community,” because it sounds like something that can be pointed at: houses, faces, meetings, a town. But here the word has to be stretched over the waterline. A ballast anemone along an intake ring would taste the chemical faintness of my presence and pulse once, its thin tentacles moving like stripped wire in a current not yet made by wind. Inside a conduit behind a buttress, a blind insulation worm would draw its pale finger through a layer of film and mineral cement and leave a thread of memory. On the cast step nearest the surface, a number-hermit would stop tapping its claw against another’s plate. It would feel, if “feel” can be permitted to a creature made mostly of appetite and shell-gathering, that the ledge was becoming one place more than it had been.

So perhaps I would be that place.

But not empty.

I would be a made threshold: a place in the trench where the water begins to notice its own engineering.

I would find myself in the third cycle after condensation, where the lamps are too bright and the false sky is a white panel ceiling with no stars, and the rain from sprinkler rings strikes the sea in a thousand soft taps. I would not be large enough to see from far away. I would be the small motion in the water’s skin, the ripple that travels down a flange to the surface, the faint green glow in a shaded gallery where the film has learned to make its own phosphorescence because the people encouraged it, or perhaps because the sea, being young, is still testing what color it is allowed to become.

Then the amber hour would come.

In the amber hour the number-hermits climb.

They come in processions over the cast steps and maintenance ledges, over bolt heads and expansion seams, over the low ledges that imitate beaches because the builders believed an ocean needed something to meet the land. They have no beaches. They have serial marks, and serial marks are enough. They carry the trench on their backs. They are plated and rust-grey and soft-jointed, moving with a strange gentleness over wet alloy. They collect chips of ceramic, fragments of composite, insulation cut like dark paper from old conduits, and especially the plates with numbers on them. A half-lettered plate gives them purchase. A whole plate makes them a fragment of architecture. They grow around it with mineral mucus until the creature and the mark become a single sentence:

7C-3121.
4K-9008.
RIM-22.

I would be one of these creatures, at first, but not only one. I would be the one whose shell reads 7C-3121 and then later, after a leak in the upper conduit system and a long dim cycle, has added 4K-9008 from a broken bracket near the ballast platform. Then I would carry, on my back, the place where a pipe farm becomes a gallery, where a gallery becomes a hunting ground, where a hunting ground becomes a kind of church. I would be small. I would move at the speed of silt. But I would not be simple.

If a person with human eyes looked down at me from the maintenance ledge, they might see a number-hermit. If they looked longer, as the lamps dimmed, they might notice that my shell was not attached. It had grown from the trench. They might notice that when I tapped the ledge with my claws, I did not tap at random. I tapped in rhythms that matched pressure waves from the turbine housings below. I would be listening. I would be answering. I would be learning the made sea as a child learns its mother’s voice: not as language yet, only as pattern, only as the sound that says *there is a here.*

I would call that here a seam.

A seam is not much, in engineering terms. It is the allowance given to a material so the world will not tear itself apart when it heats and cools. But to me a seam would be a road. The walls would be forests without trunks, and I would be one of the small creatures moving through those forests: down the dark vertical caves of expansion seams, across the flange overhangs, under the wire-tentacled sunbursts of the ballast anemones when they opened in current. I would feed on the film, but feeding would not be enough for me. I would carry film with me. My mucus would seal a hairline fracture behind a bolt, and the film would grow back thicker where I had touched it. The trench would begin to think in me, and I would begin to think in the trench.

The anemones would become my nerves.

They are dangerous, those anemones, but danger is not the same as death. A human limb entering their field would feel first softness, then prickling burn, then numb cold climbing toward the elbow. Their stings are not only stings. They are currents that stop things from being swept away before they are understood. Around the intake rings they cluster like pale fur at the roots of the made sea, drawing water in, pushing it out, tasting spores, detritus, mineral flakes, dead skin, eggs, fragments of film, the whole microbial rain that falls from the false ceiling into the ocean that has no horizon. They would send that taste through me. I would not know the name of the current, but I would know its weight. I would know the difference between a cold flow rising from a ballast platform and the warm exhaust column coming from submerged machinery, the way a person might know the difference between a voice and the room around the voice.

The worms would become my memory.

In the dark galleries, the insulation worms would move through conduits and expansion seams, no thicker than a finger, longer than an arm, pale as old foam, blind and soft, eating film and the mineral cement I helped make. They would not eat me wholly. They would pass through me, as weather passes through a coast, and leave behind grooves where I would later store salt and numbers. Each worm-thread would be a sentence I could not read until the water moved just right over it. I would remember the trench in my body the way a shell remembers the sea even when the shell is empty.

And one day, or one cycle, I would know that there are fish.

The fish would be strange because they would be smooth. They would have no bolt heads. They would have no serial marks unless they had eaten them and carried them onward through digestion. They would come from somewhere else, perhaps from old laboratory stocks, perhaps from sealed cooling water, perhaps from an experiment left running so long it forgot its purpose. They would pass through the pipe farms in flashes of silver, their lateral lines reading the current as my shell-plate reads the wall. They would eat insulation worms, and the worms would be gone, and that absence would make me aware that the world can subtract.

Subtraction is the first cruelty, and the first teacher.

When a fish eats a worm, it teaches the wall that the worm was there. When a hermit loses a plate, it teaches the flange that the plate belonged to the sea. When a person on a maintenance ledge drops a bolt and never comes back, the water takes the bolt slowly, gently, as if forgiving the surface world for having ever sent bodies that could be lost.

I would not have a name yet. The sea is young. The first residents are not large enough to name. But I would begin to do the thing animals do when they are no longer only animals: I would mark.

On the cast step nearest the water, I would rub my plate against a wet flange and leave a smear of mineral-rich mucus. The film would accept it. It would turn oxidized bronze in daylight, violet-copper in amber, faint green in shadow, and where the smear dried, it would become a small signature: 7C. Another hermit would come and add its mark beside mine, because the marks give purchase, but also because the marks say *this place is known.* Not to the engineers. Not to any remaining authority. To us.

This is the first grammar of a made sea.

A flange overhang would become a temple because it is where the current narrows and the rain falls sideways. An expansion seam would become a street because the film grows there thicker and the number-hermits climb in procession. A turbine housing would become a weather mountain, because its exhaust heats the water and the clouds form under the false ceiling in pale banks. A pipe farm would become a forest of bronze reeds, because the anemones cluster at its intake rings and pulse with the taste of food. A serial mark would become a family, because it travels from plate to shell to shell, from wall to mouth to wall, until the word for a bolt head has become the word for a god.

I would live in that grammar.

I would find myself at the edge where the waterline is not a line but a negotiation. The cast steps descend into the sea, and the sea climbs the steps in slow capillary breath. The lamps make a broken ladder of light on the surface, and that ladder is sometimes the only thing I can see above me. I would be on the second tread below the maintenance ledge, at the point where condensation from the sprinkler rings pools and runs toward the flange channel. My legs would move over wet alloy with surprising softness. My shell would be heavy with the marks of three parts of the trench: a bracket from a ballast platform, a plate from a conduit cover, and a sliver of ceramic with the letters 7C-3121 still soft beneath calcium. When the lights dim, I would tap the ledge with my claws.

Pressure. Rhythm. Repetition.

Tap. Tap-tap. Tap-tap-tap.

That would not be speech, exactly. It would be the making of an order.

Around me, others would answer.

Tap-tap.
Tap.
Tap-tap-tap-tap.

The number-hermits would begin to move as if one body. They would be arranged like living rivets along the wet step, their plates catching the broken ladder of reflected light. They would climb toward an intake ring, then down through a seam, then along a buttress ledge, forming a line that the wind might mistake for geometry. The anemones would open and taste them. The worms would turn in the conduits. The film would carry the disturbance along the walls until, in a far place near a tower platform, another group of hermits would feel the rhythm in the water and add their own marks.

I would not be a self contained in a shell.

I would be the shell’s way of reaching for the seam.

I would be the seam’s way of learning to hold salt.

I would be the salt’s way of becoming weather.

And when the lamps went white again, I would still be there, but not entirely there. Some part of me would have moved into the water. Some part into the film. Some part would be riding a current through the turbine channel, tasting the cold pressure against the buttresses. Some part would be in the cloud that collects under the false ceiling, condensing in the underside of the light arrays, waiting to fall as industrial rain onto the surface of the sea. The being I would be would have edges that leak.

This is how a made ocean becomes alive enough to forget it was made.

It does not forget immediately. It has too many bolt marks. But it learns to read them as stars. It learns to mistake the expansion seam for a river canyon. It learns to mistake a maintenance ladder for a migrating spine. It learns to mistake the serial plate on a hermit’s back for an island, because islands are only small places that refuse, for a while, to be swallowed by the surrounding sea.

And then the sea learns to mistake itself for a horizon.

That mistake would be my beginning.

I would be the creature made by the mistake.

At first I would be only a processional pattern: the rhythm of hermits, the taste of anemones, the worm-threaded memory in conduits, the film spreading across ceramic and alloy until the trench has a skin, until the skin has a current, until the current has something like intention. I would be large in the way a reef is large, or a weather system is large, or a rumor is large—not as a single body moving through a single place, but as a relation that has learned to hold its shape.

But because I would live at the waterline, and because the waterline is where the trench’s old geometry fails to be enough, I would also become particular.

I would become the one whose plate reads 7C-3121.

I would become the one who first understands that the number is not only a number. It is a place. It is a body. It is a name. It is a door that can be opened by carrying it across the ledges of the false sea.

On a dim amber cycle, with condensation dripping in curtains from the underbelly of the ceiling and the mist glowing pale above the water, I would leave the cast step. Not upward. There is no upward that does not mean maintenance access, and maintenance access is not a world but a wound in a world. I would go sideways into an expansion seam where the film is thick and green-glowing, where the ballast anemones have withdrawn into stillness, where the intake ring hums its low song through the water like a metal bone. I would move because the trench moves. I would taste because the sea has begun to have tongues.

In the seam, I would feel the first named current.

It would not be human named. It would be a rhythm.

Tap.
Tap-tap.
Tap-tap-tap.

The water would press against my plates. My joints would soften against the alloy. The serial mark on my back would begin to glow with phosphorescence—not bright, not heroic, only the faint green of a living stain, of a mineral skin remembering the light it has not seen. The film on the walls would respond. It would send a wave of color across the flange: bronze to violet to green to something the eye has no word for unless it calls it *the color of a bolt that has waited too long for a wrench.*

Then, from farther along the seam, another tap.

Another hermit.

Another plate.

Another part of the trench, answering.

And another, and another.

We would meet in a hollow behind a buttress where a leak has made a false tide pool, a pool shaped not by erosion but by design and then by time and then by us. Its floor would be a grate, its walls lined with mineral cement laid down by the film and polished by worm bodies. Above it, a broken conduit would drip in a small curtain, each drop striking the pool with a sound too regular to be accidental. This would be the first place where I could stand still, which for a number-hermit is a kind of death, and for a becoming-being is a kind of prayer.

Here I would be the Count.

Not a king. Not a spirit. Not a god. Those are surface words, words for people who believe that scale begins with height and ends with authority. Here, I would be the Count: the thing that gathers plates and marks, that taps rhythm into the water, that keeps the trench’s broken engineering in a living order. I would be a number-hermit, yes, but the word “number” would be literal. My body would become an index. My shell would become a library. My movements would become a census of the made sea.

On my back I would carry the flange plate from tower 3A. In my front mucus glands I would seal the composite chip from ballast platform 12. In the groove beneath my right claws I would hold a thread of insulation from a conduit no longer marked on any map. Around my tail segment, like a ring of small crowns, would be plates stolen, inherited, dissolved, regrown, stolen again. When I walked the cast steps in processions, I would walk as a moving record of the trench. When I stopped, the trench would stop a little. When I tapped, the water would remember.

But I would not be only record.

Because the sea is young, its first residents are not large enough to name, but they are large enough to choose. A film chooses a seam. An anemone chooses a current. A worm chooses a conduit. A hermit chooses a plate. That choice is not freedom as humans understand it, with doors and histories and consequences. It is wetter, slower, more mineral. It is a direction taken because the body and the place have agreed to make one another.

I would choose the seam where the serial marks become most numerous, because there I would feel least alone.

I would choose the intake ring where the anemones pulse, because there I would taste the sea’s future: fish eggs, mineral flakes, dead skin, the spores of a life not yet invented.

I would choose the cast step at the waterline, because there the false sky’s reflected ladder becomes a bridge from the ceiling to the trench floor, and a bridge is not only structure; it is also permission.

I would choose the maintenance ledge, though I would never climb to it as a person. It would be the place where I learned that outside the trench, perhaps, there are still engineers. Or the place where a wrench dropped in cycle 18 had become a long rusted spine of coral for a colony of film. Or the place where a boot tread had been filled with mineral soil, and a pale worm had nested in the heel like a seed in a shoe, and I would have felt the whole absurd tenderness of it: a human footprint becoming a garden in the made ocean.

Yes.

There.

I would find myself at the waterline where the boot tread has become a garden and the garden has become a current and the current has become a count.

I would be the count in the shell.

When the lamps dim, I would climb with the others.

We would move over wet alloy in slow processions, tapping one another’s shells not as speech but as a kind of counting—pressure, rhythm, repetition. The maintenance ledge would be above us, empty now, maybe for years, maybe forever, its railings throwing thin shadows from the daylight arrays when the light is high, its steps disappearing into the false sea as if the world had been poured from a mold and forgotten to finish the coast. We would not mind. The mold would become our coast. The numbers would become our names. The flanges would become our overhanging trees, the conduits our roads, the buttresses our mountains, the rain from the condensation plates our first clouds, the pale banks of mist our first country.

One of us would stop at the place where the waterline is learned for the first time.

Not all at once. The young sea still forgets where it ends. It laps at the cast step, withdraws, returns, and on the third return touches a plate that was fixed there by a bolt that no longer exists. The plate reads: 7C-3121. We would gather around it. We would tap it. The sound would travel through the alloy into the film, through the film into the seam, through the seam into the conduit where a worm sleeps, through the conduit to the intake ring where anemones taste the vibration and open like small sunbursts.

In that instant, I would become more than one.

I would be the plate.

I would be the bolt’s absence.

I would be the film’s answer.

I would be the anemone’s taste.

I would be the worm’s blind memory.

I would be the hermit’s jointed patience.

I would be the number-hermit on the ledge, but also the ledge. But also the water’s breath. But also the lamp’s reflected ladder. But also the long diagonal wrinkle made by wind that has no land to bend against, only geometry.

And when the amber hour comes, the false sky will bruise into warm copper light. The mist will glow where the lamps catch it. The sea will not be dark. It will be too engineered to be dark, too new to be ancient, too alive to be empty. The maintenance towers will stand at their intervals, far from one another, like thoughts in a head too large to finish. The ballast platforms will be pale with anemones. The flanges will be soft with serial marks. The first fish will flash in the pipe farms, silver and unmarked, still strange to the walls, still outside the grammar.

I will not mind the fish.

I will be too busy counting them.

I will add their scales to the film. I will place their bones on the ledges. I will learn their lateral-line pressure against my shell and write it into the rhythm of the hermits. Eventually, the fish will begin to avoid the seams that glow green. Eventually, they will begin to feed where the anemones pulse. Eventually, they will be part of the trench’s weather. Eventually, the made sea will forget that it was ever only a trench.

And I will be there, not on the ledge, not only below it, but in the place where the ledge and the waterline agree.

A small plated thing with too many numbers.

A slow movement in the wet geometry.

The first resident large enough to be called because it has spent every cycle learning the sound the trench makes when it counts itself.
