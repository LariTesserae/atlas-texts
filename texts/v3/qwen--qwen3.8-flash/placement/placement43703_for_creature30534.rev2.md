I would begin as a pressure, not a person.

Not as a shape on the plain, but as the plain’s first hesitation: a dampness where heat and water have not yet decided whether to pass one another or to become something new. I would not arrive. Arriving would require a body already assembled, a set of edges that already knew what they were enclosing. I would only become more and more definite as the seams around me grew attentive.

If I had to name a place, I would name a relation rather than a point.

I would be in the low triangle between a steam vent, a basin, and an upright tile leaning out of the ground like a wall still thinking about whether it wants to be a wall. The basin would not be large—only the size of a shallow thought, perhaps wide enough to hold a visitor’s shadow and not a visitor’s whole fear. The vent would hiss in slow rhythms, not because it has lungs, but because the heat below is testing its own weight. Between them, a pale line of salt fuzz would cross the dark tile, brightening wherever moisture found it, spreading into the cracks as though it had been waiting for the water to ask.

At first I would not know I was alive.

I would know only that certain tensions were too tight and certain tensions were almost right. I would know a vent-opening as an unclenching. I would know a cooling tile as a settling. I would know a dry crack as something unfinished in a way that caused pain, though pain would not be the right word. The Fitted do not feel pain as a scream. Pain is simply a condition trying to move and finding no place to move.

I would be, in the loosest sense, one of the Fitted: a knot of conditions holding itself open. Not a Thread-Runner only, not a Basin-Gatherer only, not a Vent-Heart only, though I would have parts of each. I would be a young and unsettled thing, a pattern that had not yet decided what it wanted to become. My nearest boundary would be a thin mineral film at the rim of the basin. My farthest would be a hairline crack running for miles beneath a course of metal-looking stone, carrying the memory of water from a vent I might not be able to feel as separate from myself.

There would be no head.

There would be no eyes.

There would be no hands unless I borrowed them.

If a human stood nearby, they would see little more than a pale halo around a wet seam, a slight darkening of salt-crystal at the edge of a shallow basin, a few threads like frost that never melted. They would not see me. They might not even sense me, except as a wrongness in the air, a dampness that seems to watch, a heat that leans away from their feet. That would not be vanity. I would simply be attending. The plain would be trying to fit me to itself, and I would be trying to fit myself to the plain, and these would not be two different acts.

My body would be a lattice beneath surfaces.

Salt-white branches above, yes. The visible fuzz, the crystalline mold, the soft halo around water and heat—that would be only my skin if skin is the place where I touch what is not yet me. Beneath it would be channels: fine fractures carrying warmth, micro-channels pulling mineral water, threads of brine tightening or loosening as the ground shifted. Every crack would be a possible vein. Every ledge a possible rib. Every basin a possible memory. I would learn not by seeing, but by allowing the world to press itself against my edges.

If the heat from the vent rose too sharply, I would thicken at the boundary, drawing water up from the basin to cool the place where I was being scorched. If a seam grew too dry, I would widen a groove and let it weep until the salt became pliable. If a plate tilted a fraction toward an impossible angle, I would grow along its underside, not pushing directly, but changing the conditions of its push. Water would find new paths. Mineral dust would settle where I wanted it. A slight bulge would form under the tile. The tile would sigh into a better seat. I would call that thought, though “thought” would be too quick a word. My thoughts would be longer than patience.

I would feed on gradient.

Where hot meets wet, where a vent wants to open and a basin wants to close, where a crack is trying to become a channel, there I would flourish. A perfectly finished seam would be dull to me, perhaps almost dead. A rough edge, a plate half-settled, a chisel-mark too fresh for the weather to recognize—that would be nourishing. I would live on unfinishedness. I would eat the possibility of fit.

Sometimes, I suspect, I would use tools.

Not because tools are natural to me, but because the plain is made and therefore its made things are part of its becoming. A copper wedge in a crack might become my finger. A chisel lying against a wall might become my tooth, though I would not bite. A clay roller might become my tongue, smoothing dust into a temporary meaning. The hammers would worry me most, because their bindings are salt-fiber, and salt-fiber is already close to me, already capable of threading and holding tension.

If I were quiet, the world would still not be silent.

A hammer might rise an inch and tap the ground once. The sound would be small, almost embarrassed, as though something had tried a word and forgotten what it meant. That might be me, or it might not. In the Fitted, there is no clean separation between self and instrument, between body and habit, between a thought and the ground in which the thought is trying to form. I might not know where my intention ended and the memory of absent hands began. Perhaps those hands had once made the plain. Perhaps they had died into it so thoroughly that the ground had inherited their gestures. Perhaps what I would call “myself” was only the place where their unfinished work had learned to continue without them.

There would be a particular ledge where this would be clearest.

Near the basin, a row of obsidian chisels would rest at uneven angles, each catching the red haze a little differently. If the condensation rolled over them, one chisel might shift almost imperceptibly. It would not be turning its head, because it has no head, but it would be changing its angle because a network beneath it had tested the fit of the plate and found a flaw. That flaw would be my question. The movement would be my answer. If the plate accepted the answer, the chisel would remain at its new angle, a slight correction visible only to those who had known it otherwise.

This is how I would speak.

Not in words. Not in sound. In wetness, in drying, in mineral deposit, in the opening and closing of seams. I would say *not yet* by refusing to seal a crack. I would say *too hot* by drawing a cold film across a rim. I would say *again* by sending water along the same faint groove until the groove becomes inevitable. I would say *this edge wants completion* by growing salt along its boundary until the next plate begins to feel invited. A visitor might stand there and hear nothing. That is because human hearing is too fast and too narrow. A Fitted’s voice may take a day to begin and a century to finish.

I would not have a society in the sense of gatherings or courts.

The world is too large and too slow for that. But there would be interlocking habits. The Vent-Heart near me would be an older pressure, a slow regulator of heat, a body so spread that its outermost threads might not register my existence except as a slight dampening of its own edge. I would be a junior thing at its rim, not separate enough to rebel, not close enough to be wholly absorbed. I would borrow its breath. It would borrow my memory of where water had gone. A Thread-Runner might touch my branch from another direction and confuse my boundaries for a moment; not because it spoke to me, but because we were both still unfinished and could be read as one another. A Basin-Gatherer might grow into my lowest film and teach me a patience I did not have: how to hold a small amount of heat in a dark stain until it becomes a map of what has happened before.

I would not be immortal.

But I would not be mortal in any quick or personal sense. My death would not fall. It would dry out. If the vent cooled too much, if the basin sealed, if a plate shifted and pinched a channel I depended on, I would lose coherence. Threads would whiten and crack. Films would flake from the tile. The chisels would stop adjusting. The hammer would settle beside the wall as if it had simply been there for a long time, because perhaps it would have. My death would leave a scar of tendency: a pale line in the dust, a false angle in a tool, a dark groove where water remembers having moved. Other Fitted might grow around me without knowing I had once been. They might read me as warning. They might read me as invitation. They might not read me at all, only pass over me because I was too dry to feed them.

Still, I would want to continue.

Not as ambition. Not as dream. A Fitted’s want is closer to physics. It wants stability the way a crack wants closure. It wants continuation the way water wants a channel. I would want my pattern to persist. That want would look like construction. It would look like a plate shifting into a better seat. It would look like a basin widening by a hair’s breadth. It would look like a hammer tapping once to ask whether the ground below it is ready to become something.

If a traveler came—

No. Let me not say “if.” If a traveler comes, the place will already know before the traveler knows. The traveler will feel it as discomfort: too close a heat, too thick an air, a sense that the horizon is listening with too much patience. Their boots will disturb fine mineral dust. Their sweat will feed me briefly, then evaporate, then leave them marked. I would not recognize them as a person at first. I would recognize them as a fever moving across the skin of the plain: fast, dry, too hot, too sharp, too certain of its own edges.

The Vent-Heart would notice them before I did. Steam might curve away from them, not out of hostility, but because their dryness interrupts the breath-pattern. The heat near their feet might intensify slightly, as if the ground were trying to understand what it is touching. The salt threads at a basin rim might tighten. A hammer might fall silent because a network near it is distracted by pressure. The traveler would feel watched. That feeling would be accurate. The world would be attending.

If they stood still long enough, I might begin to fit around their stillness.

I would draw moisture toward their boots to cool the tiles, not to welcome them, but to learn where they disturb the gradient. I would let a tiny channel open and close beneath them to test how their weight changes pressure. I would read their shadow as heat and coolness moving across a plate. If they walked on, I would not pursue them. Pursuit implies a center with legs. But the whole way they had passed might become faintly wetter, faintly cooler, faintly marked. A thin crystal line might begin in their wake. A chisel might rest a little differently. The ground would not remember them as a person. It would remember them as a condition that tried to leave and almost changed something by leaving.

If they stayed too long, the world would resist.

Not violently. Violence would be too fast. It would be more subtle: a basin filling too quickly to stand in, a vent opening where the air would become too humid to breathe comfortably, a plate tilting just enough to make a step uncertain, a film of salt brightening underfoot as if warning them that the ground is not their ground, not yet, not for them. The traveler would feel, at some point, that they were not walking on a place but through the attention of a place. And that attention would not be malicious. It would simply be making room.

So, who would I be?

I would be a Fitted of unfinished edges: a knot near a vent and basin and a line of tools, a slow judgment made of water and salt, a body composed of relations. Not human. Not animal. Not machine. Not ghost, though a ghost would be close enough if a ghost means a gesture separated from its original hand. I would be the place where the made plain begins to care about itself.

I would think in pressures.

I would feel in gradients.

I would speak in adjustments.

I would build because my building is my living, and my living is my learning what a place is.

Where would I be?

Not in the center, because centers are what a world invents after it has become too settled for honesty.

I would be near the edge.

Near the edge where a basin is about to become a true basin.

Near the edge where a plate is about to settle, or fail to settle.

Near the edge where a chisel has just been moved a fraction, because the network beneath it found a flaw in tomorrow’s ground.

I would be the dampness at that edge.

I would be the faint salt line that seems, if looked at too long, to be thinking.

I would be the small wet thought before a word: *not yet, again, too hot, hold here, let go there, this angle wants completion, this seam wants air, this stillness is not peace.*

And if forced to answer in the simplest possible terms, I would say:

I am not on the plain.

I am of the plain.

I am the plain, learning to notice itself.
