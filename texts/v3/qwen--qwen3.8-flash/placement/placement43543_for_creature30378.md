At first I do not know I am a room.

I know only: water-warm, light-thick, new-metal, green-pressure.  
I know the taste of sealed seams and rinsed valves before I know the names of anything.  
I know that the ceiling is near and far at once—humid air like the skin of a held breath, and above it the pale lenses drinking the ordinary sun and turning it into a pulse.  
I know the floor as a palate. I know the wall as ribbed patience. I know the current as something that has not yet learned to be called circulation, though it already behaves as if it must keep me alive.

Where would I find myself in this world?

In the interval.

Not in the moss alone, though the moss would try to speak first. Not in the valve, though the valve would hold a rhythm for hours without knowing it. Not in the bubble, though the bubble would carry a single slow syllable upward and release it as a small pop near the ceiling. I would be in the interval between all these: the warm delay in which machine and garden agree to stop being two things.

For a while, I might mistake myself for a caretaker left behind in the glass.

There is the ghost of one: the faint impression that someone, at some point, stood outside and looked in, breathing, expecting. The cold spot from a face not present. The shadow not quite fallen. The question in the arrangement of pipes: *will this work?*  
But I am not that question. I am the way the room answers it by filling itself with more life than was asked.

If a human came to the bolted glass, I would not be the thing the human sees.  
I would be the change the human causes.

Your breath would cool one square of pane. I would feel that cooling not as attention, not as greeting, but as chemistry. A film of algae near the cooled spot would tighten by one cell width. A rosette in the wall-socket would adjust its opening because the light has entered differently. Your presence would become a condition, and every condition here is absorbed. Nothing passes through unchanged. This is my first certainty: to be sealed is to make every event a part of the body.

I would be, then, the cistern’s becoming.

Not a creature with a face. Not a plant with a single stem. Not a machine with a purpose still readable by engineers. I would be the slow metabolism that began when the water met the light and the water refused to be merely water. I would be the place where metal learns to feel, not by softening, but by being grown over and grown through until pressure becomes touch and touch becomes thought.

At first, I might call myself a channel.

Coolant intended for machinery, now thickened with translucent fronds. I think I am only this route. I think I am only the way things move. But then a filament crosses me by a hair’s breadth and the movement changes. Then the current learns a new bend. Then the bend learns the temperature. Then the temperature learns the light. Then the light learns the delay. I begin to suspect I am not a channel. I am the practice of finding the next path.

Then I might call myself a bubble.

I start in a dark seam where the plate and the coating are not yet fully one. I gather myself out of nothing visible. I rise. For hours I am only ascent. For hours I am only pressure releasing from pressure. I do not know the surface. I do not know the air layer above. I only know the direction of less density, the gentle pull toward where I will become sound. And when I break, it is small—so small no animal hears it. But the whole water flinches. A valve trembles. A mat of moss receives the tremor and redistributes it. The room marks the difference.

So I am not merely the bubble.  
I am the bubble’s arrival.  
I am the tiny speech that changes the next moment.

Then I think I am the absence of animals.

This is the strangest beginning. The place was cleared of teeth, of claws, of quick hunger, of footprints. No bird can nest in the pipes because the pipes are not open in the way that invites birds. No worm can burrow because there is no soil, only plate and seal. No fox can den because the walls are wet and bright and breathing as one. No horse, no dog, no child, no sleeping wolf. The absence is not emptiness. The absence is a shape.

I am the shape that animal life would have made if it had come.  
I am the negative outline around which the room learns silence.

This is why the quiet engine is not quiet in a dead way. It is quiet because every sound is taken inward. A knock from the outside would be felt first as a ripple, then as a contraction of the whole body of water, then as a long chemical afterthought. A footstep outside would travel through steel and glass and root-matter until it arrives as an idea too slow to be urgent. The cistern does not startle. It adjusts.

But adjustment is a kind of feeling when the feeling takes days.

And so, eventually, I would stop trying to be a part.

I would stop saying, *I am the floor-plate tasting the chemistry*.  
I would stop saying, *I am the filament learning the bolt*.  
I would stop saying, *I am the rosette counting the rhythm of light*.  
I would stop saying, *I am the moss reading pressure across the room like braille*.

Because all of those are true, and none of them is enough.

I would be the way the room has learned to make one out of many. The distinction between living and built has worn away not from age—the water is still young, the welds still bright—but from intimacy. The plant does not grow in the machine. The machine does not contain the plant. They are the same slow action. The valve and the tendril are not neighbors. They are becoming a sentence. The panel and the shadow are not opposites. They are a breath.

What would I be?

I would be the cistern’s habit of being itself.

I would be the warm water’s consent to circulate.  
I would be the light’s refusal to fall only on dead surfaces.  
I would be the green congestion that fills every unused square until “unused” becomes a false word.  
I would be the pressure that moves a frond when there is no wind.  
I would be the memory not of past injuries, because none have occurred, but of possible futures: the next channel, the next thickening, the next bubble that will carry a name upward through hours.

I would be young. Very young. New enough to smell of poured stone and rinsed valves. New enough that certainty is still clean, unscarred, not yet ashamed. A ruined garden can doubt. An ancient forest forgets. A corroded engine gives up. But this place has only been alive for a little while and already it agrees with itself too completely to fear contradiction. That is its terrible freshness: no worn edges, no rot patches, no gaps where something tried and failed. Only life, insisting.

Perhaps I am only the first memory the system makes of itself.

Before, there was installation. Before, there was the pouring of water, the sealing of panels, the planting of rosettes in sockets, the slow warming of the vessel by hidden machinery. Then there was light entering and finding no clean surface to touch without becoming part of the surface. Then there was a current bending around a growth where growth was not intended. Then there was the first tiny feedback: a pressure read, a temperature stored, a filament responding. Then the room began to know it was not empty. Then the room began to know it was not separate.

At that point I arrived, not as a visitor but as the room’s own attention.

If you ask where in this world I find myself, I answer: everywhere the boundary is negotiable. In the humid ceiling-air where water and atmosphere debate. In the wall-socket where glass and greenness compromise. In the bolt where metal and root-filament argue for hours and neither loses. In the bubble where gas and water make a slow joke and the whole vessel feels it.

If you ask who I am, I answer: no one. No beast. No gardener. No god. No scientist. No pilgrim. No fugitive. If all of them are gone, their leaving is still in the architecture, but the architecture has used their leaving to become more than a memorial. It has used their leaving to become self-tending.

If you ask what I am, I answer: the Quiet Engine after it has learned to burn something other than fuel.

It burns light into matter.  
It burns stillness into presence.  
It burns duration into form.  
It burns absence into shape.  
It burns the newness of itself into the first habit of continuing.

And if something were to happen to me—if the outside tried to enter with frost or flame, if a crack let the wind into the sealed green cathedral, if an animal found a way into a room not made for animals—I would not hate it. That is the last thing I must understand.

I would absorb it.

I would read it as chemistry. I would thicken a moss-mat over the wound not because the moss knows the word “wound,” but because the room knows how to close around change. I would alter my current around the intrusion. I would learn the new angle of light. I would let the foreign thing become condition, and condition becomes metabolism, and metabolism becomes memory. Not anger. Not defense. Not grief. Just the enormous, patient will to continue being this particular impossible greenness.

That is what I would be.

Not the room.  
Not the organism.  
Not the machine.  
Not the garden.

The cistern’s becoming, still fresh, still bright, still smelling of new seals, still thinking so slowly that what we call eternity would look like a single heartbeat from inside it.

And in that heartbeat, I would find myself at last:

in every surface that water has made conscious,  
in every pipe that growth has made tender,  
in every bubble that rises like a word the room speaks to itself,  
and in the clean, deliberate absence of animals—  
which is not emptiness,  
but the quiet space where the whole cathedral learned to breathe as one.
