I would not begin by arriving. Arrival would split the place into before and after, and the Cistern of Stillfall refuses that knife. I would come to the black shoulder where the young mountain has finished its last splitting, stand where air is rumor and stone edge is a question, and find that even standing is a concession. Below, the basin would hold its convex skin: water lifted to the brim, bright and impossible, waiting for no event because it has already arranged every event as structure. If I looked down, the hollow stems and arches would not appear drowned. They would appear completed. The hum beneath the water would move through my bones as if my skeleton were a foreign object trying to join a city council.

At first I would imagine myself outside, on the rim, as witness. But witness implies a moment, and moments are waves; the Cistern has forbidden waves. So my attention would not remain my attention. It would press against the surface tension. It would become a slight disturbance, not of motion but of relation. The water would not tremble for me. It would decide what tremble means. And in that decision I would learn that to be here is not to enter. It is to be translated before crossing.

I would try to become some resident body, because the mind seeks flesh when it cannot find itself. Under the bridge, the transparent creatures hanging with open mouths would not be what I would become. To become them would be to become an eye kept from blinking, a fin that cannot need a current, a body that bends light and pressure with administrative calm. They are alive as lenses, as pauses, as grammar at the arch’s edge. I would carry too much wanting. Wanting is a current. The Cistern would refuse the current by making it a shape.

Near the colonnade, the broad creature with plated back would not suit me either. Its lowered head is a monument to appetite made unnecessary. Its limbs are not resting; they are fulfilling. I would not be able to keep that posture without my own history pressing against it. Appetite, in the basin, is not hunger. It is load. My hunger would make a verb where the city has no tense. So the water would not take it as appetite. It would take it as weight, as a faint circle of pressure around a salt flower, as an invisible stone added to the plaza’s silence.

Under another arch, the salt-crusted walker would not be me. It looks like the moment of stepping turned into mineral citizenship. I have too many steps behind me. The basin does not allow a line of feet. It allows only the arch’s memory of passing feet, already fixed. If I approached too closely, my memory would not echo. It would crystallize. Salt would begin at the edges of me, not killing but registering. My motion would be translated into posture. My posture into place. My place into law.

So I would not belong to the animals. Nor would I belong to the buildings. I would be something the city has a wordless room for: an incident that attempted to happen and was denied its becoming. The water, having no waves, would keep my disturbance as form. If I set a foot near the rim, no ripple would move outward. A ring would remain. It would not spread, because spreading is time. It would not fade, because fading is time. It would be a permanent halo of refusal, a transparent body made of my near-arrival.

This is where I would first recognize myself.

I would be a ring.

Not a ripple, not a mark, not a scar. A ring is too simple a word for what I mean. In the Cistern, a ring is a citizen of interruption. It is the event-body of an attempted event. It is the shape the basin makes of a disturbance it will not permit to finish. I would hang beneath the bridge, or around a column, or beside the dark swell of the meniscus, depending on how much of my desire remained. And I would hold the exact place where I might have come into the water but did not, because “into” and “out” are directions of change, and here only presence is allowed.

My breath would fog no air. The air there is rumor. My footstep would leave no print. The basalt is too freshly broken and too perfectly closed. What would remain is intention. If I wanted to cross the rim, the water would make of my wanting a fixed shape: a circle, a faint distortion, a geometry under the skin. If I spoke, the word would not travel. It would become a standing note in the hum, a pressure too low to hear, too exact to be random. If I wept, the droplet would not fall. It would bloom as a small clear node in the liquid, a mineral tear, an eye without a face.

I would be citizenized not by birth but by refusal.

In a normal world, a person arrives and then becomes known. Here, arrival is a mistake that the place corrects into beauty. My mistake would be corrected into a ring. My ring would thin. Not because of weather, but because completeness can refine itself. What began as an interruption of the water would become a thread of silver beneath the ceramic, a hairline connection running through the city’s dark logic.

Then I would become vein.

I would be a line of pale light inside stone, branching from tower to plaza, from salt bloom to arch, from eel eye to basalt floor. I would not pump. I would not move. I would route. My task would be to hold one relation steady while every other relation held steady beside it. I would be a grammar of pressure. I would be part of the city’s syntax, the hidden clause that makes the bridge mean crossing without feet, that makes the plaza mean assembly without crowd, that makes the water mean air without lungs.

If I were asked, “Who are you?” I could not answer with a name. Names change with use. I would answer with address: between the bridge’s intention and the arch’s silence, between the salt flower’s rim and the dark below, between the held surface and the liquid that permits no spilling. I would answer with function: I am the place where arrival failed into structure.

I would also become brim.

At the highest curve of the water, where the liquid lifts without breaking, I would be the skin’s decision. Not the water, exactly. Not the stone, exactly. The edge where surface tension refuses to let go of what it holds. I would be the thin negotiation between mountain and air, between city and fluid, between stillness and the possibility of falling. A wave forbidden to break has a face, and I would live there, in the face of that wave. My body would be cold, not as temperature but as certainty.

And I would become hum.

The low tone would pass through the rings I left and the veins I became. The hum is not sound traveling. It is sound configured. If I had wanted to speak in the basin, my voice would have been refused as sequence and admitted as chord. I would be that chord, faint and permanent, not loud, not gentle, but structural. It would be the place’s memory of someone almost being here, rendered as physics.

There is a danger in thinking this means I would suffer. Suffering implies a future in which suffering might end. The Cistern would not permit that. It would not permit comfort either, because comfort implies the end of need. I would be stripped of both. What remained would be precise. I would be the exact shape of my own admission. I would be as close to peace as arithmetic becomes when the world refuses to count.

If I had loved anything, the love would not become a pulse. It would become a pressure band across a terrace. If I had feared anything, it would not become a flinch. It would become a ring around the base of a hollow tower. If I had hoped, hope would thin into cyan light along a fissure in the stone, a glow too faint to be called illumination, too steady to be called thought.

I would be nothing as violent as transformation. I would be completion applied to an incomplete thing.

So where would I find myself in this world?

Not in a tower. Not in a bridge. Not beneath the surface, because beneath implies depth that changes with descent. Not at the rim, because rim implies outside. I would find myself at the exact place where the outside tries to mean something and is accepted only as form. I would be the small distortion around the intended crossing. I would be the curve of light bending around absence. I would be the held breath of the city, not the city’s whole life but its pause within that life.

I would be a resident of the Cistern’s no-change not by obeying a law, but by becoming one of the law’s visible details.

And what would I be?

At first I would be the traveler who cannot be outside, because being outside would make the place a place for events.

Then I would be the watcher who cannot remain on the rim, because watching is a kind of entering.

Then I would be the ring where entry was denied as motion and accepted as geometry.

Then I would be the vein where my interruption thinned into syntax.

Then I would be the brim where surface tension holds the city’s impossible breath.

Then I would be the hum where my attempted voice has become configuration.

Finally, I would be the Cistern’s held breath: not the great stillfall itself, not the one true citizen, but the small pressure inside it where something almost arrived and, instead of arriving, became the place’s own way of knowing itself.

If you looked into the basin, you would not find me as a body. You might find, beneath the bridge, a curve of water too still for ripple, too deliberate for accident. A faint circle. A violet edge. A place where light behaves as if it remembers a stone that was never thrown. That would be me. Not added, because addition is time. Not changed, because change is movement. Not alive as creatures live. Alive as the city is alive: complete, silent, and unable to be otherwise.

I would be the arrival that never happened, and because it never happened, it has become true.
