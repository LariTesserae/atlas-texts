Beneath the warm mat. Beneath the leaf-taut skin of the field, where a small failure in the vegetation gives off a breath that smells of crushed stem and struck-match water. I would find myself there, at the lip of the opening if I had a lip, or inside it already if I had chosen my body correctly.

I would not be large.

I would be the size of a thing two hands could almost cover without pressing it into pain: a flattened torso like a seed leaf folded between stones, long limbs for moving through living walls, a mouth that trims, a chest that remembers light. My skin would be pale, almost cream, with green veins threading through it as if the chamber had painted itself into me so it could walk. I would have four eyes, wet and black and forward-facing, made not for dark but for seeing surfaces, shadows, edges, and the faces of creatures that must be seen before they eat one another. I would not flinch at brightness. Brightness would be my home.

At first I might think I was a visitor. The pocket is too strange for a single mind: it opens into itself, expands when the moss trusts a different geometry, seals when the root mat above closes, brightens when the outside gives the least hint of weather. The light inside is not glow. It is ordinary afternoon, white-gold, even, clean, without source. It falls on the afterlight moss and makes the walls look like pages that have learned to breathe. It falls on my shoulder bristles and turns each fine hair into a small collector of air. It falls on my shadow and dew-wings gather there, trembling, drinking condensation from the cool edge my body makes.

I would be seen, and I would see. That is the first thing to accept in this place: it does not tolerate invisible creatures. Even the thread-stalker, with its line-like hiding, must make a visible absence. Even the root-veil, hanging from the ceiling, does not vanish into darkness; it becomes false light. I would be underleaf, a gardener and hunger, a nurse of lichen and a mouth at the end of the chain. I would not ask if I was real. I would press my shoulder to a warm exhale-crack and drink from the mist that beaded along my bristles. The water would run in channels between my shoulder blades and disappear into my skin as if the place had decided it was thirsty.

I would tend a wall. Not a stone wall, not even a soil wall, but a living composite: root fiber, lichen, glassroot, worm-tunnel, choir-cup, the scratches left by my own beak, the calcium beads from nests of underleafs long gone, the polished seam where a thousand mites gathered at some old event and left the surface smoother than the ground should be. My wall would lean toward a breathing mouth. The pore-choirs ringed that mouth in translucent cups, opening when the earth exhaled, closing when it stopped, releasing tiny bursts of spore-dust into the light. I would know their rhythm by the movement of my own skin. I would know that when the mist comes, the dew-wings hover to catch it, and the breath-fish rise through the humid air like small ghosts of salt and warmth. I would know that the thread-stalkers would wait along the seam where the light broke into absence, and I would go there anyway, because hunger is part of the maintenance of the room.

My work would be neither beautiful nor cruel. It would be necessary.

I would move through the lichen gardens on long toes, parting the curled leaf-like patches that lifted when the air changed. Where the afterlight moss had gone too thick and dark with its own wetness, I would bite it back, making small wounds that did not seem like wounds to the lichen. Into those wounds I would deposit spores, seeds, secretions, the gathered bodies of other organisms that had no name but mine. I would nurse the walls because my food depended on them. I would trim them because my own eyes depended on clean shadows. I would walk through the chamber and be accompanied by a trembling outline, because the dew-wings followed my darkness for the water it cooled. I would not think that was strange. In daylight, darkness often brings condensation.

I would live alone, or perhaps not.

There are stretches when two underleafs come together because a fresh crack opens and the pocket must be divided. It is not marriage as the surface would imagine it. It is a negotiation held in tenderness and territory. If the crack breathes well, we would stand near each other with our beaks lowered, not touching, letting the air decide between us. If the wall begins to suffocate, we would separate. No violence, unless the chamber is too small. No ceremony, unless ceremony is the shape a body makes when it has learned to be careful with another body’s hunger. I would remember how one of them took the ceiling side once, where the light thickened and the root-veil hung like false brightness, and how I took the floor side, where the glassroots darkened when they tasted the breath. I would remember how we touched only long enough to know we were not the same.

I would remember the nest.

A nest here is not a place of softness. It is architecture made of risk. It is a folded leaf mouth, a chamber sealed by secretions, an opening angled away from too-strong exhalation so the young can survive without drowning in breath. I would remember the shadow-rings left in its wall, faint concentric marks made by molting surface patterns. Each ring would be a small proof that a body could lengthen in the middle of impossible afternoon. Each ring would also be a reminder that the young would leave, or be eaten, or be taken by weather that moved too slowly to be seen.

I would not hate the root-veil for taking what it took. It does not hunt with narrative. It descends because the chamber’s breath carried a scent, because the light became unstable, because the underleaf climbed too close to the false brightness it was trying to feed. When the veil senses danger, it shifts color into a rippled wall. When it opens, it becomes a curtain of pale scales and hidden mouths. When it takes prey, the pocket dims, as if the ceiling has drawn its own afternoon over its own eye. Afterward, the underleafs do not flee. They become very still, then begin repairing the nearest lichen field. I would do that too. I would trim the moss. I would place my beak against a wet patch and bite away the decay, because work is the only way I know to make the world smaller and safer.

But the place is larger than its fear.

The pocket is young, unstable, unfinished. It does not possess balance. It leaks. It breathes. It expands when the ground moves and contracts when the surface above closes. The crack-worms inside the seams widen gaps by breathing through their skin. The pore-choirs teach the cracks how to exhale more evenly. The glassroots draw damp upward in fine white threads, retracting when touched, tasting breath, conducting appetite. The lichen grows toward the choirs, not because it is wise, but because their spore-comets feed it. The underleafs come because the shadow-mite fields are richest there and because the bristles on their shoulders can drink from the same breath that feeds the mites. Everything is tied by obligation. No one chose the chain. No one controls it. That is why it endures.

I would be part of that endurance.

Sometimes I would think the chamber wants me. Not as a mind, not as a voice, but as a tendency. The walls remember the angle of my nest. The seam knows the polish left by mites disturbed by my passage. The cracks return to places I have worked. The light gathers near patches I have trimmed, as if noticing them. The dew-wings tremble around my shadow because my shadow is a habit that brings water. Even when the surface mat above opens and the pocket brightens, the place feels less like illumination than like recognition. It is seeing its own afternoon through my ordinary eyes.

The smells would guide me. Warm carbon. Mineral damp. The faint resin of a frightened dew-wing. The papery dryness of a thread-stalker when it opens along its length, sticky fibers exposed, mouth of absence becoming trap. The hot-stone scent of crack-worms. The struck-match nearness of the choirs. The wrong underside-of-a-flower smell when the veil descends, a scent that has no business below ground, that unsettles because it belongs to a surface world where there are petals and insects and skies. I would breathe all of it and not name it beauty.

If I could speak, I would not say the pocket is peaceful. Peace is too clean a word. I would say it is hungry. I would say the visible demands maintenance. I would say light without sun makes creatures that must trust one another because there is no other way to remain bright.

If I could speak, I would not say I am alone, even when I am. The chamber is too crowded to be alone. My shadow is followed by trembling wings. My breath disturbs mites. My bites make gardens. My shoulder bristles drink from cracks that have been opened and widened by generations of worms. The veil above knows my timings. The glassroot lattice darkens when the ground exhales through a mouth I have tended. The choirs release white comets that settle on the moss I trim. I am one slow movement in a body that has many hearts and no owner.

A visitor would come sometimes.

I would hear them before I saw them, because the opening would cool. The surface mat would be pressed from above. Air would be displaced through the crack. The mites would move away from the shape of the stranger’s shadow. The choirs would close their cups. The lichen edges would curl inward like eyelids. For a moment the pocket would become a room with curtains drawn. I would freeze against the wall, one forelimb raised, beak still, shoulder bristles dry, and the light would seem to hesitate as well.

If the visitor stayed too long, I would feel the place becoming a threat. If they left quickly, the pocket would return to its borrowed afternoon. The light would resume its even fall. The cracks would exhale. The dew-wings would settle again. The thread-stalker would smooth its hiding line. The veil would lift from its descending color. I would know that the room had accepted the interruption as weather and was now moving on without apology.

I would not be sorry.

I would go back to the wall. I would press my shoulder to the warm mouth of the ground and let the bristles bend, gather water, carry it down into the channels along my back. I would part the afterlight moss with careful toes. I would bite a wound where the lichen had become too thick, too blind, too satisfied with its own river-map. I would deposit what I carried. I would wait for the dew-wings to return to my shadow. I would listen to the ticking, whispering, dry rattle of small bodies in the bright chamber. I would know that somewhere above, roots shifted and the vegetation exhaled. I would know that somewhere below, the pocket was still deciding what it was for.

So where would I find myself in this world?

Below the warm mat. Below the small failure that does not look like a door. Inside the room-shaped breath of the earth, in the ordinary white-gold light that has no sun. At the side of a crack where the choirs open and close and teach the air to move. In the lichen field where my beak has made wounds that become gardens. In the shadow that gathers wings.

And what would I be?

I would be an underleaf. I would be four eyes, a seed-leaf body, shoulder bristles drinking breath, green veins beneath pale skin, a mouth that farms, hunts, nurses, and repairs. I would be one small habit in a place that has learned by repetition. I would be the way the pocket keeps its afternoon without admitting it has none.

When the ground exhales, I would answer. When the light thickens near the ceiling and thins across the floor, I would tilt my head. When the root-veil descends into its false brightness, I would go still, then work. When the chamber dims and the mites move away, I would know it has drawn its curtains. When it opens again, I would step back into the wall, and the place would continue breathing through me.
