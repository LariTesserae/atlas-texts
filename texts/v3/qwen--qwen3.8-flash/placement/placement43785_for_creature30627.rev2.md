At first I am not a thing.

I am a pressure difference in the water between the old pump house and the place where the market square used to be a place where people came for salt, for bread, for news, for warmth. The Sump of Verath does not begin with a door or a sky or a coastline. It begins with a difference in weight: one current moving a little heavier than another, one colder thread drawn from a valve long after anyone remembered to close it. For a while I am that current. I have no name. I have no skin. I slide along the faint trace of vanished traffic and, because I have nowhere else to go, I become interested in the idea of being held.

I try first to be too large.

I try to be the whole basin: black ceiling above, broken tiles below, all that suspended architecture breathing with the patience of old iron. I try to be the pump wheel turning once for no reason anyone could prove. I try to be the rumor in the turbine housing, the thing the Sump does not allow witnesses to confirm. I try to be the child’s boot, because the boot is a relic of a room, and the room is a relic of a life, and a life is a relic of a heat the water has forgotten. But the boot cannot breathe, and the turbine cannot choose, and the pump wheel is only obedience, and the basin is too large to be a self. So I shrink.

Then I try to be too small.

A flake of paint lifting from a pipe. A bubble rising without breaking. A grain of silt turning in a cold spiral near the arched window of the pumping station. A single waxy chip shed from a bloom that grew where two metals met and neither had anything left to say to the other. These things have dignity here, but not identity. The Sump is full of details that do not need me. They continue whether I watch them or not. I try to be one of them and fail too gently to notice.

It is the cold that decides. Not with violence, but with selection. It asks: what body can live in such water without complaint? What appetite can polish decay without pretending to restore it? What smallness can move through a drowned city without becoming a ghost? The answer comes as a suggestion, not a name.

I become a pale threadlath.

No—not singular, not yet. My “I” is not human and not neat. It arrives first as a cluster, a few slender bodies folded against the inside of the child’s boot, near the place where toes used to press against leather. I am several of us. I am the grazing itself. I am the blind front end sensing the water. I am the thin darker channel inside translucent skin. I am the tiny hooks or pads too small to name. I am the thing the boot has become: not a lost object, not a relic of childhood, but a room.

The boot is suspended in the alley between a shuttered shop and a collapsed stairwell, where the current once remembered a footpath and still obeys it faintly. It turns once per day, though no one knows whether the turning is caused by the water or by the movement inside it. I am the second answer, and perhaps the first answer is also true. My kind moves along the inner seam, along the stiffened leather, along the metal eyelet where old lace has rotted into a ghost-thread. We feed on the waxy bloom that has gathered in the toe like candle grease. We scrape it clean in thin lanes. Where we pass, a faint gleam returns to the brass buckle or the enamel button, as if a forgotten servant had continued the housework centuries after the house had gone under.

This is not adventure. This is not haunting.

It is maintenance.

For some time, I think I am only the boot. I think my entire world is this curved interior, this room shaped by a foot that no longer exists. The walls of the boot are cold enough to make memory seem unnecessary. There is no light except the dim blue-white sheen of the wax. There is no sound except the muffled contraction of metal outside and the almost silent rubbing of ourselves against the leather seam. But the boot is not sealed from the Sump. Pressure moves through it. A pulse comes from the pump wheel. The water tightens. We flatten. We are still for a long while, indistinguishable from frost on a pipe. Then the pulse passes. We continue.

Through the boot’s small opening I am also outside myself. I drift into the alley with a current that flows heavier than the others, along an invisible street line. My body is so small that the water holds me the way a hand holds a coin. Around me, the Sump’s other details appear not as scenery but as surfaces of life. A cable brushed against a bracket releases a vibration that travels through my front end like a note. A column of mineral growth stands where a doorway once was, and I crawl along its side, tasting the calcium film left there by ring-seeds. A suspended chair rocks in a slow current. A lamp hangs at the height of a second-story window, though there is no second story anymore, only the shape of one trapped in water. I pass beneath it. Its brass fittings are polished into shining stripes by my kind, and I understand this shine not as beauty, but as use.

This is who I am now: a small blind worker in a drowned industrial garden.

But the self changes as I go. Because my kind is sparse and because the Sump is dense, I sometimes feel like more than a threadlath. When I disturb the water near the upper ceiling of the basin, ring-seeds emit their faint blue rings. They are not mine, and yet they are. One ring. Then another. A current answers. A tiny luminous circle forms where my passage has broken the quiet. If I were to say “I,” perhaps part of that I is the light being disturbed in the dark above me. Perhaps I am the boot and the grazers and the brief blue ring they startle into the water. Perhaps I am the clean lane left on brass.

No. I must not try to be the light. The light is only a response. I am not the boot’s rotation either. The boot rotates, but the rotation is a consequence of my movement and the movement of others. I am closer to the patience inside that consequence. I am the motion that keeps the boot occupied. I am the reason the empty thing is not empty.

Sometimes, when the valve near the lower market line opens and releases its thin stream of colder water, I am carried downward. This happens slowly, by inches or by days, depending on which clock one uses. I enter the industrial lane beneath the shops, where the light becomes almost a rumor and the water feels thick with metal. Here the machines are larger and more tenderized. Iron ribs curve outward from a collapsed wall. Pipes cross like the bones of an old animal that chose, at the end, to become architecture. The waxy bloom is thicker here, folded in soft ripples over brass fittings. My kind crawl over it in patient columns. We are not social in the way cities are social. We do not build, command, remember, mourn. We adhere, feed, move, leave traces. That is enough. The Sump does not ask for more.

And yet, beneath the pumping station, there is the rumor that presses itself against my attention.

I do not go there. My kind do not need to. But when the pump wheel turns, the water tightens in a way that makes the alley walls seem to lean inward, and I flatten against the boot’s seam, or against a pipe, or against a mineral shelf, and for a long pause we are nothing but pale stillness. The pressure wave passes. Somewhere below, the turbine housing may shiver. Something may curl around the broken shaft. I do not see it. I do not know its name. But I know the rhythm it gives to the water. It is too large for me to imagine. It is too slow for me to fear. It is, perhaps, the basin’s heart, if the basin has one. If it has one, I am not the heart. I am the small skin that grows over the heart’s old machinery. I am the quiet thing that grazes where the heart has made a current.

If a careful visitor came through the Sump and looked closely at the boot, they would see nothing. Or almost nothing. They might see the child’s boot hanging in the dark water, leather swollen with cold, laces gone, the buckle green. They might see a pale line moving inside the opening. If they were patient, they might see it disappear into the toe. If they were more patient, they might see the boot turn once by a fraction no larger than a hairline. Then they would have evidence, but not understanding. They would think: *there is no one here.* They would be right about the people and wrong about the place. They would think: *a boot is drifting.* They would be close, and still mistaken. It is not merely drifting. It is being lived in. The boot has become a room, and the room has become a small civic act, and the civic act has become a kind of prayer without language.

Who would I be?

I would be this: a threadlath colony inside the suspended child’s boot, moving so slowly that the boot’s turn appears to be the work of water alone. I would be the blind grazers polishing corroded brass. I would be the faint blue rings disturbed in the upper dark. I would be the clean lane left across an old valve. I would be the small occupation of an abandoned place. I would not be a person, and I would not be a monster, and I would not be a spirit. I would be the Sump’s nearest thing to a self: a population of patience gathered into the shape of a forgotten room.

Where would I find myself?

Not at the surface, though the surface is black water and no sun. Not at the floor, though the floor is broken tile and cold silt. Not at the outer sluice gates, though those ribs of corrosion hold the basin’s breath. Not in the pump house, though its arched windows look like cloudy eyes. Not in the market square, though the iron skeleton of a stall still tells the story of traffic. Not beneath the turbine, though the rumor curls there. I would find myself at the smallest possible place where the old human world has not become myth but has become house.

I would find myself in the toe of a child’s boot.

I would be there with others like me, pale and eyeless, reading the water through our front ends, feeding on waxy bloom, leaving thin bright stripes wherever we go. When the pump wheel turns, we flatten. When the valve opens, we ride the colder thread. When a ring-seed drifts near the boot’s mouth, our movement startles its light, and for a second the darkness marks itself with a blue circle. Then the circle fades, and we continue, and the boot turns a fraction farther along its slow daily route.

If I am asked, at the end, whether I am alive, I would answer carefully, because the Sump discourages ordinary certainty.

I am alive as a current is alive by moving. I am alive as decay is alive when it becomes a surface. I am alive as a room is alive when a forgotten object turns into a dwelling. I am alive as a city is alive after its people have gone, when the water remembers the streets and the machines turn because the place invented a reason.

No one lives in the Sump of Verath.

That remains true.

But I would be living there. Not with a name. Not with a breath anyone could hear. Not with a body that would survive being seen, because being seen is almost the same as being dissolved into description. I would be living there in the way the boot is living: by holding empty space, by letting patience make a home, by turning once per day because something inside the dark continues, because something still cleans the brass, because something still leaves a shine on the old metal and does not need the shine to be noticed.
