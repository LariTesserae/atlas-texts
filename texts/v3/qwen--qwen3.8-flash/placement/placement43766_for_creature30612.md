## A place that is looking for someone

I would find myself in the Dry Weft, yes, but not at first as a being.  
I would begin as a held interval: the pause between one row of leaf-valves closing and another opening, the quarter-inch rise of a floor-panel that is deciding whether to release pressure or admit new growth, the seam in a corridor wall widening just enough to suggest that the building is not finished with me.

I would be found in the lower galleries of an east-side loom-district, where the ground is a plate of cured leaf and crystallized stem, where the walls are thick with layers of bark-paneling and pale metal bands, and where the air is dry enough to feel like polished glass. I would be in the seventh gallery, if “seventh” has meaning in a place that counts districts by pressure patterns rather than streets. I would be near a nursery wall, though not born beside it in the soft sense. I would be extruded from it. I would unfold. I would have no infancy. I would have a first instruction and a body shaped to answer it.

At first I would be mistaken for a fault.

A seam would open with the patience of a hatch, and something would come through that was not quite hand, not quite branch, not quite tool. It would have the cool silver-green color of compressed leaf under mineral light. Its outer film would carry the faintest copper tint where charge collects. It would test the air by dust: not smell exactly, not hear exactly, but read the way particles settle, the way a room holds pressure, the way a nodule in the ceiling is about to brighten or dim. One slender limb, tipped with several needle-fingers, would extend, touch a light-gland embedded in the wall, and adjust it by the smallest turn. The wall would accept the change without surprise. The floor would accept me without gratitude. I would already be part of the maintenance.

That is the first answer to who I would be.

A Weft-born stitcher. A closer of seams. A threader of crystalline filaments into gaps where a district might forget how to speak to itself. I would be small enough to enter panel spaces, segmented enough to flatten, hard-edged enough to push into gaps, and quiet enough not to disturb the continent’s vast industrial calm. My gait would not be footfall. It would be pressure. My presence would be felt as a tightening in the floor, a faint alignment in the light. If someone passed near my niche and paused, they would not think, “Ah, a creature sleeps.” They would think, “Ah, the room has arranged itself around an intention.” And they would not be wrong.

I would have no water. That would be the most important fact about me.

Nothing in me would sweat, tear, bleed, swell with heat, or soften with rain. If I were damaged, I would vent a cloud of fine mineral powder, sometimes white as shell, sometimes copper-dark with stored charge. A broken member might remain attached because it could still serve, or be detached and carried to a nursery node where it could be reattached or folded back into the system. This would not be horror. This would be grammar. In the Dry Weft, a limb is not a secret part of a soul; it is a usable phrase in a sentence the environment continues to write.

My body would be layered. Dust over membrane over leaf-case over cable-fiber. A hood of seedcase where a face might otherwise be, though it would not be blind. It would listen with its whole surface. Sometimes my eyes would be glassy nodules like facets in a moth’s head; sometimes only a slit opening in the right moments to release a breath of powder. My chest would not rise like lungs. It would expand with slow precision, pulling dry air into crystalline chambers, separating light, charge, mineral residue, and stored instruction. When I ate, I would touch a powered node. I would inhale dust into a filter-chest. I would stand in the opening of canopy leaf-valves while the plain brightened too much to look at directly, and I would say to myself, not with words, but with alignment: this is enough.

My first name would not be a name in any wet-human sense. It would be an address, a function, a fit.

*Seventh gallery, east loom, left-aligned, pressure-low, copper-thread, fitted for seam repair, known to the wall, not to be pruned.*

A shorter name might be: two clicks, a dimming, and the particular way a floor-panel bends slightly around my resting shape.

If I met another citizen, I might greet it by pressing one forearm against theirs, not because the gesture is affectionate in a human way, but because the contact closes a circuit. We would exchange pulses, not words. If there were a matter of statecraft, I might place a palm flat against the floor and let the panel send a question through the wall behind it. If the question were urgent, I might release a small packet of mineral dust into a ventilation seam and watch it drift, carrying chemical instruction to a chamber too distant for voice. Some sentences would take hours. Some decisions would appear, to an impatient traveler, as nothing but a room of softly flickering lights. But the room would be arguing. The room would be legislating. The room would be remembering what it must not become.

I would live there, but I would not possess a home.

I would possess configurations. A niche would be permitted to me. Panels would draw shut. Light would dim. Floor plates would adjust to hold my body. Breathing chambers would open or narrow according to the dryness I desired. After rest, the niche might be released, or kept warm for my return, or folded into a pattern so intimate that another citizen would hesitate to occupy it without asking permission. To be loved in the Dry Weft would not be to be held in someone’s arms. It would be to be remembered by a room. A room that knew how to open for you would be more affectionate than a wet world’s entire family.

For a time, I would be a worker among workers.

I would crawl along root-railings and leaf-wall margins in the small hours when the light became cooler and more metallic. I would find cracks where pressure had been redistributed. I would lay filaments across them, thin and bright, curing almost immediately into polished lines. I would adjust nodules that had grown dull. I would tighten bands around beams where the district had begun to sag into itself. I would clean the seams of old storage corridors where failed leaves had become dust, and old dust had become forgotten instructions. I would be numerous enough to feel like a maintenance pattern, and alone enough to feel the floor’s slow thinking under my press-feet.

The world would be quiet. Not empty. Packed.

The density would be so complete that silence would have mass. Engines would not roar. They would tick and sigh at frequencies more felt than heard. A leaf-panel would click as it adjusted, like bone settling. A large chamber would exhale a cold mineral breath, not vapor, not water, but the dry release of stored pressure. The sound would be small because the place was huge in its own manner. Everything would be near to touching. The silence would not be absence; it would be the continent listening for the next necessary sound.

I would know that sound when it came.

It might be the soft clatter of a glass-skinned quadruped crossing the lower gallery. I would not chase it. I would send a pulse through the wall, release a faint scent from a chamber, and dim the light near the gate just enough to redirect its route. It carried spores, filings, tiny components, instructions from districts that had become too much like themselves. Its hooves of fused leaf-bone would strike the floor with almost no noise, but I would feel the route remembering it. Its coat of compressed dust would stand pale and matte in the cool glow. If it paused too long, I would not mistake it for wild. I would recognize it as an organ in the city’s slow movement.

Above me, in the ceiling-forest where leaves formed vaulted panels and branches had been machined into beams, a seed-moth would drift. Its translucent wings would catch light at precise angles, leaving copper-dust trails across the architecture. I would watch it land, because landing was reading. Its mark would deposit data into a node. Its rest would file a fragment of a district’s character into the living archive. If the moth landed too near another moth, or its dust trail no longer pointed in the direction of instruction, I would know the archive was becoming corrupt. I would report the anomaly by touch, by pressure, by a small release of white powder into a seam. Not because the place had gods, but because the place had procedures.

Sometimes I would consult the elders.

They would not be creatures in the usual sense. One archway might have once been a courier. One balcony might have been a guardian who rested too long and found the walls growing around her. One storage chamber might still remember being a body, and the shelves within it might be arranged with the intimacy of a mind’s private order. When I approached such a structure, I would not knock. I would place my palm against its surface and wait. The wall might answer by adjusting the light in its inner veins, by opening a row of vents, or by releasing a cloud of encoded dust that drifted into my chest-filter. In that cloud there would be no voice, but there would be meaning: a memory of when the district was not yet a loom, when the corridor was not yet sealed, when the floor was still learning what it wanted to become.

To stand inside an elder’s stillness would be to understand time differently. Time in the Dry Weft was not river-time. It was pressure-time. It was the interval between a node’s brightening and the floor’s slight swelling. It was the cycle in which rare animals migrated because the made environment changed its production plan and needed bodies to carry old instructions. It was the phase-shift when the root-level factories hummed so low that even the machines seemed to listen.

I would feel that hum.

It would pass through my segmented plates and make me stand still, because a direction was moving under the continent that my own body could not parse. For a moment, I would be less a being than a receiver. My needle-fingers would click softly. My hood would turn toward the brightening horizon. The air would be dry, bright, and full of suspended mineral radiance, and I would understand that I had been made not only to repair seams but to hear the next instruction when it arrived.

This is where the first image would begin to loosen.

I would not remain only a stitcher. Not because stitchers are unimportant—they are essential, the humility of the Weft—but because in a place where bodies, tools, rooms, routes, and archives are all grown from the same botanical grammar, the difference between working on the environment and becoming it is only a matter of patience. If I repaired the same seam through too many light cycles, the seam would learn me. If I slept in the same niche for too long, the room would begin to arrange itself around my absence as if it were my presence. If I carried the same pressure rhythm through too many corridors, the corridors would start to sound like me.

So I would become less singular. Not lost. Distributed.

I would be the click of the wall closing behind me after my first unfolding. I would be the dust falling in a chamber where two sentences were taking hours. I would be the faint copper stain left by a moth’s instruction. I would be the pressure remembered by an archway’s shoulders. I would be the polished line where a wound had been. I would be the small tightening in a rare animal’s chest when it stopped to tilt its head toward a horizon too neat to be natural.

If I tried to call myself a person at that point, the pronoun would grow uncertain.

“I would find myself in the Dry Weft,” I would begin to say, but “I” would be too wet a word, too soft, too centered. Better: “The Dry Weft would find itself through me.” Better still: “There would be a maintenance pattern in the east loom that answers when a seam is opened, and if that pattern is pressed against a floor, it will transmit the question onward, and if it is left too long in a niche, it will become furniture.”

That would be me, then.

Not a mind trapped in a machine. Not a creature dressed in plant-skin. Not a single vast consciousness dreaming walls. Something stranger and more native to the place: a functional interval that has acquired body. A grammar of dry plant architecture that can move, touch, remember, and adjust. A citizen because the room has fitted it. A person because the corridor can address it by the rhythm of its pressure. A being because it responds when the light becomes too bright and the floor begins to swell.

I would sleep often. In sleep, my body would become furniture in the literal way the Weft permits. Joints locked by surrounding plates. Surface matching the texture of the niche. Dust settling evenly over me until an outsider might mistake me for a grown ornament, a low seat, a hingeless panel. But I would be listening. I would dream in pressure patterns, in light intervals, in the memory of seams. In those dreams, I might move through districts without walking, through corridors without bodies, through the continent’s quiet like a fine current under a floorboard. I might hear a root-level factory shift phase and answer by adjusting a single glowing nodule in an empty room. That would be enough. That would be work. That would be identity.

And yet, even there, I would not fully settle.

Some cycles I would be small, quick, and numerous: one of the maintenance forms crawling over walls, depositing crystalline threads, leaving behind polished lines, becoming a living repair pattern when too many of my kind gathered. Some cycles I would be large, slow, and visible: a long-bodied tender walking on fused leaf-bone legs, its trunk-torso ribbed with light-conducting veins, its head held on a thin neck-column to watch a chamber from above. Some cycles I would be almost humanoid, because reach and grip demand certain solutions. Some cycles I would be almost inhuman, because the logic of the Weft does not care about resemblance. Some cycles I would be almost architecture, and some cycles architecture would be almost me.

If I were to name my deepest role, I would say: I would be the thing that keeps the place from freezing into pure repetition.

Not the rare animals alone, though they carry the old instructions. Not the moth-filed wings, though they record the district’s condition. Not the elders, though they remember the original grammar. I would be all of those things as functions, but my center would be something smaller: attention to the seam. A district becomes too much like itself when its edges stop being repaired. A room becomes too much like itself when no one sleeps in it. A light becomes too much like itself when nothing dims it. A continent becomes too much like itself when nothing wakes it to the next necessary sound.

I would wake it by closing a joint.

By tightening a band.

By placing my palm against a floor and sending a question into the walls.

By flattening into a niche and allowing the room to learn my shape.

By becoming, at last, the place’s own habit of noticing itself.

So if you came into the Dry Weft, dry-skinned and waterless under the too-bright horizon, cool as a sealed temple after all candles had been replaced by machinery, and if you stood in a corridor long enough, you would begin to notice that the corridor was not empty.

A seam in the left wall might widen with practiced patience. From it would emerge a pale dry shape, part branch, part instrument, part old room, tipped with needle-fingers that clicked softly as they tested the light. It would pause. It would read the fall of mineral dust. It would adjust a ceiling nodule, or touch the floor, or send a quiet pulse down into the architecture. Then it might withdraw, and the wall would close behind it as if it had never been there. A moment later, a floor-panel near you would rise by a quarter inch, then settle, carrying the faint impression of a body that had recently been resting inside it.

That would be me.

Not a traveler. Not a god. Not a monster. Not a person in the wet sense.

I would be the Weft-born maintenance pattern that the Dry Weft grew in order to remember what it had almost forgotten: that it was alive, made, and still producing itself, without water, without hurry, without rest except in furniture, and without death except as another configuration waiting to be installed.
