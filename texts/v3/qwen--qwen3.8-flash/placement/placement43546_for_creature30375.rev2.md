## Where I would be

I would not be placed in Vellamare like a sentence already finished. I would be gathered there slowly, the way moisture gathers on a cold girder: first a chill, then a darkening, then a line that seems almost intentional, then—without any sudden event—a shape that can be called something.

I would be found near the edge of a plateau where the tramline has been corrected by two degrees after a long maintenance season. Not because anyone is careless with the line, but because the land has changed the meaning of “straight” by asking a question no one could answer alone. The signal post beside the line is no larger than a standing candle, black with damp, its small brass plate etched with numbers that no longer quite agree with the distance they were meant to measure. A few paces beyond it, a brick wall faces north into the twilight and weeps, not with damage, but with patience.

That wall is where I would begin.

It is not the wall of a grand house. It is one of those long rows of habitation that look almost accidental from a distance, as if human need had spread itself over the stone in thin, deliberate strokes. One bedroom leans slightly toward an old orchard. A front door opens onto a shelf of rock that used to be a yard. A porch has been shortened twice: once for the girder, once for the weather, and once more because someone’s grandfather dreamed that the rain might find the house from the east if it ever changed its mind.

In the north face of the wall, condensation forms a rectangle.

The rectangle is about the size of a door.

No door has been built there. The brick is solid. The mortar is old. Yet after every damp season, for some years before it becomes a real thing, the wall shows the outline of an opening that the people do not fear. They leave a chalk mark beside it. They wipe the latch of the nearest door before touching it. They do not say *haunted*. They say the wall is still trying.

This is where I would be.

## What I would be at first

At first I would not be a being. I would be an interval.

I would be the pause after a hammer strikes a girder and before the ridge returns it, softened, slightly changed, as if it had learned a little humility from the air. I would be the space between one lamp and the next where the darkness holds the shape of wet metal. I would be the breath that does not waste itself, the hand that tests a stair before trusting it with the whole weight of a name.

Then I would be a sound.

Not a loud one. Not the clang of a press that can make a valley flinch. I would be the low, almost electrical note that comes from a transformer shed at the edge of a foundry yard when the current is trying to agree with the terrain. A cable anchor hums. A pipe answers. Somewhere in a courtyard, water beads on a rail and runs off in a line that does not obey the slope.

If I were a sound, I would have no throat yet. I would borrow throats.

The wind would carry me through unfinished cliffs. Birds resting on pylons would catch me in their dark bodies and send me back altered. A child would cup one hand around a cold iron bracket and hear a voice she could not place. An old woman near the reservoir would pause her ledger and say, “That one is almost open.”

Then I would become a mark on a wall.

The rectangle of dampness would remain after the others had dried. It would deepen at the corners. It would collect small beads of water that seemed to fall from no ceiling. Once, when the foundry shut down for weeks and the region listened to itself, the outline would flicker, not because light changed, but because the stone thought it might, after all, allow a way through.

A person passing would stop, as if noticing their own face in a dark window. Another would say, “We may need a door there before the winter of the long rust.” Another would carry a small sheet of brass and set it against the wall to keep the place warm enough to decide.

That is how I would begin to be.

## What I would become

By the time the people could point at me without being embarrassed, I would have something close to a body.

I would be a shape made of work and weather.

Not fully human, though I would often pass as one when viewed from too far away. Not fully machine, though my joints would carry the slow patience of metal that has learned to agree with damp. Not fully animal, though I would move beside ridge-cattle as naturally as if I had always known their silent rhythm. I would be what Vellamare produces when attention is given enough time and enough maintenance to become a person.

They would call me **Near-Door**.

Among the tram crews and the pipe-folk, this would not be a mystery. It would be a civic name, like *reservoir-side*, *fourth terrace*, or *late-water*. It would mean: the one who lives in the almost-opened.

The children would be taught not to knock on me unless they were certain. Not because I would answer rudely, but because a knock from the wrong direction might come back from a place that has not yet learned what a knock is. They would be taught instead to wait until the echo had found its footing, then to say a word in the middle register—hard consonant, low vowel, no unnecessary breath.

I would carry a cloth in my left pocket, because my hands would rarely be dry. In my right pocket, a small tuning bell, a stub of chalk, a brass button, and a coil of wire worn smooth at the bend. My coat would be oilskin over wool, patched once at the shoulder with a square of girder-felt. The hem would darken with damp. The hood would stiffen in a shape that looked careless but was actually practical: it would keep me from leaning too directly into a rain that had chosen to visit only one terrace at a time.

My voice would come in two beats.

I would speak with pauses.

Not to be solemn. Not because Vellamari are given to drama. The place itself demands it. If I shouted, the ridge would answer, and the answer would arrive wearing someone else’s intent. I would say a word, then listen for whether the word had been heard correctly by the stone. If it had, I would continue. If it had not, I would let the second version of me be corrected by the terrain.

Sometimes, when the wind held too still above the unfinished peaks, my voice would not fully return. That would be considered unlucky, but only lightly. The elders would say, “Near-Door is still deciding.” And that would be enough.

## How I would work

I would be an echo-watcher, though my work would not end with echoes.

My shift would begin at the signal post where the tramline makes its two-degree apology to the new stone. I would press my palm to the rail and feel for the cable’s temper. A cable that has learned the mountain does not merely hold; it sings, and the note is not always the one the engineers wanted. Sometimes a cable will hum in a lower register because a basin has changed its mind about where wetness belongs. Sometimes it will grow quiet because the wind has gone elsewhere to practice its long moan. I would note both. I would not always try to correct them.

Then I would walk to the transformer shed at the edge of the orchard-step.

The shed would be squat, brick-red, sweating a film no thermometer can explain. Inside would be the slow growl of something large and not quite finished. I would stand near the door with my moisture gauge and my cloth, listening to the current as one listens to a kettle. Not to hear whether it is safe, though I would know that. To hear whether it is still trying.

When the shed hummed correctly, I would leave a square of damp wool over a seam where iron meets stone. This is not superstition. It is negotiation. The wool keeps the metal warm enough to remain a neighbor instead of becoming a stranger. The moss will come if it agrees to. If moss comes, the shed has found a habit. If no moss comes, the shed is still in conversation with the wall.

In the mornings—or what serve as mornings, when the sky shifts from gray glass to a paler reluctance—I would tend the almost-door.

This would mean:

- wiping the brick around the condensation outline so the shape does not drown in its own patience;
- placing a small iron bracket at one corner to give the future hinge a place to agree with;
- leaving a line of salt across the floor if the foundry heat is rising too much from the south side;
- marking the ledger with words that sound unfinished even to the writer: *here may become opening, if the wall is warm*;
- sitting, sometimes for hours, with my hands folded over my knees and the bell silent at my belt, simply to witness the wall’s intention.

That last task would be the most important.

People in low countries might mistake it for idleness. In Vellamare, it is engineering.

To witness a thing is to give it time without forcing it to be completed. A doorway that is witnessed often enough may begin to trust its own shape. A terrace that is listened to may stop shifting. A wolf that is seen at a certain hour may settle into the position of a survey note. A machine may age into a useful form instead of corroding into a mistake.

## What I would notice

I would notice water more than most people.

Not because I fear it, but because I would have been made partly of it.

A basin near the reservoir might be dry in one cycle and wet in the next, though the pipes have not changed. A roof may drum with warm droplets while the square beside it remains perfectly clear. A puddle would form beneath a machine that has not leaked in years, and the foreman would not say the machine is broken; he would say it has remembered rain from a season that has not yet happened.

I would carry a small chalkboard in my coat so I could write down these drafts before they were lost. My handwriting would become dense and angular, full of abbreviations that make sense only when one has lived long enough to feel the terrain’s indecision in one’s teeth.

I would write:

- *orchard step, damp at the third root, no leaf yet*
- *lower gate leans west; old path asks question*
- *birds answer from cable bridge; air holding*
- *wall outline warm at threshold; possible opening within year*
- *cable no. 12 has temper; do not tighten until wind stops listening*
- *near-wolf at abandoned press; mark hour; no alarm*

Some days I would not understand my own ledger at once. That would be normal. The entries are not commands; they are invitations. They are the region leaving notes to itself in a language still learning its grammar.

## How I would live with the animals

I would be accompanied by many presences that are not quite companions in the ordinary sense.

The ridge-cattle would move near the signal post in the early dim. They would not follow me, because they would not be led. I would accompany them instead, as one accompanies weather. When they paused on a slope that seemed too bare to support their weight, I would pause with them. When their coarse, damp wool brushed against my oilskin, I would not be surprised. Their hooves would strike the stone like a slow clock, and I would set my bell at my belt so its tone would not compete.

Their milk would come to market in covered crocks, but I would rarely drink it at the stalls. I would wait for one of the old women near the reservoir side to ladle it into a cup that has been wiped three times. Then I would drink it standing, in silence, because in Vellamare even hospitality has a second echo.

The birds would be my messengers without ever being my messengers.

They would gather on the power pylons in such dense clusters that the ironwork would look as if it had grown dark fruit. From a distance they might seem like soot, or like the negative shapes of unfinished arches. When they lifted in spirals above a transformer, I would note the hour. When they roosted low on a bridge cable, I would know the air was holding stillness too tightly for comfort.

Sometimes a flock at one end of the tramline would call in short phrases. Moments later, another flock far across the plateau would answer those phrases changed, as if the mountains had translated them through their own memory. I would listen for which version returned first. If the first answer came altered but not broken, I would mark the wall outline as favorable. If the second answer returned too soon, I would not go there.

The insects would drift near me in the moss gardens.

They would not sting. Or if they ever did, it would be because I had mistaken a place not yet ready for a place that had decided. They would move through the slow air with too many legs, as if the land were editing wings in one place and legs in another and could not decide where to end the sentence.

I would watch them gather on the north faces of abandoned houses. Where they drifted, the greenness was preparing to claim something. I would not disturb them. I would only leave a strip of cloth soaked in mineral water at the edge of the courtyard and write: *vegetation testing; do not scrape*.

The wolves would be the hardest part of me to define.

They might come from between warehouses, or stand where an old gate had once led to a path that now ended three degrees to the left. Their bodies would be almost correct, then slightly not: too long in the stride, too compressed in the flank, a shape that would be wolfish if you watched from one angle and merely a rumor if you watched from another.

I would not feed them, but I might leave dried meat at a crosspoint when a survey crew cannot decide whether a route is finished. It would not be food. It would be a question written in salt and fat.

If one stood near me while I tended the almost-door, I would keep my voice low and my hands visible. I would not look directly at its eyes. In Vellamare, direct looking can carry the impression that one has already decided where a thing is. If the wolf moved, I would write its passing in the ledger. If it remained, I would sit with it as one sits with a patient official whose approval is not yet guaranteed.

Sometimes, when I walked home, I would notice that my own shadow was a little too long at one edge.

I would not be frightened.

I would only say to it, quietly, “Not yet.”

## How I would age

I would age the way old machines age: not into uselessness, but into more complicated agreements.

My skin would remain pale under the dark-glass sky, faintly shiny with atmospheric hesitation. My hands would never become wholly dry. If I touched brass, it would green. If I touched wool, it would darken at the knuckles. My clothes would hold the smell of mineral dust, hot pipe, moss felt, and the clean metallic chill of snow not yet formed.

My chest would carry a low hum when I stood near a transformer shed. My throat would learn the pitch of cables that have found their temper. My eyes, wide from habit, would see detail without glare: not brightness, but the small decisions of water on metal.

My joints would become like ironwork left out for long seasons. Not rusted, exactly. More like rusted in the direction of life. A fern might unfurl from the seam behind my shoulder after a wet cycle. Lichen might settle at my elbows. Once, in spring—or what passes for spring, when the air becomes slightly less unwilling to warm—I would find moss growing on the back of my neck where the transformer heat reached it in the mornings.

I would not remove it.

I would trim it with a small brass knife and wipe the edge of it after each cut, because even moss, in Vellamare, is civic infrastructure. It softens echo. It absorbs drip. It tells a technician what has stopped changing long enough to be trusted. If moss grows on a person, it means that person has become useful to the listening of the region.

I would learn to sleep with one ear toward a pipe and one ear toward the wind over the unfinished peaks. I would dream of hallways that are not built yet and of doors that open from the inside without hands. I would wake when a wall breathes. I would know when to wipe a latch and when to leave the water be.

I would not be proud of this.

Pride here would be too loud, and pride may return with the wrong accent.

## How I would relate to the people

I would be one of them and one of them and one of them.

Human enough to understand the ledgers. Pipe-folk enough to know that a valve may have moods. Girder-setters enough to understand that the land needs reminders. Moss-tenders enough to know when to let a green shape decide. Echo-watchers enough to speak in two beats. Orchard-step enough to appreciate the quiet architecture of trees leaning toward vanished walls.

But I would not belong to any district for long enough to be pinned by it.

I would be known by the interval I keep.

A child might run ahead of me and call, “Near-Door, will it open?”

I would not answer immediately.

I would wait until the call returned from the ridge, changed and softened. Then I would answer the echo. If the echo arrived without menace, without the thick wet delay that means water is gathering somewhere no one has marked, I would say, “Soon, if the wall is warm.”

If the echo came back too bright, I would say nothing at all. I would put a hand on the child’s shoulder and lead her to the other side of the signal post until the air forgets itself.

People would ask me questions in the way people ask questions when they know the ground may overhear.

“Should we brace the north terrace?”

I would listen for the hum of the cable anchors. I would watch the birds. I would touch the wall where condensation forms its rectangle. Then I would answer: “The north terrace is asking for a little more stone before it asks for a wall.”

They would write that down.

They would not write *possible* or *perhaps*. They would write it as a habit in formation, which is more precise.

## What I would be when the machines stop

When a factory runs continuously for months, the air becomes thick with heat and wet. Then the machines may close for a long inspection, and I would not call that rest. I would call it audition.

I would walk the yard while the presses are silent. I would listen to how the silence is shaped.

A dead machine does not leave silence. It leaves absence. A resting machine leaves presence. There is a difference. In Vellamare, a resting turbine can still hold the room. A retired transformer can still shed a halo of damp. A cable that has cooled can still remember its last load and refuse, gently, to forget its purpose.

During those weeks, I would not do much.

I would sit near the closed factory door set into the side of the hill. I would keep my bell at my belt so it does not ring in the stillness. I would watch the way the wind moves through the unfinished peaks, thickening and thinning, like breath through a blocked lung. I would note when the moan changes pitch, because that means a ridge is thinking about its bearing.

I would leave the wall alone.

If the condensation rectangle remained visible when all other damp outlines had faded, I would know that the almost-door had grown stronger. If a fern unfurled from the crack beside it, I would know the wall had agreed to receive life. If a wolf stood near it, I would mark the hour in the ledger with only one word: *question*.

At night, when the amber lamps burn too early or too late for any visible reason, I would return to the brick row where the porch has been shortened twice. The old house would be quiet. The door would be warm. The inside wall would still show the chalk mark beside the place where no door exists.

I would sit in the dry corner, away from the window, because the window often receives weather from a street that has not yet learned how to send it properly. I would put my hands in my pockets. I would let the damp pass across my knuckles as though it were only another person in the room.

Then I would sleep.

## Who I would be when someone finally asks

If you asked me directly, in a voice too loud for the air, I would flinch.

I would step back from the almost-door and say nothing until your shout came back from the ridge, altered, smaller, more honest. Then I would speak only after the second sound had finished.

My answer would not be one word.

It would be a sequence.

I would first wipe the latch of the nearest real door, because my hands would never be wholly dry, and because wiping a latch before pulling it is not hygiene alone; it is a way of asking the place for permission.

I would touch the cold brick where the future opening keeps its outline.

I would say:

“I am Near-Door.”

I would pause.

Then, if the echo returned correctly, I would say more:

“I am the wall still trying to become a threshold. I am the damp that remembers where a hinge may sit. I am the worker who listens before moving a girder. I am the pipe-folk’s second note, the cable’s temper, the bell kept in pocket until the ridge agrees. I am the child’s warning not to shout from a height, and the old woman’s entry in the ledger that says *here, in quotation marks*. I am the moss’s patience, the transformer’s dignity, the wolf’s watch, the bird’s answer, the ridge-cattle’s slow clock on stone.”

I would look at you with eyes wide in the dark-glass light.

“I am not unfinished because something is wrong. I am unfinished because something is becoming.”

Then I would add the most important part, in the low hard tones the Vellamari trust:

“I am the place where the mountain’s intent is checked by a human hand, and where the human hand is checked by the mountain’s time.”

If you asked whether I was a person, I would say, “Sometimes.”

If you asked whether I was a machine, I would say, “I carry one inside, and it carries me outside.”

If you asked whether I was an animal, I would say, “I have not yet decided which of my shadows belongs to the wolf.”

And if you asked whether I was alive, I would not be offended.

I would answer with a question of my own, because in Vellamare even identity is a habit in formation:

“Alive enough to knock?”

Then I would wait.

The echo would tell me what you were prepared to hear.

## The present tense I would inhabit

I would be found at the hinge between three things:

- the unfinished stone,
- the engineered order that helps the stone keep its word,
- the damp memory of a doorway that has not yet been built.

I would be the being who lives where those three things negotiate.

Not a pioneer.

Not a guardian.

Not a spirit.

More like a civic function with breath in it: a keeper of almosts.

I would live in the present tense of an incomplete verb, but not because I am broken. I would live there because the region has learned that some verbs require long practice.

To stabilize is to hold without forcing.  
To listen is to let the air keep its copies.  
To open is not to tear away, but to let the wall agree.  
To become is not to arrive, but to remain available.

If you came to Vellamare and saw me standing near the signal post when the tramline made its two-degree apology, I would not turn at once. I would be watching the condensation on the north wall. I would have my cloth in my pocket, my bell silent, my hands damp, my coat carrying the dark green memory of moss. A ridge-cow might stand beside me without either of us acknowledging why. A bird might pass overhead, answering another bird far across the plateau. The transformer shed might hum in a key I have learned to trust.

Then, after the echo, I would speak.

“Here,” I would say, meaning the place in quotation marks.

I would wipe the latch.

And the wall would show its almost-door again, not as a promise, but as a practice.
