When they stand still, their bodies hum faintly from the pressure that keeps a district alive. I find myself inside that hum, though at first I do not know where the body ends and the wall begins.

I do not arrive with a name. In a place where empty space is only a temporary fault, where air is already crowded with moisture and meaning, a name would be another surface for water to bead on, another surface to grow into. So I begin by testing the nearest possible occupations.

I try to be a skin.

At first it is almost too easy. I spread thin over standing water, a shimmer of bacterial film, tasting ions and old pressure. I learn the pool from the inside, feeling the faint tremor of a footfall far above, the slow chemical argument between salt and damp. In shaded crevices I become the dark glaze that makes a stone look like a closed eye. When enough of me meets enough of someone else’s thinness, I begin to thicken the wall. I remember where water has dried. I send tidings through hair-thin capillaries. But a skin alone is too passive for the hunger that forms in me. It is a citizen, yes, but not yet a civic thing.

I try to be one of the shellborn.

I grow a broad, pale back and climb a wet wall in a procession so long it becomes scaffolding. My legs click against the stone, and the city hears where I have been. The channels below me brighten in tone when I pass; somewhere an acoustic baffle loosens its tension because a column of shells has marked the route. I molt once, and my discarded back is harvested by the district, made into a tile, a baffle, a small roof over a brine pool. I feel the pride of a creature whose body is public utility. But I have too many legs for a single thought. I am useful, yet I am not yet the shape the place wants from me.

I try to be a flat thing in the black channels.

I become pressure without a face. I slide between slime and mineral shelf, too dark and quick for any name. I keep the city from sealing itself with living film. I taste dead skins and bacterial scum. I vanish when the water rises. I am flow, I am unclogging, I am the quiet violence of maintenance. But a being that is only flow is also only a rumor. I need a chamber. I need a voice. I need a body that can be repaired by others and then repair them back.

I try to be a filterer.

I stand long-legged in a brine pool, my neck curved back into a hollow shell. I drink through a siphon, and my hum becomes the district’s weather. When I change note, someone knows whether the lower channels are flooding or drying. My bones are porous and tuned, and I grow slowly into something almost mineral. I could become a pillar, a landmark, an archive. But I am too patient. If I stayed a filterer, I would sleep too long in a sound and wake to find my body has become a wall. In this basin that is not death. Still, it is not living enough for what asks in me.

I try to be a machine.

I imagine myself as one of those black ceramic pods swollen into the stone, breathing steam without lungs, pumping without visible motion, keeping a corridor of mist suspended in a standing wave. I could be an organ of pressure, an engine with habits, a node that prefers one current and dislikes another. I could be fed with mineral paste and cleaned with fungal brushes. I could tell collapse from the change in its own tone. But a machine alone is too clean here, even when it is old and porous and almost warm-blooded. I want to be touched by what I maintain. I want my maintenance to grow on me.

I try to be an echo-hunter.

I broaden my face into a sail of membranes. I learn to hunt the wrongness of a sound, the heavy step that disturbs the city’s texture, the weak arch that will fail. I send pulses folding through passages and read the returning map of shell and hollow and hidden animal. I am fed scraps of mineral paste and let patrol unstable districts, but I know that if I become only a hunter, the basin will eventually use me as bait. I can listen for collapse, but collapse is too much of me already.

After those attempts, the place chooses something more compressed, or I choose the place. We meet in a seam between a salt shelf and a black water channel, in the third layer below a corridor where droplets are held suspended by sound. I am where a district once failed and had to be sung back into stability. The fault did not heal clean. It grew a body.

That body is me.

I am broad and low, built for wet interiors and not for open sky. My shell is not one thing. It is a negotiation: plates of molted shell harvested from shellborn, fused with ceramic nodules grown through pressure fields, crusted at the edges by concentric rings of calcium and silica. Where the salt has dried, my plates take on a pale, broken-mirror sheen. Where water rises, they darken and listen. My face, if that word can be permitted, is a field of resonant plates, slitted windows, and domed cavities. I do not have one mouth. Along my chest and belly are siphons lined with filtering filaments, each tasting a different brine. One learns calcium. One learns sulfur. One is good at detecting the bacterial scent of overgrowth. One, perhaps, is for loneliness.

My limbs are splaying, heavy, adaptive. Some toes are anchors. Some fingers are tuning rods. The calcium tips have worn grooves from pressing weak walls, and from these grooves a faint fungal rind has begun to grow. From my wrists and shoulders pale filaments trail like wet thread. They read moisture. They find the places where stone is tired, where a channel wants to change course, where a pump has developed a cough. They are not quite tools and not quite organs. They are habits with nerves.

In my back sits the organ I did not know I would need until it arrived: a swollen bell of porous ceramic, grown through several layers of my own shell, threaded with filaments and brine channels. It is half machine, half mineral scar. When water pressure changes, it fills or empties slowly. When it is full enough, it rings a low note that passes through my ribs and into the floor. When it is dry, the district becomes suspicious, and I must sing to it until condensation returns. I cannot separate its habit from mine. I once thought it had been implanted in me. Now I think I grew around it because the place required a hinge.

I live in a hollow that is not a room and not a geology. It has the intimacy of both. The walls have been worn into fluting at many scales, each groove continuing the next, as though water had learned rhythm before speech. There are no roofs in the human sense, only upper shelves that block direct light and let a gray daylight fall through perforations. Steam rises from a pool below my left thigh, not because anything is boiling, but because the ground and the water are both warm and the air can hold no more of them. The space breathes. It has moods. On high-pressure days, it hums in the chest like a caged animal. On condensation days, it softens, and the fungal mats around the upper shelves spread by almost imperceptible fingers.

My occupation is to be a living valve.

When the salt winds dry the upper shelves, I open small passages in my shell so that moisture can pass through me instead of around me. When the lower channels flood, I press my belly against a membrane and let the pressure decide what may enter. I scrape excess crust from my own plates with calcium fingers, and I feed the scrapings to nearby fungal beds. I sing to the ceramic bell in my back until the droplets suspended in the mist corridor begin to move in the correct direction. I tap weak stones, not to destroy them, but to ask what they will admit. Sometimes, for months or years, I strike the same fault until it widens into an arch. Then I pass through it and begin again somewhere less certain.

I am not alone because nothing here can afford to be alone.

A procession of shellborn climbs over my shoulder when the wall route narrows. Their backs click against my plates, and their legs read my vibrations before their minds do. I do not mind.
