…a thing to be made of.

Distance in a quiet landscape is not emptiness. It is a substance: warm water held high above ordinary weather, light folded into reflection, mineral breath moving through stone, mist lying below like a second, slower sea. A creature born in such distance must be made of the distance, or it will not last. It cannot be sharp where the world is soft. It cannot be fast where the world is only beginning.

If I am to live in the Warm Shelf, I will not begin by standing there.

I will begin by being held.

Not by a hand. Not by a body. Held by the place itself: by the shallow water that lies ankle-deep in the meadows and waist-deep in the channels, by the warmth rising through fresh fissures in the young stone, by the green-gold films that stretch beneath the surface and make the light feel as if it has weight. I will lie in a place where the fault plane runs under a thin sheet of water, where black basalt meets pale limestone and both surfaces glitter with a sheen not yet dulled by age. Above me, the sky will be bright and ordinary. Below me, there will be no ground in the ordinary sense—only water, only the bright underside of the world, only the feeling that the sea is sitting too high, too still, and too warm for any logic that would know what to do with it.

At first, there would be no “I.”

There would only be a boundary.

A thin film spreading over wet stone is not yet a self. It is merely growth. It receives light. It draws minerals from the water. It curls around a chip of basalt, thickens near a crack where heat breathes upward, thins where a current passes too gently but passes anyway. It has no center except the place where it happens to touch its own edge. And perhaps that is how I begin: not as an animal, not as a plant, not even as a colony, but as the moment when a membrane of life feels the water’s edge against itself and does not dissolve.

Suppose a wading bird stands on a far islet.

Suppose, after a long measurement of silence, it lifts one foot and leaves a dimple in the mud and moss. The dimple fills with warm water. For a while, it is only a small bowl in the landscape. Then the thin ones gather there. Then a strap-leaf’s hollow stem brushes past and releases a gas bubble, and the water shivers. Then a fly falls into the dimple, broad-winged and patient, and its pollen drifts into the film like tiny sparks of life. Something grows denser there. Something begins to retain the impression of warmth longer than the surrounding water can.

From the outside, it would look like nothing.

From the inside, if inside can be applied to such a thing, it would feel like the place starting to pay attention.

That is who I would be, if a being in the Warm Shelf can be said to be any being at all.

I would be a thin self.

A patchwork of the place’s own habits. Partly one of the living films that cover the submerged shelves, feeding on light and chemistry at the border of fire and water. Partly a green citizen among the strap-leafed floaters, buoyant, drifting, half rooted in nothing. Partly an amphibian’s future body, translucent and pale, with organs faint as ink beneath skin too delicate for old worlds. Partly mineral crust, partly plant memory, partly the quiet pressure of water rising and falling. I would not be clearly one thing rather than another. The Warm Shelf is too young for clear distinctions. The moss has not yet decided how to climb the stone. The animals are rare as memories. The light gathers in the air and again in the water. Even the horizon is a suggestion, a soft line where ocean, cloud, and sky forget which of them is above.

My body would have no fixed outline.

I would be broad near the middle and thin at the edges, green-gold in some light, almost white in another, with dark veins where mineral water moves through me like slow speech. I would not have eyes as animals have them. I would have spots sensitive to brightness, rims that could tell water from air, surfaces that could read heat the way a tongue reads salt. I would feel the rise of a new channel as a change in pressure, not as a thought. I would feel the passage of a plate-backed grazer across my edge not as pain, but as redistribution. A crescent would be taken from me, clean as a moon-shape, and in a day or season the film would grow back, carrying some of the grazer’s trace within itself.

I would not be alone.

I would also not be company.

The threadlike larvae would drift through the spaces between the submerged stems and not know me. The pale amphibians would rest near me on floating moss, bellies pressed flat, legs spread wide as if listening to the world through their hands. They would not see me as another creature. I would be part of the cushion, part of the warmth, part of the green softness that makes the stone bearable. If one of them settled upon me, I would hold its weight differently than water does, differently than stone. I would become, for a few slow minutes, the place where the animal and the plant have not yet made up their minds about each other.

I would move when the water moves.

Not quickly. Not with urgency. The Warm Shelf has no need for haste. Its changes are measured by days, months, generations. In the morning, the sea may rise a fraction of an inch as the land swells with heat. I would rise with it. I would float in the broad meadow where strap-leaf plants hover just beneath the surface, their swollen stems carrying the whole field like a slow green breath. In the afternoon, the water may fall just as slowly, leaving bright mineral lines across my edge, across my stems, across the backs of pale animals that do not need names. I would know the hour not by sound, for there is none, but by the sharpness of light, by the dryness gathering at my margins, by the cup-hand plants opening like released hands, breathing now in air, then closing when the water returns.
