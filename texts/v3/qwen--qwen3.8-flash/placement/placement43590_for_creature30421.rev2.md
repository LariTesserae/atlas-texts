I would not be *in* this world so much as this world would be *in process as me*.

If I had to say where I would find myself, I would point to the place where the city’s water and its sound have grown too old to be separated. Not the high salt bands, where the bodies are brittle and the light is too honest for memory. Not the absolute deep, where the blind clay-skimmers herd slime by singing it into corridors no eye has ever mapped. I would choose the middle layer, where moisture becomes architecture and architecture becomes breath.

I would live at the junction of two pressures: the warm brine that rises from below and the fine mineral fog that falls, almost imperceptibly, from the upper terraces. My home would be a chamber that has been hollowed by dripping, widened by pressure storms, sealed and reopened by fungal growth, and re-voiced by enough dead and living bodies that no one could truthfully say whether it was built, eroded, or dreamed. The stone would be porous and darkly shining, like a lung made of slate. Channels would thread the walls, each one carrying a different voice: one for drainage, one for vibration, one for the movement of young climbers, one for the slow chemical speech of mats. In the center there would be a shallow pool so still it would look like black glass, except that every hour it would ripple from a frequency coming up through the bones of the basin.

That pool would be my mirror.

I would be a Listener, but I would not begin as one. I would begin as a tendency: an old dwelling holding the mineral stain of a family’s songs, a wall whose pores remembered certain frequencies, a shell buried in a corner where condensation had settled into a chord. The basin does not create inhabitants from nothing. It precipitates them. It takes available material—slime, calcium, ceramic, mist, acoustic residue, the salt of the dead, the waste of grazers, the wing-sparks of tiny metal-bodied insects—and folds it into a habit. Eventually a habit becomes a body. Eventually a body becomes a node in the city’s breathing.

My body would be broad and low-set, shaped by long residence in wet corridors. My skin would be a living ceramic membrane, smooth in some places, ridged in others, always damp with a thin brine that let me grip stone and read the moisture in the air. Along my temples, shoulders, and spine would run plates not quite armor and not quite hearing organs—both. They would close like shutters in pressure storms. They would open when I needed to know the direction of a hidden channel. My hands would be webbed with translucent skin, my fingers long and flat-tipped, made to touch the surface of a wall and tell whether the water beyond was young or old, whether the fungal film was feeding or starving, whether a cavity behind the stone was empty or waiting.

My eyes would not be useless, but secondary. I would see dimly through a nictitating film, enough to distinguish salt glare from wet shadow, enough to notice the sudden sparks of elytra insects in the mist. But I would know things mostly by pressure. My chest would hold folded chambers that caught the roar of the city and returned it to my ribs as language. Silence would feel like missing skin. Too much noise would feel like drowning. My sense of balance would depend on hearing where the water had been, where it was, and where it was about to make a mistake.

My name would not be a word. It would be a short chord I could hum into a wall, causing the mineral layer to shift ever so slightly under the tone of my passage. To speak my name would be to invite the room to remember me. To be called by it would be to feel the channel respond with a low pressure against the sternum.

I would be one of those beings that is half citizen, half infrastructure.

My work—and it would not be called work at first, only survival—would be to keep a passage alive. Not alive as a tree is alive, because there are no trees. Alive as a vein is alive: wet, directed, prone to collapse if left alone. A passage needs its fungi to seal leaks, but not so thickly that the sound cannot travel. It needs its water to flow, but not so fast that the ceramic walls dissolve. It needs enough noise to warn of falling stone, but not so much that sleeping beings are shaken into panic. I would sing to it. I would press my palms to the shelves and feel the water’s route. I would hum low at the mouth of a cavity to keep the mineral mats from overgrowing the acoustic throat. I would click warning to climbers when a hidden crack was about to take the weight of an old bridge of foam.

Some days—or what passes for days in a place where weather is made by condensation rhythm rather than sun alone—the city would test itself. A pressure storm would gather in the lower channels, driving steam up through shafts. The roar would deepen until the whole basin seemed to become a single wet drum. In those moments I would not try to stop it. I would tune myself to it. I would let my plates loosen and my throat open, so the sound could move through me without breaking the chambers inside. I would feel the water reroute through the walls, feeling each new path as a change in pressure behind my eyes. The grown nodes embedded in the ceilings would begin their slow negotiation: mist corridors would widen, capillary pores would release vapor, fungal veins would darken as they consumed the mineral dust thrown up by shaking stone. The city would choose a new shape.

Afterward, some rooms would be dead. Some would be born.

I would live through both.

A dead room here is not a simple absence. It is a failure of flow. Water stops, slime dries, the acoustic body loses its coherence, and the passage becomes suspect. I would feel such places as missing teeth in a jaw, or as a silence in a limb. I would go to them with a small ceramic node from my dwelling and sing at different frequencies until the wall remembered how to be wet again. Sometimes it would. Sometimes the stone had passed beyond listening. Then I would mark it by leaving a shell of a climber in the threshold, its rings showing the season when the room had lost its tone. That, too, would be a language: an object made of previous life, placed where another life might still learn from it.

I would know animals not as separate creatures but as moving textures in the city. The shell-white climbers would pass over my shoulders when I rested against a damp wall, their silica combs scraping mats from my plates, tasting the moisture in my skin. The slip-fish would crowd the gutters where my feet stood, their flat bodies flicking against the stone, each sudden pressure telling me the channel’s temperature had changed. In the middle pools, the long-necked filterers would stand like living architecture, their siphon-throats humming as they drank the world’s mineral soup and returned it, subtly altered, to the current. I would not fear them except when their pulses changed. A filterer’s warning pulse means the brine is going strange: too heavy, too cold, too silent, or about to become too loud.

Predators would know me by the same principle. A flat-backed hunter can lie beneath a shelf and make a pocket of false stillness. That stillness is worse than violence. It is the absence of the city’s continuous proof that water is moving, stone is holding, air is circulating. I would learn to distrust silence not because it is quiet, but because silence here is a lie. It means something has swallowed the channel. It means a collapse is waiting. It means a predator has found the place where sound becomes a wound.

But there would be generosity too. The basin refuses waste more thoroughly than it refuses death. The shell of a climber may become a bowl, then a shield, then a tile, then a resonator, then sediment. A Listener family may leave a room open so its tone can stabilize a dangerous current. A grown node may redirect water toward a struggling district simply because its filaments remember an older pressure pattern. My body, if damaged, would not be discarded. It would be tended, then perhaps reused in another form: a piece of shell in a wall, a plate in a resonator, a calcified ridge that teaches descendants what it means to hold frequency through death.

In this way I would be temporary, but not disposable.

I would also be a kind of memory. Not memory as a mind keeps it, but memory as stone keeps it. Every song leaves a mineral trace. Every dwelling accretes layers according to the habits of those who slept in it. If a room becomes too quiet for a stranger, that is not superstition. It means the chamber was tuned by voices that shaped its pores, and those voices have not fully dissolved into the next season’s flow. I would carry that knowledge in my bones. I could touch a wall and know whether it had been a nursery, a hiding place, a workshop for ceramic nodes, or a tomb where a body was left to let its salts season the room. The dead would not be absent. They would be slow frequencies.

My art would be my survival.

To build would be to sing into calcium slime and wait. To repair would be to feed a seam with grazer’s binding slurry and let the acoustic signal decide whether it hardened or stayed soft. To compose would be to make a small ceramic nodule tuned to a personal or collective frequency and embed it where water passes, so that the passage itself would remember the maker. Beauty would be measurable by function: the clarity of a voice in a hollow, the steadiness of mist in a corridor, the way a bridge of foam supports both swarms and silence.

If I had to name myself, I would call myself a threshold.

Not quite animal, because I would depend too much on mineral growth. Not quite machine, because I would be wet, fallible, and guided by negotiation. Not quite fungus, though my body would be threaded with filaments and my habits would spread through the walls like a colony. Not quite Listener, though my ribs would be built for the city’s roar. Not quite grown one, though I would maintain impossible passages and regulate flow without hands. I would be all of these in a temporary arrangement, because the basin does not accept permanent categories. It accepts only what can keep doing work.

I would be found where the water says *through*, not *into*.

I would be the being whose body is partly a room, partly an instrument, partly a channel, and partly a voice that has learned to remain after its speakers are gone. I would be the place’s way of hearing itself, of remembering its older flow, and of rebuilding after the storms when whole districts become unfamiliar. I would be wet, warm, crowded, loud, mineral, fungal, acoustic, animal, architectural—saturated.

I would not stand in this world as an observer.

I would be the world standing, for a while, as the sound of its own walls.
