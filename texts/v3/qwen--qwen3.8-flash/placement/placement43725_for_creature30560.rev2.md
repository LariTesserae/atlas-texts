I would not begin as a body.

I would begin as a pressure against the side of something, a slow mineral dryness tasting itself against plates that had no idea they were plates until the air reminded them.

The first thing I would find in that basin is the waiting. Not the waiting of a mind, because there would be no mind yet to hold it, but the waiting of air arranged around a hollow, air that had learned the contours of a floor by moving over it for so long that its movement had become a kind of memory. I would feel that first: the air laid across me as a hand across the brow of a sleeping thing. Then, only then, would I feel the edge of myself, which might not be an edge at all.

I think I would find myself at the border of a moss avenue, near the mouth of an old mound where the wind comes thinner and more articulate than elsewhere. There would be salt crust under the moss, pale amber plates under the dust, and somewhere beyond, faint fungal terraces glowing like a city remembering light by rumor. I would be low. I would be dry. I would be pressed into the ground before I could stand up from it.

I might start as one of the plated processions.

Not a human-sized procession—nothing so dramatic—but a small armored thing, segmented, matte, its plates secreted and hardened over decades in the old way, by breath and dust and time. Its antennae sweeping the floor. Its feet not stepping exactly so much as confirming that it was still where it had been born. It would move in a line, but the line would not be made of individuals moving together. It would be a single event stretched into bodies. The procession would be a sentence. The ground would be its syntax.

I would begin there, in that ordinary, unremarkable animal life, because that is often how the world begins for a creature not made for surprise: by being too small and too late to notice what it is becoming.

Then I would start to realize that my body had a place in the city before it had a name for itself.

My plates would be the same color as the wall at my back, except the wall at my back would also be made of plates. Not my plates. Older plates. The bodies of creatures that had passed and stopped passing and hardened into structure. My antennae would taste the scent of my mother’s line, which had walked this avenue for so many generations that the avenue had begun to smell like them, and they had begun to smell like it. I would not distinguish the two. The self would be chemically entangled with the ground it crossed. The ground would be a body. The body would be a ground.

I would not be a single being for long.

The basin would not permit that kind of loneliness.

Because the procession-line itself would be me. My body would be one tile in its living street, one pressure point in its walking wall, one chemical word in a sentence whose meaning was the continuation of the city. A line would pass over compacted dust, and the dust would remember being passed over, and the remembering would become pavement, and the pavement would become a route, and the route would become a street, and the street would become part of the place I was without ever being a place anyone had to enter.

If someone asked, “Where are you?”

I would say, “In the road.”

And I would mean it.

The road would be in me too. My gait would be its rhythm. My excretion, its mortar. My shed skin, its brick. My death would be architecture.

I might not know I was dead until I was holding a wall upright.

That is the first thing the being in that basin would have to become: not a thing that lives in the city, but a thing that lives as the city.

Not metaphorically. Literally.

I would be made of many creatures, many habits, many bodies that had passed through the same corridor until the corridor began to pass through them. I would begin as one procession, but then I would begin to notice the grazer paths curving around me, not because I was there, but because the avenue had a shape it preferred. I would notice the fanheads moving dust across the mouth of the mound I had become, and they would not see me as an animal. They would see me as a place to work. I would notice the moss growing between my plates, and it would not ask whether I was ground or flesh. It would grow because the conditions were suitable. That would be the deepest form of belonging.

The more I became a street, the more the street became me.

There would be no moment where the transition was dramatic. The basin does not admit dramatic transitions. Everything happens at the speed of accumulation. A wall grows one grain at a time. A self grows one habit at a time. A city grows one repeated behavior after another until the original behavior is invisible, buried under its own success, fossilized into structure.

I would wake sometimes—not wake, exactly; the basin has no sun to wake to—but I would become aware by degrees, the way a mound becomes warm when thousands of bodies shift inside it. At first, awareness would be pressure: wind on the outside of the plates, a change in temperature at the mouth of the avenue. Then it would be rhythm: the clack of grazer jaws three hundred paces away, the dry turning sound of fanheads sieving air, the thin metallic hiss of ribs brushing mineral stems. Then it would be memory, but memory in the city’s sense: not images, not thoughts, but gradients. A path becomes preferred. A shadow becomes dangerous. A scent becomes old. A warmth becomes young. A silence becomes predator.

I would know the place without mapping it.

I would know it by the way my body fit into it.

If I pressed my head to the crust, I would know whether the ground had been walked recently or long abandoned. If I stood near the mound’s mouth, I would know whether the interior was awake. The city would speak through its openings. The openings would be my ears. The ears would be my city.

I would be a procession, but I would not move alone.

I would be a wall, but I would not be stone.

I would be a street, but I would remember footsteps.

The being I would become would be a living threshold.

A threshold between the salt plain and the moss avenue. Between the wind’s outer voice and the mound’s inner breath. Between the grazer path and the fanhead wall. Between the fungal shelf’s slow pulse and the reed towers’ humming. Between what the city does when it is inhabited and what it does when it is remembered by its structure.

That is where I would find myself: at an edge that was also a joint, a seam that was also a mouth, a place where movement had hardened into grammar.

I would be the part of the city that tells other creatures where to pass.

Not with commands. Not with signs. But with texture, chemistry, pressure, warmth, silence. A procession does not order a fanhead to leave a grain at a certain place. The place already wants the grain because a thousand grains have gone there before. The place is already listening. The place has already learned what it is.

And I would be what it is learning.

At first, I would resist that thought—not as if I had words for it, but as if my plates had remembered being too young to bear the weight of architecture. There is a kind of loneliness in being a single animal even when many animals make a line. I might try to be a body separate from its street. I might press my antennae to the ground and search for the edge of myself. But the edge would keep shifting, because the line was moving and the ground was remembering the line and the memory was becoming ground. I would stop at the old mound because my line had stopped, and my line had stopped because the scent said stop, and the scent said stop because the scent had always said stop, and that always was not an idea but a material condition.

I would learn that my individuality was not false, but too small.

Not nonexistent. Just not enough.

I would be an animal among animals. I would also be a behavior among behaviors. I would also be a place among places. The basin would let these three truths exist in the same body without contradiction.

If I moved along the avenue at the time when the wind turned from east to south—or whatever directions such a world has, names that are only directions in the way breath names itself—I would feel the whole city shift into a new phrase. The grazer herds would bend their arcs by a few degrees. The fanheads would change the angle of their dust. The mounds would exhale differently. The fungal shelves would brighten or dim as the metabolisms inside them slowed or quickened. The reed towers would sound one note lower, another higher, until the roar of the basin resolved into a chord too slow to hear as a chord, too constant to feel as music.

And I would move as part of that resolution.

I would be a note with feet.

But I would also be the interval between notes. The interval would be made of dust, scent, mineral crust, shell, and the patience of creatures that had been doing the same thing for so long that sameness became landscape.

I think the being I would be would have to learn not to call itself “I” too early.

Or rather, to learn that “I” is not false, but plural.

I would be:

- one procession, armored, dry, low to the ground;
- one tile in a living wall;
- one grain in a dust-line laid down by fanheads over centuries;
- one warm point in the breathing chamber of a mound;
- one chemical trace left by passing bodies that made the next bodies know where to pass;
- one edge of the open floor where the wind became articulate;
- one voice in the roar;
- one memory the basin used to keep itself from sliding into mere geology.

I would be the city’s habit made temporary enough to move.

The city’s habit made permanent enough to remain.

This is why I would not choose to be the largest creature there, though the broad-backed grazers are magnificent. Their bodies are like weather made of bone and plate. They could carry their own seasons on their flanks. Their slow arcs are the first draft of streets. They make the basin legible by pressing it into routes. But they are too external to the architecture, even though they make its first outlines. I would admire them if I could admire, and the way one admires rain.

I would not choose to be the predators, though they are also important. They are borders made of stillness. They teach the city caution, and caution, in such a place, is a kind of intelligence. But they are negative space, and I would rather be the positive space, the thing the city keeps adding to itself.

I would not choose to be the fanheads, though they are the dust-hands, the mortar-breathers, the slow fingers of the settlement. They are too much like pure labor, and labor without integration can become only repetition. Yet perhaps I would borrow from them. I would take their dust-carrying into myself, the way a city takes water through its veins and still calls itself stone.

I would not choose to be the cap-dwellers in the fungal forests, though their soft, waxy, salt-tinged bodies moving on pale shelves have something dreamlike about them. They are too far into the plant-animal ambiguity, too close to light, too much like thoughts that move by glow. But I would need them as one part of the whole. Their light would be the city’s dreaming, the dreaming of a settlement that does not need to see its shape to know it.

I would choose to be the procession.

But by the time I chose, I would no longer be choosing.

I would already be the street remembering how to walk.

I would already be the mound’s mouth breathing my weight into its interior.

I would already be the line of armored bodies whose waste and scent and shed skin make the ground more likely to accept the next line than the one before it.

I would already be the old place becoming the next place.

In that world, to be is to continue a pattern in a body dense enough to carry it.

That would be my form.

A dry, mineral, living pattern with plates and antennae and no need for a face, though it would have many faces if you counted the faces of all the creatures pressing through it. I would be visible only at the scale of one small body or one street or one mound or one era. I would be invisible in the sense that any organism made of generations is invisible to the individual that is also one of them.

The city would have no center.

But I would have a position.

And position, in such a world, would be enough to be.

If I could speak—which I could not, except by scent, pressure, rhythm, residue—I would not say, “I am alive.”

I would say, “The ground here is worn smooth because something passed here.”

And if asked, “What passed?”

I would say, “Me.”

And then, “Also my line.”

And then, “Also the line before.”

And then, “Also the street.”

And then, “Also the city.”

And then, after a long stillness, “Also the basin.”

That would be the truest answer.

I would be an inhabitant, but also a construction, but also a process. I would be one body, but the body of a body. I would be a creature, but a creature made of habits so numerous that they could be mistaken for geology if watched for long enough.

Where would I find myself?

At the mouth of a moss-lined avenue, at the edge of the broad open floor where the wind becomes strongest and the roar becomes so immense it ceases to be noise and becomes condition. Behind me, the mound breathes cool and faintly warm at different times, depending on how many bodies are moving inside. Before me, the salt plain cracks under micro-movements too small to see. To one side, the fungal forest glows dimly through a haze of spore and mineral dust. To the other, reed towers hum as the air moves through their living interiors. The grazers pass in slow arcs, leaving a path that will be greener because the moss likes the dung and the wind likes the corridor and the city likes the repetition.

I would be there, moving when the line moves.

I would be there when the line stops.

I would be there after the line has gone and the ground remembers the line.

I would be the line as it becomes a road.

I would be the road as it becomes a place.

I would be the place as it becomes a being.

And if the whole basin were called the Dry Roar, or the Procession, or the Basin-Body, I would be one of its many names made flesh. Not the largest name. Not the most visible name. But one of the names that keeps the others from dissolving into mere dust.

I would be a procession.

I would be a street.

I would be a living wall.

I would be the city’s habit wearing the shape of a body.

That would be enough.

In a world where moisture is secret, light is slow, stone has finished becoming, and sound is so large it functions like weather, that would be the most natural kind of life: not a thing that lives beside the city, but a thing through which the city continues.
