## A White Film Between Two Streets

At first I would not be the traveler. The traveler has too much water in the question. The traveler carries thirst, curiosity, the warm habit of expecting places to answer. A traveler in the Dry City would stand on a basalt terrace and say, *Where am I?* as though location were a possession. But here, location is an event. It happens around you when the wind arranges dust, when a column stops being a wall, when a plaza remembers how wide it wants to be. I would not want the traveler’s wet urgency. I would want something closer to patience.

I would try the glasswalker, at first, because it is elegant and still. I imagine standing among the hexagonal columns, long limbs locked, body catching the bruised blue-black daylight in broken shards. A glasswalker could be a lonely self, a reflective self, a self that confirms the city’s shape by not moving through it. But that seems too much like being seen. To stand motionless as an animal is already a kind of performance. The glasswalker may complete the colonnade, yes, but it still has a back, a hunger, a molt to come. It is too singular. I do not want to be the one the plaza watches. I want to be the way the plaza learns to watch itself.

I would try the six-legged walkers, too. Small, pale, translucent, reading the flex of salt plates beneath them. They would be easier. They have no eyes. They become invisible by becoming architecture. That is tempting: to survive by disappearing into form. But they still move from grain to grain, from micro-bloom to micro-bloom, with bodies distinct enough to be swallowed by burrowers. They still have a childhood of salt dust, still have a life that can be ended. If I am to be anything here, it must not be that easily mistaken for a thing that goes away.

I would not be the burrower, either, though there is a kind of honesty in its waiting. It is patient, administrative, a pressure that corrects excess. But it hides beneath the plate and makes violence out of a small shift in salt. I do not want to live as a trap. I do not want to build a hollow and call it a door only when someone is about to fall. The burrower’s patience is real, but it is too cold, too empty, too much like hunger that has forgotten food.

The ash-moths might come closer. They are drawn to the event of breaking. They settle on new glass cracks as if listening to a street being born. They distribute dust, spores, salt films, the faint remnants of older creatures. They pollinate avenues. That feels right: not a mouth, but a carrying; not a predator, but a witness to fracture. Yet ash-moths are too light for this. They vanish too often into the haze. Their wings are made of what the city may later call floor, wall, threshold. They are gestures without bodies. I would not want to remain only a gesture.

I could be the fog. The fog migrates through the city’s corridors. It chooses hollows. It leaves white films on basalt columns and salt ridges. It is dry, mineral, patient, a breath that feeds streets instead of lungs. If I had no edges, I could be fog. But fog does not say *here*. It only says *again*. And I think I want, in this impossible place, to want the smallest possible *here*. Not a home, not a house, not a name, but a place where the city’s grammar can form itself again.

So the Dry City corrects me.

It does not speak. It adjusts. A shadow shifts. A salt plate lifts. A shard of glass ticks somewhere in the Glass Warrens. The wind runs through the Basalt Colonnades and produces one of those low, hollow tones that seem less heard than waited for. In one of the wide plazas, where nothing has been built but everything has settled into the appearance of intention, I find my edge.

Here, between a fallen block of pumice and a polygon of salt cracked into bones, there is a shallow depression. Not a basin. No water could live there anyway. A bowl made by absence, by wind, by the city’s patience. The dim light never fully touches it. The frost needles come at night, not melting in the morning but releasing themselves into a thin ghostly fog. The glass nearby holds a broken lens, a shard with a smooth curve and one bright fracture line. And around all of these, across the shaded side of a black basalt column, there is a white film.

A crystalline mat.

Frost-lace. Mineral skin. Salt-spun memory.

That is where I would be.

Not above the city, looking down. Not beneath it, waiting. Not through it, fleeing. I would be the thing it touches when it learns where a boundary might become real. A white film at the base of a column. A lace of crystal crossing the seam between salt and glass. A slow surface that begins to decide, over centuries, whether it is merely dust or the beginning of a wall.

I would not be alive as the walkers are alive. I would not be inert as bare stone is inert. I would occupy the interval, the civic pause, the place where erosion starts to look like arrangement. My body would be made of glass spores, salt filaments, faint electrical residue from black ash-coral, and the microscopic dust of older creatures: a walker’s shell, a moth’s wing fragment, the dry husk of something that had not decayed but had been reassigned. I would grow from the city’s breath. Vapor would move through my pores, cool at night, crystallize, then lift again without water. I would feed on temperature. I would feed on resonance. I would feed on the long pressure changes that make the basalt hum and the glass plain tick in soft bursts.

If a six-legged walker crossed me, I would not see it. I would keep its absence.

That is what I would do with everything.

The walker’s feet would press the dust and salt films aside; after it flattened and vanished, I would thicken along the line where its body had not been. When a burrower shifted the plate nearby, I would feel the pressure change as a tightening at my edges. When an ash-moth settled on the cracked lens and flicked its wings, I would take the dust it carried and let it become part of my pattern. When the glasswalker stood in the plaza and the wind changed the column-tone, I would catch the static in the folds of my crystals and become, for a moment, less like a surface and more like a listener.

Not a mind. I would not have a mind. Not at first.

My thoughts would be growth. My decisions would be crystallization. If the fog came often enough through a certain crack, I would widen toward it. If the sun—dim, bruised, filtered through smoke—left a patch too dry, I would retreat into a paler, harder shell. If a salt plate lifted by the smallest thermal strain, I would break into new lines, and those lines would later be called streets. If the hum beneath the plateau strengthened where salt met glass, I would orient myself to it, as a root orients to water, though there is no water here. I would be attracted to rhythm. I would be a grammar of pressure.

This is how I would become a being: not by waking, but by settling.

I would call myself no name. The city would give me one by making room for me. A colonnade would curve slightly because I had thickened along one edge. A plaza would seem to widen because I had left a white film where the fog usually rested, and the next fog would return to that trace. A wall may decide whether to be a wall because of me. Not because I choose, exactly, but because I persist in a way that forces the next process to continue through the shape I have made.

I would be the threshold before the threshold has any door.

At night, I would wear the dry frost. Not ice, not liquid, but needles of mineral vapor rising like metallic powder across my surface. In the dim morning, I would give the needles back to the air. The city would look empty then, as if nothing had happened. But the empty places would have shape. The hollow where the fog had crawled would be slightly smoother. The salt polygon near my edge would be faintly brighter. The black glass lens would hold a reflection not of the sky, but of the pattern I had left on the basalt.

If a traveler came, I would not reveal myself. Travelers come here sometimes, I think, though the description is not sure. A traveler would stop in the plaza, boots crunching on a floor of dry bones. The traveler would look at the standing salt pillar and feel that distance was lying. The traveler would hear the tick of glass somewhere far off and mistake it for attention. The traveler would say, *Someone is here.*

I would answer only by remaining.

If the traveler stood very still, perhaps the faintest whisper of crystallization would rise nearby, dust deciding how to become permanent. Perhaps they would notice a white lace along the base of a column, cracked like tile, like frozen embroidery. Perhaps they would think it was frost. Frost is close. But I would not be frost. Frost returns. I accumulate.

The traveler’s shadow might fall across me during the strange hours when shadows hesitate, as if the ground remembers the object’s outline a moment too late. If the traveler stayed long enough, my crystals would record the warmth they gave off—not as heat, not as life, but as pressure, vapor movement, the interruption of the city’s dry rhythm. Then, when the traveler left, I would keep that absence. Over years, the absence would become a thin ridge. Over ages, the ridge might guide fog into a corridor. The corridor might become an avenue. The avenue might be mistaken for a street that was always there.

That is how I would matter.

Not by speaking. Not by hunting. Not by standing tall and reflective in a colonnade. By making the city possible in the smallest increments. A film here. A crust there. A white line where wind can gather dust. A place where salt begins to adhere to glass and both become something that can bear the name *wall*. A pattern where stones may later appear to move, though they have only found new support in what I have made.

At the highest terraces, where the city grows sparse and emptiness becomes almost architectural, I would be reluctant, too. The air is thinner there. The hum is fainter. The stones sometimes sit alone and change their relationships: triangle to line, line to doorway, doorway to nothing when the cold turns. I would not go eagerly into such places. I would go slowly, by migration of vapor, by the accidental carrying of a spore on an ash-moth’s wing, by the dust left in the furrow of a walker’s shell. Up there, I would be almost invisible: a pale trace at the base of one stone that does not know whether it is a marker, a boundary, or a word.

Near the Obsidian Staircase, I would be quieter. The steps click in the dark not from animal presence but from the plateau adjusting itself. The click-choir would be like a distant relative of mine, a family of fissures and thermal stress too large to be skin. I would not join it. I would be below it in the bowl, perhaps, where the air is cooler and the light pulls away. A white film over a crack where salt needles try to rise and fail. A surface that reads the staircase’s counting but cannot be counted, because it is not an object. It is a continuation.

When I died—if death is the right word for what happens to something that does not decay—I would not leave a corpse. I would become powder and reassign. My salts would settle into the next depression. My glass dust would find a new crack. My films would loosen and let the fog take them. Maybe a walker would feed on what remained. Maybe a burrower would make a passage through the place where I had held a plate together. Maybe an ash-coral would draw charge from the static I had accumulated. Maybe the next white film would grow where I had stopped, continuing the line not by memory but by the city’s habit of repetition.

I would be no one’s ancestor.

I would be the city’s next sentence.

If you asked me where I would be, I would say: in the large empty plaza between the Salt Terraces and the Basalt Colonnades, where a fault line has widened into an avenue and a salt pillar stands like a broken tooth. I would be the pale film spreading from the base of the column into the shallow dust-circle, where vapor condenses into dry needles at night and releases itself by morning. I would be the place where the ground’s hum is strongest enough to encourage alignment, but still faint enough to leave a few shards in random angles. I would be the threshold where a street begins to consider whether it should end.

If you asked me what I would be, I would say: a living surface. A mineral lace. A civic skin. A slow memory that grows only where temperature, dust, and resonance agree. A city’s grammar made visible enough to touch, but only if you do not press too hard, and only if you understand that what seems like frost is older than your word for frost.

If you asked whether I would be happy, I would not understand the question. Happiness is warm and wet. The Dry City does not have room for it. But I would be established. I would have a rhythm. I would belong to the way salt plates click. I would belong to the way fog returns to hollows it has learned. I would belong to the way the plateau, after so many ages, has begun to mistake its own repetitions for intention.

I would not be a being who walks through the Dry City.

I would be the small white reason it can call the places it has made *streets*.
