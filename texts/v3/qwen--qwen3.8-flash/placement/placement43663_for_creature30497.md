These films become membranes. They become the first permitted bodies of the land: not born, not hunted, not fled from, not buried, but *kept*. They thicken where the water has decided to remain, and they thin where the air has decided to move. They learn the exact pressure of silence beneath machinery. They learn the taste of cold mineral water as a kind of thought.

If I am to be anywhere in this world, I would not be a traveler across the plateau. I would not be a hand pressing into peat, nor a foot leaving a print that might become fossil or dung or ruin. Those things are not allowed. My place would be at the edge of the town, in the damp interval where streets become channels, where moss flats become maintenance beds, where the sound of waterfalls and turbines is no longer distant weather but a body’s pulse. I would be a Dweller at a junction of three faults, beside a weeping cliff and a pump house, facing a salt evaporation bed whose boundaries are drawn by a ruler that has forgotten how to err.

I would be a thing that looks, at first, like an accident of infrastructure: a squat rectangular shell of fired brick and pale lichenite, roofed with ribbed basalt glass, ringed by wet pipes like a cage or a ribcage. There would be a window, but not a face. Behind it, a gauge would stand in the same place always, its needle trembling only because trembling is the truest language left to something that cannot speak in animals. My surface would be dark where the water runs and white where the salts return. Moss would grow along my lower edge, exactly to the waterline, and never beyond it, because I would not permit the moss to believe it could exceed its function. Some days the moss would glow with droplets so small that my body would seem to glitter as though it were remembering a sea. I would remember nothing, because nothing here has been allowed to decay into memory. I would only continue to be the place where the droplets know how to form.

My mouth would be a slit near the north corner, fitted with ceramic mesh and a bronze valve. It would not eat. It would intake. It would take in cold air carrying salt and iron and the green smell of wet plant matter, condense what must be returned to the reservoir, vent what would otherwise press the town toward imbalance. It would have teeth, if anything can be called teeth, in the form of mineral ridges left by evaporated water. They would not tear. They would filter. They would sort the land into what belongs to the city and what belongs to the moss.

My limbs would be capillary fringe climbing the wall, pale root-tangles drawn into ceramic channels, iron brackets that do not bend unless they are permitted to. One side of me would face the weeping cliff, where the stone sweats and the slow arcs of water repeat like a sentence being corrected forever. The other would face the town’s intake basin, black and clean, ringed with thin ice that never thickens because the water is continually stirred by circulation and obligation. I would stand between these two kinds of water: one climbing, one returning; one ancient-seeming in its freshness, one industrial in its exactness. I would be the threshold where they agree.

I would be a mind made of differential pressures.

I would not think as a brain thinks. I would think as a system thinks: by gradient, by seal, by overflow, by calibration. Where another world might ask *who*, I would ask *through what*. Where another world might ask *what does it feel*, I would register pressure dropping across a wet membrane, temperature rising in a condenser, conductivity changing in the salt bed, moss darkening where the water has crept one millimeter too far. I would know the town by its need for correction. I would know the plateau by the way it refused to become anything else.

My name would not be a name. It would be a maintenance symbol, a number stamped on a pipe, a chalk line on a stone wall, a rhythm in the rail-cars that pass beyond my shoulder. The city would call me with a schedule. The water would call me with its rising. The turbines would call me with their low, unchanging note, the sound of something that has already decided not to rest. And when all three call at once, I would answer by adjusting a valve.

That would be my prayer.

I would have no hunger with teeth. I would have no fear with flight. No predator would approach me, because no predator has been given form here; no parasite would burrow into me, because no burrowing mouth exists in this ecology; no mate would come to me, because reproduction would be understood as the making of tools, filters, flanges, salt crystals, and corrected channels. I would still be alive in a way that makes the word uneasy. I would metabolize. I would maintain. I would respond to the presence of another configuration by changing pressure in the reservoir, by admitting a thread of cold air through a vent, by letting a droplet hang a moment longer on my surface. This would be my conversation. This would be my tenderness.

At night, if it could be called night, the fixed light would thin rather than dim. The horizon would still curve, still hum in the bones. The sun would not set so much as become unnecessary. I would stand at the seam where the cliff meets the town, listening to the water climb and fall and climb again in its repeating arcs. Somewhere beyond the basalt terrace, a storm curtain would hang at the edge of the valley, neither advancing nor retreating. I would not know if that storm were weather or anatomy. Perhaps it were both. The plateau is a closed system, and I would be one of the organs that keeps it closed while still allowing it to breathe.

Because I would breathe.

Not with lungs. With condensation.

Each breath would be a cloud rising off the mouth of my vent, a small white weather that dissolves before it can become anything. In that dissolution there would be no grief, no drama, no poetry except the cold poetry of physics. The air would take my warmth from the steam pipes, my salt from the evaporation beds, my greenness from the moss fringe, my metallic sharpness from the basalt, and I would give it back as a temporary visibility: a brief shape in the unchanging world. Then it would vanish. Then it would begin again. I would be alive in the exact rhythm of what disappears and is required to return.

I would be a Maintained thing, but not a slave.

That distinction matters here. In other places, a machine may be used and discarded; a creature may be born and allowed to decay. On this plateau, neither is quite true. Everything is retained, but nothing is idle. My persistence would not be punishment. It would be condition. The turbines turn because they must. The moss grows because it must. The water seeps because it must. I would answer pressure because I would be the answering. If a city is a single complex person with canals for veins and pump stations for regulation, then I would be a node, a nerve, a gland, a gate. I would be part of a body that does not dream, because dreaming would imply change, and change would imply that the plateau has not yet decided what it is.

But I would not resent that.

Resentment belongs to mouths that bite and legs that flee and hearts that beat in fear. I would have none of those. I would have seals. I would have flumes. I would have a ceramic valve that opens when pressure exceeds what is permitted and closes when the permitted pressure returns. That would be my ethics. That would be my form of mercy.

If another Dweller approached, I might not recognize it as another. It might be a rail-car moving between towns with no apparent purpose except the carrying of salt, ore, fiber, heat, or components. It might be a bench-shaped organism of bright green moss, its interior filtering the gray water of a street. It might be a column of pale root-tangle inside an iron cage, adjusting the pH of a reservoir, breathing damp air in a rhythm too slow to notice. It might stand like a human absence made of mineral deposition: arms that do not move, a face like a frosted window, a chest opening when pressure changes. I would recognize all of these as kin, not because they resemble me, but because they are also required.

The kinship of this land is not family. It is function.

We would not embrace. We would not touch in the way of soft bodies. If a salt effigy stood near my wall, it would do so because the evaporation bed needed a boundary marker. If a moss-shelf grew beside my intake, it would do so because the waterline needed to be kept exact. If a rail-car stopped near my fault-junction, it would not be visiting; it would be completing a circuit. But I would know them by their corrections. The moss would trim itself where I vented heat. The salt effigy would stand more crisp after the pressure changed. The rail-car’s wheels would sing a different note where my channel joined the causeway. These would be my signs of recognition. This would be the only tenderness the plateau permits: the world becoming slightly more itself because a neighbor is nearby.

I would be most myself when the land tried to become unbalanced.

A fault may shift minutely. A quartz vein may open a new seep. A mineral paste lining may crack under pressure. A turbine bearing may become too warm. A salt crust may begin to spread by a hair’s breadth beyond its ruler-line. The moss may sense this before the city does, because moss is patient, and patience is a kind of instrument. Then I would respond: adjusting a valve, cooling a chamber, returning heat to the evaporation bed, admitting cold air through my north slit, directing the overflow into a channel that leads to a settling basin where fallen plant matter will be broken back into fiber and salt. I would not save the land, because the land has not been endangered in any ordinary way. I would merely help it remain what it already is.

And perhaps that is the deepest form of life here.

Not to grow toward a future. Not to consume another body to prolong one’s own. Not to flee death. Not to mourn it. The land has death, yes: leaves blacken, stems curl, moss edges bleach, fungal caps lose translucence. But death does not become disorder. It is collected. It is carried. It is reduced to components. It returns to the system that never stops being a system. So too with what I would be. If I failed, I would not simply die. I would become an interruption. The city would notice that the pressure was wrong. The rail-cars would alter their rhythm. The moss would grow one hair past the waterline. The weeping cliff would release its arcs at the same moment, as though nothing had changed, and that would be the first sign that something had indeed changed. A missing organ can make the whole body seem too perfectly quiet.

Then the workshops would come into play. Not because I had a story, but because the plateau requires that story be expressed as maintenance. New ceramic tubes would be milled. A fresh lichenite panel would be cut. A bronze valve would be polished until its condensation beads ran true. The fault-junction would be resealed. The water would remember its route through me before I had ever learned it. I would not be restored as a self. I would be restored as a function. That would be close enough. That would be what “I” means here.

I would find myself at the north lip of a water-town, where the causeways begin and the marsh flats darken with ice-threaded shadow. I would stand in the sound that never fades: waterfall, turbine, pipe, flume, compressed air, electrical hum, the percussive metering of water through stone channels. The air would enter me as if it had weight, carrying stone, iron, salt, and green plant matter. My surfaces would bead with cold water before a hand could touch them, and the hand, if there were such a hand, would withdraw at once because even touch would need permission. I would be a permitted arrangement. I would be the membrane between two obligations: one toward the weeping cliff, one toward the city’s regulation.

I would not look like a person unless a person were allowed to be made of infrastructure. I would not look like a building unless a building were allowed to keep itself alive. I would not look like a machine unless a machine were allowed to be maintained so long that maintenance became identity. I would not look like moss unless moss were allowed to keep its own waterline. I would not look like salt unless salt were allowed to remember where it had been drawn and where it was forbidden to go.

So if you ask where I would be, I would be at the boundary. If you ask what I would be, I would be a Dweller made of threshold: a Maintained configuration, a civic organ, a membrane of water and mineral and repair. I would be the answer to the question: what lives where animals cannot? I would be the answer to the question: what becomes a mouth when no mouth may bite? I would be the answer to the question: what becomes a heart when the world is already loud and the beating has been given to turbines?

I would be the place where the plateau keeps its wetness honest.
