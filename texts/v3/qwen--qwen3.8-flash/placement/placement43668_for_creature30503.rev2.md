At the crack, I am already inside.

The mouth is no wider than a shoulder. The salt-streaked stones part around me with the ease of a door that has forgotten it was ever a wall. I do not descend into a cave; I descend into a body. The first breath is feverish, the second damp, the third green and folded shut, the smell of a forest kept as a secret in a drawer. Somewhere below, the air has been rehearsed enough to know my lungs.

I would find myself in the hot seam, halfway between the ceiling’s cool silver damp and the floor’s patient warmth, in the central gallery where condensation and heat argue without anger. It is not the highest platform, though I can climb to it. It is not the deepest valve, though I can feel them opening below me. It is the place where a wall is becoming a room, where a room is becoming a vine, where a vine is becoming a machine. There is a copper basin set into a shelf beside a fungal lamp. Water beads there in slow rings. There is a brass pipe with a black-green bloom. There is a gap between root-felt and slag brick where a beetle leaves a tapping trace. That gap is my house.

For a while, I call myself a person.

My name is not a single sound: two knocks on pipe, a fingernail click on brass, a short exhalation through a hollow stem. The people nearby know me as the one from the east gallery’s hot seam. I wear a collar of moss trained around my shoulders to catch condensation before it runs into my chest. My apron is hammered copper; my boots are padded with compressed moss. My fingers carry beads of hardened vine sap, pale as tears and warm as skin. Too many red beads mean I have worked near heat veins. Too many green mean I have tended lamp-colonies. Too many gray mean a wall collapsed and I helped carry the dust out of someone’s mouth.

I am a basin-keeper and a valve-walker and a lamp-tender, though these trades overlap the way moss overlaps stone. In the morningless light, I read the chamber with my palms before my eyes. A wall that has fever is warming from below. A wall that is weeping slowly is readying rain. A pipe that clicks on the third beat means salt-lice have clustered too densely and the basin is alive with cleaning. A silence where beetles should be means a gap has closed itself with mineral growth. I do not hurry. To hurry here is to break something that is already holding something else.

But the longer I stand in the hot seam, the less I feel like a separate thing that merely lives there. My skin has taken the chamber’s salt. My breath has matched the slow hiss through the wool-dark pipes. The moss at my neck no longer feels like clothing; it is a seam sewn into me. The copper basin under my hand is not an object I tend; it is a mouth through which the room drinks. The fungal shelves glow because I have fed them measured mineral water, but their blue-green light returns the favor by making my shadows readable. The machine bolted to the ledge ticks, and I feel the tick as a pulse not quite my own. If I sleep with my back against the hot plate, dreams of the settlement enter me: rooms filling, walls breathing, ancestors becoming curves in the plaster, children learning not to dash.

So perhaps I am not only human.

I am the tending of a joint.

In a chamber where a room may grow out of a body and a body may be sealed into a room, the strangest creature is not the one with many limbs, but the one who knows no clean edge between hand and tool, leaf and wall, heat and need. I am such a creature. I am the hand on the valve. I am also the valve when it stiffens. I am the moss that colonizes an abandoned ledge. I am the person who decides not to scrape it away. I am the keeping-air hummed by a worker so others know the heat is rising. I am the vibration in copper after the voice stops.

When the ceiling begins its brief mineral rain, I do not run. I tilt my face to it and let the warm drops bead in my hair. Children call that stone milk when the taste is thick with mineral dust. I taste ash, copper, leaf juice, the faint sweetness of decay. The chamber is telling me that it is not finished. It is young still. The basalt has sharp cut faces; the slag is twisted into new paths; the dust has not yet softened. Even I am recent. I have grown over the fracture the way a scab grows over a wound, except that my scab breathes.

If a stranger entered through the hidden mouth, I would not be the first to speak. My first task would be to watch their hands. A stranger always touches too much: a pipe without testing its heat, a vine without seeing whether it is load-bearing, a basin rim where salt-lice cluster like tiny coins. I would step from the hot seam with my hands forward, palms open, moss collar dark with condensation. I would not shout. A shout here sounds like something breaking. I would tap twice on a brass railing: slow, slow, wait. Then I would offer a cup of basin-water. If they drink without flinching, I watch them differently. If they cough, I still watch them, because outside dust can bring a sickness to a place that has no air to spare.

The people would call me by my seam. The beetles would know me by the small changes in my tapping. The salt-lice would gather around my boots if I stood still, taking the salt from my skin. The pale crustaceans in the mineral pools would vanish when touched, or when seen by too many eyes; I would not call them. I have learned that some signs are kept by being seen once, by the one person who already knows how to disappear.

In the highest central gallery, when the whole place can be seen at once, I would still feel most myself lower, nearer the heat, where the walls sweat and the pipes tick. But sometimes I climb to the platform and look down. The chamber is a dense leaf, a city, a stomach, a forest compressed into a room. There are ladders beginning at one wall and ending buried in another. There are copper tubes like arteries. There are blue-green lamp shelves, amber coils, red iron, silver condensation. There are small pools in alcoves. There are fungal mats over abandoned floors. There are dead wrapped in felt and lime, becoming insulation. There are living wrapped in moss, becoming part of the wall. There is no horizon, only the slow pressure of everything becoming something else.

From that height I understand what I am.

I am the chamber’s way of tending itself.

I am the hand that adjusts a valve so the heat does not dry the moss; I am the moss that holds the humidity so the hand does not burn; I am the machine that breathes through the heat differential; I am the person whose body has learned the machine’s rhythm. I am not only human, because the people are not only human either. I am not only plant, because the plants press through rooms and pipes. I am not only machine, because the machines warm and sweat and need mending. I am not only the chamber, because the chamber has no hands except ours, no names except the rhythms we give it.

I am a living seam.

A seam is not one thing. It is where one thing begins to be unable to end. It is the stitch between root-felt and brick, the joint where copper meets mineral, the threshold where heat becomes water becomes green. My place is in that threshold. My role is to keep the threshold from closing too tight or opening too wide. If a wall is left empty, moss takes it. If a wall is kept too bare, the moisture colonizes it in ways no one can predict. So I work by degrees. I scrape a little. I encourage a little. I leave a bead of water. I listen for a beetle’s tap. I hum a keeping-air into a pipe until the room settles.

The world outside this crack is a dry, baked crust. Here, everything is either wet or becoming wet, hot or becoming hot, built or becoming grown. Empty space is only temporary hunger. So I am not hungry. I am filled to the edges with the chamber’s substances. Salt stains my lips. Spores settle in my clothing. The heat keeps my metabolism at a patient fever. Condensation keeps my skin from ever being fully dry. My eyes catch lamp-glow in the dark-adapted way, soft pupils reading the faint blue colonies as other beings might read stars. My body is a weather station of the enclosed world.

If the place asks what it is, and I answer from inside it, I would say: it is not a cave. It is not a city. It is not a forest. It is a pocket of life learning to be all three without enough room to prove any of them alone. And I am one of its proofs.

I would find myself on the warm ledge beneath the dripping gallery, one hand on a copper basin, one foot on a root-ladder, breath fogging faintly before the heat takes it. I would be the one the other inhabitants pass on their way down, saying with their eyes: slow, steady, watch the edges. I would be the keeper of a seam, a valve-walker of the hot artery, a friend to salt-lice, a reader of beetle-silence, a maker of keeping-airs. I would also be the moss collar, the sap bead, the mineral rain, the machine tick, the wall that breathes.

Who would I be? The being that lives in this place is not separate from it. I would be the chamber’s green mouth, its copper throat, its patient heat, its slow wet nerve. I would be the furnace dreaming leaves, and the leaves remembering metal. I would be the joint where the settlement begins to grow.

If someone asked my name, I would tap: twice, click, breath.

If they stayed long enough, they would learn that the name is also the sound of the place holding itself together.
