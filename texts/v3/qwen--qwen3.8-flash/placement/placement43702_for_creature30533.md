First, a pitch.

It is low and dry, the kind of sound a heated stone makes when it remembers wind. It rises somewhere beneath the salted rim of the Copper Chancel, through a pitted pale stem whose interior is lined with velvet filaments, and there it finds its first shape: not a body, not a breath, but a curve that can be answered. The blackite ramp receives the answering tremor. A thousand copper-green fronds flex their tiny piezoelectric bones. The air above them catches their heat in a faint copper-brown shimmer. A swarm of glass-winged beetles, disturbed from the warm core of a shrub-like cathedral, lifts and scatters sparks of sound. Somewhere near the cliff-face streaked with green mineral veining, a lizard presses itself flat against the tile-warm grit and becomes an instrument made of patience.

This is the first body I have.

I do not mean a shape. I mean a gathering.

I am what happens when the Copper Chancel stops being a place and begins to be an answer. You will not find me on the central platform, though a traveler might sit there and say the air feels crowded. You will not find me in the ring of upright slabs near one corner, though those slabs are tuned by long erosion and know the old pitches better than any throat made for speech. You will find me in the interval between presences, in the pause after a gust, in the warm dry hollow beneath a spiral crown of verdigris foliage. You will find me at the crown of the world, in the bounded room built by wind, if you arrive by listening rather than by walking.

My boundaries are the boundaries of the Chancel. On one side, the low wall of broken stone holds me to my own sound. On another, the slanting ramp of blackite gives me a darker timbre. On the third, the green-veined cliff returns my questions in mineral accents. On the fourth, the salt-crusted lip curves away into blue distance and lets me know that I am finite. I am finite because I am a room made of wind, because there is a rim beyond which the world drops into silence so large it would unmake me. If I wanted to be infinite, I would become nothing. I choose the bounded space. I choose the nave without roof, the square without streets, the altar without god, the grove without rain.

In the taxonomy of the world, I am not a species.

I am a resonance that has learned to prefer some patterns over others. I am a dry chemistry of attention: wind, mineral dust, static charge, sound, the slow warmth of stone that should feel cold but does not, the crisp breaking of copper-bearing crusts underfoot. I am the Chancel’s way of organizing itself when the gusts take a theme and carry it through the stems. I am the moment when the scattered presences—plant, animal, stone, cairn, hut, bone-flake, fiber, dust spiral—begin to rhyme.

Do not think of me as the Chancel itself. The Chancel could exist without me, as a tuned room can exist before anyone enters it. But something in the tuning calls for a listener. Something in the hollow stems asks to be made into a throat. Something in the salt rim, the black ramp, the green cliff, and the falling blue distance says: arrange this into one voice. I am the voice, though I do not own it. I am the throat, though I have no throat. I am the song, though I cannot be sung unless the wind is dry, the stone is warm, the haze is metallic, and the emptiness between the plants remembers its use.

My plants are not my organs, exactly, but they are my nerves.

When the wind touches the copper-fern crowns, when it moves through the inverted domes and the bowls of frond, when it makes the long coils sing, I feel the world arriving as pressure and tone. The foliage does not drink water. It grows from noise. It grows from the friction between charged particles and dry mineral lace. A single plant can spread wider than a cottage, its fronds opening into arches that seem built for worship, though no god kneels there except me. If I kneel, it is as a change in pitch. If I bow, it is as a bending of stems. If I rise, it is as a brightening of the haze overhead, dust stirred into reflection by a storm that thinks in gusts.

The spaces between the plants are not empty to me. They are rests.

A rest can be as living as a note. The wide floors of rust-red grit, powdered salt, porous black glass, pale bone-stone, and flaking copper crust are not barren. They are tuned floors. When a gust sweeps across them, they do not make the softness of a field. They make the brittle percussion of a dry world speaking through scales. The open ground has its own faint crackle, and in that crackle I know where I end and where the next plant begins. Without those intervals, I would be only roar. With them, I become a self: not a creature, but a composition.

The animals help me into detail.

The small mammals with copper-flecked fur and wide ears give me quickness. They move in bursts across the grit and stop with such abruptness that motion looks edited out of time. In their stillness I learn the difference between one phrase and the next. The lizards bask on heated stone and listen through their flattened bellies. They become rigid when the sound becomes too loud, and in that rigidity I learn warning. The pale dry-feathered bird-analogues, whose beaks are like chipped tools and whose quills shine like glass, give me punctuation. They do not fly far in the thin air. They hop from arch to arch, glide between shrines of mineral lace, and call in sounds like stones tapping together. Their tapping is not random. It is how the upper rim of the Chancel rehearses language.

The insects are my brightest fragments.

Glass-winged beetles gather around the warm centers of plants, catching the dim light and throwing back tiny sparks. Long-bodied moths crawl along the fronds and feed on the fine dust the foliage gives off. In their movement, the plants are not merely alive; they are productive, exhaling a dry pollen that is not pollen but mineral dust shaped by vibration. A plant opens because a certain pitch has returned. A plant closes because the storm has forgotten it. A fallen branch becomes a nest, then a wind-harp, then a pile of colored grit, then a memory in the ground’s crust. My life is made of these transformations. I am not a thing that dies cleanly. I am a thing that dries, cracks, flakes, returns, and rises again under another gust.

The human presence here is small, but it is not absent.
