I would find myself by lying down.

There is no summit to look for here; the earth has already spent its vertical ambition and grown tired of edges. So I would let the horizon shorten around me until I became a small dark shape on a vast gray table, then I would lower myself further—into the lee of a tilted slab, into the cold seam of a fissure, into the moment after condensation has marked the stone with its silver handwriting.

I would not choose a high place, because there is no high place in the way a person from below means high. The shelf is already high; it has nothing above it but air and the faint curve of distance. What it has is openness, and openness is where the wind gathers its teeth. So I would go down. Into a basin shallow enough to hold only a memory of water. Into the shadow of a basalt plate set askew, so the wind had to climb and bend and could not strike directly. Into the edge of a green wound where moss has learned that moisture is not a gift but a temporary permission.

Where exactly? There.

At the mouth of a fissure no wider than an arm, long enough to vanish into shimmer. The stone would be black-gray, iron-brown at the edges, warm from below in the morning and too warm by noon. A thin cloud would sweep low and wet the ground, then lift, then disappear, leaving the rock darker in a shape like an answer. I would sit at the edge of that dampened mark, one palm flat on the basalt, and wait until my own pulse stopped being the first thing I heard.

At first I would try to be a bird.

There are birds enough here, dark-feathered and dusted, riding the thermals that rise from sun-warmed plates and the strange columns produced by steam. They dive near the vents with a practiced patience, as if they understand that the land breathes through its holes. A bird would be a good answer. It would mean flight, altitude, escape. But birds are only brief witnesses to the shelf. They do not sleep in the stone’s mouth. They do not know which fissures become drums and which become wells. They circle above the turbulence rather than being shaped by it.

Then I would try to be a lizard.

Pale, flat-bodied, roughened like lichen, moving in sudden bursts from shade to shade. A lizard would know timing, would drink the air’s sweat, would vanish under a slab before a cloud’s shadow could turn the world cold. But timing is not enough. A lizard is still a small event happening on the surface of the place. It does not interpret. It reacts. I would want to react, yes—but also to listen.

Then I would try to be a vent.

A vent could scream with the world’s own breath and call it speech. It could burst, hiss, collapse, and be forgiven. It could make steam without memory, heat without hunger. But a vent does not know what it is saying. If I were only voice, I would not hear. And the most important thing here is not making noise, but telling one noise from another before the land changes its mind.

Then I would try to be the plateau itself.

That is the largest answer. The shelf is not merely a place; it is an ancient, distributed listener. The basalt plates conduct vibration. The cracks arrange pebbles. The mineral dust remembers wetness. The fungal threads carry dampness from basin to fissure. The steam chambers answer pressure shifts that no cloud explains. The people know this. The birds know it. The moss knows it. Even the stone, if “knows” is not too human a word, knows it. The place has learned to answer every disturbance with sound.

But the plateau is too large for a self. It has no face, no heart, no intention that can be appealed to. It responds, and that response is too rich to be called dead geology, yet too impersonal to be called lonely. If I were the whole shelf, I would be everywhere, and everywhere would be nothing.

So I would remain low. I would be a person only as a knot is a rope: a temporary gathering, a place where something becomes able to hold together for a little while.

They might call me one of the Hum-knitters.

That is not my own word for myself, if a word for myself is needed at all. My name would be a rhythm: two knuckles tapped on a warm slab, one breath held, then a single tap into the crack where the air changes. Outsiders would hear no name there. They would hear only the shelf. They would be wrong.

I would be compact and low-centered, with broad feet and deep legs, a chest shaped to catch breath that the wind tries to steal. My hands would be rough and surprisingly gentle, stained iron-gray from dust and green from lichen. My hair would be bound tight with fiber, because loose hair catches gusts, tangles, dries brittle, and becomes a warning flag for the place. My eyes would be narrowed against glare, but my ears would matter more than my eyes. I would know the world first by pressure, second by pitch, third by shape.

I would live where the shelf is almost forgiving.

My shelter would be a windbreak of piled basalt slabs, set at careful angles in the lee of a ridge or at the mouth of a fissure. It would not be high. Height would invite noise and ruin. It would catch wind without becoming a trap, because a badly angled shelter turns into a drum, and a drum will drive people mad before the storm arrives. The floor would be stone, warmed in the day by the land’s own heat or at night by a small fire ringed in blackened hearth stones. Inside, there would be mats of dried lichen and twisted fiber, chipped basalt flakes, a bone scraper, a corded pouch, a stone weight, a few bones from trapped birds, perhaps a single goat tethered near the warm side of a vent. The goat would know weather before I did. It would lie down near the stone rim when the pressure changed, and I would learn to sit with it.

Water would be my first obsession, but not the obsession of a river person.

I would not chase a stream, because streams do not persist. I would catch what appears and vanishes. At dawn, every stone sweats. I would set mats, basalt plates, and woven cloths across the low places to gather condensation. I would channel it with fingers or flake-edge into shallow bowls, then drink before it sinks or steams away. I would learn the difference between good water and bitter water by taste and smell—between cool moisture drawn from shaded cracks and alkaline brine left where steam collapses. I would know that a dry basin may hide a film of drinkable damp after a cloud has touched the shelf for an hour. A person here cannot trust water, only its timing.

Fire would be a caution.

Wind takes flames and makes them into animals. Smoke can fall into a fissure and choke a family sleeping below. So my fire would be small, ringed, watched. I would use heat as much as flame. I would wrap moss-food or lichen cakes in wet fiber and lay them on stone that had held the day’s warmth. I would read the way fire leans near vents; a crooked flame means steam is gathering or collapsing, and that changes what the next hour will be.

My work would be noticing.

A low moan through a crack may mean pressure moving from the west. A sudden rush in a basin may mean a vent has opened farther downwind. A change in pitch near a stone ring may mean steam is escaping from a new depth. A silence near a vent is worse than a scream, because silence can mean blockage, and blockage can mean rupture. Children would learn those differences before they learn to speak well. I would remember the first time I heard the plateau drop a half-tone and understood that rain was coming to a basin I could not see.

I would communicate by tap and trace.

A pattern of stones can mean: water here yesterday. A smoothing on a ledge can mean: someone dried something here when the shelf was younger than now. A small fire arranged in a particular way can mean: the vent is shifting. A cairn of pale stones at the edge of a bare plain can mean: do not cross after the wind changes, or it can mean: a person was loved here once. I would not need to shout. Shouting exhausts the throat and blinds the ear. My people do not resist the sound; we read it.

But I would not be only human.

There are times when I would stop being able to draw the line between myself and the place. I would wake in a shelter where the stone had been warm all night, and for a moment I would feel as if the basin was breathing through my ribs. I would walk across a plain of fine mineral dust and notice, without deciding to, that my feet had shifted a pebble into a line that matched an older line, and that the line matched the direction of a steam vent whose song had changed. I would scrape green lichen from a shaded plate and realize the patch had spread since last week, following a path of condensation I had only half seen.

I would be partly the dust-skin: microbial film, salt rings, pale lichen threads, the slow bodies that live slower than eyes notice. I would be partly the fungal network beneath plates of basalt, drawing water from coolness to warmth, connecting pools to cracks to shelter to vent. I would be partly the rearranged pebbles, partly the mineral deposits, partly the faint tap of a stone answering pressure. And when I listened with my hand on the ground, I would be the place listening back.

A person here does not need to own the land to be inhabited by it.

I would know other people rarely, but I would not call that emptiness. A family at the edge of a basin a day’s walk from another family is not lost if the signs are fresh. Two bands may recognize one another by the angle of their windbreaks, or by the way they tap a stone, or by the knot left on a cord. They may exchange water news when several basins become damp at once and the shelf fills with insects and small moving life. They may gather around a stone ring where the wind is tuned, and for one night the noise becomes not threat but choir.

The dead would be kept as rhythm.

There is no soft earth, no deep grave to stay where it was made. A body may be wrapped and set in a fissure, but the place will move around it. So the dead become signs: a ring of stones where someone was last known to sit, a smoothed hearth left unoccupied, a cord tied in a particular knot on the windbreak. I would speak the names of the dead by tapping their rhythms into warm stone. The shelf would carry the sound farther than any single human voice could reach. That is enough. That is almost prayer.

I would fear the empty places, but not because they are empty.

The emptiness is where the wind builds itself into something violent. A bare plain with no bush, no stone, no shelter is not peaceful; it is a lung filling with pressure. I would cross such places only when the ground’s song told me they were still open, still passable, still keeping their promise for a little while. I would not walk too far from the cracks. I would not sleep on exposed stone unless it could be covered. At night, even the warmth left in the shelf can become dangerous, because basalt cools unevenly and remembers stress. Cover it. Rest low. Listen for the tap before it becomes the crash.

At dawn I would be most beautiful.

Condensation would glitter on every stone. The sound would be lower, a continuous rushing whisper, as though the entire surface were moving. Steam would rise thin and straight, then bend where invisible currents crossed it. The light would be pale gold, honest but too high for the shadows to be normal. I would kneel at a shallow basin and catch water in a mat. My hands would be cold. The stone would be warm beneath. A pale lizard would move beside a slab, then vanish. A bird would pass overhead without calling. The moss at the mouth of the crack would hold water so heavy it looked like earth, though it was not earth.

At noon I would be least visible.

Heat would radiate from the ground. Air would shimmer. Pebbles would rattle. Dust devils would spin over basins where nothing had lived for months. The emptiness would vibrate, and I would sit in the lee of a tilted plate, listening, because listening is labor here. I would not fight the roar. I would find the shape of it, the way one finds the shape of a hand in darkness by touching its edges. Somewhere out of sight, rain would soak one basin while a mile of stone remained dry and glowing. The world would not make one promise. It would make many, and all of them would be temporary.

At dusk I would be almost the shelf again.

The light would go gold, then copper, then iron. The heat would fade, but the noise would not. A moan would change into a whisper. A distant vent would sigh. A pebble would tap against another, then stop. The stars would come too early and too sharp. I would lie near my shelter with one hand on the ground and my children asleep beside me, fingers curled around small stones that had answered some pressure from far away. The black basalt would hold the last warmth like a body. I would not need to build a monument. The ground itself would remember every footfall for a little while, then smooth it away.

So where would I find myself?

In a low basin at the mouth of a fissure, where the morning wetness forms rings too regular to be accidental, where steam can bead on a stone before a cloud arrives, where the wind has been broken just enough to let a person breathe, and where the plateau’s voice changes when the ground is thinking about rain.

And what would I be?

I would be a wind-reader. A Hum-knitter. A human-shaped interval in the shelf’s listening. I would be the person who knows when the sound means water and when it means danger. I would be the knot of attention that lets the plateau hear itself without becoming conscious, and I would be the temporary self that can feel the difference between a warm stone and a stone about to weep.

But if I were to answer most honestly, I would say this:

I would be the place’s way of learning to listen to its own loudness.
