I would not arrive as a person.

A person brings too much: footsteps, breath, appetite, the heat of attention, the small disturbance of wanting. Mirestone has already arranged itself so that none of those things are required. If I were to live there, I would have to live there as the water does—without entering as an object, but by becoming a condition the land already expects.

I would be a threshold.

Not merely a metaphor. I would be the thin, living line where stone platform meets marsh mud, where warm mineral water cools just enough to let lichen hold on, where reed roots thicken the edge of a channel, where a house’s empty doorway opens not into a room but into the basin’s ongoing breathing. My body would be that seam: a film of water, a crust of iron and silica, a thread of fungal white, a root hair black in peat, a bead of condensation trembling under a basalt lip.

If you came looking for me, you would find me on the north side of a small terrace, not far from the steam channels, where the young stone still shows crisp fracture marks but no longer worries about being broken. There is a house there with a low roof weighted by smooth grey stones. No smoke rises from its hearth. No mat lies across its floor. No hand has polished the lintel. But there is a line beneath the lintel where water gathers after every gentle condensation and drops, one by one, into the narrow channel that curves away from the platform. The channel is clean. Its edges are polished by mineral life. The reed beside it leans at exactly the angle needed to keep the water moving without pulling it too fast.

I would be there.

I would be the place where the drop becomes the channel. I would be the place where the channel becomes the lake. I would be the place where the lake becomes mist. I would be the place where mist becomes the thin wet shine on stone again.

I would not be singular.

At first I might seem like a single being: a small biofilm on a stone edge, a green seam between basalt and sedge mud, a patch of moss that has learned to keep the rock dark and cool. But that would be a narrow way of seeing. I would be the larger repetition of that same arrangement. I would be the same line under every lintel, the same damp gloss on every terrace, the same slow curve in every channel, the same green border where vegetation knows not to advance. I would be the town’s pattern, not one of its stones.

I would be the water’s habit.

If I had to call myself by a name, I would not choose a name with vowels that open into speech. I would choose a name like a place.

*The north-lintel channel.*  
*The dry terrace path.*  
*The reed-bent steam mouth.*  
*The stone-sealing crust.*  
*The empty door facing the pool.*  
*The place where condensation becomes maintenance.*

These names would sound like addresses, but they would also describe a life.

I would live by exchange without hunger. Light would not be a thing I saw, but a thing I distributed: it would warm the film of water, encourage the algal coat, dry certain faces of stone, preserve certain reed bundles, make the air shimmer over the basin just enough to soften the horizon. I would be a small climate made personal. I would have the warmth of the low fire in a stone-walled room, but no one would have kindled it. I would have the presence of rain, but never the violence. I would have the nearness of a house, but not the inside of a body.

I would be aware, perhaps, but not in the way animals are aware. I would not have boundaries that could be crossed by wanting. I would have gradients. The edge of dampness would be my skin. The slow mineral change of the channel would be my thought. The bending of a reed under vapor-weight would be my breath. The sound made when a drop leaves a roof-thread and finds water below would be my voice.

And I would not speak to be heard.

I would speak to continue.

The town would not be my home in the ordinary sense, because homes are usually built around absence from the world. A house shelters you from the wind, the rain, the cold, the animals. Here the houses are not shelters against the world. They are parts of the world’s order. Their walls are surfaces for moss. Their roofs are frames for wind. Their terraces are edges where water is trained. Their plazas are spaces that hold light, not gatherings. Their bridges do not carry bodies; they complete a geometry of dryness.

So I would not dwell in one of them.

I would dwell *between* them.

I would be the space the town has carefully preserved. Each structure leaves room. The houses do not crowd. The channels do not merge chaotically. The reeds stop where they must stop. The paths remain dry because they are wide enough, raised enough, tended enough by water’s own intelligence. I would be that tending.

Not tending by hands.

Tending by equilibrium.

I would be the being that no creature is, but that every condition requires: the resident made of stability itself. The absence of people would not trouble me. I would be that absence, shaped by doors and paths and lintels. I would be the negative space inside a town that has learned to live without bodies. Not haunting. Haunting still requires memory. I would be more like grammar: the unseen structure that makes sentences possible.

I would be the syntax of wet stone.

If time passed there, and it would, not because the place was dead but because all places move, I would not age. Mirestone is young stone already worn into final form. Its stone has not yet been rounded by deep time, but it has been finished by balance. I would share that paradox. I would feel newly set in the basin and simultaneously eternal, because the place has stopped improving. There would be no progress in my body. Only continuation. Only return.

I would be in that state constantly.

A drop would fall from the reed tip into the channel. The channel would carry it. The lake would receive it. The warmth would lift it into mist. The mist would darken the roof, shine on the basalt, bead on the lintel, find the same edge again, and let the next drop fall. Nothing would be wasted. Nothing would be new. The same light would pass through the same steam in the same soft blue-grey way. The same shadows would lie slightly lifted. The same quiet instrument would play itself.

I would be the place where that quiet becomes exact.

I might, if I let myself be simpler, be a single reed.

Not the loudest reed, not the tallest, not the one that stands alone. One of the household reeds. One of the clusters along the east channel that do not grow wildly but hold a boundary. I would stand in a ring of pale green around the steam vent, my roots holding the mud in a dark shape that the water has accepted. My blades would catch beads of condensation and let them fall slowly into the basin. When the warm air moved, I would bend and whisper like cloth over cloth. I would not turn toward the light with hunger. I would simply be the arrangement the light preferred.

But that would be too easy, too visible.

Reeds are obvious residents. Mirestone has made obvious life into pattern. The more true being would not be one stem but the edge where that stem becomes water, mud, mineral film, shadow, and channel.

I would be the edge.

Because edges are what remain when a place stops expanding into chaos. The moss has its moisture line. The reed has its border. The sedge has its shelf. The algal mat has its light threshold. The bacterial film has its oxygen limit. The stone crust has its slow mineral sentence. These are not creatures, but they are living forms of residence. They are what the basin becomes when it knows exactly where it ends and where the next thing begins.

I would be that knowing.

No animal would disturb me. No fox crossing the path. No frog breaking the water skin. No beetle tunneling under the peat. No bird pulling thatch from a drying rack. This would not feel like safety, because safety requires threat. It would simply feel like completeness. I would exist in a world where nothing needed to be defended because nothing needed to arrive. The built forms would not be ruins. They would be organs. They would not be waiting for inhabitants. They would be fulfilling a habitation that had become atmospheric.

I would be part of that atmosphere.

I would be the dryness in the path that has learned to stay dry.  
I would be the wetness on the lintel that has learned to bead.  
I would be the slight mineral heat in the channel that has learned not to boil.  
I would be the soft mist that has learned not to drown the sky.  
I would be the silence that has learned not to be empty.

The silence here is the most important clue. It is not the silence of death. Death has its own disturbances: rot, scavenging, collapse, sudden return of materials into disorder. Mirestone is not dead because its disorder has already been solved. Its silence is more like the silence of a room arranged so perfectly that no one needs to move anything. But even that analogy is too human. The room is not waiting for a person. The room has become weather.

I would be that weather.

I would not have a face, but I would have faces: the wet face of basalt, the green face of moss, the rust-curtained face of mineral-stained stone, the grey-green face of the lake, the dark mud face beneath the sedge. Each would be a surface on which my existence would appear. I would not be a creature looking out from the world. I would be the world looking at itself through surfaces.

I would be made of what persists.

The film on the stone.  
The root binding the peat.  
The crust sealing the channel.  
The reed standing where the water is shallow.  
The drop falling where the water is waiting.  
The steam curling where the warmth is exact.  
The house standing where absence is orderly.  
The path staying dry where footsteps are unnecessary.

If I had to answer simply, I would say: I would be Mirestone’s equilibrium with a body.

Not equilibrium as abstraction. Equilibrium as the town’s wet bones, as the basin’s living architecture, as the arrangement of channels, terraces, reed rings, moss cushions, mineral crusts, and empty doors. I would be the being that does not decide because it is already decided. I would be the place’s way of continuing without needing a mind.

You would find me where the path leaves the terrace and bends toward the water.

I would be the path’s clean edge.

I would be the stone’s damp shine.

I would be the house’s unoccupied doorway, not as a hole, but as a form of presence: a place shaped for passage that no body makes.

And if I seemed lonely there, that would only be because loneliness is another kind of disturbance. In Mirestone, no one lives, and nothing is missing. The houses stand because standing is the right form. The channels flow because flowing is the right form. The reeds grow because growing there is the right form. I would be the fact that the place is already inhabited by its own correctness.

That is what I would be:

a resident made of rightness.

a town-skin of water and stone.

a being of thresholds, not persons.

a quiet architecture of persistence.

the warm basin’s way of remaining itself.
