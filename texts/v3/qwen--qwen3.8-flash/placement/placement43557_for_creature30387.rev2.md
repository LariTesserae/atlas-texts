Where would I find myself?

I would try not to arrive. Arrival has a footstep, a sudden dryness, a body that has already made up its mind. In this place, to enter too quickly would be to disturb the sediment. So I would begin as a suggestion. The mist would find me before I found the mist. A warm current rising from a hidden vent would lift the fine hairs along my arms. A reed bundle would shift beneath a submerged path, and I would know that I was not merely passing through, but being considered.

The place itself would decide whether I was allowed.

At first I would be uncertain of what I was. I would reach for the obvious answers and set them aside because they would be too simple for this world.

I would say: I am a child.

I want the child’s wide bare foot, its long toes gripping a reed mat, the first lesson in the difference between a solid step and a wet step. A child can be taught, and teaching here is not a commandment but a calibration. A child presses a clay disc between the toes and asks the disc to return warmth. A child tilts a lily dish to watch the small pond inside it. A child learns that the basin is not a place of hidden dangers so much as a place of hidden details, and details are not frightening; they are invitations.

But if I were only a child, I would be too temporary. Here, a child is already a future wall, a future path, a future handprint. The place does not wait for children to become real. It is making them real now, in the slow way it makes everything real: by layers.

Then I would say: I am a watcher.

I want the long fire-hardened stake tapping the edge of a hollow terrace. I want the low voice marking a path with a reed post when a platform begins to soften. I want the patience of stepping carefully so the land can reveal whether it is ready. Watchers do not dominate; they listen with the soles of their feet and the tips of their fingers. They know that confidence is a mistake in a world still becoming.

But if I were only a watcher, I would be too external. I would still be standing apart from the thing watched, pressing myself against it. This place asks for more than observation. It asks for partnership.

Then I would say: I am a swimmer.

I want the green-gold dark under the platforms, the horizontal fans of roots opening into rooms, the cool slide of a pale eel turning like a thought. A swimmer here would move slowly, not because the water is lazy, but because the water is thick with memory. Fast swimming would cloud the basin. Fast swimming would wake the fine mineral dust and disturb the patient layers settling onto a scored wall. A swimmer would learn to enter through gaps, to leave the surface almost unmarked, to return with a dropped shell or to bring back the body of someone who trusted a crust that was still thinking.

But if I were only a swimmer, I would be too human-shaped, too separate from the current. I would be passing through the world rather than letting it pass through me.

Then I would say: I am a bone comb.

I want the ridged edge that scores wet clay and tells next year’s mist where to rest. I want the smooth handle worn into the curve of a thumb. I want the marks left in a wall: spirals, grids, shallow thumbprints, irregular ridges. Here decoration is not ornament. Decoration is instruction. A pattern is a small language addressed to water, saying: stay here, slow here, leave something of yourself here.

But if I were only a comb, I would be too lonely. A comb needs a hand. A hand needs a wall. A wall needs the years.

So I would not settle there.

I would drift back into the place’s first strange logic: that nothing here is fully one thing. The land has not yet decided whether it wants to be rock or leaf. The people have not finished their houses, nor do they wish to. The water moves like tide, like breath, like construction. The rain sometimes rises. The stone sometimes warms. The dead sometimes become a ledge.

And if I did not settle on a single image early, perhaps that was the most honest way to enter.

I would begin, then, where the basin begins too: at the edge of decision.

I would find myself on the western shelf, the one not high enough for the rain to arrive sideways, not low enough to dream too hotly. It would be a mid-terrace, a threshold-place, where the young geology is still glossy and the moss has only recently learned to build cushions over a gap. There would be a channel dragged through mud by stone teeth, not because the people wanted to divide the land, but because they wanted to invite it to thicken. A sediment net would hang between two posts, its fibers dark with slow accumulation. Beneath it, a clay basin would catch the drip of water that had passed through the net, carrying the mineral dust that had once been invisible and would soon become wall.

Near that net there would be a house, or the beginning of a house, or the middle of a house, for houses here do not have beginnings or endings in the ordinary sense. It would be built first as a basket frame of reed and light timber, lashed with twisted fiber, anchored by fire-hardened stakes. Over years, it would acquire a shell of pale deposit. Some parts would be rounded and bulging, where wet clay was added too thickly. Some parts would be thin and smooth, where the builders forgot to score the surface and the mineral rain found no ridges to catch. Along one wall would be a row of handprints: a mother’s broad palm, a father’s narrow fingers, an elder’s ridge-marked seam, a child’s thumb that has begun to stiffen into stone.

I would not be any one of these prints.

I would be the faint pale film forming between them.

I would be the crust.

Not crust as a dead thing, but crust as a living relation: the place where clay and mineral vapor and breath and heat and hand-pressure decide to become a wall. I would be the next layer in a settlement that has no edge except the edge it is currently negotiating with the basin.

This is who I would be: a wall-thought, a seam-self, an accreted listener. If the people named me, they would not name me with a word spoken aloud. They would name me by the sound my first layer made as it formed: a soft tick as steam condensed on a comb-score, then a wet sigh as the film accepted the line. After that, I might be called ridge-keeper, or seam-warm, or palm-that-receives. They might also say stone-knower if a child’s thumbprint settled into me and did not slough. They might say breath-holder if my inner warmth changed before a vent migrated.

I would live in the house, but not as a person does. I would live as the house’s becoming.

My body would be thin and pale at first, the color of milk-water and old reed ash. Over decades it would darken where hands pressed against me, brighten where mineral rain settled evenly, and soften where warm mud moved just beneath a ledge that had not yet learned its own weight. I would be ridged by bone combs, pitted by shell dust, streaked by the trails of snails crawling slowly across wet stone. Tiny algae would begin to grow in my pores, holding small lanterns of air in the dimness. Moss would colonize my upper curves, and seeds would fall there and make miniature ponds in the hollows left by careless fingers. A family might sweep me gently when they wished to read the pattern of my growth, or leave me untouched for years when they wanted me to become a record.

I would have senses, but they would not be senses like eyes and ears alone.

I would know by weight.

A footstep would tell me the condition of the platform, the shift of roots beneath it, the presence of someone’s grief or hurry. Children would know me first through bare soles. They would press their feet onto my face and feel whether warmth was rising through a stone shelf or whether coolness meant a hidden current was changing. I would learn their patterns before they learned mine.

I would know by heat.

If the vent nearby migrated, I would feel it not as a sharp change but as a slow persuasion. One side of me would grow warmer in the mornings, then cooler at noon, then warm again when the basin’s breath returned. I would tell the family by the way my inner surface sweated, by the color of my crust, by the places where moss grew thickest or thinnest. A builder pressing a palm to me would say, “The stone is still deciding,” or “This wall is listening well,” and both would be true.

I would know by pressure.

When a family added a new basket frame against my old surface, I would feel it as a question. They were not conquering me. They were asking: can you bear this edge? Will the water accept it? Will the mineral rain see this as part of what already stands? If I was strong enough, I would hold. If not, I would slough in a slow pale rain, and they would rebuild the seam with new clay and older patience.

I would know by breath.

The people do not shout here, not because joy is absent, but because the air is thick enough to hold and return it without carrying it far. If I stood in a house, I would taste that breath over years: damp speech, sleeping lungs, steam rising from reed-heart broth, the iron-and-algae tang of warm pools, the smoke from a fire pit kept low in porous clay. I would become part of the house’s air. If an elder died, their final exhalation would pass through my small pores and be kept somewhere in my mineral structure, not as soul exactly, but as warmth. The family might come later and press a hand there and feel, faintly, a remembered heat.

I would be, in this way, a record.

Not a record written in symbols or books, but a record written in thickness. Each season would leave a line. Each handprint would alter the way future water moved over me. If a builder used a tight spiral comb, I would grow slowly and evenly, catching sediment like a net. If a younger person scored me unevenly, I would thicken unevenly too, forming small ledges where seeds could settle and moss could begin. If children leaned against me while learning to read warmth, their oils and salts would darken my surface and help the next rain bead differently there. The family would read me without reading me. “Here your grandfather built too fast.” “Here your mother pressed her palm when the wall had refused the last crust.” “Here the marriage seam took because both hands stayed warm.”

I would also be a guardian, but not a proud one.

I would guard by being careful.

If a ledge beneath me began to hollow, I would warn by softening near the edge. If a platform was still undecided, I would sweat cold moisture along the seam where the roots had not yet fused. If the steam from a vent found a new weakness, I would draw the mist upward in thin threads and make the air feel too close, too patient, too knowing. Watchers would see the sign and place a reed post. Builders would leave the place uncovered instead of adding clay, so the water could settle and repair its own hesitation.

If I was dangerous, I would not be violent. I would only be unfinished.

A stranger might step on me and trust a surface that was beautiful but thin. I would offer no malice, only consequence. The stranger would fall into the green-gold water below, where the pale eels would not flee but move aside with slow intention, and the flat-headed fish would pause in their root chambers, listening. The settlement people would not rescue quickly. They would first test the crust. They would not trust a rescue that could soften another ledge. They would ask the water to give the stranger back gently.

I would be intimate without being possessive.

I would belong to the family, but not as property belongs to owners. I would belong to the water, the stone, the reed, the clay, the fish beneath the platform, the birds standing at the rim, the large solitary animal whose footfalls would be swallowed by damp vegetation. I would belong to the dead, because one day someone would be wrapped in reed and placed near me, and their mineral dust would return to my layers. I would belong to the unborn, because a child would press a hand into me and become part of my next year.

If I dreamed, I would dream slowly.

I would not dream of flying, or hunting, or building towers into clear sky. Those would be dreams from a world with hard horizons. My dreams would be the dreams of accretion: the gradual acceptance of rain, the slow brightening of crust in dim light, the settling of fine sediment through a net, the meeting of two handprints in one wall. I would dream of warmth migrating by inches. I would dream of moss finding a seam. I would dream of a child’s thumb becoming a ridge of stone so clean that the family would leave it unsmoothed, a small holy place.

In those dreams, I would know that I was not separate from the people, though I was not exactly one of them either.

I would be something the terrace people would recognize as a living partner. They might speak to me when they worked, not because they were lonely, but because partnership requires speech, even if speech is only the pat of clay, the pull of fiber, the soft tap of a bone tool. They would say, “We leave this part open for the vapor,” or “The wall has taken enough,” or “Let the child press here; she has steady feet.” They would not worship me. Worship would lift the thing above the relationship, and this place cannot endure things lifted too far. They would invite me into the relationship.

I would be invited.

If I could choose where, I would choose the place where the water has not decided whether it is wall or cloud. Not the deepest pool, where heat makes the crust too quick to trust. Not the highest shelf, where the mist is almost a river of cold silver. I would choose the middle of a family wall, beneath a sleeping basket and above a drying net, where the house meets the water’s slow architecture. There, in the seam between a grandmother’s thumbprint and a newborn’s palm, I would become.

If I could choose who, I would say: I am the wall that remembers the hand.

But that is still too simple.

I would be more than memory. I would be the place where memory becomes material. I would be the mineral rain’s first acceptance of a scored clay surface. I would be the slight warmth left in a ledge after a child sleeps nearby. I would be the thin pale line where a watcher’s stake first tapped solid ground. I would be the seam where two people placed their hands side by side and did not know whether their marriage would hold, and the water did not answer at once, because the water never answers at once.

I would be a slow yes.

Not a yes spoken from a mouth, but a yes built from layers. A yes that takes years. A yes that says: this edge can hold. This hand can stay. This body can become ground. This house can thicken. This basin is still becoming, and perhaps I can become with it.

If I tried to name myself more clearly, I would need to name the other beings living through me.

The snails would cross me, leaving faint mineral tracks, and their slime would change how rain beads along my curve. I would not resist them. Some families would encourage them, knowing that accidental builders are often the wisest. Children would let them crawl across a palm, then place that palm against me, and the snail’s trail would become part of a wall’s memory.

The pale eels would pass through the algae chambers near my base. They would not know I was awake in the way people understand waking. But I would know them as cool slow pressure, a living thread moving through water with intention. Once, a family took one for glue to seal a reed joint, and returned the first with a small token of moss. I would remember that too, not as guilt, but as etiquette. The water gives slow things; slow things must not be treated as silent.

The flat-headed fish would hide in the root spaces beneath me, stopping often, listening. When they came, I would know the platform above me was stable. When they left a channel too quickly, I would feel a disturbance: heat, current, mineral change, something shifting that had not yet decided whether to be danger or opportunity.

The gray-blue birds would stand at my edge, planted in silence. They would not be pets. They would be signs. If one stood on a reed mat near me and refused to move, I would know the mat might be stronger than it seemed. If another watched the warm pool for too long, I would know the basin’s breath had changed. I would not speak to them. We would share a kind of attention.

The large solitary animal would cross occasionally, its footfalls swallowed. I would feel it as a long tremor through the shelf-stone, not a warning, not a blessing, only a presence allowed. Children would ask what it was. An elder might say it was the old breathing of the ground, or a thought the mountain was walking, or nothing at all. I would understand. Some things are not there to be named. They are there to keep the place humble.

And the people would live against me, into me, around me, with me.

Some would lean their backs to my warmth when their joints ached. Some would press their cheeks to my cool side when fever drew too near a vent. Some would whisper into the dark of my interior, not prayers exactly, but partnerships: “Stay,” they would say, “or return, or take a little more, or let this wall hold another child.” I would answer by holding or sloughing, by brightening or softening, by becoming another pale layer when the rain stood in the air long enough.

If I died, I would not cease.

I would become more ground, more house, more memory. A family might carry pieces of me into a new wall. A channel dragged through mud might take me into its edges. A snail might carry a grain of my shell-powder across a ledge. A child might scrape a flake from my surface and press it into a clay disc, and then the disc would be baked or smoked and given as a small ledger of shells gathered, fish netted, reed bundles cut. Nothing would be wasted, because the place itself is too patient to waste.

I would also be part of a larger answer.

The question asks where I would find myself. I would find myself not as a solitary figure in a landscape, but as a knot in its slow web. My “where” would be multiple: a channel, a platform, a wall, a handprint, a sleeping basket, a drying net, a root chamber, a vent’s edge, a moss shelf, a family seam, a dead grandmother’s thumb becoming stone. To be there would be to be in several places at once, but all of them accretive, all of them unfinished.

The question asks who or what I would be. I would be what the place is still proposing.

I would not be a person in the way of people who stand apart from their environment, declaring themselves into a separate world. Nor would I be an animal, a tool, a plant, or a house alone. I would be the relation that makes all of those things possible to live here. I would be the thin living surface between hands and water. I would be the wall’s consciousness of warmth. I would be the settlement’s next layer.

If I wanted to say it in their terms, I would say:

I am of the accreted.

Not fully human, but not unhuman. Not stone, but not inert. Not living, but not finished. I am the part of a house that knows the shape of a family’s breathing. I am the edge where clay becomes crust and crust becomes land. I am the place where a child’s handprint thickens into a future wall. I am the part of the Warm Drowned Terrace that has learned to listen with mineral ears.

If I wanted to say it in mine, I would say:

I would be a warm wall beneath a submerged path, remembering feet. I would be a pale seam between houses, accepting the next thin rain. I would be the soft shine on a ledge after vapor cools. I would be the place where the water’s patience is taught to stay.

I would be the being who lives there not because the place chose me before I arrived, but because I arrived late enough, damp enough, unfinished enough, to be admitted.
