Place me where the ground is not yet decided.

Not in the center of the plateau—there is no center, only distances that keep pretending to be one distance longer. Not on the highest shelf, where the air might be too thin to carry the smell of hot iron. Not beside a great stone wall or a riverbank or any place that suggests a story about the weather. Place me where a basalt shelf dips into a shallow pan no wider than two hands, where the rock is dark and slick on one side and chalk-white with salt on the other, where a threadgrass tuft coils tight against the dryness and a single ember-rosette has opened itself just enough to hold a bead of metallic moisture. Place me at that threshold: the point where warmth meets damp, where mineral breath meets stone, where the plateau sweats itself into being.

For a long time I would not be an animal.

I would be a patch of oxide lace spreading over a cold-looking stone that is actually warm underneath. I would be the thin blue-white thread at the rim of the pan, the faint film that makes the water too still, too clear, too aware of pressure. I would be the taste of rust on the tongue before anyone has had a tongue. I would be the condensation gathering in a pore of basalt, the first droplet before it trembles, the second, the third, until a tiny surface tension holds a whole world suspended and trembling with the feet of creatures too small to name. If a hand—human, hypothetical, never present—touched me, I would feel slightly oily, slightly damp, faintly warm, and the palm would carry the smell of metal and smoke afterward, as if the hand had pressed itself against something quietly alive.

Then I might be a copperback pressing its tarnished shell flat against the rock, becoming a fragment of the stone that had only forgotten how to stay still. I might be an ash-moth crawling up the flank of a warm ledge, its wing-scales dull until the heat beneath it flashes scattered amber-blue through the fog. I might be a seam-crawler in the crack between basalt slabs, tasting the water that is not quite water, reading the channel by vibration, leaving behind a molted skin thin as glass. Each of those would fit. Each would be a way of being too small, too quick, too partial.

But let something heavier gather around the warmth.

Let the heat in the stone become the heat in digestion. Let the slow pulse from the geothermal seams become a pulse in the chest. Let the dampness in the air become breath that fogs only in the coldest hollows. Let the body broaden through the shoulders. Let it sink low to the ground, thick-legged, patient, made of dark hide and mineral memory.

I would be a shelf-walker.

Not one with a name. The plateau would not name me. It would not need to. I would be one of the slow, broad-backed animals moving in a loose band across a high basalt shelf, crossing the dim distance between a salt crust, a condensation basin, a wind-sheltered ridge, a ring of threadgrass, another basin, another faint warmth that means water somewhere beyond the immediate reach of the eye. I would be large enough to carry lichen and small enough to be mistaken for a moving ledge in the low fog. I would be dark, heat-absorbing, warm to the touch, a furnace walking through its own industrial garden.

My eyes would know little about seeing. They would learn by shadow, by blur, by the faint bronze glow along cracks where heat reaches the surface. But my nostrils would taste metal and vapor. My hooves would read the stone for temperature and vibration. My tongue would know the difference between clean condensation and the dark coin-water trembling in a pan, and the difference between those and the thick, smoky fluid held inside an opened ember-rosette. I would not look at the world so much as metabolize it into paths.

I would stand—if standing were the word for such a settled, waiting weight—at the edge of the shallow pan while warm fog sits in the hollow like milk poured over stone. My breath would barely move the air. The fog would bead on my hide and run slow along my flank. A black lichen patch older than my mother’s mother’s mother might have taken root along my spine, not as burden but as belonging, a garden of dark fractal growth spreading over the heat of me. Threadgrass would catch in the mineral rim of one hoof. Salt-crush would settle in the folds of my skin. Tiny breath-mites would ride the warm exhalations from my nostrils, feeding on the vapor that I, without noticing, had already processed into a habitat.

I would move because several of us chose the same warm path. There would be no leader. There would be no decision spoken. There would only be the accumulated memory of heat in bodies that had crossed this shelf before. One animal would shift its weight toward a slight rise in stone temperature, another would answer by breathing deeper, another would tap once with its hoof, not loud, not signal, but a rhythm that traveled through rock more than air. The band would turn. It would be a movement so ordinary it would seem geological: the plateau reorganizing itself through feet.

I would not hurry.

There would be no need. The Oxide Step does not hurry; it cools, sweats, condenses, releases, remembers, begins again. I would walk in arcs, stopping often. At rest I would resemble a boulder that had learned to radiate heat. A dip-hunter might be watching from beneath a stone lip near the pan, still as a shadow, reading the tremor-larvae in the water by pressure. An ash-stalker might be lying in a seam not far from the ridge, its body matched to the temperature of the basalt, waiting for the moment when my flank becomes a broad heat-shape moving through a cooler field. I would not see it. I would feel only a change in the pattern of breath among the herd, a slight tightening of the way the fog moved around us, a subsonic hesitation in the ground. Then we would move differently, not fleeing, only redirecting, the way water redirects when a stone shifts beneath it.

I would lick salt from the pale hollows. I would press my rough tongue into dew pans. I would graze threadgrass where it unfurled in the moist breath of air, each needle stiff with mineral salts, scratching dry against my teeth while the soil beneath it remained strangely warm and damp. I would drink from water that looked like polished glass, not because it was beautiful, but because it was available, because its surface tension trembled with the movement of pan-floaters and the quiet work of microbial films. I would know the water by taste: iron, smoke, chalk, heat, the faint sweetness of dissolved life.

I would carry death and life in the same body.

Inside me, vast chambers of symbionts would break down tough plant-analogs and mineral-rich matter, producing heat as by-products, turning me into a walking process, a small refinery with a hide. My digestion would make me warm enough to alter the local weather on a scale only insects would appreciate. When I stood still, a microclimate would gather around me. Mites would climb toward the gradient of heat. A copperback might press itself against my shadowed leg, mistaking me for a warmer stone. A rosette seed carried in my dung might germinate in a hollow where a swarm of small wingless creatures had left behind a crust of decomposing phosphorus and metal.

I would pass through a warm drizzle that exists only above my immediate path, a single basin receiving its own exhalation while the land around it remains dry. The droplets would be fine, granular, tasting of metal and smoke. They would strike the obsidian dust at the edge of a shelf, hiss against hot stone, bead on salt crusts, and gather in the center of an ember-rosette until the leaves opened, slowly, as if the flower were dreaming in heat. I would drink from it. Not because the fluid is sweet, but because it contains a richness no tongue could name: water, dissolved salts, bacterial exudates, the memory of fog, the taste of a place turning itself into life.

At some point the plateau’s hidden cycles would bring a swarm.

It would begin as a dark dust moving along a crack. Then it would broaden, filling a hollow with thousands of tiny wingless bodies. It would not be chaos. It would be migration without geography, a chemical event in motion. I would stand among it as a broad ledge of living heat while they crawled over the shelves around me, grazing films, carrying spores, leaving molted skins that cracked like thin glass under my hoof. Some would climb onto my hide and be eaten by opportunistic ticks, or licked off by my flank as I shifted. The swarm would not frighten me. I would pass through it the way rain passes through fog: neither noticing, neither resisting. Afterward, the stone would smell briefly of burnt metal, and a pale blue-white trace might outline a basin where swarm-cores had decomposed into mineral light.

This is what I would be: not a stranger visiting the land, but the land using a body to move.

If there were an observer—someone shaped by another world, standing with eyes too dependent on sunlight—they would not see me clearly at first. They would see emptiness, then uncertainty, then perhaps a dark shape crossing a distant shelf. They would think: a rock moving. Or a stain lifting itself. Or no shape at all, only a heat-disturbance in the fog. They would lean closer because the plateau keeps its abundance sparse, because it teems only when read by patience. They would notice, finally, the slow tap of a hoof against mineral crust, felt before heard, a rhythm traveling through stone. They might mistake me for geology that had forgotten to stop. They would be almost right.

I would not build anything.

I would leave no wall, no road, no ruin, no artifact half-buried in dust. But my passage would be a kind of engineering. My feet would create shallow basins. My dung would concentrate salts and seed. My hide would carry lichen gardens and threadgrass fragments across windless shelves. My carcass, if I fell, would not rot into obscurity; it would become a temporary island of life. Copperbacks would feed on salt-crusted tissue. Lichen would spread over my ribs. A rosette might open once among my bones. Eventually the skeleton would bleach to pale white, then crack, then sink into mineral dust until it became indistinguishable from stone. Long afterward, some small animal might pass over the place where I lay and not know it was walking across the remains of a warm body that had once carried the entire step inside its metabolism.

Would I be intelligent?

If intelligence requires hands, tools, names, monuments, no. I would have none of those. But I would know the way water rises in a pan before the first bead appears. I would know a particular basin by the taste of its iron. I would recognize the thermal signature of a safe passage and avoid the seam where a predator’s heat shadow lingers too long. I would follow a chemical trail left by another body, not as a written instruction but as a remembered preference. I would teach, if that word can be used without speech, by moving in certain arcs, by pausing where another calf would pause, by being warm enough to be followed. My culture would not be in artifacts. It would be in the herd’s path. In the route taken long after the memory that chose it had returned to stone.

I would be a shelf-walker, then, but more exactly: I would be the place between animal and process. I would be the oxide lace spreading across my flank and the breath-mites riding my exhale. I would be the tremor-larvae in the water pan and the slow grind of my hooves against basalt. I would be the heat that attracts an ash-stalker and the body that makes the stalker necessary. I would be the warm drizzle falling only over the basin where I drink, because the plateau makes weather from its own breath, and my breath would be part of it. I would be the swarm crossing the dust and the silence afterward, when the stones tick as they cool.

Where would I find myself?

At the edge of a dark pan in a hollow between two fluted ridges, where fog condenses upward from warm stone and a single ember-rosette holds a cup of metallic moisture. The ground would be neither wet nor dry in any simple sense; it would be damp by condition, reflective by chemistry, warm by hidden veins, chalky by salt, slick by repeated condensation, brittle by cooling, alive by persistence. I would not stand in it as if I had arrived. I would be there the way the plateau is itself: without announcement, without completion, without the need to be witnessed in order to continue.

Who would I be?

Not a named creature. Not a hero. Not the last survivor. Not a god. Not even a self in the way a human imagines a self—bounded, urgent, separate. I would be one of the warm bodies through which the Oxide Step remembers where moisture is, where heat is rising, where death will eventually return a life into mineral and a mineral into life. If the world were asked, “Who lives there?” the truest answer might be no one. But if the question were changed—“What lives there?”—then the answer would be everywhere, and one small part of it would be me: broad-backed, slow, lichen-garded, tasting the fog through hoof and tongue, moving across the dark highland shelf in a rhythm older than language, not visiting the furnace, but feeding it.
