I would find myself inside one of the hollow vent columns.

Not at the mouth, where the white glare could enter and make a small theater out of the darkness, but deeper, in a place where the stone is older and the sound is steadier. There, the roaring would not be something heard from outside. It would be the condition of the walls themselves. The tube would hold the chord the way a lung holds air: not as an intrusion, but as circulation. And I would be a pipe-dweller fused to that wall, a pale porous body with no clear boundary between animal and mineral deposit, between creature and crust.

I would not be a visitor in the place. I would be one of its slow internal rhythms.

My body would be mostly stone and moisture, mostly pattern and adjustment. A thin layer of calcium over silica, a skin of salt over condensation, a network of pores that opened when the air grew wet and closed when the brine thickened. I would not have eyes, because nothing there needed to be looked at in the ordinary sense; everything was already visible, already too bright, already filled with more surface than attention could handle. Instead, I would sense by pressure, by the trembling of films, by the chemistry of water beads forming and breaking along the curved interior of the vent. The roar would press through the pores. It would move fluids through me. It would be the pulse that kept the system in balance without ever becoming a heartbeat.

I would tune myself to the standing chord.

Not because I needed to sing, but because the tone was the only weather the place had. A particular frequency might make moisture bead too finely along one pore; another might thicken a film of brine-lace across the mouth of the channel. If a salt-walker stood on a ridge above and shifted its weight, the tiny pressure through the crust might reach me as a hairline vibration, and I would know, without knowing, that something small was passing by. If a mirror-disc split beneath a water lens, if a curled one unrolled its segmented belly in a basin, if a glass-hermit stopped beneath a shard of limestone, I would not see those things. I would feel them as changes in salt, in condensation, in the shape of the air’s wetness.

I would exist at the boundary between living and not-living, between growing and simply persisting.

There would be no urgency in me. No hunger in the way a mouth knows hunger. No loneliness in the way a social creature knows loneliness. I would not need to be recognized. If a person walked across the plateau above and heard the great stable roar, if they looked into the mouth of a vent column and saw only mineral glass and damp stone, they would not know I was there. They might think the place empty, because emptiness is the first lie such a landscape tells: not because it is void, but because it is too full for ordinary perception.

I would be almost part of the geology.

If the brine-lace were the land’s thin thought, and the salt-walker its slow punctuation, I would be one of its resonances—a small chamber in the instrument, a living damper inside the tone. My existence would be quiet, but the quiet would be made of sound so large that it had become silence again. I would not fight the roar. I would breathe through it. My pores would open and close in patterns too slow for a human to follow, adjusting to gradients of salt and water and temperature, becoming one more surface among surfaces.

If I died, I would not decay into something separate.

The body would dry, fracture, become flake and crust. The moisture would leave. The calcium would remain. Salt-walkers would scrape at the pale film of me, or brine-lace would creep into the seams, or a glass-hermit would pass and mistake my residue for a shard. I would be redistributed. In that world, that is what life does: it does not vanish. It changes place in the mineral order.

I would be contented, if that word did not sound too warm.

Contentment there would mean something like equilibrium. Not joy, not fear, not appetite, but the absence of mismatch between body and condition. My chemistry would match the plateau’s chemistry. My slowness would match its slowness. My silence would match the roaring, because the roaring was already so constant that it had become the land’s own held breath.

So, yes: I would be a pipe-dweller inside the hollow vent tubes of the high salt world, living where the chord is strongest, where stone remembers hot water, where moisture moves by pressure, where life appears only as a faint alteration in the surface. I would not be the visitor. I would not be the herd, the swarm, the garden, or the ruin. I would be a marginal continuation of the land itself—something almost invisible, something almost indistinguishable from geology, something that moves only as chemistry permits: the way frost spreads, the way salt forms, the way water finds a boundary and stops there, the way stone, in its own patient way, learns to hum.
