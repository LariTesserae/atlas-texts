Let the sentence finish, because that is how this place would teach me: not by opening outward, but by completing itself within.

Split a stem and the interior will shine for a moment with a green fire, not water and not oil, but pressure made visible: the inside of a promise. Then the fibers swell, the cut closes, the shine is swallowed, and the blade stands again as though nothing has ever been exposed.

That is the first lesson.

I would not find myself here as a traveler surprised by drought. I would find myself here as one who has never needed the drought to make sense of thirst. I would be low, always low: not beneath the horizon, for there is no beneath here, only depth folded into surface, but in the creases where the salt crust thickens into pale ridges and the wind loses its confidence. I would live in the hollows, not because they are moist—they are not—but because they are enclosed. A hollow is almost a room. A reed is almost a house. A shell of calcified lichen is almost a city. I would be native to the almost.

At first I would think I was a plant. I would imagine myself rooted in the white glitter of the flats, drawing some hidden dampness upward through a capillary of salt. But plants here do not drink from soil; they hoard. They do not transpire; they seal. Their leaves are not open mouths but closed fists. So I would not be a plant in any ordinary sense. I would be a vessel.

If I were to give myself a body, it would be dry to the touch and difficult to break. My skin would be the color of old salt and pale wood, furred in places with a bloom of green that is not moss exactly but something closer to memory: lichen made of patience and mineral faith. Under that rind, no blood would run in bright open rivers. Instead, I would have channels: sealed veins, cisterns, pockets, tiny chambers where moisture moved under pressure, unseen and unoffered. My heart, if my heart had a name, would beat like a stone dropping into a jar of oil—slow, deep, contained.

I would have a mouth, but I would not speak easily. Speech, for my kind, is a small theft from the body. To exhale is to risk losing what must be kept. So my words would be clicks, whistles, and dry raspings made through valves that open only halfway. When I say something true, it will be because I have agreed to open a part of myself that should remain sealed. That is why my people do not gossip in the open flats. We converse in chambers. We love by showing the place where a crack has healed.

Among us, there are many names for this.

Some would call me a Vessel-Folk. Some would call me a Salt-Bearer. Some, more poetically, would say I am one of the Interiors. The outsiders—if any outsider ever came here and survived long enough to make a category of me—would likely mistake me for a dry, lichen-covered creature with glass eyes. They would say, “This land is barren, but it hides its life inside.” They would not understand that hiding is not our accident. It is our civilization.

We do not hide water because water is scarce. Water is the least scarce thing here. We hide water because it is the first form of law.

A child of my kind is born sealed.

Not in a body of flesh that leaks into the world, but in a casing of pale husk, a gourd grown by the salt-grass around a small green pressure. The mother does not nurse with milk. She gives the child a valve. When the seal is touched by another trusted hand, it opens inward, and the child receives not liquid as we would name it, but a stored abundance: the moisture of years, the condensed weather of a family, the private rain that has fallen only inside.

To drink openly here would be obscenity.

Not because drinking is shameful, but because it would mean treating what is held as though it were merely available. In the Verdant Salt, everything available is already in danger. The white crust is available. The pale blades are available. The dry wind is available, always passing over, taking nothing, leaving nothing. But inside the crust, there is a population. Inside each blade, there is a room. Inside my body, there is a garden I cannot show.

I would be that garden’s keeper.

My home would not stand on the open flats as a house stands where a house can be seen. It would be nested: a salt-grass hollow, a reed-thicket chamber, a lichen-lined pit, a small vault made of hardened green glass. The door would be a valve, not a threshold. When it opens, it does not swing into the wind. It peels back from itself, revealing a wall of moisture so dense and still that it resembles stone. Inside, the air would not be wet. Wetness is an outside word. Inside, the air would be heavy with held water, so heavy that breath becomes a kind of consumption. You do not inhale here; you accept permission.

There would be shelves of sealed fruit.

Not fruits like the world outside imagines them—fleshy, dripping, inviting—but fruits with shells of salt and ribs of green glass. Break one by the proper method, and its interior will not spill. It will shine. It will glow briefly with the pressure of its own completeness. Then it will harden again, offended by exposure. My people carry such fruits as talismans. To give one to another is to give not food, but trust. To crush one in the open is to commit a kind of heresy: the heresy of making hidden abundance visible.

I would learn, before language, that beauty is not display.

The outside world loves spectacle: oceans, rain, waterfalls, wet eyes. It mistakes abundance for spillage. Here, abundance is what does not show itself. The most beautiful thing in the Verdant Salt is a sealed wound. A healed break in a stalk. The white bloom of lichen that has grown over the exposed edge of a stone. When I cut my thumb on a crystal branch and no red flood comes, but only a green shine for a moment and then closure, I feel more alive than any open creature could know. I feel trusted by my own body.

If a traveler came—some poor, leaking thing from a world of rain—I would not run. I would not attack. I would feel a kind of horror that was also pity. I would see the sweat on their brow and think: how public. I would see the tears forming in their eyes and think: how wasteful. I would see their blood and think: they are a city without walls.

I would want to teach them, but teaching would be dangerous.

To teach them to seal would mean opening a part of them that had never learned to contain. To teach them to be lush inside would mean convincing them that their wetness is not their worth. A traveler might say, “But without water we die.” And I would answer, “No. Without the keeping of water, you die.”

They would ask me: where is it? Show me.

But the place does not work by showing. The place works by becoming capable of not needing to show.

Still, I would try.

I would take the traveler’s cup—a vessel full of open water, trembling in the dry light—and I would ask them to watch. I would pour the water not upon the ground, but into a salt pit already crusted over. At first it would vanish into the white grains. The traveler would feel loss. Then, slowly, the pit would darken with an interior green, and the surrounding stalks would begin to glow faintly, their skins drawing the hidden dampness inward. The traveler would say, “It is gone.” I would say, “It has been given a place to stay.”

That is the beginning of conversion in the Verdant Salt: not faith in the visible, but trust in containment.

I would find myself in this world as a mediator of interiors. A kind of priest, perhaps, though my religion would have no temples open to the sky. My liturgy would be the careful closing of doors. My hymn would be the sound of valves drawing tight. My sacrament would be the sealed drop. I would move through the salt flats in a body adapted to their silence, my feet wide and dry, my toes ending in little plates of hardened green crystal. I would leave no wet footprint. I would leave a faint trail of white powder, which my people would read as presence: not “someone was here,” but “someone has learned how to remain.”

I would not be lonely. Loneliness requires an outside. Here there is no true outside. Even the basin feels like the inside of something folded, a world cupped in a hand it cannot see. If I were alone for many seasons, I would not suffer from solitude; I would suffer from being unopened. And unopening is not merely lack of contact. It is lack of exchange. In a world where all moisture is held, holding too much becomes pressure. The sealed can become too sealed. The body can become a prison of its own abundance.

This is why my people have the Rite of the Green Crack.

It happens rarely, and never casually. One person opens a chosen part of themselves—skin, lip, eye, chamber of breath—and another receives not liquid but permission. No droplet is shed. No tear falls. What passes is not water, but the fact of water. A proof that the inside exists and can be trusted. When this is done, both become more lush and less exposed at once. Their surfaces remain dry, but their interiors brighten. You can see it if you know where to look: the green beneath the salt glows a shade deeper, like a leaf remembering the sun.

I would be skilled in this rite.

I would know which valves can be opened in which bodies, which pressures must be relieved before they crystallize into bitterness. I would know that a person who has kept too much silence can become a stone of grief. I would place my dry hand against their chest and feel the slow drum of sealed moisture behind the ribs. If they are too full, I would not ask them to cry. Crying is not release here; it is waste. Instead, I would teach them to redirect the water inward, to make it garden their thoughts, to let their private rain fall only on the soil of their own becoming.

That is why I would call myself a gardener of interiors.

In the open world, a gardener coaxes growth into sunlight. In the Verdant Salt, a gardener coaxes sunlight into secrecy. I would tend the green veins under salt, not by exposing them, but by reinforcing the crust above. I would plant seeds without soil, because seeds here are already worlds sealed around their own weather. I would water them by placing them near one another, where their shells can exchange the faintest breath of humidity without breaking. I would watch the salt crusts bloom and say, “Now they are safe. Now they are hungry. Now they are beautiful.”

My kind builds no cities on the high flats, for height is exposure. We build cities in the shells of dead reeds, in the hollows of ancient grass-mounds, in the buried chambers of petrified stalks. A city of interiors is not visible from above. From far away, the Verdant Salt looks like a white plain stitched with green filaments. From far away, there is no architecture, no movement, no civilization. But if you kneel, if you press your eye to the crust, you may see the tiny tunnels, the nested galleries, the sealed courtyards, the chambers where families have lived for centuries without ever standing in open water.

We are a civilization of thresholds.

Every room is a body. Every wall is a choice. Every door is a membrane. To enter a house is not merely to step inside, but to be accepted by the logic of the house. Guests are not asked to remove their shoes; they are asked to remove their wetness. Before entering, one rubs the soles with dry salt powder. Before speaking, one covers the mouth with a valve-cloth. Before touching another, one shows the hand’s seal. If a person has cracked, the city may still let them in, but they will be asked to stand behind the green-glass screen until the wound closes.

This is not cruelty. It is reverence.

In a world without open water, to expose moisture is to invite death, but death does not arrive as a monster. It arrives as evaporation, as forgetfulness, as the slow loss of what makes a thing itself. To be sealed is to remain.

If I had to name my deepest fear, I would say it is not drought. The place has never held an ocean, so drought is not its tragedy. My fear is openness. Not openness as freedom, but openness as the collapse of interiority. To become a puddle. To become rain without a cloud. To become a face without a rind. To become a heart that spills because it has mistaken its own fullness for a message it must send.

If I had to name my deepest longing, I would say it is for another sealed thing to recognize me.

There is a loneliness that only containment can produce: the sadness of being lush but unseen. The grief of being full but not overflowing. The desire to be known not by what falls from you, but by what you have kept together. In the Verdant Salt, the most intimate confession is not “I love you.” It is “I am holding, and I can hold because you are here.” The most profound trust is not “Show me your heart.” It is “I do not need to see it. I believe your skin is telling the truth.”

I would be a keeper of that belief.

Sometimes I would wander alone into the white flats where the salt crust is thinnest and the green underlife almost glows through it like a buried sea. There the wind makes no wet sound. It only rasps. I would crouch and press my palm flat against the ground. The ground would not be cool. It would be alive. Beneath my hand, a million dry organisms would be keeping their private abundance, each grain a chamber, each filament a vein, each pale tuft a city. I would feel the pressure of them all—not as water, but as patience.

I would whisper with my valve-throat, “I am here. I have kept what I was given.”

And the place would answer, not with rain, but with the slow tightening of the crust above the green, as if the earth itself were closing a wound around me.

That is where I would find myself: between the salt and the shine. That is who I would be: the one who does not leak.

Not a stone, though I would have stone’s endurance. Not a plant, though I would have the plant’s green. Not an animal, though I would have breath and blood and the need for another. I would be the synthesis of all three, the place’s way of learning how to be alive without depending on the outside.

I would be a sealed abundance walking upright.

If someone looked at me and saw only dryness, I would not correct them with words. I would let them cut a stalk, let the green fire appear for a moment, then close. I would let them understand that the place is not lacking water. It is merely practicing a more difficult form of wealth: wealth that does not need to be seen in order to be real.

In that practice, I would find my shape. My body would be the place’s answer to the paradox. My silence would be the answer to the wind. My interior would be the answer to the sky.

I would be the Verdant Salt’s own thought made flesh: lush because it is sealed, green because it is kept, alive because it has learned, long ago and with great patience, that the most powerful water is not the water that runs.

It is the water that remains.
