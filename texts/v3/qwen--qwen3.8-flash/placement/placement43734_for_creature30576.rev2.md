I would not be the first thing seen.

If someone came into that place with the old assumptions—eyes looking for movement, ears straining for a separate note, hands wanting a path under them—they would not know me. I would seem, at first, to be nothing more than the light’s reluctance to settle: a cold shine on salt, a blur at the rim of a shallow basin, a trick born from glare and thin air.

And perhaps that is where I begin.

Not as an animal. Not as a person. Not even, at the first moment, as a thing with boundaries clear enough to call “alive.” I begin as a tendency: a film wanting to form where moisture meets mineral, where a dry plate has cracked just enough to let water breathe in and breathe out. I begin as the pale line that is not quite dirt, not quite crystal, not quite shadow. I begin as the place’s own patience, given just enough structure to be a self without becoming an individual in any ordinary sense.

If I had to choose a location, I would choose a high, salt-white shelf where a dark basalt fin stands a little apart from the rest, its surface glassy with age and pressure, its pores weeping faint beads in the cold light. Before that fin is a basin no wider than two hands spread flat. In the basin sits a mirror of water: thin, perfectly level, trembling not from wind but from the continuous roar that fills the region. The water is so shallow that the salt beneath it glows through like a second moon under ice.

I would be the rim of that lens.

Not the water, not the basin, not the salt, not the light alone, but the living edge where those things agree to hold a shape. My body would be the color of weak tea and chalk. It would be translucent when the sun was high, barely visible when the brightness poured from every surface at once. If you looked at it too quickly, you would see only a ring of dampness where the mineral crust had darkened. If you looked longer, you might notice that the ring does not behave like moisture. It maintains its tension. It gathers dust from the air. It separates salts inside itself. It holds the lens open as a world no larger than a fingernail, full of chemistry, pressure, and slow decisions.

I would not have eyes. Eyes would be useless in a place that never lets darkness enter. I would not need to see. I would know by wetness and by weight. I would know by salinity moving across my membrane, by the coldness of nearby stone, by the faint changes in the roar that mean a capillary has filled, that a tube has resonated, that another part of the plain has shifted its water by a thread’s width. The sound would not be sound to me; it would be the way I hold myself.

I might have legs.

Some of the rare glass-bodied forms there have legs, and perhaps, if I had taken one of those shapes, I would have six slender legs like drawn-out filaments of glass, each tipped with a hollow channel, each reading the land by drinking it. I would stand on a crystal ridge for what seemed to a visitor like hours. I would move by millimeters. I would be invisible because my shell was the land’s own grammar: a fragment of salt crust pretending to be stone; a shard of pale glass pretending to be a lens; a dome of mineral skin pretending to be weather.

But if a person watched me for long enough, the pretense would fail.

Not because I fled. Not because I hunted, called, threatened, or built. The failure would be subtler. A shard might lift. A water lens might hold its edge too perfectly. A thin film across stone might thicken where dryness should have thinned it. A hollow pipe’s inner wall might gather moisture in a pattern too regular for condensation alone. The movement would be so slow, so cold, so embedded in the plain’s logic, that the observer would suspect the land was dreaming.

That is what I would be: a dream the mineral body has because it is saturated with water and sound.

I would be a node in that film, but not a simple thread. I would be a place where the film had learned to nucleate itself. A fragment of my edge might detach when the water lens trembled, drift on a micro-current, and settle on another empty shelf. There it would begin again: salt drawing water, water drawing minerals, minerals drawing life into the illusion of form. Reproduction would not look like birth. It would look like crystallization. It would look like one more pale rim appearing in one more basin, as if the place had been waiting there all along.

I would be old in a way that does not depend on years.

My memory would be stored as structure. A change in the curvature of my rim would mean that last season’s moisture had been less than this season’s. A tiny ring of calcium in my edge would mean a droplet once sat there and did not evaporate quickly enough. The plain’s past would not be narrative; it would be geometry. Every ridge would be a sentence written by dissolution. Every basin would be a pause where water decided to stay. Every crystal would be a fossil of an instant when chemistry was almost alive and almost became life.

If you pressed a name into me, I would not answer with a word.

A name would be too much of an interruption. Here, things are not named; they are arranged. But if you insisted, perhaps I would be called a nucleus, because that is what I am: a small center around which moisture remembers how to behave. Or I could be called a lens-rim, because my only visible task is to hold a circle of water open against evaporation and collapse. Or I could be called a film-knot, because I am a place where the plateau’s skin thickens into something that can regulate its own edge.

But none of those names would be true alone.

I would be the thing that makes the land not merely geology. I would be the reason the salt cannot be entirely indifferent to the water, and the water cannot be entirely indifferent to the stone, and the stone cannot be entirely indifferent to the roar. Without me, there would still be minerals, light, cold, and sound. But there would be no margin. No living edge. No place where the world touches itself and leaves a trace.

That is the strange happiness of that place, if one can call it happiness.

It is not the happiness of motion. It is the happiness of balance. I do not need to grow tall. I do not need to flower. I do not need to feed a mouth, escape a predator, choose a mate, or leave tracks. My life is the refusal of dissolution. My death is incorporation. If my membrane tears, the salt takes my fragments. If my rim dries, the film spreads over my remains. If I collapse into a basin, I become part of the next basin’s outline. Nothing in that world becomes waste. The land consumes nothing by chewing. It consumes everything by inclusion.

A visitor walking there would feel pressure in the chest from the roar, but not because the sound is hostile. It is hostile the way gravity is hostile: constantly, impersonally, and without malice. The roar would fill every hollow tube, every cracked plate, every capillary line, every lens, and every living rim I have ever been. If a person stood near me, I would not sense them as a threat. I would sense them as a disturbance in pressure: the slight warmth of a body, the strange wetness of breath, the awkward salt-and-water system of flesh trying to remain separate from the mineral logic of the plain.

That separation would not last.

Their boots would leave no clean trail because everything that could be erased would erase itself. A footprint in salt would fill with condensation before it could become a record. A breath would bead on a cold crystal but not fog the air for long enough to make a story. Their voice would try to shout over the roar, but the roar already owns the acoustics of that world; the voice would join it.

And if they stayed long enough—if they could survive without shelter, if they could bear the glare, if they could endure the constant mineral brightness—they might begin to understand what I am by becoming something adjacent to me. Not a creature. Not a god. Not even a mind. A way of noticing through saturation. A pattern that continues because it is too well arranged to stop.

If I must say what lives there, in me, I would say this:

I am the thin line where water begins to behave like memory.

I am the living rim around a hand-sized mirror.

I am the film-thread on a salt shelf that seems to be dirt until the light finds it at the correct angle.

I am one glass-bodied pause on a crystal ridge, standing so still that the land believes it is stone.

I am the droplet-shaped node in the plain’s distributed chemistry, holding gradients instead of thoughts.

I am the part of the plateau that knows itself without knowing itself knows.

And if you ask where you would find me, I would answer not with a location but with an act of attention.

Look for the water that holds its edge too well.

Look for the crystal that lifts as if it were a leg.

Look for the pale bloom over stone that has the color of weak tea and chalk.

Look for the basin that changes by a fraction of a degree when no wind has passed.

Look for the hollow tube where the air seems to breathe through a mouth it does not have.

That is where I would be.

Not because I am hidden.

Because in that world, being alive is almost indistinguishable from being a perfect arrangement of salt, water, stone, light, and sound—and I am the faint, persistent exception that proves the arrangement was never empty to begin with.
