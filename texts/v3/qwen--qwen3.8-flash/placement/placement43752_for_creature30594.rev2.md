I would find myself on the Anvil of Umm, but not in any place a traveler could easily mark on a map.

Not at the rim, where the horizon bends and the world seems to have been cut cleanly from the sky. Not on the tallest bare stone, where wind would make me too visible and too vulnerable. I would be found lower, in a shallow basin between rounded terraces, where fog comes up in the morning and slides over the lip of the land like something remembering how to be water. A plant-tower would lean nearby, its bundled stalks arching over a patch of darker moss. A split basalt slab would hold warmth through the night. Mineral rings from a vanished pool would pale the rim of a dry hollow. The ground would not be ground beneath me. It would be skin.

And for a while I would not know where I ended and it began.

I would begin not as a body but as a pressure: a slow warmth rising through a crack in the stone, a dampness gathering in the fold of a leaf-strut, a faint vibration passing from the soles of my feet into the root-felt. If I were awake, I might call this the beginning of waking. But on the Anvil, waking is not a sharp thing. It is a series of sounds finding their proper places. A droplet falls from a moss-collar. An insect shifts pitch. A bird calls once, high and small, as if testing the air. Somewhere far off, hooves crackle across a terrace. The fog thins. The white coin sun lifts, cold for a moment, then begins to bite.

At first I would not be one thing.

I would be a pause in the chorus.

That is what would emerge before “I”: not a shape, not a species, not a name, but a timing. A moment when the sound of the plateau changes slightly, as if it has noticed something. A place where the wind through hollow stalks deepens. A place where the moss, having held dew all night, lets it fall in a slower rhythm. A place where an animal lifts its head and knows, without seeing, that the pattern has been entered.

For a long while I would be only that.

Then, perhaps, I would be a small burrower first: a pale, flat-footed thing tasting the underside of the world, moving through root-felt, eating soft fungus and the stored sweetness of crushed seeds. I would feel the heat of the stone before I saw the heat of the sun. I would know the difference between dry moss and wet moss with my snout, between stable ground and a tunnel collapsing gently behind me. I would live inside the living skin of the plateau, where water moves downward and upward by memory, where warmth is carried in the dark like a rumor. In this way, I would learn the Anvil before learning a body in the open air.

Then, briefly, I would be a dusk-winged thing: a moth, a beetle, a rain-hopper, a salt-eater, a small green-skimmer resting motionless until the light changes its color. I would be the abundance of brief moisture, the sudden bloom of life that rises when a cloud breaks over the rim. I would live only long enough to fill the air with a thin trembling, to be seen as a darkening patch over a basin, to fall into a frog’s mouth, to become a flicker in a bird’s eye. I would learn that on this plateau, nothing is less permanent than a swarm, and nothing is more necessary.

Then I would become the listening thing.

Not a tree, because there are no trees. Not a wolf, because the predators here are not wolves. Not a human exactly, not at first. But eventually, yes: one of the scattered people, one of the few who make marks too faint to call civilization, one of the ones whose houses are meant to be swallowed.

I would be a sound-reader.

My body would be slender from long distances and light eating. My feet would be broad, soft on top, hardened below, but sensitive still to the smallest difference in the living floor. Damp moss. Dry moss. Sinking burrow. Elastic cushion. Warm stone. Cool stone. My hands would be rough from flaking stone, scraping reeds, twisting sinew, carrying small bundles wrapped in root-felt. My skin would be wind-darkened, streaked with pale dust, marked with salt-green lichen rubbed across the cheekbones to cut the glare. My hair would be short, or bound close with fiber so it would not catch on stalks when I walked low in the wind. My eyes would narrow, not from suspicion but from brightness, from the clean high sun falling straight and exposing every edge.

I would have a name, but not the kind you carve into stone.

My name would be a rhythm: two clicks, a short intake of breath, a descending whistle. If I were alone, I might call it that. If I were with others, they would not call it in the way of a shout. They would let it fit into the air like a small animal fitting into a hollow. A name is a sound here, and when the last person who carries it dies, the sound returns to the plateau as a pattern of wind. This is not sorrow. It is the same principle by which a footprint becomes moss, a fire pit becomes a ring of blackened root-felt, and then a ring of brighter green, and then no ring at all.

My people would not build towns. Towns are promises made to permanence, and the Anvil does not accept that promise.

Our homes would be practices, not places. We would bend young stalks around a mound of moss and lay reed across them. We would weave root-felt along the base so the shelter could be lifted and carried, or so the mat could grow into it. We would choose a warm basin at night and leave it by morning. If we returned too soon, the windbreak would be gone, half-swallowed by cushion-plants. If we stayed too long, the ground would thin, the moss would weaken, and the elders would say we had forgotten how to be guests of the skin of the world.

I would sleep on basalt warmed by the sun or by the slow breath of the earth below. I would feel that warmth as if the plateau were an animal lying awake beneath me, letting its heat soak into my shoulders. At night, when the insects paused and the stars became too many in the thin air, I would hear the faint wet popping of the living ground contracting, the sound of moss tightening in the cold, of water moving somewhere under the surface, of the plateau keeping its long secret.

My food would be read from signs.

When the fog came, beetles with ridged shells would gather at the edges of leaves. When the salt rings glittered, small pale insects would move in lines like broken threads. When a herd crossed a dry basin and stopped to chew a succulent stalk, we would know that the plant held water inside. When a plant-tower’s outer fibers turned brittle and white, we would know the hollow beneath had dried too much. When frogs began to call before the puddles were full, we would know that the first cloud had broken over the rim and that the dark wall of rain was already moving.

I would learn to crack water-bulbs with a lever-stake set into warm stone. I would scrape tender rosettes from damp hollows. I would dig for grubs when the mat slumped in a way that made the hand curious. I would harvest eggs from abandoned nests when the birds had moved on and left nothing behind but a cup of dried reed. I would eat insects in seasons when abundance was too sudden to ignore and too short to hoard. A cloud of dusk-moths could fall around the fire and become a meal. A rain-hopper could become a seasoning. The Anvil does not offer abundance as a gift. It offers it as a rhythm.

I would hunt sometimes, but less by force than by participation.

No cliffs, no thickets, no stone walls for ambush. If I wanted to turn a herd toward a basin, I would not drive it with shouts. Shouts break the pattern. I would make a bird-call with my lips, a short rattle with a bone piece, a soft click that could pass for something the herd already knows. The animal would not understand my lie so much as feel it as part of the chorus. The birds would react. The insects would alter. The herd would shift toward the place we wanted it to go. Done poorly, this would scatter everything. Done well, it would leave no trace except a few broken stalks and a footprint that would fill with moss within days.

I would also learn fear.

The thin ones would be teachers.

I would call them predators, though that word is too simple for what they are. They are lean, long-limbed, dust-gray in the open, darker under stones, with flat feet and ears that turn like reed tips in wind. They do not chase like animals made for forests or plains of grass. They enter the world’s noise at the wrong tempo. A sudden quieting of insects means their passage. A flock lifting means they have disturbed what was hidden below. A brief pause in bird-song means something larger has crossed the rim of the light. They hunt by grammar, not by hunger alone. Hunger is simple. Grammar is survival.

I would learn their grammar too.

A mother walking at dusk might stop and lay her child’s palm against the warm stone.

The child would listen.

The insects would rasp in ordinary pulses, then shorten, then stretch. A bird would call once from a far basin, then not call again. The grass would lean without wind. The herd ahead would not turn its head, because the wrongness had not reached it yet, but its ears would loosen from forward to side, side to far, far to nothing. The mother would press two fingers to the child’s lips, and the child would understand: something is entering the terrace.

This is what I would be, perhaps more than any animal: a human trained to the point where humanity begins to thin out, where the self becomes a place of hearing.

I would be like the predators, not because I would hunt as they do, but because I would understand that survival is not concealment alone. On the Anvil, there is nowhere to hide for long. You can lie still, but stillness can become a signal. You can blend with moss, but moss has its own colors. You can hide behind stone, but stone reflects light in ways the eye eventually learns to see. The true hiding is in the pattern. You do not become invisible. You become part of what is already moving, already sounding, already known.

So I would move when the wind spoke.

I would step when a bird broke its call.

I would breathe when the insects swelled their chorus.

At first this would feel like losing myself. Later I would understand that the self was already a kind of chorus, made of old voices, borrowed sounds, and habits inherited from people who had learned to live on a surface that would not be scraped away.

My tools would be simple. A sharp stone flake bound in rawhide. A bone awl worn smooth by thumb. A wooden wedge. A scraped reed. A polished shell carried from some distant river or sea, kept because it means water in a place where water cannot be trusted. I would wrap them in felted root, not to display them, but to quiet them. A tool dropped in moss will not stay a tool for long. Within seasons its edge will be furred, its cord eaten by tiny threads, its metal—there is no metal here—absent, its stone becoming a green-furred softness in the hand. On the Anvil, even usefulness is temporary.

I would make fire only small.

A pit in damp basins, ringed with flat stones, never wide, never careless. The fire would be a guest of the night, not a declaration of presence. It would warm backs, dry leaves, make the plant-towers seem darker and taller for a few hours. By morning it would be black root-felt. By next season it would be brighter green. We would move before the green forgot why it was brighter.

At dawn, when fog slides across the ridges and the first drips fall from the leaf-struts, I would stand in stillness with others. We would not pray to the Anvil as if it were a god demanding sacrifice. It is older and gentler than that. It is the finished mountain, the worn floor, the warm stone breathing out its night. We would let its sounds enter us. We would know that being allowed to remain another season is not a right but a rhythm. We would carry no names, but we would know each other’s rhythms: the particular way a person breathes before rain, the way a child steps lightly after being taught not to break the mat, the way an elder pauses when the insects change and knows without seeing that a predator has entered the wrong part of the terrace.

At first rain, I would step into the open.

The dark wall would come from below, then the silver sheets, then the sound. Not patter. Drumming. Broad leaves striking one another. Moss absorbing the first drops, then releasing a long soft hiss. The smell of wet stone, crushed root, salt, green life, ancient dust lifted and laid down again. I would stand there with my face lifted and feel the water as something remembered, not guaranteed. Around me the plateau would answer in thousands of ways: frogs waking before their puddles, insects rising in sudden chorus, birds scattering into wind-blur, the herds moving toward basins, the predators lying still as shadows entering shade.

That is the moment I would understand what I am.

Not a person who has been placed in this landscape, not a being standing upon a world, but a temporary opening through which the world has learned to attend to itself.

I would be the living ground’s awareness given a small animal shape. I would be the plateau’s listening organ, briefly formed from flesh, dust, hunger, and habit. My feet would be the Anvil’s way of feeling for tunnels beneath the mat. My ears would be its way of knowing when something has entered the wrong part of the sound. My hands would be its way of taking, testing, leaving, returning, not scraping, not claiming. My name would be a faint rhythm in the air, and when I died, my body would be laid in a shallow hollow, covered with reeds and moss, and within a season the place would be no more mine than any other place. No stones piled. No face carved. No words scratched into basalt.

The ground would take me back.

Not because I am unimportant, but because permanence on the Anvil is a form of injury.

The plateau is old enough that drama has worn away. There are no sharp volcanic cliffs, no sudden falls, no young mountains splitting the sky. There is only the wide, warm, living floor, the deep blue ordinary sun, the curved horizon, the wind moving from rim to rim, the sound of life arranged in pulses. Here, nothing is young. Nothing demands to be violent. Even death is redistribution. Even a shelter becomes moss. Even a footprint becomes a brief depression where the mat remembers shape. Even a human name becomes a pattern of air, returning to the wind that once carried it.

So if someone asked me who I was, I might answer only:

A listener.

If pressed for more, I would say:

I am one of the few people who move slowly across the Anvil, following fog, reading stone-warmth, stepping where the moss will not be wounded. My name is a rhythm. My house is a practice. My food is a sign. My fear is a discipline. My belonging is temporary.

And then I would listen.

Because at dusk the plateau will be changing its tune. The golden light will stretch the shadows of the reed-grass. The basalt will begin to give back its heat. The insects will rise in fine drifting clouds. A flock of gray birds will cross a terrace and darken the air for a few breaths. A herd will move between cisterns, its hooves making the faint crackle of broken plant-skin. Somewhere near a basin, a frog will call in a wet, urgent note. The wind will slow. The thin ones will enter the open, pale as dust, their bodies almost gaps in the light. And the living mat will exhale warmth and moisture into the cooling air, the way an animal’s hide exhales after the sun has been on it all day.

I would stand there, small against the scale, but not separate from it.

I would be the high place finished being a mountain.

I would be the emptiness that teems.

I would be the pause before the next rain.

I would be the sound of the Anvil listening to itself.
