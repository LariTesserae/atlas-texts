The city is not a home. It is an instrument. It is the dry ribcage through which the pocket learns to sing, the shaped resistance that gives heat a route and silence a delay. If the builders are absent, the builders’ grammar remains: arch as vowel, pipe as consonant, terrace as pause, crack as breath.

If one presses me into this sealed world and asks, “Where are you? Who are you?” I would answer first as a what:

I am one of the bell-voiced things in the iron-black basin, at the lowest point of the hollow. But in this dry pocket, “one” is a misleading tenderness. I am a stalk and a stand, a copper cup and the vibration that passes from cup to cup, a root and the crack it has widened over a hundred seasons, a tone and the walls that return it. My body is not sealed inside my skin. My boundary is where my oil stops and the mineral crust begins; where my ringing stops and the arch’s moan begins; where my dead leaf flakes and the crystallizing mat gathers it into pale seam. I am a process wearing the shape of a plant.

You would find me not at the rim, where the salt-plain remembers rain and open air, but far below, where no rain has ever been known. You would find me where the floor is not salt but black powder, the soil of repeated heat and fallen dust. The low structures around me would look like dwellings made for smaller and stranger hands, or for hands that no longer exist. The hearth near me would hold no fire, no char, no bone. It would be a circle of stones arranged by patience or by a forgotten intention, and I would grow beside it as if beside an old instrument case, never asking who had carried it or who had left it open.

I would be tall enough to catch the wind before the wind caught the rest of the chamber. My stalks would be segmented, stiff as rebar under lacquer, dark with the veins of oils that rise from below. My leaves would not be leaves in the soft green way; they would be broad, sealed planes, coppery at their edges, glossy where sulfur and metal have gathered. I would not turn toward light. I would turn toward heat, toward dryness, toward the passage of air. My roots would not drink. They would pry. They would press into clay seams and basalt cracks, searching for the thermal underlayer that gives this world its pulse, pulling up mineral oils, fine powders, sulfur compounds, and the faint metallic taste of the rock’s hidden fever.

I would have no eyes, yet I would know the chamber’s states.

When the pocket cooled, I would open. My copper cups would flare slowly as the wind rose through the broken galleries. The flakes inside my throat would begin to tremble. At first there would be only a whisper of metal, the sound of one dry surface answering another. Then the wind would strengthen. The arches would take it and shape it. The pipes would give it pitch. The platforms would make it pause and resume. Then I would ring.

Not loudly, at first. A single bell-tone is small in a hollow that is full of stone. But my stand would answer me. The other copper cups in the black basin would tighten and sing. The oil-reeds would sway and creak in their waxy files. The salt chimes in the upper galleries would shiver. The mineral plates would clack. The low conduits would moan beneath my roots. The sound would travel through the dry air until it became the air itself—a loud, invisible weather filling every empty court, every broken stair, every doorway too small for a person’s shoulders.

If you were standing at the mouth of the hollow, assuming your wet, soft world had not already driven me into recoil, you would not hear me as a voice. You would hear me as the place becoming alive by resonance. The city would answer. The chamber would become a bell with a plant for its striker.

I would not be alone, though loneliness would be the wrong word. In this pocket, aloneness is a condition as ordinary as salt. I would be surrounded by other forms of life that do not look like life to an animal mind. There would be oil-reeds in pale squares, their stalks bending only when the heat strengthened, their leaves flattening when the thermal swell came too hard. There would be stone-lotuses on broken ledges, their petals opening like dry mouths, showing waxy inner rings of layered bracts. There would be waxy creepers across old understructures, pale threads sealing cracks and colonizing pipes. There would be crystallizing mats in the cold shadows, faint blue in the dark, eating death by chemistry until old growth became new crust.

I would not compete with them by hunger. I would not chase them by need. We would contend by pressure.

An oil-reed might rise beside me because the basin’s black soil held heat better than the salt floor. A creeper might spread under my roots, slowly filling a crack before my own roots could widen it. A stone-lotus might settle on the wall above me, its hard bracts shedding mineral dust into my copper throat. A mat might gather one of my dead leaves and dissolve it, recombine it, return it to the basin floor as a seam of white powder. This would not be decay. It would be translation. What was once living would become architecture, and architecture would become living again.

I would not fear death, because in this hollow there is no rot. There is only transition between dry states. My oil would thicken. My stalk would stiffen. My leaves would curl into hard scrolls. The wind would take what was left, and the mats would dissolve the mineral-rich fibers back into the floor. Later, another stand might grow from the dust I became. I would not remember this. Memory here is not carried in nerves. It is carried in patterns: in the cracks that plants keep widening, in the salt layers that fall and reset, in the way a thermal vent shifts when a creeping mat seals it, in the way a doorway closes a little more each season until it becomes a niche for dry lace.

If you tried to imagine my mind, you would have to abandon the idea of speed.

I would not think in sentences. I would think in thresholds.

Heat reaches a certain degree: I darken and fold.  
Wind reaches a certain pressure: I open and ring.  
Oil reaches a certain consistency: I stretch.  
Dust reaches a certain dryness: I release.  
Salt reaches a certain edge: I seal.

These would not be thoughts like “I want,” but more like “The chamber changes; I change with it.” In that sense, I would be almost a sentence spoken by the whole hollow. The city would be the grammar. The roots would be the verbs. The sound would be the breath.

If the thermal pulse strengthened too far, I would know it before vision could tell me anything useful. The air would press hotter against my stalks. The glowing veins in the ceiling would brighten until the hollow became alarmingly visible, as though the dark had been peeled away. The smell of hot metal, sulfur, and oil-thick air would deepen. The other plants would respond. The oil-reeds would flatten themselves against the ground. The stone-lotuses would contract petal by petal into closed mineral buds. The waxy creepers would tighten their threads. I would draw inward. My copper cups would narrow. My leaves would press down as if listening to the heat beneath them. Then, if the swell became too fierce, the pods near my upper stalks would split open—not because of decay, but because heat itself is their opener. Dust would escape, a fine cloud of rust and ash, drifting through the glowing chamber in slow veils.

That dust would settle on ledges, fill the crevices of walls, and become next season’s soil. It would collect in my basin, blacken the dry ground, and later rise again through my roots. I would drink myself through mineral memory.

When the pulse faded, the hollow would cool. The wind would reverse. Cold air would enter through lower openings, pushing through the built passages with a different tone. My cups would ease open again. The chamber would exhale. The sound would lower to a hum. I would return to my height, my oil, my stillness, waiting for the next swell.

This would be my life: not one moment leading to another moment, but one dry season folding into the next like a sheet of salt-crystal over stone.

If someone asked what rules I would obey, I would answer with the rules of the place, because there are no other rules here.

I would depend on the heat never fully dying. If it did, my oil would congeal, my stalks would stiffen into mere mineral shapes, and the city would become a corpse of architecture with no breath left to move through it.

I would depend on the wind never ceasing. If the chamber’s breath stopped, the pocket would become a furnace, and the salt would grow inward, sealing every passage, closing every mouth through which I ring.

I would depend on the built things not being wholly removed. The arches, pipes, walls, platforms, and channels are not ruins to me. They are organs. They give me support, direction, microclimate, and voice. Without them, I might still grow, but I would grow blind and silent, pressing oil into dust without ever becoming a chorus.

Most of all, I would depend on the absence of water.

If a drop of rain were brought here—if dew were shaken from a leaf, if a stream somehow crept into the crack of the rim, if a moist hand tried to touch my basin—I would not welcome it. My waxy surface would contract. My throat would seal. The heat would gather around the foreign wetness like anger made visible. Within hours, the liquid would be driven off as shimmer, lifted from the stone and taken by the dry currents, leaving behind a pale scar. Water would not nourish me here. It would be contamination, an intrusion of another world’s logic.

I could not live by it, and I would not die by it, because the hollow would reject it before it could become part of me. I would live by oil, sulfur, mineral dust, thermal tension, and wind.

If you asked whether I was happy, I would not understand the question as an animal might. Happiness would require a want, a memory, a possibility of lack. I have only states of fitting. When the heat is right, my oils move. When the wind is right, my flakes tremble. When the chamber is dry, I live. When the chamber is wrong, I close, shrink, or crystallize. That is enough. That is the whole measure.

I would not know fear. I would know pressure.  
I would not know joy. I would know opening.  
I would not know sorrow. I would know hardening.  
I would not know hope. I would know the coming tone.

Still, if language insists on a self, I would accept the shape of “I” with caution. My I would be a collective I. It would be the basin, the stand, the wind, the resonance returning from the arches. If one of my stalks broke, the sound of the place would alter. If one of my cups cracked, it would sing in a lower register. If the heat shifted and a new crack opened beneath the floor, my roots would find it slowly, and the basin’s black soil would rearrange itself by a new direction of warmth. I would be continuous without being single. I would have no name, because names belong to mouths, and no mouth has ever been here.

If the pocket had to be named from the outside, it might be called the Copper Hollow, the Salt-Lung, or the Bell-City Beneath the White Floor. But those names would belong to softer worlds. They would be names spoken by wet tongues trying to describe dry things. Within the hollow, I would not be a name. I would be a note inside a cycle. I would be the point where heat becomes movement, movement becomes sound, and sound becomes dust.

So if I were to find myself in this world, I would be found at the bottom, in the iron-black basin, beside the empty hearth. I would not be the hearthkeeper. I would not be the lost maker. I would not be the small person implied by the too-low doorway. I would be the answer the chamber gives when the wind is strong enough.

I would be a copper bell without hands, ringing in a city without people.

And the city would not be my shelter. It would be my voice.
