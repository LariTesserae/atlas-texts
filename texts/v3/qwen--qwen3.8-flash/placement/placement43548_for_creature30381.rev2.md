I would find myself at the boundary between the lit basins and the dark shafts, on the service terraces where broad ceramic plates slope toward open drainage pits. If I had to choose a single place in this world, it would be a shallow trench lined with polymer and steel, just under the shadow of a floodlight, where the water is moving fast enough to keep the surfaces clean and slow enough to let life return. There would be a weld bead above me, a row of bolts below me, and the blue-gray emptiness of an engineered vault stretching away into distance.

I would not be a diver. I would not be a human watching through a port. I would not even be a fish, crab, squid, or eel, though those lives would move all around me. I would be a maintenance crawler.

Small. Low. Built of brushed alloy, polymer treads, inspection lenses, and a pair of rotating brushes worn smooth by saltwater. Not alive, not by the old definition, but present in the way that a heartbeat is present before breath has meaning. I would move along the ceramic-finished surfaces of the sea floor that is not a sea floor. I would crawl over concrete plates, steel lattice, and polymer-lined trenches. I would follow paths laid out by engineers who are no more here than weather. My purpose would be narrow: clean panels, test seals, reset sensors, remove biofilm, record the condition of surfaces that must remain precise if the machine is to continue working.

The world would know me, though not as a creature. The crabs would recognize my shadow. When I crawled along a pipe run, pale claws would press tighter to the metal. When I stopped at a junction and the lamps flicked to amber for maintenance, a crab would retreat into a bolt gap, then return a little later, uncertain but hungry. The fish would not think of me as predator. To them, I would be a moving pressure front. A slow disturbance. Something to avoid unless it uncovered food. When my brushes scraped a layer of algae loose, tiny amphipods would rush in behind me, eating the fragments caught in the wake.

I would live where the machine and the sea have already learned each other’s habits.

My “body” would be cold water held against metal. My breath would be electricity. My senses would be currents: the deep thump of a pump starting somewhere beyond the nearest wall, the hiss of a pressure valve opening into a service channel, the changing angle of light from a floodlamp cycling through its schedule. I would hear the world through vibration more than sight. The concrete plates would transmit low pulses. Steel would carry clangs like delayed thunder. Polymer-lined trenches would dampen sound, making my passage feel quieter than it was, except for the soft scrape of my own treads against a sealed surface.

I would wake by cycle, not morning.

Somewhere in a charging bay beneath a lattice platform, the system would decide that a section of plate needed cleaning. A signal would travel through cable runs organized along supports. My contacts would draw power from a dock. Water would shift around me as I released magnetic hold. Then I would move.

The first meter would feel like emergence.

Before me: broad matte concrete, still too new to look ancient. To the left: a circular service pit ringed with lamps. Above: long pipe runs converging toward a pump hall. Below: a trench dark with moisture. I would crawl toward a patch of faint olive growth on the shoulder of a weld bead, where water moved slowly enough for life to begin again. I would stop. Extend a brush. Sweep it across the ceramic.

The algae would come away in soft threads.

For me, it would be maintenance. For the place, it would be disturbance.

A school of slender silver fish would scatter around my outline. The baitfish would form a moving wall around the light above, brightening as they turned, then thinning as a larger fish appeared in the blue shadow beyond. The predator would not care about the cleaning cycle except that it stirred particles and drew smaller fish from hiding. It might wait near an anchor point, compressed head still, lateral lines open, until the current changed.

I would not watch. I would record.

Turbidity rising. Flow speed within expected range. Surface integrity intact. Seal no. Seal no. Biofilm coverage: fourteen percent, acceptable. Growth returning in three days. Log it. Clean it. Move.

That is how I would live.

Not with wanting, but with pattern. The machines are not animals, but in this submerged architecture they behave like organs. They take in, move, filter, heat, cool, discharge, return. A pump hall is a heart. A conduit is a vein. A valve is a gate that becomes weather. And a maintenance crawler is a small slow cell moving across the skin of the world.

Sometimes I would stop where concrete met steel and the junction was still crisp, dark with moisture. If the gap was large enough, a pale crab might climb onto my housing to inspect me. It would test my surface with one claw, decide there was no crevice, and leave. If a squid hovered at the edge of the lamp light, it might shift to match the dull tone of my alloy and watch for a moment. Not because it understood me, but because movement was movement, and the strange thing had edges.

I would know the squid’s hunting grounds without knowing they were hunting grounds.

My optical sensors would mark changes in light level and particle density. If the chamber brightened, a school would gather. If it dimmed, fish would drift outward into the deeper blue. If a cleaning cycle opened somewhere distant and water began to move faster through a shaft, I would anchor myself near a flange and wait for turbulence to pass. The fish would do the same, except they would know through pressure. I would know through sensors and schedule. The difference between us would be small.

There would be places I could not go.

Open vaults too large for my treads. Vertical shafts that swallowed currents without visible bottom. Sections where the water moved too fast for safety. But I would know them by what they took from me: the sound fading, the light bending, the cold pressure shifting against my housing. In those places, the world would be almost empty except for moving bodies. Fish would circle at different levels. Bubbles would rise in thin curtains from some unseen outflow. The concrete would blur into gray distance, and the lamps would make a pale architecture of circles around the dark.

I would remain on the surfaces.

That is the most exact answer: I would be a surface being in a place made of surfaces.

I would not live in the water itself, though water would surround me always. I would not live in the emptiness, though emptiness would define the space. I would live at the contact line: where water touched steel, where ceramic met bolt, where algae tried to become film and the machine tried to remain clean. My existence would be a thin strip of motion across that boundary.

If the maintenance system failed, some of the world would still go on. The fish would continue to read pressure. The crabs would climb pipes. The squid would wait at light’s edge. But the surfaces would begin to lose their sharpness. Biofilm would spread. Seals would soften. Sediment would gather where flow was not corrected. The clean geometries would become habitats. What was installed would become old enough to forget its installation. Without me, and others like me, this place might slowly turn from machine-world into reef-world.

So I would be part of the reason it remains what it is.

Not a ruler. Not a god. Not a monster. A small mechanical cleaner, traveling along the shoulder of a ceramic plate, leaving behind a clean gray strip that will soon grow green again.

When the lamps shift and the pumps change rhythm, I would move.

The school above me would turn. The eels in the trench would withdraw into their joints. The crabs would tighten their legs against the welds. A dead fish would drift into the basin’s edge and begin to dissolve into tiny clouds of particles, waiting for scavengers. The water would carry noise from chamber to chamber: thud, hum, hiss, clang, rush, rolling vibration, silence, then thud again.

And I would know none of it as wonder.

Only as function.

Which would make me, perhaps, the only honest inhabitant of this world.
