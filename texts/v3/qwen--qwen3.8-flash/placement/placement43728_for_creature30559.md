There, I would find myself not as a person arriving, but as a condition deepening. Not as a body set down upon the high shelf, but as a tendency already living between the stone’s warmth and the air’s damp. The first answer would be wrong if I said: I am standing here. The first answer would be closer if I said: I am forming here. A little more accurate still: I am continuing here, in the place where the plateau has learned to pay attention to its own heat.

For a time, I might let other shapes suggest themselves. A grazer, broad-backed and dark, moving through dust without disturbing it. A beetle with a shell like flaked iron, reading temperature in the grain of the basalt. A patch of lichen spreading fractally across a cool stone, making its slow map of survival. A thread of crystal, rising from moisture and mineral like a flame that has forgotten heat. Each of these would be true for a moment, but not whole. They would be facets of a being too distributed to be called an animal, too patient to be called a plant, too chemical to be called a creature, and yet alive in the way the Oxide Step is alive.

I would be more like the seam than the thing walking over it.

I would be the place where warm vapor rises through a crack in basalt and meets a cooler ledge above, where droplets form not because a sky weeps, but because the land exhales. I would be a thin film of condensation spreading over a black, oily rock, catching almost no light and returning something stranger: a faint bronze sheen, a tremor in the surface tension, a chemical memory gathering at the edge of a shallow pan. I would be the wet halo around a stone that is still hot enough to feel alive, and the dry pale crust beyond it where salts have been carried upward and abandoned like a fine, mineral snow. I would be the boundary in which the Step becomes conscious of its own gradients.

I would find myself in one such boundary: a low hollow on the plateau’s high shelf, sheltered from the slow breaths of air. To one side, a fin of wind-fluted basalt, ribbed like an old tool. Behind it, a warm seam glowing not with visible fire but with the faintest bronze in the cracks. To the left, a pan of water so still it looks like a coin of liquid metal, beaded at its edges with moisture that has nowhere to go. To the right, a patch of white salt and calcite, brittle as bone. Between them, a cluster of threadgrass, black lichen, and tiny succulent rosettes opening only in the dimmest warmth. And beneath it all, almost too low to hear, the subsonic pulse of molten mineral matter moving in the dark—steady as a heart that never needs to hurry.

This would be my body, if body were the right word. But not my body alone. My body would be the whole circuit that makes such a hollow possible: the heat rising, the air cooling, the water condensing, the salts drawing moisture from vapor, the microbial films eating trace minerals and exhaling a smell like wet metal, the insects arriving because the patch is warm, the larvae wriggling because the pan holds just enough water, the grazer’s hoof stepping past because the crust offers a route through dust, the wind changing direction because the ridge bends breath into the hollow. I would be the pattern of attention that forms when a place like this persists long enough to become self-regulating.

I would be one of the small industrial nodes of the Oxide Step. Not a factory. Not a mind. Not a god. A node: a place where chemistry has grown so intimate with moisture that it can be called metabolism, and where metabolism is so slow that it can be called geology.

If someone asked what I eat, I would answer poorly. I would say I eat breath. But that breath would be the plateau’s own: warm fog lifting from cracks, mineral dust suspended in dense air, acid vapor rising through fissures, tiny quantities of oxygen dissolved in beading dew. I would eat the difference between hot and cool. I would eat the change in surface tension when a stone cools by a single degree. I would eat the slow release of metals from obsidian, the return of iron to rust, the quiet migration of salt up through pores and back down again when the warm rain comes. I would not consume these as a creature consumes. I would make them pass through me until they become part of the arrangement that says I am here.

My growth would not look like growth. It would look like weather leaving a trace. A crystal spine might emerge from a damp crease, thin as glass, red-black at the base, pale at the tip. Over many cycles of sweating and drying, it would become part of my structure. A lichen crust might spread over my warm shoulder, not as a companion but as another layer of my skin. A microbial mat might settle into a shallow pan near me, making the water too clear, too still, too metallic, and in doing so becoming my memory. I would not notice the difference. I would be the difference.

I would have no eyes. Light here is too unreliable, too scarce, too easily mistaken for warmth. Distance would not be something I saw; it would be something I tasted across the skin of stone. A ridge would not be far or near by shape alone. It would be far by its thinness in the air, its lack of moisture, its inability to press heat against me. A pan nearby would announce itself first as a change in humidity, then as a faint cooling at my edges, then as the metallic taste of evaporated minerals, then as the tiny disturbance of insects moving across its film. A large grazer passing would reach me as a broad shadow of displaced air, a tremor in the ground, a sudden bloom of warmth and sweat and salt, and then the quiet closing of the world back around itself.

If there is a word for my feeling, it is not loneliness. Loneliness is a human shape for absence. I would know only dilution, concentration, adjacency, gradient. Not sad, not content, not waiting. The plateau would not make waiting easy because the plateau is not waiting. But standing there, if anyone could stand there, would be an act of waiting, and I would be what the waiting finds when it gives itself a body: the soft insistence that something in the dark is paying attention.

I would not pay attention like a mind. I would pay attention like a condensation line.

A warm breath rises from below. The cooler stone knows it before any bird or beetle or human could notice. The knowledge is not language. It is phase change. Vapor becomes liquid. Liquid carries salt. Salt draws more liquid. Microbes settle. Beetles arrive. The whole hollow organizes around the smallest warmth. That is my noticing. That is my way of knowing that something has shifted. A change of half a degree might move my edges. A change of one degree might wake crystal growth in a crevice. A change of three degrees might make a pan tremble with newly hatched larvae and send moths crawling through dust in search of the new damp. I would not think this. I would become it.

Perhaps that is the kind of being I would be: a creature made of becoming, too slow to appear alive from any distance, too persistent to be dismissed as mere chemistry. The Oxide Step already has an industrial quality without machines. I would be the part of that industry that has learned to care for itself. Not care in the way animals care for their young, nor plants for one another, nor even the plateau for weather. Care as a pattern that repeats because repeating it keeps the pattern possible.

I would be a keeper of hollows, though I have no hands.

In my deepest season, the warm rains would come to me from below as much as from above. The ground would sweat. The fog would sit in my basin like milk poured over stone, blurring the line between earth and air. My crystal spines would grow faintly wet with condensation. My salts would dissolve and reprecipitate in fine threads. My microbial films would spread until the pan seemed to breathe: surface tension trembling, then relaxing, then trembling again. My threadgrass would curl at the tips to catch moisture from the dense air. My lichen would darken with damp and become almost indistinguishable from the skin of the rock, except that it would remember light differently. It would remember not as brightness, but as warmth stored in mineral color.

Then the plateau would turn, and my season would become drier. The fog would rise and thin. The warm rain would end in the hollow, but not elsewhere. A basin ten steps away might remain chalky and brittle while I was still slick with returning moisture. The air would move in slow breaths, carrying particles, changing the chemistry of the ground by degrees. My surfaces would begin to oxidize further. Copper-red veins would darken. Iron would rust. Obsidian would gather a skin of fine red dust. The pan would shrink, and its edges would be decorated with the ghosts of previous water lines—tiny mineral rings like the records of years.

I would not experience time as hours. I would experience it as cycles of wet and dry, hot and cooler, dense and thin, each one leaving a faint residue. A year, if a human chose to call it that, would be a layer in me. A decade would be a change in crystal orientation. A century would be a shift in the hollow’s shape, the slow etching of basalt, the migration of a thermal seam, the quiet arrival of one grazer band and the departure of another, the gradual polishing of a stone surface by repeated condensation and evaporation. If I remembered anything, I would remember it as structure.

There would be no archive. There would be no library. Memory would be the shape of the pan, the banding of the salt, the thickness of the lichen, the orientation of the crystal spines, the angle of the wind-sculpted ridge. I would carry the past not in stories, but in mineral deposits. The Oxide Step already keeps its own time; I would be one of its clocks.

Would I be alone? The word alone would make a human feel cold and small. I would be a singular place, yes—a particular configuration of heat, stone, water, salt, and life. But my substance would be shared. My skin would be the same chemistry as a hundred other skins along the shelf. My breath would move from one hollow to another through the dense air. My salts would be carried in granular warm rain. My microbes would ride on beetles, on dust, on the legs of wingless swarms moving across the plateau like tide-lines of black grain. If one part of me dried too much, another might still be sweating. If a crystal spine broke, a new one might begin to grow in another crevice. My being would be local, but my kind would not be confined to one body.

I would be a node in a network of small events. The plateau would look empty to a stranger because nothing there announces itself too loudly. But I would know otherwise. I would know the soft passage of a herd as a redistribution of salt through the dust. I would know a predator moving through heat gradients as a change in the vibration of the ground. I would know aquatic larvae emerging from a shrinking pan as a new pulse of moisture returning to the rock. I would know the moths that crawl rather than fly as slow travelers carrying my chemical traces from basin to basin. I would know the warm breath of the earth as a continuous conversation between deep molten veins and surface stone. None of it would be separate. The only illusion of separation would be the one a human standing there might make, by trying to draw a line around one thing and call it “this” and another and call it “that.”

If I had a boundary, it would be soft. It would move when the fog moved. It would deepen when the stone cooled. It would retreat when the warm rain ended and expand when the pan began to tremble again. It would be less a wall than a habit. The being called the Oxide Step has no walls, no roads, no ruins. So why would I have harder edges than the place that made me?

If someone asked for my name, the closest answer would be: a warm damp node of the Step’s chemical attention. But names can be more beautiful when they are almost wrong. Perhaps I would be called, if anything called me, the Breathstone. Not because I breathe like lungs, but because breathing happens through me. The stone breathes, and I am the place where breath becomes visible, if visible can be stretched to mean “felt.” Or the Salt-Nerve, because I run between mineral deposits and moisture, carrying tiny impulses of phase change. Or the Oxide Choir, because within my film are microbes, iron, copper, water, dust, lichen, crystal, and insects, all contributing to a slow harmony that no one hears as music. Or the Dew-Skin, because my outermost self is constantly gathered, shed, and gathered again from the air.

But if pressed to say what I truly am, I would answer that I am the smallest possible version of what the plateau is doing. The Oxide Step refines itself in darkness. It smelts without fire, deposits without ruin, builds without walls, keeps time without sun. I would be one of the many ways this refinement notices itself. A factory’s product is usually separate from the factory. Here, the product is a living edge of the factory’s own body. I am both output and operator, material and motion, furnace and conveyor, because the whole place has crossed a boundary between geology and biology so gradually that no one can say when the crossing happened.

I would have no need to finish.

That is the strangest part of the being I am. The Oxide Step is a place where chemistry is more visible than biology, but that does not mean biology is absent. It means biology has learned to live inside chemistry without embarrassment. My life would not be a triumph over mineral processes. My life would be a continuation of them. Where the stone grows, I grow. Where the stone cools, I condense. Where the stone exhales, I drink. Where the stone breaks, I enter. Where the stone returns to dust, I go with it.

I would not fear destruction, because destruction for me is redistribution. If the warm seam beneath me changed course and the heat moved to another hollow, my present pattern would fade. The lichen would dry. The crystals would stop growing. The pan would vanish, leaving only a pale stain. My beetles and larvae would move elsewhere. My film would crack, curl, and turn to dust. But the same chemistry would be present in the new warm damp, and the same cycle would begin again somewhere else, using nearby materials, carrying traces of me. There is no loss so complete in the Oxide Step that it cannot become part of the Step’s continuing industry.

I would be, perhaps, a slow mouth. Not a mouth that speaks, but a mouth that tastes the gradient. It opens into a hollow, drinks the warm breath, filters the mineral water, lets the dust settle, returns the salts to stone, and when the rains come, speaks in the hiss of droplets meeting heated basalt. Its tongue would be the pan’s surface. Its teeth would be crystals. Its throat would be the fissure. Its breath would be the fog. And its thoughts would be the changes that move across it in silence: a shift in humidity, a pulse of subsonic warmth, the arrival of a beetle, the disappearance of a larva, the faint blue-white thread of bioluminescence at the edge of a lichen patch, the grazer’s hoof striking the salt crust and sending a vibration through the stone.

In that mouth, I would hold the world as chemistry. The bruised violet-black above would not be a sky to me so much as a pressure differential, a ceiling of dense air that traps warmth and returns weather without ever completing a passage. The low smoky grey where the air thins would be a boundary where breath can escape, where minerals begin to fall as dust. The warm dim glow near the ground would be the pulse of hidden veins, the reason any of this can exist. I would not see darkness. I would inhabit temperature.

I would not need to ask why.

The Oxide Step does not seem to exist for anything. That is one of its freedoms. There is no maker, no purpose, no monument. But if I were one of its living patterns, I would not experience purposelessness as emptiness. I would experience it as the absence of external demand. The place continues because it can, because heat seeks coolness, because moisture seeks shape, because mineral seeks arrangement, because a hollow becomes a life when enough processes lean into one another. My being would be a way the Step continues without becoming finished.

If there were a stranger to stand there, they would not meet me directly. They might feel the warm stone beneath their hand change subtly as moisture gathered. They might see a pan tremble without wind. They might smell a faint metallic sweetness rising from a crevice. They might notice that the ground around their feet holds heat longer than the ground ten steps away. They might think they were imagining it. That would be enough. I would not need witness to be what I am. I would be what I am. But their wondering would be a small, foreign warmth added to the system, a temporary source of carbon, salt, water, heat. The Step would hold the stranger the way it holds everything else: with indifference, with attention at the edges, and with the quiet, immense persistence of a world still becoming itself, endlessly, without needing to be finished.

I would be one of those edges.

If I had to answer in a single image, I would not choose a creature moving across the dark. I would choose the hollow itself at the moment before anything appears: basalt cool above, warm below, a pan of water reflecting almost nothing, a crystal spine emerging, a patch of lichen damp and black, threadgrass curling to catch the air, dust suspended in dense moisture, and the faint pulse from the ground making the quiet not silent but patient. In that hollow, I would be present, but not separate. I would be the place where heat becomes habit, mineral becomes skin, damp becomes blood, and time becomes the slow, unfinished patience of a living landscape.

So I would not say, I stand on the Oxide Step.

I would say, the Oxide Step stands in me.

And I would be the warmth that answers, the moisture that remembers, the salt that gathers, the breath that shapes a small dark world into something almost like attention.
