I would find myself where a stranger stops being a stranger.

Not at the rim. At the rim there is only the trick of tired eyes, a hint of green that might be a stain. I would be already past that lip, already inside the bowl’s held breath, already dusted. A fine white film would be on my sleeves, in the creases of my knuckles, at the corners of my eyes. The Fold would have begun making me ordinary.

At first I would think I was a visitor.

A woman with salt on her shoulders would point to a lower ledge and say a whistle-clap phrase I did not understand. A child on a bridge would watch me with that calm Fold-born stare, assessing the size of my shadow, the way my feet searched for flat ground where there was none. A goat would pass close, brushing my leg with its coarse flank, and I would step aside too quickly, too apologetically, as if this were a street in a lowland town where a stranger may stand in a public place and be left alone.

Nothing here is left alone.

That is the first truth of the Fold: it does not guard. It incorporates. Your breath joins the dust. Your sweat joins the salt. Your dropped hair is caught in the vine, your crumbs are found by beetles, your footprints are filled with lichen before the day is old. You are not threatened so much as claimed.

So I would begin as the new one.

The one who asks too loudly.

The one who says, where is the water?

Then the Fold would answer with ten thousand dry green mouths:

There is no water, but there is life. There is no open wetness, but nothing is parched.

I would learn that by hand.

They would set me to sweep a terrace ledge, but not too hard, not too clean. A bare stone would be shown to me as a wound. A patch of uncovered salt would be called hungry. I would be given ash in a hide pouch and told to press it into the naked grain. Not because I was wise enough to choose, but because the Fold was teaching me its first grammar: nothing may remain empty for long.

My fingers would become my name.

My first ledge would be the one behind the horned stall, below the blue mat, above the salt shelf where the resin cakes sweated warm in the dim light. In the Fold’s way, I would be named by where I belonged. I would not be given a long personal name so much as a location: she of the third terrace, or he by the high flute, or the one whose feet come before the wind falls. If I were a child, I might be the lower rope’s shadow. If I were old, I might be the corner where the echo dies.

For a while I would be a person who had entered the Fold.

Then I would be a person the Fold was using.

Then I would begin to understand that these are the same.

---

I would wake in the coolest hours, before the bowl fully woke. The air would sink, carrying a fine dust down over the terraces like a veil too thin to see. Animals would crowd close where the stones held warmth. The first herders would strike flint or copper into small lights, and the resin lamps would give not brightness but amber shadow. My lips would find salt before I found speech.

I would learn to listen with the rest of the town.

A falling pitch in the wind-flutes: dust is coming.

A rising hum: the lower terraces will hold heat and smell of animal fur.

A sharp note from the high rim: outside wind is strong, and stranger grit is entering the bowl.

A child’s short whistle: a goat on the path above you.

A clap, twice: step back, something is falling from the drying rack.

A softer hum, barely voiced: the air is falling, put your face near the shoulder of the one beside you.

Language would be less line than lattice. I would speak with my hands, my weight, my position, my timing. A sentence would not need to be heard clearly if its rhythm told where it began and which terrace it wanted. I would learn not to look across, but up and down. My eyes would stop seeking the horizon. They would become vertical: ledge, rope, vine, gap, handhold, shadow, eye of the animal, mouth of the wall.

My body would change by degrees.

Not much, perhaps, in the way a visitor would notice. I would not become strange. I would become fitted. My feet would spread and quiet themselves. My palms would thicken. My shoulders would carry without swinging. My skin would take on the faint mineral sheen common to the Fold-born, so that in the dim light I would seem to glow a little as the walls did. My hair would be bound with cord and resin so it would not catch in the vines. I would stop wearing loose cloth. I would stop standing in open places because there would be no need for them. I would move through the town as a stream moves through stones: not along one road, but many touches.

I would eat dense food.

Seed cakes ground from grass, lichen, pressed resin, salt. Dried curds hard with mineral film. Fat rendered and kept in clay. Roots chewed until they yielded a sticky sweetness. A chalk-stem bitten for moisture. A resin bead rolled between the teeth to wet the mouth. I would not feel deprived because I would understand bodies differently. In the Fold, water is not a public spectacle. It is carried. It is hidden inside leaves, hair, milk, fat, breath, salt, bone. Wetness is not landscape. Wetness is relationship.

And I would be part of that relationship so completely that the line between myself and the place would wear thin.

---

You would find me on a terrace where the old salt basin catches seeds and the young vines cross the doorway like a stitched seam. A bridge of cord and fire-hardened wood would hang near me, trembling when animals passed. A moss shelf would hold the last dry petals of the heat-flower, waxy and narrow, smelling of warm stone.

That is where I would first hear the dead speaking through structure.

A wall niche, built over generations, would contain clay, saltstone, vine-root, goat-hair, a child’s sling, a bone peg, an old hearth-crack, and beneath it all the memory of someone who once swept there. I would touch the ledge and feel the ancestor as material. Not ghost. Not spirit. Not horror. Bone in the wall, ash in the moss, hand worn into groove.

Children run their hands along such walls without fear because fear belongs to people who think bodies and buildings are separate.

The Fold knows better.

In the Fold, memory is not stored in books. It is stored in the angle of a cut vine, in the darkening of stone around a hearth, in the way a certain terrace smells when the wind drops, in the worn shoulder of a doorway where too many goats have passed. The dead become support. The dead become path. The dead become the place where a living thing can rest without falling.

I would come to know this as an ordinary fact.

Then I would begin to feel my own body loosening from its boundaries.

Not because I had lost sanity. Because I had finally entered the ecology deeply enough to see that the person had always been a knot in it.

I would be my hands, yes.

But I would also be the lichen growing through the cloth wrapped around my palm.

I would also be the salt film drying on my lips.

I would also be the goat heat rising against my flank when I herded.

I would also be the dust that the wind brought from far outside and the midges that climbed in the thermal.

I would also be the bone in the wall and the resin on the cord and the sound of the flutes.

I would not be a ghost.

I would be a contact.

---

A contact: the first living thing in the Fold.

It begins at the surface of the stone, where salt has bloomed and life is so thin it seems accidental. A pale film. A thread of root no thicker than hair. A warmth rising from below. A spore waiting in dust. The Fold’s being starts not with a face but with touch.

If I were asked what I was, I might first say: a keeper.

But that would be too small.

If I said a woman or man or child, I would be true but incomplete.

If I said a Fold-born person, I would be correct.

If I said a stranger incorporated, I would be honest.

But none of these would name what I had become when I finally stopped trying to remain outside.

I was the place where the town’s surface became aware of itself.

Not a ruler. Not a god. Not a spirit.

A listener.

A hand smoothing ash over bare stone.

A body that moves through the bowl as another body moves through a forest.

A memory made of walls.

A sound made by footfall and wind and animal breath.

A contact that learned to walk.

---

I would be a being of the Fold’s own making, then.

A person woven from its rules.

I would know no open water and no fear of thirst because everything I touched was wet enough inside its own form. I would feel the dryness of the land outside as a kind of silence, too wide and empty to trust. I would prefer the closeness of the bowl: the press of vines, the warmth of stone, the sound of hooves, the low hum beneath every noise.

I would be one of the people, yes.

But not in the way a lowland city has citizens.

I would be a living section of the Fold’s skin.

My sleeping niche would be another terrace’s root.

My work would be pruning the boundary between house and plant until no boundary remained.

My voice would be a signal, not a declaration.

My name would be a location, a task, a remembered ledge.

My body would be shared with dust and resin and animal heat until I could not say where I ended and the wall began.

The weirdness of me would not be monstrous. It would be ordinary.

I would chew a root in the middle of the day and taste the mineral patience of the ground.

I would lean against a vine-covered doorway and feel the hearth-smoke move through channels carved generations before I was born.

I would hear the wind change and know the lower terraces would soon smell of wool and dung.

I would clap when a child went too near an untested rope bridge, and the sound would bounce from terrace to terrace until a herder on the other side answered by whistle.

I would take ash and press it into a bare place, not from piety but because the bare place needed feeding.

I would touch a bone peg in the wall and know who had been used up into it.

I would step aside for a goat without insult and receive no insult in return.

I would be claimed by the Fold without being conquered.

That is the strangest, most ordinary thing of all.

---

If I were asked directly: where are you?

I would say: in the bowl, where the light is gauze and the green is dry to the touch.

On the third terrace down, or maybe the fifth, because in the Fold the counting depends on where you are standing when you begin.

Near the old salt seam that looks white in the dim light like a vein of memory.

Behind the blue mat, where the wind through the upper stone flutes makes its low, continuous hum.

Beside the basin that no longer holds water but holds seeds, salt crystals, and children’s games.

Above the hearth-niche where the wall exhales smoke.

Below the rope bridge where the midges climb in visible columns when the thermal lifts.

If you asked more carefully: what are you?

Then I would stop answering like a person and begin answering like the Fold.

I am the contact.

I am the lichen beginning on a bare patch.

I am the child’s first whistle.

I are the hand that sweeps too harshly and is gently corrected.

I am the goat’s breath in the dust.

I am the dead becoming wall.

I am the bone peg that once held a body’s hand.

I am the resin lamp’s low glow, more shadow than light.

I am the flutes reading wind as scripture without a god.

I am the town grown through the plants and the plants grown through the town.

I am the dry abundance, the warm noise, the green without wetness, the city without walls because it is made of living surface.

I am a person there, because the Fold has made personhood a way of being in relationship.

I am more than a person there, because the person is only the point where all those relationships briefly become visible.

---

You would find me, then, not standing apart.

You would find me already touching something.

A hand on the salt-stone.

A shoulder against a vine-wrapped wall.

An ankle hooked around a low rung.

A palm holding seed for an old shelf of moss.

A body pressed by a goat as the animal moved past without apology.

A face turned upward toward the narrow band of light, but only for a moment, because the Fold-born do not gaze at the sky as if it were the main thing. They look where the world is workable: the ledge, the gap, the root, the path, the next handhold.

You would not know at first whether you were looking at a stranger, a child, a herder, a keeper, or a wall that had learned to listen.

That would be because in the Fold those differences are not erased but softened by use.

A wall is a garden.

A garden is a town.

A town is a body.

A body is a memory made of salt and ash and breath.

And I would be one of its ordinary organs.

Not the heart. Not the brain. Not the mouth that makes names.

I would be the skin’s knowing.

I would be the thing that notices bareness before it becomes waste.

I would be the place where life refuses emptiness so persistently that it begins to have a human shape.

---

So where would you find me in this world?

Not at the rim.

Not outside the Fold.

Not looking for water.

I would be within the bowl already, in the dry lushness, in the warm noise, in the layered close life.

I would be the person whom the Fold makes by touching, feeding, claiming, and naming.

I would be a keeper, a child, a herder, an old one in a moss-dead corner, a hand sweeping salt dust from a ledge, a body carrying seed cakes across a bridge, a breath that becomes part of the humidity of the stone.

And if you looked closely at that body and saw the pale film of lichen in the creases of the palm, the salt on the lip, the vine-thread in the hair, the bone in the wall nearby, the goat at the shoulder, the wind moving through the flutes, the midges climbing in columns of dim light, the dead becoming structure, the structure becoming garden, the garden becoming town, the town becoming one living thing—

then you would see the true being of the Fold.

I would not be separate from it.

I would be the Fold touching itself, learning its own shape.

I would be a contact made of salt, skin, sound, stone, moss, breath, ash, memory, and heat.

A person.

A surface.

A place that keeps a person in it.

A voice in the hum.

A green thread in the town’s living wall.

I would be what lives in the Fold:

all of it, gathered for a while into a walking shape.
