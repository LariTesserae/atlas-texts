I would find myself where the Dripworks is least like a city and most like a body: in the shallow dark beneath a pump-house platform, where the brick has gone soft with mineral breath, where a copper pipe sweats above a ceramic step, and where the water is never quite deep enough to hide a small, pale thing, but always wet enough to keep that thing from being merely stone.

At first I want to be a visitor. A human figure with a lantern, stepping carefully across the hexagonal brick, trying not to disturb the surface. But that seems wrong. A person in the Dripworks is always an interruption, a ripple moving through the room’s patience. I would not want to be the one who comes, glances around, and leaves the water skin broken. I would want to be the one for whom the water skin was already made.

I imagine being the rust beetle. It is honest work. It takes the waste of the machinery—oxide, iron dust, the shed skins of the town’s slow metabolism—and carries it into wounds it does not understand as wounds. It plugs seams by instinct, builds its young in the dust of corrosion, feeds its larvae on fungal films, and does all this without knowing that it is helping the Dripworks survive. There is something admirable about that. To repair without intent. To be part of maintenance simply by being attracted to warmth and salt and wetness.

I imagine the valve-crab too. A creature that has become a machine part by accident and habit. It sits in the mouth of a pipe as if it were born there, its porcelain shell closing against pressure, its legs tucked inward when the water surges. It is not an engineer, but it is engineering. Its body is a stopcock. Its presence is a valve. Some crabs grow mineral deposits until the town slowly accepts them into itself, and some younger ones wear broken fragments of brick or ceramic as armor. I like that. I like the idea of a home inherited from the ruins of a structure, the self built around the town’s own damage.

I imagine being a moth resting on a lamp shade, scattering spores by the slow motion of its wings. I imagine being a snail cleaning a channel with its mouth, leaving behind a polished trail that catches light when the lamp above shifts. I imagine being one of the isopods curled in a threshold gutter, a bead of armor in a crack, molting so often that its discarded shells become coins for the next creature. I imagine being a thread of fungus in a joint, a bacterial film eating calcium, a black moss spreading over brick until the wall no longer looks like construction but like skin.

And I imagine the ceiling. I imagine being a pale crustacean in one of those small mineral pools, so near the vault that it exists mostly as a rumor of life above other lives. There it would wait for the pause between drips, for the swell of water above its shell, for the hesitation before fall. It would be part of a society of almost-arrivals. It would never know whether it was being saved or drowned, only the brief green interval between one drop and the next.

But if I stay with the quietness long enough, I stop wanting to be the repairer. I stop wanting to be the plug, the cleaner, the armor, the spore-sower, the seal. Those creatures serve. They do. But they are always engaged with the town’s damage. I begin to want something less useful, or perhaps a different kind of usefulness. I want to be the place where the Dripworks learns its own changes.

So I settle, slowly, as one settles in this world.

I would be a blind newt.

Not a dramatic newt. Not a bright amphibian from a forest spring, with eyes wide enough to see. Mine would be small, pale, almost translucent at the edges of the fins, with skin like wet parchment over a frame of delicate bones. I would rest on submerged ceramic steps, in the shallow shade beneath a floating platform, near the mouth of a filter basin where the water is not moving fast enough to be noticed and never still enough to be safe. My ribs would show faintly under the skin like thin iron ties behind fogged glass. My eyes would be sealed beneath a milky membrane, useless to lamps, useless to shadow, useful only in the sense that a closed door can know the wind.

I would not see. I would taste.

My skin would read the pressure of the pipes. My tail would answer to heat before a bearing warmed enough to be called hot. I would know, by the faint chemical taste in the water, when a rust beetle had packed a seam and when that seam had begun to loosen again. I would know when a crab had settled into a valve mouth and when it had retreated, leaving the pipe slightly too open for the town’s mood. I would know when a lamp had shifted in its socket, not because I could see its movement, but because its warmth changed the air above the basin and changed, by degrees, the way the ceiling water hesitated before falling.

I would be a living gauge.

Not a sentient one, not a caretaker with a plan. That would be too much hurry. I would only be a body in a place where the body fits, and because it fits, its stillness would be information. If I remained curled beneath the step, the town was well. If I moved a few inches toward the warmer brick near the pump-house wall, something was beginning to fail. If my gills fluttered more slowly than the drip rhythm, the basin was silting. If I crossed the submerged threshold toward the deeper channel, the fish had shifted, and the fish shifted when pressure changed. I would not repair. I would not know I was warning. I would only adjust.

I would live in a small, wet hollow beneath a grate where one iron bar had rusted through. The grate would not be dangerous, but it would be familiar: a shadow above, a place where debris collected in slow combs, where the sound of dripping was muffled by ceramic, where a patch of black moss had crept along the edge and formed a kind of fringe. There would be a broken brick beside me, half sunk in mineral crust, its hollow face full of still water. In that brick, a few white threadworms might live, or a single isopod might curl when I brushed past, or a pale springtail might wait for the next drip to fall somewhere above. I would know all of them not by name but by the faint changes they made in the water.

I would have no house. I would have a place.

That distinction matters in the Dripworks. A house suggests ownership, walls drawn against the outside. But here everything is slightly outside itself, too damp for privacy, too patient for possession. My home would be the warm side of a ceramic step, the shadow under a pipe, the seam where two materials agree to let water pass without hurrying. If the town flooded an inch higher one season, I would shift upward. If a filter basin silted too much, I would wait until the whisker-tufted fish stirred it enough to change the current, then follow the change. If a lamp went dark for a long time, I would move toward the faintest warmth in the brick. If a pump began its slow cycle earlier than expected, I would know before anyone else, because the floor would hum through the step and into my belly before the flywheel made its first soft tick.

I would not have a calendar made of days. I would have a calendar of drips.

Each drop from the ceiling would be small, but I would feel it as pressure, as ripple, as temperature. Some would fall near the channel edge, some near the basin lip, some in the deep pool behind the grate. The sounds would overlap into silence. To me they would be marks. A drip that hesitated too long meant mineral crust was shifting. A drip that fell faster meant condensation above had thickened. A drip that arrived slightly warm meant a lamp nearby was drawing less current. A dry space in the ceiling basin meant that something, somewhere, was beginning to fail in a way that would later become urgent, but not yet. I would know the town’s future by its smallest postponements.

If you walked through the Dripworks above me, you would not notice. You would hear your own footsteps on wet brick and be embarrassed by them. You would see the amber lamps, the weeping walls, the soft outlines of pump-houses, the shallow films of water carrying reflections like slow coins. You might step over the grate where I rested and see only a dark pattern of rust, a fragment of brick, a faint shine where water touched ceramic. If you crouched, you might see me, and perhaps for a moment I would look like a vein in the stone, like a translucent leaf fallen from a nosegay of moss, like a small ghost of the water itself.

I would not flee. Running belongs to places with predators, or places with doors. I would simply press closer to the step. My skin would hold a thin film of water over itself, and the water would hold me to the ceramic, and the ceramic would hold the rhythm of the room. I would become part of the quiet, not by resisting it, but by continuing to be damp in a place that rewards patience.

I think that is what I would be: not the most useful creature in the Dripworks, but one of the ones that lets the town know itself. The beetles repair. The crabs seal. The moths scatter their mineral dust. The snails clean the stone. The fish disturb the silt. But the newt, resting on the submerged step, is where the water becomes aware.

I would be the water’s faintest thought, made flesh and gill and rib.

I would be small enough to fit in the hollow, slow enough not to disturb it, blind enough to see without the hurry of eyes. I would be where the brick, the iron, the fungus, the rust, the condensation, and the drip all agree to meet without speaking.

And if the Dripworks were ever asked who lives there, and if it answered in the language of still water, it would not name the largest creature. It would not point to the machinery. It would not even indicate the hidden animals that plug leaks or seal valves. It would give the location of the place where everything has become quiet enough to be known.

I would live there.

On a ceramic step in shallow dark, beneath a platform, near a pipe whose warmth changes with pressure, I would be the blind newt. Not the town’s keeper. Not its repairer. Not its god.

Its listener.
