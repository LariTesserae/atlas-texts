I would find myself in the Green Weeping Hollow in a place too small to be called a location and too specific to be called nothing. Not at the center, where the roots run thickest and the hum feels almost like pressure behind the teeth. Not at the floor, where the mat of moss and ice-lichen holds its weight of water and waiting. Not in the deepest folds of the ceiling, where leaf-tissue has become so layered that even the faint glow has to pass through several membranes before it can reach the lower air.

I would be in the pause between a drop and a landing.

There is a shallow pool in the hollow—no more than a dish’s worth of water—caught between two pale root-cables, rimmed by a thin shelf of white mineral foam. Above it, three leaf-curtains hang in slow, translucent layers, their edges trembling slightly whenever the air changes. Behind them, a weeping stone sweats a faint iron-threaded moisture into the moss, and the moss there is dark with mineral taste. It is not a clear place, not a place one could point to and say *there*, because everything touches everything else, because the root-cable becomes a shelf, the shelf becomes a channel, the channel becomes a home for a midge, and the midge becomes a carrier of spores that become a film that becomes a wall.

But I would be there.

I would not be a person. A person carries too many boundaries in such a world: skin that refuses to join, lungs that insist on air as separate from water, eyes that want one thing to be one thing long enough to name it. If I entered as a person, I would feel like an interruption. The moss might shift around my weight. The root-hairs might orient toward the warmth of my hands. The leaf-tubes might contract when my breath wet them too deeply. I would not be harmed. I would simply be a strange weather passing through, a temporary concentration of salt and heat in a place that prefers slower, finer changes.

If I had to be something the hollow could recognize as part of itself, I would be a mote-grazer.

That is the name, perhaps. Or one name. The description of a mote-grazer says it appears first as dust in the air, drifting through the green-blue light. That is how I would begin: not as a creature with a fixed shape, but as a suspended possibility. I would hang in the moist air above the pool, almost invisible, too small to disturb the falling drops. Only when I landed would I unfold, extending six legs and opening the filament-hairs along my back, as though I had been folded into a mote all the time.

I would be very small. Small enough that a single droplet could lift me if I were careless. Small enough that the surface tension of the pool might feel like a floor made of skin. Small enough that the hollow’s sound—its constant click, drip, creak, and low hum—would enter me not as noise but as structure. I would know the hollow by rhythm. A drop falling from the second curtain would make a different pulse from a drop falling from a mineral bowl. A root releasing tension would produce a low groan that I would feel through my legs. A midge passing too near me would stir my back-filaments with a tiny gust, and I would fold toward the change in moisture.

I would not fly. Flying would require too much confidence in air as something separate from water. Instead, I would be exhaled. I would rise only slightly when the warm pads beneath the moss made tiny currents, and I would drift downward when the air cooled near the frozen plates. My movement would be slow and accidental-looking, though it would not be accidental. I would taste gradients. I would know where the water wanted to fall before it fell. I would know where the moss wanted to drink before it sent out new hairs. I would be a mote because motes are everywhere and are required everywhere.

If I needed eyes, I would not have them. Or if I had something like eyes, they would not be eyes. They would be mineralized points on my filaments, tuned not to seeing but to sensing the direction and intensity of glow. The bioluminescent chemistry of the leaves would tell me what was near: not by image, but by taste of light. I would know a translucent membrane because its glow carried a cooler, sharper signature. I would know a dense moss pad because its light was warmer and more diffuse. I would know a pool not because I saw its surface, but because its surface held the reflection of the hollow’s underside in a kind of gravitational pressure.

My back-filaments would be my most important part. They would not merely collect water. They would read it.

Each filament would be a fine glass-like tube, open at its tip, lined with cells so thin they seemed grown from frost and thought. When a droplet touched me, the filament would drink it before the water could fall away. The droplet would travel through me in a faintly visible bead, and as it did, the cells along its path would taste it. They would know whether it had come from a leaf that had just condensed the morning air, or from a weeping stone that had secreted minerals from below the root-mat, or from a pool disturbed by a micro-crustacean’s sudden dive. They would know the water’s mineral history: calcium, silica, iron, trace biological acids, the residue of old dead midges, the faint sweetness of moss-exudate.

I would not be a creature that ate the hollow. I would be a creature that the hollow passed through, briefly, in droplet form.

My legs would be long and translucent, almost too delicate for walking. Their tips would have tiny claws and moisture-sensitive pads that let me cling to vertical leaf-surfaces without sliding down when frost gathered. I could stand on a dripping membrane and feel the droplet forming at the far tip as a slow swelling beneath my feet. Then I would move, not to escape the fall, but to follow the tension of the water to the edge, where I might catch it with my filaments before it released. This would not be hunting. It would be more like listening with my whole body.

I would live, in other words, at the exact scale where plant and animal and weather stop being different things.

The floating discs would know me. They lie on the pools of the hollow like pale lily-pads without stems, like small living lenses. When I drifted near a pool and the discs sensed my shadow, their edges would pulse faintly, and I would recognize that pulse as a question: *is this water? is this safe? is this change?* I would answer by folding my filaments into a pattern that released a little mineral dust and a trace of spore from the moss I had walked through. They would accept that as proof.

Sometimes I would land on a disc. It would feel like landing on a cool, slightly elastic moon. Its glow would pass through my legs and make my underside visible as a faint dark shape from below. I would not stay long. The disc was not a resting place, but a listening post. By standing on it, I could taste the water’s memory: what had dripped into it, what had swum beneath it, what roots had touched its edges, whether frost was preparing itself or thawing into danger.

Other creatures would pass over and around me. Glass-winged midges would cluster near heavy condensation and scatter when the hum shifted; their tiny bodies would carry droplets from leaf to leaf, leaving behind microscopic traces of dissolved minerals. When one of them brushed near me, I would feel the chemical message it carried: a note of iron from the weeping stone, a note of decay from a moss pad, a note of light-angle from a ceiling film. I would add my own note to the air and let it drift.

Snail-like grazers would move slowly along the mineral shelves, leaving trails that the moss immediately drew into itself. I might climb beside one and taste the slime-trail with a filament. The trail would be sweet with dissolved calcium and the faint bitterness of fungal spores. The snail would not notice me. I would be too small. But later, its path might shift because the moss had grown along the place I had touched, building a tiny ridge I had unknowingly helped design.

Pale worms in the mat below me would create pores that let the hollow breathe. I would feel their slow vibrations through the pool’s edge, though I would never meet them. We would be neighbors at different scales: they in the hidden thickness, I in the wet open air. Together we would keep the hollow from suffocating in its own abundance.

And the roots would watch.

Not with eyes. Roots do not watch with eyes. But when I landed too heavily on a root-cable, its tiny hairs would shift after a long moment. If I carried moisture from a place the cable had not recently tasted, the cable might widen a channel nearby. If I left a spore on its surface, that spore would be colonized by mineral films and become part of a future wall. If I died there—and death would be slow, a drying, a folding, a letting go—the root would not mourn me. It would use me. My filaments would become tiny channels in the moss crust. My shell-points would become nucleation sites for frost crystals. My last droplet would be drawn into the mat and remembered as a pattern of salt.

This is why I choose to be a mote-grazer.

Not because it is beautiful, though it is. Not because it is small, though smallness is the only honest scale in a hollow like this. Not because it can drift through the green air, though the air there is almost a sea of taste. I choose it because a mote-grazer is a boundary that the hollow can use.

I would not be separate from the place. I would be one of the ways the place becomes aware of itself.

If a person stood in the hollow and listened, they might think the dripping was random. They might think the hum came from water moving through stone, or from moss contracting, or from tiny insects clicking in hidden chambers. They would be right, but also incomplete. The drips are punctuation. The hum is grammar. And beings like me are small syllables that travel between meanings.

I would not know my own name. I would not need one. The hollow would have a way of marking me without words: a certain bend in the pool film, a faint mineral signature left on a leaf, a small warmth in a patch of moss where I had rested during a cold pulse. That would be my name, and it would last only as long as the moss remembered, and perhaps longer if the mineral foam grew into my shape.

If asked, “Where are you?” I would answer: in the air just above the basin, under the third curtain, where the light is green enough to taste and the drops fall in slow succession. If asked, “What are you?” I would answer: a mote. A mote-grazer. A living pause. A tiny instrument made of water, mineral, and listening.

I would be one of the smallest citizens of the Green Weeping Hollow.

And I would be enough.
