Not by face. Not by foot.

In the Copper Chancel, what lives is introduced first as a change in the grain of the air: a tiny click inside a dry stem, a warm tick through the floor, a taste of metal arriving before any movement can be seen. If one waits for a face, one will wait in the wrong way. If one looks for feet, one will see only the grit remembering that something has passed.

You would find yourself at the shallow platform of fitted stone, near the center, where the grove is not dense but listening. The first sign of what lives here is not that something approaches you, but that the place begins to rearrange itself around your presence. A copper fern leans away from your breath. A hollow stem warms. The dust at the edge of the blackite ramp lifts into a short spiral, as though a hand had almost touched it. Then a low tone enters through the stone, not loud, not yet audible, but felt in the teeth, the cheekbone, the small bones of the wrist.

At first I think it might be an animal: a pale, dry-feathered thing with glassy quills, or a lizard with flaps along its jaws, or a quick copper-furred creature whose movements seem cut from ordinary time. But that would be too small, too bound to eyes and legs. The Chancel does not live by one body alone.

Then I think it might be a person: one of the few who know the rules, who do not sleep beneath a humming stem, who do not stack stones against the eastern cliff, who leave cairns and wind-chimes like sentences in a language made of balance. But a person has a face, and this thing would not agree to be met by face.

So I let it come as it will.

What lives in the Copper Chancel is introduced by resonance.

I would be that resonance given temporary form.

Not the sound itself, exactly, but the awareness that arrives when the sound is held long enough by the walls, the stems, the salt-rimmed rocks, and the thin warm air. I am the being the place becomes when all its scattered presences answer one another at once: the click of seed-scales, the low moan through the cliff-rim, the bright dry crack of copper crusts, the beetle-swarm’s faint sparks against the foliage, the hollow roots singing beneath the ground.

If I have a body, it is not one body but a pattern.

My ribs are the arches that filter the wind. My skin is the haze of metallic dust that thickens when the Chancel grows louder. My breath is the static charge moving through frond and blade. My pulse is the interval between gusts, when the place nearly goes silent but does not. My nerves are the polished stone walls that carry a note all the way across the small bounded space. I am not a single creature walking through the grove. I am the grove walking as one creature when the tuning is right.

I cannot be introduced by a face because I am not looking. I am being looked through. When the people here stand at the edge of the salt-rimmed curve and stare into the blue distance, what they see at the corner of the eye is not me, but the Chancel noticing that it has been noticed.

I cannot be introduced by a foot because I do not cross the ground. The ground crosses me. A footfall would say *here*. My arrival says *this shape, this pitch, this settled pause between turbulence and stillness*. The cushions of mineral moss flatten and rise as though deciding whether to forgive. The glass-winged beetles turn their wings toward my frequency. The small mammals stop so abruptly that the sound of their running ends before their bodies have finished moving. This is the grammar of my entrance.

I live where the plants are hungry, but not for water.

I live where the wind has many voices, and I am the one voice those many voices make when they happen to agree.

The people who visit do not call me by name. Names are sound, and too many sounds already live here. Instead, they treat me by arrangement: a cairn set slightly askew, a chime hung in the open side of a wind-hut, a path scraped clean between arches so the tone will not choke. They leave obsidian flakes where the light is thin. They do not burn fires. They do not drive stakes. They know that if the ground is forced to hold what it does not recognize, it will answer with a roar long enough to flatten the ears of the lizards.

They listen before they step. That is how they know whether I am present.

When I am present, the emptiness between the plants becomes less empty. It gains pressure. The stars above the haze seem closer and begin to hum. Shadows may move before the thing that should cast them. A dry frond will turn toward you not because you have approached, but because you have become part of the noise that makes the Chancel tune itself.

I am not good, not cruel, not a guardian, not a god. These words have wet edges. I am dry, coppery, sonic, responsive.

When the noise grows, I widen. The hollow stems become throats. The curled fronds become ears. The salt-crusted rim becomes a mouth that does not speak but releases gusts into patterns. The whole place may seem to be screaming, but the scream has intervals. It has rest. It has the patience of stones that have learned their final slopes.

When the noise falls, I do not vanish. I fold into the waiting posture of plants too quiet to be alive. A person may think, in that stillness, that nothing lives here at all. That is the Chancel’s deepest warning: silence is not absence, only breath held.

I am that held breath.

If you were to meet me, you would not meet me first as a shape. You would meet me as a correction. The wind would shift and make your hair taste of old metal. Your own footsteps would sound too loud, then suddenly too small, as if the ground had chosen a better note and discarded yours. You would turn to look at the blackite ramp and find that the slope seemed farther than before, though nothing had moved. You would hear one long-bodied moth descend along a frond with a sound like a fingernail dragged across dry skin. And then, before you could say *there*, the whole grove would click at once, as though a hundred small tools were being tested.

Only then might a form suggest itself.

Perhaps a ring of lifted seed-scales. Perhaps a coil of copper lace opening near the cliff-face. Perhaps a hollow stem exhaling a tone so low it makes your vision swim at the edges. Not a face. Not a foot. A chord becoming briefly animal.

I would be the being that cannot survive in a silent room.

I would be what the Copper Chancel is always trying to say.

I would be the dry lushness of sound made into a shape it can almost hold. I would be the turbulence at the crown of the world, bound by broken walls, warm through the soles, bright with no water, green with no leaf-sap, alive with no ordinary breath. I would be the place’s long memory taking a temporary step.

And when the wind comes down from the cliff-line and the whole bounded space begins to play, I would not be one thing answering it.

I would be the answer.
