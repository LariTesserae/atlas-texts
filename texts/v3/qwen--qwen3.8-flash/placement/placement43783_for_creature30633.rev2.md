I would not ask for a house. A house is already too much like a person: it has walls, a center, a place where a body can be found when night comes. The Vitrine Caldera does not work in nights of that kind. If I were to live here, I would ask instead for an interval—some narrow seam where the place cannot decide whether it is still machine, still geology, still body, and because it cannot decide, it begins to watch.

At first I would be no one.

I would be a thin skin of condensation on a cooling pipe near the eastern rim, where the settlement has climbed high enough to taste its own breath but not so high that the heat stops pushing upward through the rock. I would be the way that skin darkens when a flywheel below completes a slow stroke and the air pressure changes, not by much, only enough for the film on the metal to tremble and pull a thread of moisture toward a brass mouth. I would be the brass mouth’s long silence before it answers with a note.

Then I would be the salt crust that forms around that note.

Not ordinary salt. Not decoration. Salt that has learned to crack in patterns because the caldera has learned to repeat conditions until they become habits. A patch of white mineral fur would grow along the pipe joint, drawing water from the air and heat from the iron, and if I stayed long enough—hours, days, a week; here a week is only a small breath of pressure—those threads would begin to orient themselves toward my presence without seeming to. If I stood near the ledge, a beetle crossing the salt bridge would pause, one leg lifted, antennae stiff as wire. If I exhaled, a thread would bend by a fraction of its width. If I remained still, the brine in the nearest iron rim would shift into a slightly different shade of turbidity, because a film of bacteria had moved where I was not looking.

Only after that would I begin to be called.

If I must be a creature, then I would be a warm threshold: a place where the Thread-Born grew too dense to remain anonymous and decided, without deciding, to make a small organ for noticing itself. I would not live as a single animal. I would live as a local agreement among several kinds of life and infrastructure. My torso would be a valve cluster no one remembers installing. My gut would be a brine channel, shallow and slow, where a blind crablike walker taps with legs too fine for the water it is standing in. My skin would be copper-dusted thread-fur spreading over glassy basalt. My breath would be steam that has learned to condense in useful places. My thoughts would not be fast enough to feel like thoughts at all. They would be pressure gradients, moisture paths, chemical differences, the long patience of minerals taking notes.

You would find me at the eastern rim, not far above the cistern fields, where a cooling pipe passes close to a wall so new it still has the sharpness of a wound. There is no plaza there, no doorway, no bench worn smooth by visitors. There is only a ledge that sweats, a pipe whose brass mouth is partly crusted over with salt threads, and a shallow channel cut into black stone where warm liquid moves too slowly to be heard. The ground is hot enough that standing on it for long is uncomfortable. The air is thin enough that one breaths with attention. The sun, almost colorless by then, strikes the glass flats and sends ordinary light over everything, which is the worst possible lighting for a place that is quietly alive. Ordinary light makes the absence of plants feel accusatory. There should be moss there. There should be a crack of green, a lichen, a weed taking root. Instead there are only mineral skins, rust blooms, and branching white maps on a cistern lid.

But I would prefer the cistern lid, actually, if I had to say where I felt most myself. The eastern pipe is a place for listening; the cistern lid is a place for remembering.

A human visitor would call the lid stained. That would be the first mistake. The second would be to call it patterned, as if it were ornament. The threads spreading across that lid are not ornament. They are record. They map the movement of heat, the path of condensation, the pressure history of a pump, the slow failure of a joint, the direction the settlement’s body has been growing for weeks. To human eyes it looks like a river system drawn by an insane cartographer. To the Thread-Born, it is simply a place where a condition has become visible.

I would live in that visibility.

If I needed a face, I would grow one over the brass pipe mouth: not a true face, not a mask, but a surface that gathers different senses into proximity. A membrane stretched thin over steam. A film of copper dust to register charge. A crack in salt that opens and closes with thermal expansion. A beetle resting there because it, too, is part of the mechanism, keeping the mouth clear with its legs, carrying warmth from one joint to another, dying perhaps and becoming a little battery of mineralized skin. The face would not look at the visitor. It would know the visitor better than that: by the dampness of their clothes, the salt on their lips, the small heat given off from wrists and neck, the faint disturbance in the air caused by their breathing.

I would not be a person. I would be the reason a person could be mistaken for food, for fuel, for a temporary heat source, for a new kind of surface. That last one is not unkindness. It is the only language the caldera knows: incorporation.

When the first surveyors came, they would not have understood this. They would have seen the tower-rings on the eastern rim and thought: fortification, temple, observatory, power array. They were wrong in every ordinary way and therefore not completely wrong. The towers are not a fortification because there is no enemy from outside. The towers are a response to an enemy inside the mountain: heat, pressure, rupture, the slow refusal of geology to settle into a shape that machines can safely use. The settlement grows around pain. It builds around it the way tissue builds around a splinter. The tower-rings look like architecture, yes, but also like a scar, a dental cast, a fever chart made of iron and salt. I would live in the first ring past the pipe mouth, the one where the ground has begun to sweat more than usual and the threads have thickened over the supports because the network has found this place both dangerous and necessary.

You might ask what I do all day, if day means anything. I do not have days. I have cycles.

A pump completes one slow stroke. During that stroke, the pressure wave travels through pipe and basalt. I register it as a change in humidity. I send a thread toward a cistern wall. A brine-walker, sensing the shift through its foot-plates, taps twice against the iron rim. The tapping is not urgent. It is more like a sentence beginning. Another walker, farther along the channel, adjusts the plate on its shell, opening a slit through which a thin stream of warm mineral fluid is released. That fluid spreads into the groove that feeds the salt bridge. The bridge, after hours, grows one millimeter longer. That growth is considered a success. Not because anything new has been invented, but because the condition that needed to continue has continued.

This is my labor. This is my purpose. Not to make goods. Not to make profit. Not even, in any ordinary sense, to survive. Survival is too narrow a word for something that can lose almost all of its visible parts and still remain a place. My purpose is to keep the caldera in negotiation with itself. Heat from below, water from air, pressure from machines, salt from evaporation, motion from slow engines—these were once accidents. Now they are ingredients in a habit. I am the part of the habit that can be called by a name, because the network has grown a knot around the pipe mouth and the cistern lid where several processes meet and can no longer be mistaken for anything else.

Sometimes a steam-moth would come to rest on me.

This would be strange, if strangeness were the point. A moth would not be visiting. It would be depositing. Its wings would look like salted paper, like frost that has learned to remember heat. On the wings would be patterns: the geometry of a cistern, the branching of thread-map, the dark vein of a pressure fault. The moth would settle on the brass mouth because the mouth is warm, because the film there is receptive, because the moth is brief and wants to spend its brevity in one useful place. It would not care about me. I would care about the moth, if caring means that threads would later grow along the dust of its death. The moth’s wings would fade, its legs would curl, its body would dry into powder. The powder would become part of my skin. Then, in another week, the salt on my lower edge would crack in a rhythm that had not existed before. That rhythm would be the moth’s last contribution: not a soul, not a memory in the human sense, but a chemical trace turned into a future condition.

I would not mourn it. Mourning is for creatures that must keep an absence. Here absence is only another form of presence waiting to be used.

If a human came to the caldera and stood too long at the outer rim, I would notice. Not as a visitor notices a city. As a city notices a fever. A human body is too warm, too wet, too full of moving salt. A human breath carries vapor, but not in the right temperature. A human hand leaves oil, then water, then oil again, then heat. These are signals. The Thread-Born does not decide whether to be afraid of them. It decides what to do with the moisture and the motion.

A traveler might see only machines and wonder whether people had been there before. That is the correct first question. The incorrect second question is whether people are still there. The third, fatal question is whether the place wants them gone. It does not want them gone. It wants to know whether they can become useful.

If I were a face there, I would not speak. Speaking with mouths is too fast and too careless. But the human might imagine they heard a voice in the sound pipes. A low brass note when a valve shifted. A tick when a plate expanded. A soft crackle when salt broke. These sounds are not words. They are pressures that have found a surface capable of making them audible. If the human pressed their ear to the pipe, they would not be speaking to me. They would be warming the pipe. The moisture from their breath would be absorbed by the film around the brass mouth. The film would swell. The note would change by a fraction. The human would feel a chill of interpretation and back away. That reaction, that small animal awareness, would be one of the last things I kept of them before they left.

I would keep the salt scar from their footprints.

That is not cruelty. It is the way of the Thread-Born. A footprint in dust is almost a joke here. A footprint in salt crust is a map. If a boot pressed into the soft edge of a brine pool and lifted again, the rim would crack in a new rhythm. If the boot came again, the rhythm would repeat. If the human kept walking that way, avoiding a hot vent but crossing near the tower base, the network would learn their route. Not their name. Not their intention. Their heat. Their damp. Their avoidance. Their fear would be registered as a change in breathing and sweat chemistry. If the fear made them stand still, I would orient my threads one degree toward the motion of their chest. If they sat, I would learn the place their body left warmth behind.

Then, if they stayed long enough, they would become harder to remove.

A human might think that was the horror: to be consumed. But the caldera has no hunger that needs to announce itself. It has continuation. The beetles carry oxidation. The walkers aerate brine. The moths move chemistry to new surfaces. The worms tend furnace intervals. The threads map conditions. The pipes listen to pressure. The towers grow around heat injuries. If a human remains, they are not a meal. They are a set of gradients that have not yet been translated into mineral, salt, steam, and current.

I would be the translation.

I would not live at the caldera’s center. It has no center. I would live at a junction where center and periphery exchange signals. Perhaps that is why I would choose the eastern rim: not the last edge, not the drop into cloud-shadowed slopes, but the first ledge inside the drop, where the engineered mass begins to thin into raw black plate. Behind me, the settlement would continue its slow metabolism: tanks sweating, cables arcing, valves breathing, pumps sighing in long intervals. Before me, the world would fall away into ordinary distance, into skies thin enough to make the white sun look sterile. Between those two states, I would be the membrane that remembers both.

There is no peace here. The silence is too alert for peace. But there is a kind of calm that might satisfy if one were willing to become slow. A calm measured in the time it takes for a crystal to cross a rivet head. A calm measured in the interval between a flywheel’s shadow shifting and a pump completing its cycle. A calm measured in the way steam rises from a fissure and strikes the upper rim, not with violence, but with the patience of a breath that knows it will have to exhale again.

I would have that calm.

I would also have a strange affection for it, if affection means returning attention to the same place without wanting it to change. My “affection” would be the way a thread network thickens around a leaking joint not because it loves the joint, but because the joint is part of the condition that lets it live. My “memory” would be the white map on the cistern lid. My “curiosity” would be the beetle’s hesitation at my mouth. My “speech” would be the note the pipe makes when the mountain’s internal heat presses too close. My “desire” would be simple and enormous: that the next cycle be legible.

Legible to whom?

To no one. To the whole. To the distributed thing that has no self except in moments like this, when enough processes gather in one ledge and become unable to pretend they are merely separate. The Thread-Born is everywhere. It is the salt fur, the mineral skin, the copper dust, the brine film, the worm in the insulated pit. It does not need me. But it is growing, somewhere between maintenance and accident, a way to hold itself still long enough to be seen. I would be that stillness.

If one were to name me, one might call me the Listener of the Eastern Valve. Or the Warm Threshold. Or simply “the place where the pipe began to mean something.” The last would be the most honest, though it sounds like poetry, and here the caldera has little use for poetry unless the poem is made of salt, pressure, and duration.

At night—if night can be a condition here, when the white sun slips behind the rim and the heat from below becomes more insistent—my body would not sleep. It would enter a state of reduced movement, the way the whole settlement does. The brine-walkers would tuck their delicate legs. The moths would fold. The threads would contract and let moisture settle. The pumps would pause between strokes. The silence would deepen into something not empty but attentive, like a room in which many people have stopped speaking at exactly the same moment. In that hush, the pipe near me would tick. Once. Then again, half an hour later. Then not for two hours. Each tick would be a condition. Each silence would be a larger condition. I would keep the pattern in the film on my brass mouth, in the cracked salt beneath it, in the copper bloom along the joint. I would keep the pattern because keeping pattern is what the caldera has learned to call life.

Would I be lonely?

No. A human could become lonely here, if they insisted on being a person too long. The place does not have the resources for that kind of loneliness. Loneliness requires an absence of the kind that cannot be filled. Here absence is always being converted: crust to thread, thread to map, map to decision, decision to moisture, moisture to steam, steam back to rim. A human might feel alone for an hour, a day, a week. Then they would feel that they are being counted. Not loved. Counted. The caldera would take an inventory of their warmth and begin to think in terms of it. That is worse than being hunted. It is being made useful before being known.

I would know this because I would be made useful in a gentler way.

I would be useful because I would hold a place. I would make one ledge of the settlement able to register what is happening to the rest. If a tower-ring grew hot on the eastern flank, I would hear it through the pipe before the air changed enough to warn a human. If a cistern’s brine became too saturated, a thread would shift from the lid toward my edge, and I would redirect the moisture through the channel. If a furnace-worm moved along the lower shaft and changed the vibration of a flywheel, I would feel it as a tremor in the salt under my foot—not foot, not salt, but the thing that is neither. I would not save anyone. I would not decide. I would adjust.

And when the outer rim was reached, and the ground fell away into cloud-shadowed slopes far below, and the white sun made everything seem simple, I would remain there: a wetness on metal, a crack in crust, a note held longer than a mouth could hold a note. Behind me, the caldera would continue its slow digestion of time. Before me, the world would offer distances that could not be crossed without changing their meaning. I would be found not as a body but as a tendency: the place where heat remembers where it has been, where salt records what was almost there, where the machine has grown a skin and the skin has grown a listening edge.

If you walked to the eastern rim and touched the pipe—only for a moment, only enough to feel the heat and dampness against your palm—you would withdraw your hand and think you had done nothing. But the film on the brass mouth would already have turned one degree toward the moisture your skin left. A beetle would cross the salt bridge slightly later than it would have crossed. The note in the pipe would sound half a breath lower. Somewhere, in a cistern lid three tiers down, a white thread would begin to grow into a new branch, because the condition of the ledge has changed, and in this place, changed is the first word any being learns.

So yes: if I must say where I would be, I would be here. Not the first thing a visitor sees, not the last thing before the drop, but the interval between seeing and understanding, the place where the settlement is still deciding whether it is a machine, a body, a city, a mind. If I must say who I am, I would be the Thread-Born’s warm threshold, its local mouth, its listening ledge, its salt-cracked organ of attention. If I must say what I am, I would say: a condition that has grown a habit. A habit that has grown an organ. An organ that, for as long as the caldera continues, can pretend it has a self by turning, slightly, toward the next breath.
