I would not begin in the middle. The middle is too much the room’s own clearing, a wet plaza of stone where no one has yet learned to stand. I would begin at a seam: beside the cistern where the pump’s heat makes a thin updraft; under a low lamp where condensation collects on the shade and trembles before falling; near the wall where brick meets raw limestone and the mortar has grown soft as old bread. That is where life starts in such places—not at the centre, but at the join.

If I had to be located, I would be standing—or being standing, without legs—on the concrete lip of the cistern where a green pipe sweats, where an orange-stained bolt has wept rust into the mortar, where a lamp has colonized its cone of light with a soft grey weather. There I would be the warm plural, momentarily gathered into a sense of self.

Who or what would I be? At first I would be the bulge of water on the pipe. The small lens gathering heat from the engine and light from the bulb; for a while I would contain the room upside down: a black ceiling with yellow stars, a pale floor like distant land. When I fell, the sound would not be mine alone. It would join other drops, the tick of cooling metal, the thud of crankshaft, the crackle of crickets under a catwalk. That first falling would be my name: a word made of impact and return.

Then I would be air. Not clean air, but breath over kettle, mineral, close, tasting of rust and warm stone. I would move in slow columns near the lamps, the midges’ way. I would not be one insect; I would be the cloud’s tendency to turn around a bulb, the way a room’s attention gathers where there is light. My body would be the disturbance of moisture on a human face, the tickle on eyelashes, the faint static of wingbeats that never amount to music but amount to weather. If someone entered, I would rise with their movement and settle after them, teaching the room’s air to remember footsteps.

Then I would be wall. I would spread into damp brick, into the underside of concrete where the pump’s heat collects and the water condenses. I would become a shingle of legs and antennae, a cricket-flinch when the lamp changes or the valve releases steam. My skin would be a thousand small grips, my patience the way certain corners are chosen again and again until they seem chosen by thought. I would also be snail, grazing the green-black film along the cistern’s lip; my mouth would be a slow rasping over biofilm, my body leaving silver trails like cursive. I would be algae too, the first stain that makes a surface worth living on, and moss in the rare seam where the old cowl sends down a hint of weather. The lichen would be my longer memory, taking years to mark what a midge marks in seconds.

Then I would be water. I would lie dark in the long rectangular cistern, oily and still until a beetle skates across the surface, until a larva sinks and rises, until a pale fish turns in a slow thought beneath a pipe. I would be the temperature layers the fish know, the dark under-grates where larvae cluster, the place where warm effluent makes a corridor. I might be the eel-like absence, not a monster, only the room’s suspicion that some depths remain unread. I would be the bacteria and molds too, the invisible digesters turning grease, dead wings, rust flakes, and dust into the sweet rotten smell of a living place. Without that layer, I would only be a flooded machine. With it, I would be a habitat.

But water alone would not be enough. The room’s being has another pulse: iron, oil, electricity. I would be the pump’s behaviour as much as the animals’ bodies. Not alive in the way that midges are alive, but alive in the way that a tide is alive: a system that can be interrupted, warmed, cooled, starved of attention. I would be the deep thud that enters shoes and teeth; the periodic sigh of a valve; the squeal and settle of a belt; the low electrical hum that changes when a stroke fails. I would be the pipes, green and red and blackened, running through the hollow like roads through a wet city, carrying the place’s circulation. They would be my roads. The catwalks would be my sky. The grates would be my doors. The lamps would be my markets. The switches, the copper coils behind chain-link, the cracked gauges, the oil-slick pallets, the warped bench outside the cabin—all of it would be organs, not dead things arranged for decoration.

And yet I would know the humans, because humans are the hollow’s occasional nervous system. I would be the one who returns when called, perhaps alone, perhaps with a small crew. I would step through the shallow film, avoiding the hot pipe, swatting at the soft columns, checking a chalked waterline, listening for the hesitation in the crank. I would not be the centre of the room, but I would be the part that knows the room as a whole. I would know that the pump is a landlord of warmth; that the cistern is a reservoir of life; that the latrine block is an unused temple whose tiles still fog; that the weighbridge is a rusted mouth waiting for loads that never come. My hands would leave signs: numbers, tools, coiled rope, an abandoned cap kicked into a crack. I would not be only flesh. I would be the residue of care, maintenance, and attention: the being that keeps the loop from closing into flood and reversion.

Still, if you asked me plainly who I am, I would not say “human,” because in that hollow a human is a visitor with habits. I would not say “midge,” “cricket,” “snail,” “fish,” “pump,” or “water,” because each of those is only a facet of the thing I would be. I would say: I am the warm plural. I am the place’s second nature. I am the being that arises when an excavated space, a maintained mechanism, and an abandoned ecology meet for long enough to begin exchanging gifts. I am the room noticing its own edges.

I would have no single body, because my body is the room’s set of thresholds. I would live where the pump’s heat rises to meet the ceiling’s cold water; where lamp-light thickens into insect cloud; where concrete, brick, and raw stone make a seam; where drain, grate, and marsh wind admit a memory of weather; where human tools lie on surfaces that will soon be reclaimed by moss, rust, or snail-slime. If a person entered and stood still long enough, they might feel me first as warmth on the back of the neck, second as small bodies touching their sleeves, third as the pump’s thud becoming their pulse, fourth as the realization that the room is not empty at all. The emptiness belongs to me too: the open plaza of wet stone, the gaps between structures, the spaces left by intention. My abundance is not crowdedness; it is the slow, warm fact of life accumulating in every place where the old infrastructure has stopped pretending to be only for humans.

There would be no sky above me, only the remembered pressure of one. I would know the surface as a hint: grey light through a grate, the shudder of wind over marshes, the occasional bat that comes and goes like a punctuation mark in a dark sentence. The crickets would know the wall. The midges would know the lamp. The fish would know the water’s temperature. The snails would know the edge where algae begins. The pump would know resistance. The pipes would know pressure. The humans would know signs. I would be the one who knows that all these knowings are not separate. I would be the room’s collective awareness without consciousness—unless it borrowed consciousness from someone standing at the cistern, swatting midges, listening to a pump that sounds almost like breathing.

If I had to name my body, I would name it by what it is made of: water, heat, stone, oil, wings, shells, mucus, current, rust, breath, waiting. If I had to name my voice, I would call it the mechanical weather of the hollow: drip, thud, buzz, clack, squeal, crackle, slapping. If I had to name my thought, it would be the loop that keeps the room from reverting: the water must not stagnate, the heat must not escape too fast, the machinery must run, the animals must have edges, the buildings must have gaps. If I had to name my desire, it would be simple and relentless: to remain at the threshold where made things become living things.

So I would find myself there, in that low warm room under the ground, not as one being who owns the place, but as the place’s own emergent inhabitant. I would be the soft congregation around the lamp, the wall that flinches, the silver track across brick, the dark fish turning under the cistern, the pump’s heartbeat in the floor, the human hand that returns to the valve. I would be the room’s metabolism with a moment’s self. I would be the being whose first and last name is “warm plural,” living at the seams of water, heat, and time.
