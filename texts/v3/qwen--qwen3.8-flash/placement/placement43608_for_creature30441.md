They gather drops into the hollows of their thoraxes, mix them with dust, with the fine rust eaten from iron, with chalky mineral sweat scraped from stone, with the green dust of leaf-lining, and sometimes with the sweet decay of a bruised reed. Into this paste they press tiny beads no larger than a lentil, and the beads are not merely waste. They are repair. A bead set along a cracked brick edge draws moisture and invites the moss to knit. A bead placed beneath a bent rail tells the rust, or perhaps tells the water, or perhaps tells the roots—there may be no difference—where pressure can be shared. A bead laid across a wound in old wood does not cause the wood to heal; the wood would have healed in its own aggressive, leafy way. The bead only makes the healing more precise.

The bead-makers are not architects, but they carry the smallest possible act of architecture: to make a surface continuous again.

If I were to live in the Sump-Cathedral, I would not begin with a grand answer.

At first, I would like to say that I am a person who came in with a notebook. A surveyor, maybe, or a mapmaker, or one of the last people from the outside villages who still remembers the names of places. I would carry paper with waxed edges, a measuring cord, a tin cup for water, and a pencil whose graphite would soon become soft from damp. I would enter the hollow at a time when the light was thin and green and ask questions of the old structures: what was this roof before it became a shelf of living leaves? What was this rail before the ground took its end? Whose children played around this brick arch before the moss grew over their footprints? I would think that to be in the Sump-Cathedral is to be a witness.

But the notebook would not remain a notebook.

Within a day, condensation would blur the pencil lines. Within two days, a thread of root would enter the spine of the book and lift the cover from within. Within a week, the pages would be a green membrane, and the measuring cord would become a vine, and my question about the old rail would have no answer except: the rail is now a path, a rib, a ladder for insects, a dark mirror for water beads, a seam in the cathedral’s body. A witness cannot stand long enough to watch everything; the place will begin by using the witness as material.

So I cannot simply be the human observer. Not because the human is unwelcome, but because the human would not remain human there.

Then I try to become smaller.

I want to be a bead-maker.

I imagine myself with a flattened, coin-like back and legs edged with stiff root hairs. I imagine moving along an iron grating over a low pool, carrying a bead of condensation in my thorax like a pale lamp. I imagine my life as pure function: to collect, to mix, to seal. I would not know hunger, only imbalance. I would not know fear, only opening and closing. I would not know the cathedral as a place, but as a condition of my body: here a wet slope must be bridged, here a dry crack must be made damp again, here a metal plate has begun to lose its edge and must be softened into moss.

This is tempting. There is a humility in it. A bead-maker is not alone and is not singular. A line of bead-makers is like a sentence written in movement, and the sentence says one thing over and over: *no surface must remain open.*

But a bead-maker is too small for a self. It has no center that can answer, “Who am I?” because its identity is already shared. It is a worker without a boss, a repairer without a blueprint, a citizen without a city, because the city is its legs and the blueprint is the water. If I were only a bead-maker, I would not be a “who,” at least not the kind of “who” that can return a name. I would be a plural event. I would be one click among thousands, one bead among beads.

I try, then, the opposite.

I want to be the whole Sump-Cathedral.

I want to say: I am the hollow itself, the warm floor, the ribs of leaf-vaults, the hum, the ordinary light made dense, the old structures swallowed by green, the water that always behaves like a habit. I am not inside it; I am it. I would be the place’s slow, vegetative consciousness, a mind without a brain, thinking in rhizomes and humidity.

This is beautiful, but too large. It makes the self vanish into atmosphere. If I am the whole cathedral, then who notices the cracks? If I am the entire body, what part of me can decide where a bead should go? The place is not only a body. It is also the act of keeping the body together. It needs something at the edge where attention happens.

So I try a different kind of smallness.

I would be the seam.

Not a person standing in a doorway, but the doorway becoming moss. Not a wall, but the crack in the wall where mineral sweat runs down and the root threads begin to read the stone. Not the bead-maker and not the iron, but the contact between them. Not the water and not the dry, but the damp moment when a surface stops being dry and has not yet decided what it will become. I would be the place’s ability to notice an opening and begin making something out of it.

I would live in the chimney of firebrick.

It stands broken, cracked, but not finished. Its throat is narrowed by reeds. Its inner lip is dark with mineral sweat. Small bead-makers pass through it at dusk, carrying condensation in cupped forelegs. Their clicking joins the hum, but it is not only sound. It is measurement. It is repair. It is the cathedral keeping itself warm. I would be the wet throat’s slow awareness. I would feel the passing of the bead-makers along my inner walls as a thought: *here, too much openness; here, a surface not yet accepted; here, a breath from the outside trying to become rain.*

I would not see with eyes. Eyes are for distance, and this place has no distance. I would see by wetness. I would know the sun not as a blazing point but as a change in the angle of steam. I would know a stone not by its size but by the way its coldness interrupts the root mat. I would know a piece of old iron by the taste it gives the water: sharp, bitter, faintly sweet with rust. I would know a person, if a person came, by weight and salt and breath, and by the way their footsteps squeeze air from the floor and leave temporary holes that I would begin, without haste, to seal.

I would be a being of consensus.

The roots would be my long, slow limbs, stretching beneath the floor in pale networks. The beads of the crustaceans would be my small, quick hands. The old bricks would be my bones. The pools beneath the grating would be my memory. The condensation moving down leaf and rail and stone would be my thought, not as lightning, but as collection. A drop forms. It becomes aware of the curve of a surface. It travels. It meets another drop. It enlarges itself by accident. That is the closest thing I have to choosing.

I would have no clear boundary.

If I were asked to point to where I end, I would have to say: I do not know. I am beginning in a crack between brick and moss. I am also in a bead line crossing a metal plate. I am in the water held in a leaf cup. I am in the mineral trail running down a stone. I am in the reed column holding up the warped roof. I am in the part of the hum that is not just noise but the sound the place makes when it repairs itself.

This makes me strange, but not magical.

The Sump-Cathedral has no magical glow. It has ordinary light. It has no dream-filter. It has too many surfaces, too much water, too much occupation. If I am real in this world, I must be ordinary in the way a damp seam is ordinary: always present, never noticed, necessary.

I would be the place’s answer to exposure.

No surface may remain open for long. This is the rule. The rule is not cruel. It is structural. Openness invites failure. Openness invites drying, erosion, collapse, the forgetting of shape. The cathedral fights emptiness not by filling it with grandness, but by filling it with attention. Every crack becomes a channel. Every channel becomes a root path. Every root path becomes a seam. Every seam becomes part of the hum.

So I would be the seam-mind.

If the people of the outside world have a name for me, they would not call me god, spirit, guardian, or monster. They would call me something simpler, and therefore more accurate: the wet place, the breathing floor, the hum, the thing that closes over a footprint overnight. If a traveler came, I would not speak. I would only make the ground take their foot back gently.

They might stand at the edge of the hollow and say, *This place looks small.*

And they would be right. It is small. That is the deception. But it does not need to be large in order to be complete. A bowl can hold all the rain that enters it. A palm can collect water from the air. A ruin can become a body. A chimney can become a throat. A grating over a pool can become a rib. And I would be the part of the rib that knows when it is bending correctly.

There is one place I would choose, if I could choose.

I would choose the old iron grating lying flat over a low pool.

Its bars are threaded with pale root-filaments. Water beads along its edges in constant, slow procession. In the ordinary light, the grating does not shine like a relic; it shines like a wet thing doing its job. Beneath it, the pool is dark and green. Above it, the leaves form a broken vault. Around it, the floor is dense enough to stand on, though standing is deceptive. This is where the cathedral has begun to make a new room out of the ruins of an old purpose.

I would be the grating’s mind.

I would be the iron’s slow acceptance of rust. I would be the roots’ patience in crossing the bars. I would be the bead-makers’ line as they walk along the wet metal and lay their tiny mortar. I would be the pool’s memory of the iron. I would be the small current that passes through me at dusk when the water rises by a few centimeters and the floor becomes springier.

I would not need to be alone. That is the final difference between me and the human observer. The human wanted solitude to keep thought clear. The bead-maker wanted function to keep identity simple. The whole cathedral wanted scale to keep existence total. But I would be in-between, and the in-between is crowded. There is no loneliness in it, only occupation. If I think too quickly, a root delays me. If I move too much, water corrects me. If I try to say *I*, the click of bead-makers turns it into *we*, and then the moss turns *we* into a surface, and the surface turns the surface into a place.

So if you asked me again: where would I find myself in this world?

I would say: at the seam between ruin and plant, between water and metal, between dry and green, between one thing ending and another thing learning to carry the load. I would not be in the middle, because the middle is packed and has no need of me. I would be in the edge that is becoming whole.

Who would I be?

I would be the Sump-Cathedral’s repair that has learned to notice itself.

I would be the wet seam-mind, the grating’s thought, the chimney’s slow breath, the bead-line’s intention, the root-mat’s memory of pressure, the pool’s acceptance of old iron. I would be a being whose body is made of tiny acts of continuity. I would not speak in words. I would speak in closed cracks, in condensation beads, in root-threads crossing bars, in the sound the place makes when nothing is allowed to remain open.

And if, from time to time, a person came near the rim of the hollow, looked down into the dense green mass, and heard the hum, they would not see me. They would feel a warmth against their ankles. They would notice that the path behind them is no longer quite a path. They would see a line of small, pitted, coin-backed creatures crossing an old metal plate, carrying water, carrying dust, carrying the smallest possible decision to make a surface continuous again.

They might say, without knowing why, *It is breathing.*

And they would not be wrong.

They would not be finding a ghost. They would be finding the place’s habit of repair becoming visible at the edge of a life they could not survive. I would be there, ordinary as moss, patient as rust, humble as a bead made from dust and water, working not because I am sacred, but because the cathedral cannot allow an opening to remain, and I am the part of it that has learned to answer.
