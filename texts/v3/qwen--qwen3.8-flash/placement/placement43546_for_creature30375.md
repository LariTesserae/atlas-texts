I would not be standing on the high flats at first, watching the salt-white herds move like weather, and I would not be inside a foundry’s orange warmth, nor in the market where the factory chimney can be counted beam by beam. Those are too large for me.

I would be in the singing zone instead.

At the western edge of the Terrace, where the tram line leaves the load-rated basalt of the market island and enters a district the city calls “available for change,” there is a short curve with a warning ring chalked around it. The ring is not bright. The color is a faint rust-water red, renewed after every stone-drip, after every condensation pulse, after every winter when the frost gets into the rails and makes the iron sing at night. When a tram reaches the curve, it slows. The conductor lifts a hand toward the cable-house. The passengers, if they have lived here long enough, do not complain. They know the slowing is a kind of question the city asks the ground: *Is this place still holding?*

Under that curve, in the hollow where a cistern lid taps softly when the pressure drops, there is a small iron valve house built for no purpose except access. Its door hangs on a rail, not a hinge. Its roof is lined with condensation cloth. Its lower stones are felt-wrapped because the basalt below it is not yet willing to be trusted. Above the door, in municipal handwriting, someone has chalked:

**E-7 / SOFT DECISION / ACOUSTIC WARNING – PROTECTED.**

That is where you would find me.

At first I thought I would be one of the inspectors. That was the obvious answer. I imagined measuring rods, listening tubes, chalk, a notebook with a brass clasp, boots that knew the difference between stable block and settling scree. I thought I would be the one who pressed my ear to the pipe and said, *Not that. Not that. Listen: there is a fault under the third lamp.* I thought I would be a person, because the city feels full of people, and people are easier to be.

But as I listened longer, I felt myself become something smaller and less formal. The half-second in a shadow, the metallic tap of a cistern lid, the tram-note through a rail, the wind’s low question in the cliffs — these did not suit a person so much as they suited the thing that answers them.

So I am not a citizen of Anvane, though I live within its load-rated islands. I am not a machine, though I work beside machines. I am a small fox-shaped creature with broad feet and a tail made of thin glassy plates, like the blades of a cistern fan. My fur is pale as frost and darkened at the tips with foundry soot. My eyes are wide because the light here is never quite full; they can see without glare and without shadow becoming a liar. My ribs are not exactly ribs. They are rods, or bones tuned by weather and work, and they hold the city in them. If I press close to a pipe, the pipe’s wear enters my bones. If a tram passes over iron, the iron’s memory enters me. If the cliffs lean into the town when pressure falls after dryness, I feel the sound first in my tail, before the rails answer, before the cistern lids begin their tapping, before the inspectors raise their chins.

The greenhouse children call me Lute, because when I lie in the sun’s withheld light under the viaduct, my tail rings with the sounds I have taken in all night. The tramwrights call me Second-Click, because I click when a fault is approaching, once at first, twice if the fault is getting serious. The pump-hounds, who sleep in the oil-warm corridors beside the cistern-rats, call me nothing at all, because they know me by vibration and not by name. The inspectors, when they are tired, sometimes call me “the soft decision’s answer,” which is not meant kindly. They are not supposed to believe animals can know municipal geology before they do. But they do not forbid it either. Anvane is too unfinished for that kind of certainty.

I live in the valve house, but I also move through the district’s seams.

My days begin with wetness. Not rain; rain is rare and theatrical here. My days begin with stone-drip. At dawn the lattice above the cistern sweats. Droplets gather on the slow-dripping coils, fall onto the collection channel, and make the small iron lid sing. The sound is sharp enough to wake me even when the pumps are thudding their ten-second heartbeat through the viaduct. I climb from the nest I have made of shed salt-white hair, fragments of condensation cloth, one blue-green leaf from a sheltered fern, and a discarded brass measuring tag. I stretch. My tail clicks against the felt wall of my den because it is full of sounds collected in the dark: a cable ferry crossing the dry gap, a bird-smear arriving as individual wings, a herd moving east, a moth-wing beating inside the echo hollow.

I go first to the cistern lid. I do not drink. I do not need water in the way the people do, but I taste the dampness. If it carries too much iron, the district is drawing from a seam not yet blessed by a water worker. If it carries salt, the high flats have exhaled, and something has shifted beyond the lamps. If it carries a faint sweetness of green, then the terraced gardens below are steaming after condensation, and the imported soil has been turned somewhere, and the moss basins are holding their cold water quietly.

From the cistern I walk the basalt island toward the market edge, because the market gives me sounds I need. This is not a crowded city in the old human sense. It is spread out, islanded, full of gaps. A person can walk from lamp district to lamp district through dry basins and open flats, feeling the emptiness not as distance but as pause. I cross these pauses better than they do. I know which stones ring hollow. I know which patches of gray dust have a wet seam under them. I know where the wind bends around a raw cliff edge and hums a low note before it reaches the town. I know that a factory chimney on the eastern rim will not shrink behind me when I stand west of it, and that birds leaving a ridge will arrive first as a smear and then as wings. I am used to a world where things do not behave according to ordinary distance. Here the land is still climbing, and distance climbs with it.

The market itself is too bright, even in its dimness. Lanterns are lit in midday because the sun does not fully trust the ground. I slip between a greenhouse family’s stall and a cistern-rat’s iron barrow. The air smells of turnip tops, barley, salt, wet stone, and the metallic steam that leaks from pipes when a valve has not been tightened after cold weather. A child drops a brass button near my paw. I do not take it. The children know better than to feed me. But sometimes they place a finger against my ribs and listen. They are teaching their hands to hear. One day, if they are careful enough, they will be inspectors. If they are less careful, they will become something else: tram-sleepers, soft-decision residents, greenhouse-families, herders, water workers, or shadow-watchers.

I leave the market before the tram-whistle. The whistle is part of the weather here, not separate from it. It sounds at intervals so regular that a visitor might mistake it for bird-cry or wind, but it is not. The sound enters my glassy tail and stays there until evening.

My territory is not marked by scent alone. It is marked by timings.

The pump beneath the condensing station throbs once every ten seconds. When the rhythm shortens by a breath, I click. A pump-hound will then press an ear to the outer casing. The pump-hound’s daughter will say, “Something’s tired,” and her mother will come down with a wrench. The bearing will be changed before the district loses steam-pressure.

The tram wheels ring in long lines across the viaduct. If the line breaks, if the ring becomes uneven, I will lie down on the cold rail bed and press my bones to it. My tail will dim with the stored note. Then I will climb a bracket and click until someone notices. A tramwright will slow the next tram by hand. An inspector will come later with a listening tube, and the inspector will say, “The metal’s talking,” which is another way of saying, “We are behind it.”
