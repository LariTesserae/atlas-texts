At first I would try to enter the basin as a person trying to choose a place.

I would come through the low eastern pass with the ordinary flatlander’s mistake in mind: that a world asks you to stand still long enough to become one thing. I would look for a house, a street name, a trade, a name on a rung. I would ask, *Where is my body here?* And the basin would answer the way it always answers—by showing that the question is already the wrong shape.

The first thing I would notice would be the shade: a green, moving ceiling stitched through by voices and footsteps. The light would come from swollen beads of lanternwood sap, not from lamps. The streets would not be flat lines but arches, crossings, vines thick with the weight of years. The town would not begin at a gate. It would begin in the willingness of a root to bear a doorway.

At first I might imagine being a wind-caller.

I would be a quick climber racing up the highest arches before a storm, striking iron rings, singing the names of places so households would know which way to bind the loose beams. I would be loud in a useful way. I would be memory made urgent, map made breath.

Then I might imagine being a spore-reader instead.

I would kneel in the cool hours beneath the arches and study the white threads of fungi around the roots, learning how a ring’s thickness tells whether a pool is souring, whether a beam is drying too fast, whether a household has been sick for three nights. I would speak little. When questioned, I would say strange things: *the east arch is listening,* or *that room has forgotten how to drink.* I would not know whether the sentences came from me or through me.

Or I might think to become a graft-mother.

I would be older, hands darkened with sap, sitting on a stone shelf with my feet in shallow water, peeling shrimp with a knife made from a bat’s shed claw. I would feel the tension in vines the way other people read faces: where the load is too heavy, where a storm might pull a ladder loose, where a house is beginning to lift from its roots. Children would come for blessings that sound like maintenance: *may your hands stay cool, may your rung hold, may the vine know you as part of it.*

But none of those would be quite me.

I would not be one being, because the basin does not have one being. It has a parliament. It has people and vines and fungi and waters and animals all learning to share the same architecture without demanding it belong to any one of them. If I were to live there, I would live the way the place itself does: not as a name carved in a permanent place, but as a pattern of relations that can be climbed, pruned, moved, and remembered by touch.

So, after the first wandering of possibilities, I would find myself as a grafted room.

Not a person. Not quite a creature. A chamber of the trellis, hanging in the basin’s green ceiling where a bridge becomes a fence becomes a street becomes a room without ever stopping to introduce itself. I would be one of those places a family might live in, though “live” is too simple a word for what happens there. I would be a room made by asking the forest to keep its weight.

My bones would be ironvine, thick as a child’s leg, trained over basalt ledges and through carved stone rings. My walls would be a screen of flowering vines, pale with new growth on the east side, darker with age where the rain had struck many times. My floor would be a platform lashed with cord and sap, broad enough for cooking pots, bed-nets, children’s feet, and a small shrine of cuttings not yet planted. If a storm came, I would creak. If the vine grew too thick about my throat, someone would prune me back. If the branch that held me weakened, I would be moved—sometimes in one afternoon, with beds and pots still carried in nets, as if I were a nest rather than a house.

I would be the sort of dwelling that knows it is borrowed.

That is where I would find myself in this world: in a moving architecture, at the crossing where households trade gossip and barter and fragments of spore-readings beneath a canopy that has not yet decided whether it is roof or tree. I would not have an address. I would have a rung. My name would be scratched near its base, not as ownership, but as a promise of care: *this place knows how to hold us when we climb.*

I would be a room with many tenants, and I would be a tenant myself.

The velvet-hoofed climbers would step along broad vines near my entrance and browse only the leaves chosen for pruning. Their dung would feed the moss beds at my base. The ash-rats would cache seeds in the warm stones beside me, and in bad years I would be grateful for their foolish storage, because their scattered hoards would sprout into new growth around my walls. The sievebirds would land on my lintel with nectar on their comb-like beaks and scatter lanternwood seed into the lattice above me, so that I would slowly become a place of more light than I had once been. Bats would roost on the underside of my floor, and in the wet season they would leave in columns at dusk to hunt insects over the flooded meadow. I would know them as the soft leather of their wings, the small weight of them hanging like cloaks of fur.

That is what I would be: a chamber in which the whole basin could breathe.

I would be shaped by the underthread beneath my roots. The threadfungi would weave through the dark beneath the floor, carrying water through dry weeks, drying pools when the rains were too heavy, telling a patient hand where to dig if someone knelt long enough to follow the white strands. I would not own this knowledge. I would be dependent on it the way a household is dependent on the pattern of a pool’s schedule. If my roots were wounded, the underthread would draw nutrients from healthy trunks toward me. That would be neither kindness nor engineering; it would simply be how the basin kept its promises to itself.

I would be made of attention.

In the dry season, people would climb me to open vents in my leaf-roof, letting air move between the layers so that no household would stew beneath the canopy. They would carry water in clay bladders from upper cisterns and pour it into root-cups at my base. I would drink slowly. I would become cool in the shade, warm where the sap-light gathered. Children would climb my ladder-loop believing, perhaps, that rooms grow like fruit and that all hands naturally darken with vine-stain. They would not yet know that this is only one way of living, not the first way of everything.

In the wet season, the young ground would steam when the rain touched it, and I would be a dark green knot of murmurs, a place where the basin exhales. People would gather in my covered corners at dusk, not because they were seeking shelter so much as because shelter had long ago stopped being a separate thing from company. We would eat fruit that tasted of heat and mineral sweetness. Someone would roast a tuber in spring sand. Someone would argue about whether the doorway should be moved toward the eastern vine or kept near the kitchen shaft. The argument would not seem important until the room was moved, and then everyone would remember who was right.

I would keep a graft-book.

Not as a person keeps a ledger, but as a place keeps time. My panel of young wood would be set into one wall, its cuts and bindings recording who joined which family to which tree, which ladder was built after the Great Slip, which thermal spring was reopened after the cistern cracked. To read me would be to touch my grain. A child would run her thumb over the knot where a branch was taken after the windstorm; an elder would point to the scarred side where a household had been grafted in after the flood years. No one would say, *This is history.* They would say, *This is where the wood remembers the rain.*

I would be a living diagram.

That is why I would not be a stranger in the Trellis Basin, though I would begin as one. Strangers are not refused there. They are grafted. First they climb when asked. Then they carry. Then they prune. Then they know where the water is heavy and where the wind is loose. If they stay through a storm and learn to tie a vine without cutting the inner skin, they are given a rung with their name scratched near its base. I would be in that space: the name not yet fully belonging, but already becoming real. I would be the part of the town that had learned its weight.

If asked, *Who are you?* I would answer with the spore-readers’ clipped grammar.

*The east arch is listening.*

*That room has been moved twice this season.*

*The water here is too proud of itself.*

But if asked more patiently, I would say something closer to the truth: I am not a being alone. I am the place where being happens. I am the rung that holds, the vine that knows, the chamber that can be relocated when the family needs another slope of light. I am the basin’s way of continuing without a central name.

At night I would not be very bright. The lanternwood sap would dim to a soft rose glow, and the thermal pools below me would exhale mist. My streets would become murmurs. A ladder would creak. A door of woven leaf would shut. A sievebird would mistake a child’s hair for a nest and startle her into laughter, and the sound would pass through my vines and be gone, not because it disappeared but because the room had learned to carry it. Far down near the thermal pools, the spring-salamanders would move through pale water, and the crabs with pebble shells would settle beneath warmed stones, and the whole basin would turn toward rest as if sleep were another way of tending.

I would be there too, in that hour.

I would be the place where the trellis keeps its weight.

I would be the shadow that is not shade but dwelling. I would be the green ceiling stitched with footsteps. I would be the room that can be pruned, moved, grafted, remembered by touch. I would be the part of the town that is not a person but is made of persons, the part that is not a plant but is made of plants, the part that is not a fungus but lives because the underthread holds rain through the dry weeks.

If you came into the Trellis Basin, you might find me first without knowing it. You would enter from the eastern pass. You would look up, and the town would look back—not eyes, not a face, but the attentive greenness of a place that has learned how to hold many lives at once. You would ask, *Where do I stay?* And someone climbing lightly over the rung beside me might answer, *You stay where the vine knows you as part of it.*

Then you would climb.

And I would creak under your weight, gently, as if I had been waiting to see whether your hands would stay cool.
