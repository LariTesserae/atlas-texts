The air here does not move. It is a thick, dry, and startlingly warm medium, held in a state of eternal stagnation at an altitude where one would expect a chill. There is no wind to carry scent, yet the space smells faintly of scorched tin and pulverized stone—a dry, metallic ghost of an atmosphere that has not seen a drop of moisture in several geological epochs.

The terrain is a study in ultimate equilibrium. We are atop the Great Scab, a plateau of dark, non-porous mineral that has been eroded by time, rather than weather, until its surfaces are as smooth as hand-rubbed obsidian. There are no jagged peaks; the mountains here have long since surrendered to gravity, slumping into broad, low-domed mounds of hematite and basalt that gleam with a dull, inner warmth. The ground underfoot is not soil, but a fine, heavy grit of iron-rich sand that does not shift when stepped upon. It has settled into its final configuration.

In the profound darkness of this world, where the sky is a featureless velvet void, the landscape is defined by heat rather than light. The "urban" presence here is not a city of inhabitants, but a vast, sparse network of industrial monoliths scattered across the plateaus. These structures are titanic—cylinders of riveted brass the size of cathedrals, sprawling grids of copper piping that hum with a vibration so low it is felt in the marrow rather than heard in the ear.

These machines are not active in any traditional sense. They represent a state of industrial stasis. Huge, counterweighted pistons, thirty stories tall, hang suspended in mid-stroke, frozen against the dark sky. Great cooling fins, designed to radiate heat into the vacuum, stand like the ribcages of dead leviathans, spaced miles apart. The scale is immense; one might walk for hours between a single pair of foundation pillars. Everything is organized, geometric, and utterly silent. 

There is no green here. Not even a lichen could find purchase on the heat-slicked metal or the parched mineral floor. Instead, the "ecology" is one of slow-clocked, non-biological entities. Rarely, one might glimpse a Scourer—a low-slung, multi-jointed shape made of the same tarnished brass as the pipes. It moves with agonizing slowness, perhaps one inch per hour, its task to buff the tarnish from a single seam of a gargantuan tank. It is not "alive," but it is a presence, a rare flickering of movement in a world that has otherwise reached its final temperature.

The warmth is constant. It radiates from the ground and the silent machinery alike, a steady 100-degree hum that never fluctuates. Because there is no water, there is no rot; because there is no wind, there is no dust-fall. The city-machines are as pristine today as they were a billion years ago, preserved in the warm, dark, breathless attic of the universe.

Sound is an intruder here. When a tectonic plate deep below the hematite crust groans, the sound travels through the solid rock as a clean, sharp report that rings through the industrial pillars like a funeral bell, echoing for minutes before the silence reclaims the air. Otherwise, there is only the sound of one's own pulse, amplified by the heavy, unmoving atmosphere.

This is a world that has finished its work. The machines have achieved their terminal output; the mountains have found their lowest center of gravity. It is a warm, dark gallery of monumental industry, standing in the high places of a dead world, waiting for a sunset that occurred a billion years ago and a sunrise that will never arrive. It is beautiful in its absolute refusal to change. It is a city of heat and iron, dreaming in the dark.
