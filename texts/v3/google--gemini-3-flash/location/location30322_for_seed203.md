The environment is a vast, subterranean hollow—a world-sized rupture within the crust of a planet, so immense that its ceiling is lost in a high, radiant haze. It is called the **White Fray**.

Despite being deep below the surface, the Fray is flooded with a blinding, sterile light. This light does not come from a sun, but from the very air itself—a high-energy luminescence caused by the friction of microscopic, airborne spores. The light is brilliant but offers no warmth; the air is perennially cold, hovering just above freezing, smelling of ozone and crushed mint.

The landscape is defined by its paradoxical density. It is an environment of "total vegetation," yet it feels hauntingly empty. Imagine a void the size of a continent, crisscrossed by gargantuan, miles-long filaments of pale, translucent flora. These are not trees, but structural vines the thickness of city blocks, stretching from the unseen floor to the blinding ceiling. There is no soil, no rock, no dirt. Everything that exists is plant matter—either living, calcified, or decaying into a fibrous peat.

The scale is so immense that the Fray generates its own internal weather. Because the environment is "turbulent," the landscape is never static. These massive vines grow with a violent, terrifying speed—shifting, coiling, and snapping in a process of constant, chaotic renewal. A traveler might look out across a ten-mile gap of empty air to see a "mountain" of moss-like fluff form in a matter of hours, only to be torn apart by the sudden, whipping motion of a neighboring vine-stalk. The "young" nature of the erosion is evident in the sharpness of the flora; the leaves are like sheets of serrated glass, and the broken ends of snapped branches are jagged and splintered, having not yet been smoothed by the slow rot of time.

Water is a rare, precious presence. It does not flow in rivers but clings to the vegetation in the form of massive, trembling globes of condensation. These "water-pockets" are often suspended thousands of feet in the air, trapped in the crooks of giant, silver-barked limbs. Occasionally, a "growth-quake" will shake these globes loose, sending a vertical river crashing down through the void, which freezes into a slushy mist before it ever hits a solid surface.

The sound of the Fray is a heavy, pressurized quiet, punctuated by the sudden, gunshot-cracks of wood splintering under the stress of its own growth. It is the sound of a forest trying to become a planet in a hurry.

Life here is scarce and precarious. Small, multi-limbed creatures with hook-like claws—the rare fauna—skitter along the undersides of the great vines, moving with frantic speed to avoid being crushed by the shifting topography. 

Human presence is a faint, primitive blemish on this grand biological machine. Scattered across the more stable "dead-zones"—where the vines have calcified into a bone-white, wooden substrate—are tiny outposts. These are not cities, but fragile nests made of woven fiber and hardened sap. The technology is elementary: bone-needles, hand-spun rope made from vine-hair, and tools of sharpened flint scavenged from the rare pockets of mineral-rich bark. There is no fire here; the air is too damp and the wood too green. Instead, the inhabitants use bioluminescent mosses trapped in gourds to provide a softer light than the blinding glare of the void.

To live in the White Fray is to live in a state of constant, vertical vertigo. There is no "ground," only the next limb, the next vine, and the endless, freezing brightness above and below. You are a microscopic inhabitant of a giant, thrashing lung, watching as the horizon reconfigures itself every single day.
