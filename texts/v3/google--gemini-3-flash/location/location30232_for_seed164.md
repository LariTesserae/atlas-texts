The scale of the Aethel-Peaks is not measured in leagues, but in the terrifying distance between breaths of air. Here, at an elevation where the atmosphere begins to fray into the black of the void, the world is a vertical archipelago of razor-edged obsidian and polished steel.

The terrain is impossibly young. There are no rounded hills or gentle valleys; the mountains are jagged, geometric splinters that seem to have been thrust upward only hours ago. They are sharp enough to cut the wind. Every precipice is a clean, surgical break, untouched by the smoothing hand of time. Yet, despite this geological youth, the peaks are encrusted with a lush, suffocating carpet of cold-weather flora. Massive, translucent mosses the color of bruised plums cling to the vertical faces, and thick, ropey lichens weave through the teeth of the rocks. Everything is wet. The air is a constant, heavy suspension of ice-chilled mist that never quite congeals into rain, but instead "weeps" off the surfaces in a perpetual, silent drainage. Water slickens every inch of the world, turning the black stone into mirrors.

Integrated into this jagged wilderness is a colossal industrial infrastructure. This is not a ruin, but a functioning, automated city-machine of continental proportions. Massive brass pistons, three hundred feet in diameter, are driven directly into the heart of the peaks, rhythmically hammering into the stone with a force that vibrates the very marrow of one’s bones. Elevated railways, slick with condensation, bridge the mile-wide chasms between spires, though no trains run upon them. Instead, they serve as conduits for thick, pulsating cables and steaming pipes.

The density of this place is its most unsettling feature. While the machinery is complex and the vegetation is thick, there is almost nothing else. The gaps between the spires are immense, hollow voids of freezing fog where the eye finds no purchase for miles. It is an urbanity of ghosts—a sprawling, mechanized civilization that occupies only a fraction of the available space, leaving vast, empty silences between its throbbing nodes.

There is no animal life. No birds wheel in the thin air; no insects crawl through the sodden moss. The only "ecology" is the symbiotic relationship between the plants and the heat of the machines. The mosses grow thickest around the steam vents, drawing warmth from the rhythmic exhales of the mountain-foundries.

The sound is absolute. It is a monolithic, unchanging roar—the bass note of ten thousand furnaces and the high-tensile scream of spinning turbines. Because the environment is static, the sound never wavers. There is no Doppler effect, no gust of wind to carry the noise away; it is a permanent, physical weight, a wall of sound that feels as solid as the stone. It is the sound of a world that has reached a perfect, grinding equilibrium. The machines perform tasks that never end, producing a product that is never collected, fueled by an energy source that never depletes.

The light is flat and honest, filtered through the high-altitude haze. It catches the steam and the weeping water, creating a silver-grey glow that makes the black rock and the industrial brass shine with a cold, metallic luster. Everything is sharp, wet, loud, and utterly indifferent.

It is a world caught in a frozen moment of creation and industry. The mountains are fresh, the machines are new, the plants are thriving—and yet, nothing has changed in a thousand years, and nothing will change in a thousand more. It is a vast, cold factory at the roof of the sky, manufacturing nothing but its own terrifying, beautiful existence.
