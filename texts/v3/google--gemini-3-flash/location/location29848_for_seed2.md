The world is a colossal, flooded engine room, a planetary-scale vessel or cistern where the horizon is not a line of sky, but a receding perspective of machined steel lost in the blue-grey gloom of the deep. Here, the water is everything. It is a heavy, cool medium, pressurized and thick, filling every cubic centimeter of a space so vast that the far-flung industrial monoliths appearing out of the murk seem like lonely planets in a liquid void.

There is no stone here, no silt, no sand. The substrate is entirely artificial. The floor, located kilometers beneath the upper currents, is a series of interlocking plates of dark, unweathered alloy, held together by rivets the size of turret tanks. Because the environment is young—recently submerged or newly constructed—the edges of these structures are impossibly sharp. There is no rounding from the sea, no soft coat of algae to blunt the geometry. The corners of the great intake vents and the teeth of the massive submerged gears are crisp, dangerous, and pristine.

The scale is difficult for the mind to hold. A single cooling fin may rise two miles from the deck plates, a sheer wall of corrugated metal that disappears upward into the brighter, shallower reaches. Between these structures lies a terrifying emptiness. One can travel for leagues through the open water without seeing anything but the occasional flicker of a distant beacon or the slow, rhythmic sweep of a colossal stabilizer vane. The density of objects is so low that the world feels like a hollow sphere, a vacuum filled with water.

Yet, it is a loud world. Sound travels with brutal efficiency through the cool liquid. The environment thrums with the mechanical heartbeat of a thousand massive turbines. There is the low-frequency groan of metal expanding under pressure, the sharp *clack-hiss* of hydraulic gates opening miles away, and the constant, rushing roar of water being forced through gargantuan filtration systems. It is an industrial symphony, omnipresent and vibrating in the marrow of anything that enters.

Light is not a rarity, though its source is entirely artificial. Great banks of floodlights, mounted to the sides of the superstructures, cast long, stable beams through the water. The light is clean and functional, illuminating the suspension of fine particulates that drift like snow through the currents. In these beams, the fauna is revealed.

The life here is abundant but curiously detached from the machinery. Massive shoals of silver-scaled fish, each the size of a small boat, move in synchronized clouds, navigating the industrial canyons. They use the slipstreams created by the giant propellers to travel across the vast reaches. Occasionally, larger predators—long, eel-like shapes with translucent skin and multiple rows of needle-teeth—patrol the dark zones between the lights. They are not strange or magical; they are muscular, biological realities of a high-pressure, high-energy environment.

Vegetation is a mere afterthought, a minor glitch in the system. Sparse, wiry clumps of pale, synthetic-looking kelp occasionally take root in the seams where two great plates meet, or around the rim of a leaking lubricant valve. These plants don't sway so much as vibrate in the mechanical chop, their fronds short and tough to withstand the violent pull of the intake cycles.

The world is intensely dynamic. It is a place of constant process. Giant shutters the size of cities slowly grind open to reveal glowing internal furnaces that flash-boil the water, creating upward-rushing plumes of bubbles that act as localized weather systems. The currents are predictable but powerful, dictated by the needs of the machine. One can feel the shift in the water's weight when a primary pump engages, a subtle change in pressure that signals the start of a new cycle.

It is a clean, cold, and echoing realm. It feels less like a drowned world and more like a world that was always meant to be this way—a planetary machine that requires the crushing weight of an ocean to keep its titanic components from shaking themselves apart. It is a place of hard edges, loud noises, and the lonely, beautiful movement of silver fish against a backdrop of endless, unyielding steel.
