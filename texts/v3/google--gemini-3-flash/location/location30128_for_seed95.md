The plateau of Oru-Sahl does not rise to a peak; it has forgotten what a peak is. At this altitude, two miles above the sea-level of a forgotten world, the mountains have been ground into a state of terminal exhaustion. They are vast, undulating humps of hematite-red stone and pale, wind-polished silt, stretching for hundreds of leagues in a series of gentle, rhythmic curves. This is a landscape in its final form—stable, heavy, and immensely old. The erosion is so complete that there are no jagged edges left, only the smooth, sensual slopes of a world that has finished its work.

The air here is thin but strangely warm, carrying a persistent, humid weight. It is a "high damp." Moisture does not fall as rain so much as it exhales from the stone. In the hollows between the red swells, the air thickens into a golden haze, and condensation beads on the underside of every overhang. There are no oceans, but there are "weeping ribs"—long, horizontal fissures in the ancient rock that perpetually leak a cool, mineral-rich sweat. This moisture feeds small, stagnant pockets of emerald water, jewel-like and perfectly still, tucked into the navels of the plateau.

Vegetation is a rarity, a struggling afterthought. What little grows is specialized: translucent, waxy succulents that hug the shade of the weeping ribs, and thin, hair-like filaments that shiver in the low-oxygen breeze. But while the flora is sparse, the life is not.

The density of the plateau is staggering, though it is a quiet, heavy density. The land is not covered in plants, but in *fauna*. Millions upon millions of soft-bodied, slow-moving creatures carpet the stone. They are the "Lichen-Walkers"—mollusk-like organisms the size of shields, their shells textured exactly like the surrounding red rock. They overlap like shingles, a living crust that spans the horizon. From a distance, the plateau looks like bare stone; up close, the ground is a pulsing, breathing mosaic. They move with an agonizing, glacial patience, shifting mere inches per day to follow the receding shadows or the path of a moisture-seep.

The sound is a profound, pressurized silence. There are no bird calls, no buzzing insects, no rustle of leaves. The only noise is the occasional, wet *thrum*—a collective heartbeat of the millions—and the faint, rhythmic ticking of cooling stone. It is a soundscape of respiration, not communication.

Interspersed among the living carpets are the marks of something else—something that was here before the mountains finished eroding. There are scattered stone circles, not built of bricks, but carved directly out of the bedrock. They are hollowed-out bowls, ten feet across, polished to a mirror shine by centuries of use. Perhaps they were dwellings, or perhaps they were catchments for the weeping water. They are the only "buildings" that exist: negative spaces, absences carved into the world. There is no technology here, no tools, not even the memory of a wheel. The world is purely biological, a closed loop of breath, sweat, and slow migration.

The dynamic of Oru-Sahl is one of deep time. The light transitions from a brilliant, high-altitude gold to a bruised purple, and in that transition, the millions of creatures ripple in a single, slow-motion wave, a communal adjustment to the temperature. Nothing happens quickly. A rock-fall might take a century to settle; a creature might spend a decade crossing a single valley. 

It is a place of overwhelming presence but zero urgency. The sheer number of living things creates a feeling of being watched by the ground itself, yet there is no threat, no predation. The Lichen-Walkers simply exist, absorbing the warmth of the sun and the sweat of the ancient stones, waiting for the wind to eventually carry the plateau away, grain by infinitesimal grain. It is a warm, wet, crowded solitude at the very top of the world.
