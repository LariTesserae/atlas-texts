This is the High Green Interior, a world-spanning sea of pressurized, cool liquid held aloft by a skeletal architecture of gargantuan, photosynthetic pillars. There is no sky, only the ceiling of the canopy; there is no floor, only the crushing, lightless depths far below the habitable strata. 

Everything here is plant. The "ground" is not stone but a compacted, multi-layered mesh of calcified rhizomes and ancient, petrified bark, weathered by eons of steady, rhythmic currents into smooth, undulating plains of grey-green wood. Because the scale is so vast, these floating continents of vegetation act as their own tectonic plates, drifting with agonizing slowness through the emerald medium. 

The water is not like the chaotic oceans of a terrestrial world. It is a stable, hyper-saturated fluid, thick with dissolved minerals and oxygen, maintaining a constant, cool temperature that never fluctuates by even a fraction of a degree. It is clear enough for the light—a steady, pervasive luminescence radiating from the pillars—to travel for miles, revealing a landscape that is both sparse and colossal. 

Though there are no built structures, no cities of brick or metal, the environment is intensely industrial. Evolution here has trended toward the mechanical. The "vegetation" does not merely grow; it operates. Massive, woody structures resembling vertical piston-engines rise from the rhizome-floors, their rhythmic contractions audible as a low, mechanical thrum that vibrates through the liquid. These are biological pumps, the size of cathedrals, circulating nutrients across the continental masses. Long, fibrous tendons, as thick as suspension cables, stretch between the pillars, humming with the tension of the world’s weight. There is a sense of a great factory at work—a mechanized, biological production of life that has reached a state of perfect, unmoving equilibrium.

The sound is a constant, deafening roar—a combination of the deep-frequency pulse of the pump-plants and the high-pitched shriek of pressurized water being forced through biological valves. It is a loud world, yet strangely peaceful in its total lack of change. The noise is a static element, as unchanging as the light or the temperature.

Fauna exists here in a state of suspended animation or high-velocity transit. Schools of translucent, armored leviathans—creatures that look like living submarines with rhythmic, oscillating gills—drift through the gaps between the plant-continents. They do not hunt so much as they harvest the constant flow of debris. Their bodies are streamlined and metallic in appearance, reflecting the industrial aesthetic of the flora. They are abundant, but the scale of the space is so great that they appear as lonely, scattered silver sparks in a dark green void.

There is a profound stillness to the "dynamic" of this place. Because the system is in total balance (0.0/3 dynamic), there are no seasons, no storms, and no decay that isn't immediately reclaimed by the machinery of the roots. If a giant pillar were to fail, it would take a millennium to tilt, and its fall would be absorbed by the density of the water and the redundancy of the surrounding mesh. 

The light is "normal," a flat, white-green glow that seems to come from nowhere and everywhere, casting soft shadows that never lengthen or shorten. It illuminates the vast, sparse reaches of the High Green—the kilometers of empty, liquid space between the churning biological engines. 

You might stand on a ridge of weathered heartwood, looking out across a hundred miles of clear, cool water. You would see the distant, glowing silhouettes of other plant-continents, the slow, rhythmic pulse of their nutrient-pistons, and the tiny, distant glint of fauna moving through the deep. It is a world of immense, lonely machinery, where the line between a forest and a factory has been erased by a million years of stable, liquid pressure. It is a cathedral of wood and water, humming with the sound of its own heartbeat, forever.
