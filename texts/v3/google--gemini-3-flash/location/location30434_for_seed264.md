The horizon here does not peak; it rolls in long, low-slung undulations of oxidized silt and pulverized magnetite. This is a basin world, a wide expanse of shallow depressions where the air hangs heavy, warm, and perpetually damp. The heat is not the sharp sting of a sun, but a pervasive, humid radiation rising from the ground itself. 

There is no green here. In place of forests, the landscape is populated by "geological engines"—massive, weathered monoliths of basalt and iron that function with the rhythmic, mindless precision of a factory, though they have never known a builder. These structures are not built; they are emergent. Great obsidian-black pistons, the size of cathedral spires, rise and fall with agonizing slowness from the crust, driven by the subterranean pressure of a heavy, boiling mantle. They grind against their sleeves of polished flint with a sound that defines the world: a deep, sub-bass thrum that vibrates in the marrow of the bone, punctuated by the screech of metal-on-stone and the rhythmic hiss of escaping pressure.

The erosion is ancient and absolute. Every edge has been smoothed by eons of friction and the slow, persistent lick of condensation. The ground is a mosaic of rusted iron-dust and smooth, dark glass. There are no buildings, no ruins, no signs of a civilization that passed through; the technology of this place is its geology. Thick, cable-like veins of copper and quartz lace through the ground, occasionally surfacing to bridge two low hills like high-tension wires, humming with a static charge that makes the fine hairs on the skin stand on end.

Water exists in a state of constant transition. It does not flow in rivers but clings to the surfaces of the machines. The warm air meets the cooler, conductive surfaces of the mineral-cranks, resulting in a perpetual, slick dew. In the low basins between the grinding monoliths, shallow pools of a viscous, oily liquid collect, reflecting the normal, steady light of a hazy sky. Occasionally, a rhythmic release of pressure from a ground-vent sends a spray of fine, warm mist into the air, creating a brief, local dampness before it settles back into the grit.

Life is an afterthought here. There are no leaves to rustle, no stalks to bend. The only biological presence is the "scavenger-crabs"—rare, hard-shelled organisms with segmented, metallic-looking carapaces that scuttle between the grinding joints of the world-engines. They feed on the friction-heat and the mineral salts that crystallize around the steam vents. They move with a strange, jerky cadence, their carapaces clacking against the stone, a small sound lost in the overwhelming industrial roar of the planet’s crust.

The scale of the place is deceptive. Because the features are so sparse—a piston here, a copper-vein bridge there—the distances seem manageable until one tries to cross them. The landscape stretches out for hundreds of miles in every direction, a repetitive, grinding gallery of tectonic machinery. 

Nothing changes. The cycle of the pistons has been the same for a billion years and will remain the same for a billion more. There is no weather beyond the condensation, no seasons beyond the steady pulse of the heat. The world is a closed system of kinetic energy and mineral decay, a factory with no product other than its own continued motion. The sound is a constant, deafening roar of existence, a loud, warm, and eternal equilibrium where the distinction between the mechanical and the natural has been eroded into a single, thrumming reality.
