High upon the Rounded Teeth—a range of mountains so ancient their peaks have softened into humps of weeping slate—lies the Sump of Veils. It is a geological thumbprint, a bowl-shaped depression no larger than a village square, caught in a perpetual, cold stasis. 

The air here is a thick, frigid suspension of water. It does not fall as rain, nor does it drift as fog; it simply exists as a saturated weight, a heavy curtain of moisture that clings to every surface until the world feels as though it were submerged in a shallow, invisible sea. The temperature is a constant, biting chill that numbs the skin but never reaches the killing point of frost. It is a stable, breathless cold.

Everything here is soft. The erosion is so complete, so settled, that there are no jagged edges, no sharp transitions. The stone beneath is visible only as smooth, obsidian-dark protrusions that bleed water from invisible pores—weeping rocks that have been crying into the moss for eons without changing their shape by a single millimeter.

The density of life is claustrophobic. The Sump is a cathedral of biomass where the floor, walls, and even the ceiling of overhanging cliff-faces are matted with a foot-thick carpet of liverworts, clubmosses, and black-leafed ferns. There is no bare earth. To walk here—though one does not walk so much as wade—is to sink ankle-deep into a sodden, spongy velvet that swallows sound and movement. The vegetation does not reach for a sun that isn't there; it spreads outward in thick, overlapping plates, gorging on the mineral-rich sweat of the mountain.

In the near-total darkness, the scale of the place feels both intimate and infinite. With visibility limited to arm’s length by the blackness and the mist, the Sump feels like a single room, a private sanctuary of dampness. Yet the density of the fauna suggests a vast, teeming empire. The silence is not an absence of life, but a deliberate choice. 

Thousands—perhaps millions—of small, pale organisms occupy every square inch of the mossy strata. There are slow-moving, soft-shelled mollusks the size of a human fist, their translucent bodies pulsing with a faint, internal rhythm. Tiny, flightless moths with velvet wings crawl in endless, interlocking patterns over the fern fronds. Large, eyeless amphibians, their skin the color of bruised plums, sit motionless in the crevices, their gills waving slowly in the heavy air. They do not hunt; they seem to filter the very mist for nutrients. The fauna is overwhelming in its presence, a carpet of breathing, twitching bodies that fill the silence with a low-frequency vibration, a collective thrum that is felt in the marrow rather than heard by the ear.

Nothing changes. There are no seasons in the Sump, no day or night that the eye can discern, no wind to stir the heavy fronds. The water drips from a hanging rock into a moss-pool with a rhythm that has not faltered in a thousand years. It is a frozen equilibrium.

In the center of this sodden grove, tucked into the hollow of a weeping stone, are the only signs of agency: primitive, manual crafts. These are not buildings, but subtle interventions. Strands of woven lichen-fiber, heavy with moisture, are stretched between two ferns to catch the slow-moving mollusks. A few flat stones have been chipped—not with metal, but by patient grinding—into shallow bowls to collect the purest distillations of rock-sweat. There are no fires, no metals, no wheels. Only these simple, damp tools, left by something that moves as slowly and silently as the mountain itself. 

The world of the Sump is a heavy, dark, and crowded peace. It is a place where time has been crushed under the weight of its own moisture, leaving behind a saturated sanctuary where life does not compete, but simply accumulates, layer upon layer, in the cold and the quiet.
