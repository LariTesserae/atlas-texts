This is a world of bone-dry monumentalism, a continental basin where the concept of "nature" and "city" have fused into a single, breathless metabolism. 

The ground is raw and embryonic. It is a vast, flat expanse of cooling mineral salts and unweathered silicates, pale and translucent like the skin of a drum. There is no sand here, nor soil; the geology hasn't had time to break down. The terrain is a series of sharp, crystalline plates and obsidian-slick flats that ring like porcelain underfoot. Because erosion is almost non-existent, the world feels brand new, as if it were poured from a cosmic kiln only yesterday. It is a low world, a boundless horizontal shelf that offers no mountains to break the steady, warm light of an unwavering sun.

Though there is no liquid water—no lakes, no rivers, no rain—the land is undeniably lush. 

Life here has bypassed the need for hydration, subsisting instead on the rich, heavy gases that cling to the ground and the intense mineral wealth of the raw crust. What appears to be a forest is, upon closer inspection, a dense, biological urbanity. Imagine a coral reef the size of a continent, but dry and warm. Stalks of calcified resin rise hundreds of feet into the air, packed so tightly together that there is rarely room for a creature to walk between them. These are not trees in the traditional sense; they are vertical colonies, self-organizing structures that grow with the geometric precision of skyscrapers. 

The density is total. Looking across the horizon, there is no "empty" space. Every square inch of the mineral floor is occupied by either a rising spire, a creeping lichen-analog that hardens into a ceramic shell, or the complex, woven bridges of "vegetation" that connect the towers. It is a city grown by instinct. There are no tools here, no electronics, no metalwork. The "infrastructure" is purely biological—massive, hollowed-out conduits for gas exchange that look like subway tunnels, and high-altitude canopies that serve as transit veltways for the fauna.

The atmosphere is heavy and warm, a constant, balmy pressure that carries the faint scent of curing wax and dry stone. The light is bright and golden, filtered through the upper layers of the translucent canopy so that the lower "streets" of this biological city are bathed in a perpetual, cathedral-like amber glow.

Movement is everywhere, but it is a silent movement. The fauna is abundant—six-legged, chitinous grazers that move with a liquid grace over the crystalline floor, and iridescent flyers with wings like stiff parchment that glide between the spires. They do not cry out or sing. The only sound is the infinitesimal creak of the towers growing, the dry rustle of chitin against resin, and the low, rhythmic thrum of the planet’s gas cycles moving through the vents. It is a hushed, busy world, a silent megalopolis of frantic growth.

Because the world is so new (low erosion) and yet so vast and packed, there is a sense of urgent competition. The "buildings"—the spire-colonies—are constantly overgrowing one another, layering new calcified floors over the old. The ecology is in a state of high-speed succession. Predators, shaped like elongated, multi-jointed hounds with needle-thin legs, stalk the shadowed alleys between the spires, moving with a terrifying, muffled efficiency.

The rules of this world are dictated by space and mineral access. To be high is to receive the best light; to be low is to receive the richest gases. Everything is connected, everything is built upon the remains of what came minutes before, and everything is dry. It is a masterpiece of organic architecture, a planet-wide hive that knows nothing of the softness of water or the decay of wind, only the hard, bright reality of perpetual expansion.
