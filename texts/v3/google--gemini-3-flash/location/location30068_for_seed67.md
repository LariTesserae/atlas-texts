The horizon is not a line of earth and sky, but a perfect, razor-sharp seam where the pale gold light meets a flat, infinite expanse of gunmetal grey. This is the Floor—a continental shelf of interlocking leaden plates, etched with micro-grooves that channel a constant, shallow flow of warm, recycled water. There is no soil here, no sand, no stone that wasn't cast in a mold. 

The air is a heavy, pressurized gauze of steam. At a temperature that hovers just above the threshold of discomfort, the atmosphere is a perpetual greenhouse without a single leaf. Moisture does not fall as rain; it weeps from the undersides of the massive, overhead conduits that span the sky like the ribs of a planetary cathedral. These pipes, some miles wide, pulse with the rhythmic transit of unseen fluids, their surfaces slick with a condensation that never dries.

Everything is new. Despite the vastness, there is no rust, no pitting, no soft rounding of the edges. The industrial geometry is terrifyingly fresh, as if the entire world were manufactured seconds ago and then instantly frozen in its operational state. The joints between the massive floor-plates are tight enough to exclude a hair, yet they bleed a constant, tepid mineral broth that feeds the landscape's primary occupants.

In the absence of vegetation, the world has been surrendered to the fauna. They are everywhere, a dense, multi-layered carpet of biological presence that clings to every vertical pipe and horizontal surface. They are not animals in any recognizable sense, but pale, soft-bodied organisms—somewhere between a mollusk and a colony of polyps. They are translucent, revealing internal pulses of amber light that synchronize across millions of individuals. They do not hunt; they do not move. They are sessile, suctioned to the warm metal, filtering the heavy mist and the weeping conduits. Their bodies are stacked in mounds, mounds that mimic the shape of dunes but are made of shivering, wet flesh.

The density is suffocating. To walk—if there were a path—would be to step on a living, yielding mat of pulse and membrane. They occupy every niche of the industrial architecture, forming living insulation around the thrumming transformers and clogging the shallow marshes of the floor-plates with their bloated, pearl-colored forms.

Despite this biological saturation and the titanic scale of the machinery, the world is profoundly quiet. The machines do not grind; they are perfectly lubricated, perhaps magnetically suspended. The sound is a singular, low-frequency hum, a subsonic vibration that one feels in the marrow rather than hears in the ear. It is the sound of a heart beating in a body the size of a moon. The fauna, too, are silent, producing only the wet, rhythmic slap of their membranes against the metal as they expand and contract in the heat.

Time has no purchase here. The light never wanes, fixed in a perpetual 10:00 AM glare that filters through the white steam. There is no wind, only the slow, vertical drift of heat rising from the floor. Nothing breaks, nothing grows, and nothing dies. The organisms do not seem to age; they exist in a state of high-energy equilibrium, fueled by the warmth of the Great Built Environment. 

It is a masterpiece of stasis. A factory that produces only itself, maintained by a biology that has forgotten how to evolve, all contained within a geography that has no end and no beginning. The wet, warm metal stretches out in every direction, a pristine monument to a design that reached perfection and simply stopped, leaving the pulse of its engines to echo forever through the silence of the steam.
