The peaks here do not pierce the sky so much as they anchor it. At this extreme elevation, the air should be thin and freezing, but instead, it is a pressurized, sweltering soup. The atmosphere is a heavy cloak of silver-white mist, so thick with moisture that it feels less like weather and more like a permanent state of the medium. Everything is slick. The rock faces—vast, vertical curtains of dark, porous basalt—do not just hold water; they exude it. They are "weeping rocks," where the very stone seems to be sweating under the intense, oppressive heat.

This is a landscape of vertical saturation.

Because there is no "built" presence, the "industrial" character of the region emerges from a startling geological mimicry. The mountains function as a series of natural heat-engines. Deep beneath the roots of these sky-scraping spires, geothermal pressure forces boiling water through a labyrinth of hollow, tubular mineral veins. The result is a rhythmic, mechanical thrum that vibrates through the soles of one’s feet. In the narrow, fern-choked canyons, basalt columns have fractured into perfect, hexagonal pistons that rise and fall with the ebb of subterranean steam, hissing with a sound indistinguishable from a releasing valve. 

It is a factory of nature, producing nothing but its own climate.

The vegetation is a riotous, aggressive green, layered so densely that the ground is a myth. Giant, broad-leafed canopy trees, their bark shimmering with a metallic, copper-colored lichen, grow in tiers. They are draped in "cable-vines"—thick, fibrous ropes that pulse with the movement of internal fluids, looking like thick electrical conduits running between the floors of a power plant. These vines hang in heavy, organized swags, dripping hot condensation onto the mosses below. The moss itself is thick enough to swallow a limb, glowing with a faint, bio-luminescent emerald light that mimics the steady hum of a live wire.

Despite the "industrial" noise of the earth—the rhythmic clack of stone on stone and the shriek of steam vents—the atmosphere is strangely quiet. The humidity acts as a dampener, swallowing sound before it can travel. A massive rock-slide a mile away might sound like nothing more than a muffled cough. 

The fauna has adapted to this mechanical rhythm. Large, heavy-shelled beetles with iridescent carapaces clamber over the "piston" rocks, their movements timed to the geological pulses to avoid being crushed. Birds with oily, waterproof plumage and wide, flat beaks glide through the thermal vents, using the updrafts of hot steam to rise thousands of feet without flapping a wing. There is a sense of busy, automated life—abundant but preoccupied.

Erosion here has reached a state of beautiful, stagnant equilibrium. The water has carved what it can, creating deep, smooth flutes in the mountainsides that look like the cooling fins of a massive engine block. The balance is held between the violent upward thrust of the tectonic plates and the relentless dissolving power of the hot rain. The landscape feels old, settled in its ways, a machine that has been running at high RPM for a million years without a single part needing replacement.

Looking out from a high ledge, the scale is staggering. You are above the clouds of the lower world, but within a secondary sea of mist that belongs only to these peaks. The vista is a series of receding, dark-green silhouettes, each one crowned with plumes of white vapor. There are no paths, no ruins, no footprints. Only the endless, wet heat, the smell of ozone and crushed ferns, and the deep, geological heartbeat of a world that is working very hard at being itself.
