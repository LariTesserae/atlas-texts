The air here is a thick, motionless weight, held at the steady temperature of a cooling hearth. It is a dry heat, devoid of the softening influence of moisture; there is no scent of rain, no humidity to carry the smell of earth. Instead, the atmosphere tastes of fine, alkaline dust and the faint, metallic tang of desiccated minerals.

The landscape is a shallow basin, perhaps three hundred yards across, hemmed in by low, rounded ridges of dark basalt. These ridges are not jagged; they have been worn down by unthinkable stretches of time into smooth, slumped shoulders that transition seamlessly into the floor. The ground itself is a level expanse of silt, so fine it behaves like fluid underfoot, though it has not shifted in centuries. It is a graveyard of momentum.

In the near-total darkness, the terrain reveals itself only through textures and the radiating warmth of the stones. There is no sun here, no moon, and no bioluminescence to break the gloom. The "warmth" is a subterranean gift, a slow, geological pulse rising through the crust, keeping the basin at a constant, unfluctuating simmer.

Vegetation exists here, though it bears little resemblance to the lushness of a wetter world. The plants are sparse, standing in isolation from one another like lonely sentinels. They are leafless, consisting of rigid, vertical spikes of fibrous cellulose, hardened by mineral deposits until they are as brittle as glass. They do not sway, for there is no wind. They do not grow in any observable timeframe. They simply *are*—stiff, charcoal-colored pillars that have reached an equilibrium with the dryness, neither dying nor thriving, merely persisting in the dark.

Between these pillars, the "tech" of the place reveals itself in its most skeletal form. There are no walls, no roofs, no foundations. Instead, there are small, intentional arrangements of stone. On a flat slab of rock, one might find a collection of obsidian flakes, chipped carefully to a razor edge and then laid down, never to be picked up again. In another spot, a handful of the stiff plant-fibers have been stripped and bundled together with a simple, two-ply twist of cordage, left resting in the dust. These are the marks of hand-tools and manual craft, but they are orphaned objects. They sit in the silence, untouched by the hands that shaped them, appearing less like trash and more like a permanent part of the geology.

Sound is almost entirely absent. The silt floor acts as an acoustic sponge, drinking up the sound of a footfall before it can echo. There is no rustle of leaves, no drone of insects, no trickle of water. The only sound is the internal rhythm of one’s own pulse, and perhaps, if one listens with absolute focus, the microscopic creak of a mineral pillar expanding by a fraction of a millimeter in the constant heat.

The scale of the place is intimate. The ridges provide a clear boundary, creating the sensation of an outdoor room—a natural cathedral dedicated to stillness. Because there are no seasons, no weather patterns, and no animals to disturb the dust, the basin exists in a state of frozen equilibrium. A stone placed on a ledge today will remain there for a thousand years. A thumbprint in the silt would be a permanent monument. It is a landscape that has finished its work; it has settled into its final form and now simply waits, warm and dark, in the hollow of the world.
