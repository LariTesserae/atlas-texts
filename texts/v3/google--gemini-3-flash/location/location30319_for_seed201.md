This is the Needle of the Morning, a singular, jagged spire of unweathered obsidian and blue-grey basalt that pierces the atmosphere at a staggering altitude. The rock here is young, having surged upward in a geological blink of an eye; its edges are sharp enough to draw blood, and its surfaces are glassy, lacking the soft rounding of eons of wind. It is a vertical cathedral, a narrow world bounded by the terrifying drop into the cloud-oceans below.

The light is blinding. At this elevation, the air is too thin to filter the sun, which hangs like a white-hot coin in a sky of deep, bruised indigo. The light doesn't warm; it merely illuminates with a surgical, shadowless intensity. Despite the glare, the air is freezing, a dry cold that catches in the throat, yet the Needle is paradoxically damp. It is a magnet for the high-altitude mists. Clouds are shredded against its sharp protrusions, leaving behind a constant, shimmering condensation that slickens the stone and gathers in tiny, crystal-clear basins no larger than a bowl.

Life here is an explosion of emerald against the black glass. Because the spire is so narrow, the "lushness" is concentrated in vertical hanging gardens. Thick, spongy mosses—vibrant as lime zest—carpet the sheer faces where the mist clings. Miniature, broad-leafed succulents, their skins translucent and filled with antifreeze-like sap, wedge themselves into every hairline fracture. The vegetation is dense but isolated; you might stand on a ledge thick with growth, looking across a hundred-foot void of empty air to another solitary pocket of green. The "emptiness" of the place is felt in these gaps—the vast, hollow distances between one inhabited outcrop and the next.

The fauna is busy, frantic, and unnervingly quiet. Swarms of high-altitude humming-moths, their wings iridescent and stiff like mica, dart between the succulent blooms. They move with a blurring speed, their carapaces clinking softly against the rock, but they make no sound of flight. Small, six-limbed lizards with padded, suction-cup feet skitter across the vertical glass, their movements a constant, silent flicker of motion. The ecosystem is in a state of high-speed turnover—predatory dragonflies with four-inch wingspans snatch the moths mid-air, yet the only sound is the occasional, distant *crack* of ice shifting in a deep crevice or the low, mournful whistle of wind passing over a sharp edge. It is a place of intense biological activity conducted in a profound, ringing silence.

Human presence is felt in the scattered, primitive marks left upon the stone. There are narrow paths, less than a foot wide, chipped painstakingly into the obsidian with hand-tools. Here and there, a small circular shelter is tucked into a lee—built of stacked, flat stones without mortar, their low roofs weighed down by heavy rocks. These are not permanent dwellings but temporary perches. Faded, hand-woven ropes made of tough plant fiber dangle over abysses, connecting one verdant ledge to another. There are no machines here, no metals other than perhaps a stray, cold-hammered copper spike. 

The world here is a series of "rooms" without floors. You move from a sun-drenched, moss-slicked balcony to a narrow chimney of rock, always aware of the miles of nothingness pressing in from all sides. The dynamic is one of constant, subtle change: a cloud drifts through, turning the world into a grey, dripping ghost-scape for ten minutes, before the piercing sun returns to flash-dry the stone and set the insects into their silent, frantic dance once more. It is a fragile, high-speed paradise clinging to a frozen knife-edge.
