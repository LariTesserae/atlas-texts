The basin of Aethel-Rau is a place where the earth has only just arrived, yet has already been colonized by a desperate, teeming density. 

The terrain is a raw, embryonic slab of dark basalt and jagged obsidian. Because erosion has not yet had its say, the edges of the world are sharp enough to draw blood. There is no soil here, no soft transition from stone to silt. The ground is a series of overlapping, unweathered plates, like the scales of a vast, newborn beast. These plates form a low-lying valley, a wide and shallow depression that stretches for miles, caught in a perpetual, warm dimness.

Water is the constant companion of this stone. It does not fall as rain so much as it exudes from the atmosphere. The air is heavy, a thick, warm soup of suspended droplets that condense on every available surface. The rock faces do not merely get wet; they weep. Thin, glassy sheets of water slide over the sharp mineral ridges, pooling in shallow, crystalline basins that never drain and never overflow. The humidity is a physical weight, wrapping around the body like a damp wool cloak.

In this landscape, the concept of a plant is non-existent. There is no green, no cellulose, no soft reach of a leaf toward a sun that remains hidden behind a static, charcoal-colored sky. Instead, the biology of the basin is entirely faunal and fungal. The rocks are encrusted with thick, rubbery mats of pale, liver-colored molds and calcified lichen-forms that feel like bone to the touch. 

The scale of life here is overwhelming. The basin is dense—suffocatingly so. Everywhere there is a surface, something is living on it, under it, or within it. Legions of heavy-bodied, six-legged arthropods, the size of hounds, scuttle over the basalt plates. Their carapaces are the color of wet slate, providing a perfect, grim camouflage. Above them, the air is thick with clouds of translucent, wing-beating insects that move in synchronized, oily waves. 

This is not a wilderness, however. It is an urban sprawl of the most primitive, grueling kind. A city has been hewn directly into the raw geology. The architecture is a frantic, layered accumulation of hand-carved cells and low-slung stone hovels. There is no metal here, no machinery, no combustion. The "buildings" are mounds of stacked, unmortared flat-stone and chambers chiseled out with flint and bone. The streets are narrow, lightless crevices where the population moves shoulder-to-shoulder, their bodies slick with the constant condensation. It is a settlement of immense proportions, a regional capital of stone-age industry, where millions live in a state of hyper-proximate stasis.

The most defining characteristic of Aethel-Rau is the sound. It is not a noise one hears, but a noise one endures. The collective vibration of a billion insect wings, the rhythmic clicking of chitinous limbs against stone, and the low, guttural croaks of the larger basin-dwellers combine into a monolithic, deafening roar. It is a white-noise wall that drowns out thought, a permanent acoustic pressure that never fluctuates in volume or pitch.

Time in the basin does not move. The environment is in a state of frozen equilibrium. The heat never breaks into a storm; the dim light never fades to black or brightens to day. The water continues to weep from the stones at the exact same rate it always has. The city does not grow, nor does it decay; the stones are too new to crumble, and the inhabitants are too locked in their dense, repetitive patterns to change their ways. 

You stand on a ridge of razor-sharp glass, looking out over a valley of steaming stone and crowded, primitive masonry. The air is warm, wet, and loud enough to make your teeth ache. Below you, the movement of a million bodies creates a shimmering, dark tide. Nothing has changed here for an age, and nothing shows the slightest sign of ever doing so. It is a masterpiece of stagnation, a thriving, deafening monument to a world that has refused to evolve past its first, wet, crowded breath.
