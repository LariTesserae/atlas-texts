The world is a hollowed-out gall, a fossilized fruit the size of a small attic, buried within a substrate that no longer matters. It is a pocket of absolute, crowded permanence.

Everything here is manufactured, yet there is no trace of a tool, a gear, or a circuit. The environment is "built" in the way a bone is built, or a seashell—dense, structural, and intentional, but entirely biological. The walls are not walls but a recursive folding of ivory-colored plates and amber resins, interlocking with such precision that there is no "ground," only a continuous, ridged floor that curves upward to become a ceiling of identical texture.

The scale is claustrophobic and intimate. It is a world measured in inches, a dense thicket of architectural growth. There is no empty space. Every cubic centimeter is occupied by either the structural lattice of the environment or the sparse, stiff vegetation that has claimed the narrow crevices. This vegetation consists of calcified, needle-like mosses and dry, papery fronds that do not sway. They are brittle and white, having long ago abandoned chlorophyll for a diet of pure, unrelenting light.

The light is the most aggressive inhabitant of this space. It does not come from a sun or a lamp; it radiates from the very grain of the built environment. Every ivory plate and amber strut glows with a fierce, shadowless intensity (2.8/3). It is a bright, cold illumination that reveals every microscopic scratch and weathered groove in the landscape. Because the space is so densely packed, the light reflects and refracts infinitely, creating a visual field so saturated that depth becomes difficult to perceive.

Water is a historical myth here. The air is chemically dry, a void of moisture that has turned the surfaces into something resembling ceramic or petrified wood. There is no scent of decay, only the smell of old salt and cold mineral dust. The landscape is "established"—it has reached a state of terminal erosion where the sharp edges of its initial construction have been smoothed down by eons of micro-vibrations into a polished, settled equilibrium.

The sound is a constant, deafening roar (2.5/3). It is not the sound of wind or machinery, but the structural resonance of the gall itself. The intense light and the cooling of the dense materials create a high-frequency thrum, a static shriek that never wavers. It is the sound of a world under immense internal tension, a bell that was struck a thousand years ago and refuses to stop ringing.

Nothing moves. The dynamic of the world is a flatline (0.2/3). The air is still; the plants are frozen in their brittle, crystalline postures; the light never dims or flickers. There are no seasons, no day-night cycles, and no animals to scuttle through the white thickets. It is a museum of its own existence, a perfectly preserved, brightly lit, and incredibly loud tomb of biological architecture. 

If you were to stand in the center of one of the narrow, low-ceilinged corridors, you would feel the cool, dry air pressing against you, the roar of the walls vibrating in your teeth, and the blinding light erasing the contours of your own hands. You are in a place that has finished becoming what it is. It is a total, packed environment that requires nothing from the outside world and offers nothing but its own immutable, screaming stillness.
