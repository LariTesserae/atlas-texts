This is the Basin-Height, a vertical expanse of fired earth and weeping stone. 

At this elevation, the air is thin and habitually cold, yet the light is blinding—a raw, high-altitude glare that bounces off a million wet surfaces. The entire environment is a construct; there is no visible soil, no raw cliffside, no wild tumble of scree. Every square inch of the valley, from the jagged peaks that frame the horizon to the deepest troughs of the ravines, has been surfaced in a dense, interlocking mosaic of terracotta tiles, hand-carved stone blocks, and primitive ceramic pipes. 

The architecture is not for living; it is for gathering. The landscape is a labyrinth of billions of small, sharp-edged basins, each no larger than a cupped hand. They are packed so tightly together that there is no path between them that does not involve treading upon the rim of another. Because the erosion is so young, the edges of these vessels remain razor-sharp, their fired glazes glinting under the relentless sun. The construction is total—a mountain range encased in a shell of pottery.

Water is the primary inhabitant. Even in the bright, cool air, the atmosphere is saturated. Mists roll over the sharp ceramic ridges, and as they touch the cold, fired surfaces, they condense instantly. The entire world is weeping. A constant, slow-motion drenching occurs as water beads on the high rims, silvering the orange and red clay, and begins a tedious, zig-zag descent through the billions of basins. 

There are no trees here. There are no shrubs, no grasses, not even the suggestion of a lichen. The only hint of biology is a thin, translucent film that coats the interiors of the lower basins—a pre-life primordial slick that smells faintly of copper and wet dust. It does not grow so much as it accumulates, a slow thickening of mineral-rich gel in the troughs where the water lingers longest.

The technology of this place is heavy and manual. You can see the marks of the makers: thumbprints in the mortar, the uneven strike of a stone chisel, the primitive lashings of rot-resistant cord that hold massive ceramic conduits against the steeper slopes. There are no machines, no hum of power. The only sound is the "clock-clock" of drips hitting stone and the low, mournful whistle of the wind as it is sliced by a trillion sharp, kiln-fired edges. It is a quiet world, save for the rhythmic, gravitational labor of the water.

Everything here moves at the pace of the mineral. The basins do not overflow in a rush; they fill drop by drop, surface tension holding the water in a trembling dome until a single, final molecule breaks the balance and sends a trickle to the next tier down. It is a landscape of profound patience. The scale is immense—a regional watershed transformed into a hand-built machine of clay—but the focus is infinitesimal, centered on the slow, cool birth of a drop of water on a bright, sharp edge.

There is a sense of recent completion, or perhaps a perpetual state of "just finished." The terracotta hasn't had time to crack under the frost; the stone hasn't been smoothed by the very water it carries. It is a fresh, jagged, saturated monument to a civilization that understood only two things: the scarcity of the mist and the infinite capacity of the hand to shape the earth to catch it.
