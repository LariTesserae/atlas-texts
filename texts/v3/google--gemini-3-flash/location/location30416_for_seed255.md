This is the Great Masonry, a world that has never known a seed or a sun. It is a regional expanse, a valley-sized basin where the concept of "ground" has been entirely replaced by the concept of "floor."

Everything here is made. There is no soil, no tectonic plate, no volcanic upheaval. Instead, the substrate is a dense, multi-layered accumulation of hand-hewn basalt blocks, kiln-fired bricks, and interlocking limestone slabs. The architecture does not rise into an open sky; it stacks infinitely upward into a ceiling of heavy timber beams and cross-braced scaffolding that vanishes into the gloom.

The air is cool, carrying the sharp, mineral scent of wet lime and cold iron. It is a damp world, though not a drowned one. Moisture does not fall as rain but manifests as a persistent, heavy condensation that beads on the undersides of every archway and drips rhythmically from the wooden rafters. Small, shallow basins have been carved into the flagstones to catch these slow leaks, creating a network of stagnant, dark pools that reflect the meager light.

There is no green here. The walls are bare of moss; the corners are free of weeds. In place of biology, there is only the raw geometry of the work. Because the world is "emerging" and "under construction," the surfaces are not worn smooth by time. The edges of the stones are sharp and jagged, still bearing the rhythmic scars of the chisel. Piles of unused gravel and stacks of fresh-cut blocks sit in every corridor, waiting to be moved. The erosion is not from wind or water, but from the friction of labor—the scraping of sledges and the chipping of mallets.

The scale is oppressive. It is a dense, high-saturation environment where every cubic meter is occupied by a pillar, a staircase, a buttress, or a suspended walkway. Despite this density, the world feels vast, extending for miles in every direction, a labyrinthine town of masonry that serves no clear purpose other than its own expansion.

It is a dark place. The only light comes from the occasional, sputtering glow of pitch-soaked torches held in iron brackets or the dim, orange friction-heat of a smith’s forge tucked into a structural alcove. The shadows are absolute, deep, and velvet-thick, hiding the true height of the vaults.

The silence is nonexistent. The Great Masonry is a loud world, ringing with the cacophony of primitive industry. From somewhere in the dark above, there is the constant, booming *thud* of heavy stones being dropped into place. The air vibrates with the *clink-clink-clink* of a thousand iron hammers against stone, a sound that echoes and multiplies through the stone chambers until it becomes a rhythmic, industrial heartbeat. Ropes creak under the strain of massive pulleys; wooden rollers groan across the floors. The world is in motion, a dynamic system of manual construction that never rests.

Fauna are rare and unsettling. There are no birds or insects. Occasionally, something pale and hairless—a scavenger of the masonry—skitters across a high beam, its claws clicking like dry bone on wood. These creatures are blind, navigated by the same vibrations that define the landscape, living off the scraps of the builders or the thick, translucent molds that grow in the deepest, wettest cracks of the foundation.

The rules of this world are simple: the work must continue. There is no evidence of high technology, no electricity, no steam. The entire structure is moved by muscle, lever, and wedge. The builders themselves are rarely seen in the darkness, perceived only as heavy breathing in the shadows or the rhythmic swing of a hammer. 

It is a world of heavy, damp permanence that is paradoxically still being born. It feels like the inside of a massive, unfinished tomb that has decided to become a continent. Every step taken on the cold, wet stone resonates, a reminder that beneath this floor are a thousand more floors, and above this ceiling, the hammers are already preparing the next.
