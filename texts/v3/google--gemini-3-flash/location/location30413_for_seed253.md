In this place, the concept of a horizon has been forgotten. There is only the Vertical, an endless, lightless plunge through cold, pressurized water. 

The environment is a monolithic construct of biological architecture, a "built" world where every square inch of substrate was once intentional, woven from the fibers of a single, world-spanning genus of macro-flora. This is the Lattice. Imagine a cathedral the size of a continent, composed not of stone, but of petrified, lignified heartwood, grown and braided into impossible geometries. The structures are ancient beyond reckoning, their surfaces worn to a glass-like smoothness by the slow, relentless friction of deep-water currents. There are no jagged edges left; the erosion is total, a final state of equilibrium where the wood has become as hard as basalt and as sleek as a shark’s skin.

The scale is vast and lonely. Great, hollowed-out pillars of woven vine—each the width of a city—rise and fall into the dark, spaced miles apart. Between them lies the Void: a cold, silent volume of water so immense that the presence of the pillars feels like a rare intrusion. Despite the abundance of life, the density of the physical world is sparse. One can travel for hours through the indigo-black water without touching a solid surface, seeing only the distant, ghostly silhouettes of other pillars, miles away, illuminated by nothing but the faint, cold bioluminescence of the things that live within them.

It is cold here—a steady, unvarying chill that saps heat but never reaches the point of freezing. The water is heavy, saturated with the tannins and minerals of the ancient wood, giving it a viscous, syrupy quality. 

The sound is the defining feature of the Lattice. It is a constant, booming thrum, a low-frequency roar that vibrates in the marrow of the bone. This is the sound of the world’s plumbing: billions of gallons of water forced through the hollow interiors of the great pillars by the sheer pressure of the vertical stack. It is a rhythmic, mechanical sound produced by purely biological means—the groaning of trillion-ton timbers and the hiss of water through valves the size of houses. It never changes. There is no weather, no tide, no day or night. The sound is the heartbeat of a machine that has forgotten its purpose but continues to run.

The vegetation is the world itself. There is no soil, no rock, no silt. Every surface is a living, or once-living, plant mass. Lichens that feel like cold velvet coat the pillars; long, ropy kelp-structures, miles in length, anchor themselves to the Lattice and trail upward into the dark like the frayed cables of a sunken elevator. These plants do not photosynthesize; they chemisynthesize, drawing energy from the thermal gradients and the nutrient-rich flow within the pillars.

Life here is abundant but alien. Great, pale crustaceans with shells like polished porcelain cling to the braided wood, filter-feeding in the roar of the vents. Schools of needle-thin fish, blind and translucent, move in synchronized clouds through the voids between the pillars, their movements creating sudden, sharp flickers of silver in the dark. There are larger things, too—massive, slow-moving cephalopods with skin like mottled bark, who have evolved to mimic the texture of the Lattice so perfectly that they are invisible until they move.

Everything in this world is a monument to stability. The "built" nature of the environment suggests a creator, but there are no tools here, no metal, no fire. It is a masterpiece of prehistoric bio-engineering, a settled-into-final-form reality where evolution has reached its end-state. The Lattice does not grow anymore; it simply *is*. It is a drowned, vertical city of plants, standing in the eternal dark, humming a low, thunderous note that will never end.
