The air at the summit of the Ocher Plateau is a physical weight, a thick, saturating presence that clings to the skin like a hot, wet wool blanket. At this elevation, one might expect the thin, frigid air of a mountain peak, but the atmosphere here is an inversion—a heavy, pressurized soup of condensing vapor and thermal exhaust. It is dim, lit only by a low, bruised-purple glow that filters through a permanent ceiling of smog and heavy cloud, casting long, indistinct shadows across the slick mineral world.

There is no green here. The concept of a leaf or a stem feels alien to this landscape. Instead, the ground is a vast expanse of weathered basalt and oxidized iron-plate, polished to a dull shine by the relentless weeping of the atmosphere. Water does not fall as rain so much as it manifests; it beads on every vertical surface, trickling down the sides of great monolithic structures in constant, rhythmic rivulets. The landscape is a marsh of stone—shallow, scalding pools collect in the depressions of the rock, their surfaces shimmering with oily iridescences, fed by the constant perspiration of the world itself.

Emerging from this hot, wet gloom are the structures: an urbanity of impossible scale and agonizing loneliness. The built environment is industrial and dense in its construction, yet the world feels utterly empty. These are not buildings for habitation, but a titanic infrastructure of nameless purpose. Copper-clad towers, green with verdigris and slick with moisture, rise hundreds of feet into the dark, spaced miles apart. Between them, the "city" consists of massive, rusted iron piers and suspended walkways that disappear into the mist, connecting nothing to nothing. 

The scale is deceptive. You can stand on a high ledge and look out across a valley-sized expanse, seeing the silhouettes of five or six gargantuan factories, their chimneys venting silent, white plumes of steam into the humid dark. But between these structures, there is a void. The density of the *objects* is high, but the density of *presence* is zero. There are no crowds, no traffic, no lights in windows. The city is a skeleton, a massive, mechanized carcass that is still, somehow, functioning.

It is mostly quiet, but it is not a dead silence. It is a silence composed of distant, low-frequency vibrations. Every few minutes, a rhythmic *thrum-hiss* echoes from deep beneath the crust, a sound like a planetary lung exhaling. It is the sound of steam-driven pistons the size of cathedrals moving somewhere in the dark. The technology here is heavy, tactile, and ancient—massive gears, exposed flywheels turning with agonizing slowness, and glowing amber coils that hum with a languid, electrical heat.

Movement in this world is rare and strange. There are no birds in the hot sky, no insects in the mineral pools. Occasionally, a pale, slug-like shape, the size of a ground-car and translucent as gelatin, might be seen adhering to the side of a rusted pylon, slowly absorbing the mineral-rich condensation. These are the rare inhabitants: silent, slow-moving scavengers of heat and moisture, possessing no recognizable biology.

The world is active, but its cycles are geological and mechanical rather than biological. The water cycles from the weeping rocks to the steaming vents, back to the heavy sky. The great machines perform their inscrutable tasks, moving massive weights or refining invisible gases, their brass fittings polished by a million years of hot mist. 

To stand here is to feel the pressure of time and steam. The ground beneath your feet is always vibrating, always warm. You are in a place that has reached an equilibrium of decay and function—an industrial marsh at the roof of the world, where the heat never breaks, the light never rises, and the vast, empty halls of the machine-city continue to weep in the dark.
