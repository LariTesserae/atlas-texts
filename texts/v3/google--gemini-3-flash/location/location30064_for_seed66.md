The horizon is not a line, but a series of receding, flat-topped silhouettes known as the Silt-Steppes. Here, at an elevation where the air should be thin and frigid, it is instead heavy, warm, and perpetually damp. The heat does not radiate from a sun—though the sky is bright and clear—but seems to rise from the stone itself, a geological fever that keeps the moisture in a state of constant, languid transition.

The landscape is a vast, high-altitude marsh of stone. There is no green here. Not a single blade of grass or leaf breaks the monotony of the mineral palette. Instead, the ground is composed of weathered, ochre-colored sedimentary rock, worn smooth by eons of weeping. The water does not fall as rain; it seeps. Every vertical face of the massive mesas is a "weeping wall," where warm, mineral-rich water oozes from the pores of the stone, coating everything in a slick, translucent film. In the basins between the mesas, shallow pools of alkaline brine shimmer, their surfaces broken not by lilies, but by the slow, rhythmic bubbling of submerged gas vents.

Across this continental expanse, the "built" world has fused with the "natural" one. This is a civilization of rust and masonry, an urban sprawl that defies typical settlement patterns. Enormous industrial spires, the color of dried blood and oxidized copper, rise from the canyon floors to pierce the cloud deck. They are not sleek; they are architectural fossils—thick-walled, buttressed, and covered in a patina of salt and lime. 

The technology is heavy, mechanical, and patient. Massive, slow-turning flywheels, some the size of city blocks, rotate with a deep, subsonic thrum that vibrates in the marrow of one's bones. Great chains, each link weighing tons, groan as they haul ore-sleds across the slick plateaus. Everything is electrified, but the power is primal—arc-flashes of blue light occasionally dance between the spires, illuminating the perpetual mist. The sound is a constant, cacophonous layering: the rhythmic *thud-hiss* of steam-pistons, the grinding of stone on stone, and the omnipresent dripping of the weeping walls.

The air is not empty. The fauna is overwhelming, a biological tide that fills every cubic meter of space. Without plants to provide a foundation, the ecology is built on the mineral wealth of the water and the heat of the machines. Clouds of chitinous, six-winged flyers, each the size of a human hand, swarm in densities that can obscure the light. They feed on the mineral crusts of the weeping rocks. Larger, translucent drifters—beings like aerial jellyfish—float on the warm updrafts generated by the factories, their long, sensory filaments trailing over the industrial rooftops. In the pools below, heavy, armored crustaceans with ceramic-like shells scavenge the slag heaps of the city-mills.

Movement in this world is deceptively slow. The great machines cycle over hours; the massive fauna drifts with the lethargic grace of deep-sea giants. The erosion has reached a state of equilibrium; the water has carved its permanent paths, and the stone no longer fights the flow. The urban sprawl does not seem to grow or decay; it simply *is*, a permanent fixture of the geology.

It is a world of saturated density. Every ledge is occupied by a nesting creature; every flat surface is etched with industrial conduits; every breath is thick with the scent of wet copper and warm salt. Despite the industrial noise and the swarming life, there is a profound sense of ancient stability. The heat is consistent, the light is unwavering, and the slow, heavy heartbeat of the flywheels suggests a world that has long since figured out how to endure, turning its own exhaustion into a permanent, humming stasis.
