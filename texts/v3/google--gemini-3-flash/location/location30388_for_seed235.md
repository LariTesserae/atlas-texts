At this altitude, the air should be thin and freezing, but here the atmosphere is a thick, sweltering weight. We are in the **High Citadels of Aethel-Root**, a vertical urban sprawl that clings to the jagged, impossible peaks of a mountain range that pierces the very roof of the world. 

The heat is a physical presence, a damp, tropical pressure that suggests a sun much closer than it appears. The light is relentless and steady, filtered through a permanent veil of suspended droplets that catch the glare and diffuse it into a blinding, pearlescent haze. Everything is wet. Water does not merely fall here; it emerges. It weeps from the pores of the great structures, it sweats from the undersides of the massive, serrated leaves, and it hangs in the air in heavy, unmoving globes that refuse to fall, held in place by a local breakdown of gravitational norms.

The "city" is a paradox. From a distance, it possesses the undeniable silhouette of a metropolis—a dense, layered grid of towers, plazas, and interconnected bridges. But there is no stone, no steel, no glass. The architecture is entirely biological, a result of "established erosion" where the very mountains have been sculpted by trillion-year-old mosses and calcifying vines into the shape of a capital. These are not buildings, but colossal, hollowed-out gourds and skeletal wooden cathedrals, their walls three feet thick with emerald lichen that feels like wet velvet. The structures are multi-storied, featuring balconies that overlook mile-deep abysses, yet there are no stairs, only tangled lattices of root-ladders that have remained unchanged for eons.

It is a lush, green nightmare of density. Every square inch of the "urban" surface is claimed by vegetation. Broad-leafed ferns the size of sailing ships drape over the edges of the high-rise plazas, their tips dipping into the mists below. The flora does not sway or grow; it exists in a state of frozen equilibrium. A flower caught in mid-bloom a thousand years ago remains exactly so, its nectar a sticky, hot syrup that never drips. 

There is no fauna. No insects buzz in the humid heat; no birds circle the spires. The silence that should accompany such a vacancy is obliterated by the sound.

The sound is a monolithic, deafening roar—a singular, unchanging note that vibrates in the marrow of the teeth. It is the sound of the planet screaming through the narrow, organ-pipe flutes of the hollow mountains. Because the wind never changes direction or intensity, the chord is eternal. It is loud enough to shatter glass, were there any glass to shatter. It is a physical force that pins the heavy mist against the green walls. 

In this place, reality has begun to fray at the edges of the "streets." In the central plazas, where the density of the biological buildings is highest, the perspective warps. You might walk down a "boulevard" of towering banyan-skyscrapers only to find yourself stepping onto the ceiling of the district you just left, the water droplets now "falling" upward toward a sky that looks like a floor. The geometry is non-Euclidean; the city wraps around itself in a way that defies the geography of the mountain peaks it occupies.

Nothing moves. The heat never breaks. The roar never falters. It is a city built by the sheer will of the vegetation to mimic order, a sprawling, urban monument to a civilization that never existed, inhabited by nothing but the steam and the sound. The water-slicked surfaces reflect the green of the ferns and the white of the mist, creating a world of two colors, held in a hot, screaming, and eternal stasis.
