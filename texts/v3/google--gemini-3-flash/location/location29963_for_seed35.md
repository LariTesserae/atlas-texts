Deep within the crust, where the weight of the world presses inward, lies a chamber known as the Copper Throat. It is a space no larger than a village square, yet it feels suffocatingly full, a dense subterranean pocket where the air is not composed of moisture or oxygen, but a dry, scouring suspension of powdered mica and ionized gas. Water is an impossibility here; the environment is a desiccated void of salt-crusted minerals and jagged, unweathered stone. 

The terrain is a nightmare of "young" geology. The floor is not worn smooth by time or feet but is composed of razor-sharp obsidian flakes and crystalline spurs that have only recently—perhaps in the last few centuries—shattered away from the ceiling. There are no rounded edges. Every surface is a fresh wound in the earth, gleaming with a dark, oily luster under the dim, amber light.

Heat radiates from the walls, a steady 100 degrees Fahrenheit that never fluctuates. This warmth is not the warmth of a sun, but the friction of a massive, stalled industry. Throughout the chamber, half-buried in the jagged floor, are the remnants of a titanic, nameless machine. Brass pistons the size of redwood trunks stand frozen in mid-stroke, their surfaces etched with frost-like patterns of salt. Copper conduits, thick as a human waist, weave through the rock like petrified vines. These are not ruins in the traditional sense; they are too clean, too sharp, as if the factory was built yesterday and then immediately abandoned by its makers.

The "vegetation" is sparse and alien: brittle, hair-like filaments of conductive silica that grow in the cracks of the machinery, feeding on the stray electricity that hums through the air. These glass-weeds do not sway; there is no wind. They stand perfectly rigid, glowing with a faint, sickly violet light.

The space is incredibly dense. It is crowded with life that does not move. Thousands of metallic, beetle-like organisms, each the size of a fist, are clamped onto the copper conduits and the sharp rock walls. They are packed so tightly they resemble a living armor, their iridescent shells reflecting the dim amber glow. These creatures are the source of the sound—a constant, deafening roar that fills the chamber. It is a sound of high-frequency vibration, a million wings shivering at a speed that renders them invisible to the eye, yet they never take flight. They remain anchored, vibrating in a state of frozen equilibrium.

This is the central rule of the Copper Throat: it is a place of static intensity. The sound is a physical pressure against the eardrums, a loud, unwavering drone that has not changed its pitch or volume for an eternity. There are no seasons here, no day or night, no cycles of growth or decay. The machine hums, the beetles vibrate, the glass-weeds glow, and the temperature remains a constant, baking heat.

In one corner of the chamber, a single structure stands apart—a small, rectangular kiosk made of a dark, non-reflective alloy. Its door is fused shut, and a series of gauges on its exterior are stuck at their maximum readings, the needles vibrating so fast they are merely a blur. It is a mark of intent, a sign that someone once monitored this roar, but they are long gone, leaving behind a world that is perfectly finished and utterly unchanging. 

To stand in the Copper Throat is to feel the air drink the moisture from your eyes in seconds. It is to be surrounded by a billion sharp edges and a wall of sound so thick it feels like a solid material. It is a cathedral of industry and biology, locked in a permanent, screaming moment of "now."
