The horizon does not exist here. Instead, there is the Layering—a vast, dim expanse of hand-pressed terracotta and wet, unfired clay that stretches in every direction until it dissolves into a thick, amber-tinted haze. Everything in this world has been made, but it has been made with the clumsy, urgent rhythm of a thumb pressing into mud.

It is a world of total architecture, yet it possesses no sophistication. There are no smooth surfaces, no machined angles. The ground is a sequence of endless, shallow basins and rising mounds of stacked, sun-dried brick, each the size of a torso, slapped together with thick, dripping mortar that never quite sets. The elevation is low and repetitive, a continental sprawl of unfinished plazas and hollow, windowless ziggurats that rise only a few stories before slumping back into the earth.

The heat is heavy and wet, a stifling 2.6-scale thermal weight that feels like being trapped inside a giant’s mouth. Water does not fall as rain; it exudes. The walls weep. The very air is a saturated sponge, and moisture beads on every thumb-print indent in the clay, running down the primitive masonry in silent, warm rivulets. These small streams collect in the low-lying basins, forming shallow, tepid marshes of silt where the "water" is more of a liquid mud, thick and opaque.

There is a profound absence of green. Not a single sprout or lichen breaks the monochromatic rust-red and ochre of the landscape. It is a mineral world, a place of geology forced into the shape of a city by an absent, primitive hand. Because the world is "emerging"—only 0.2 on the erosion scale—the edges of the bricks are sharp and raw, the mortar is still tacky, and the fingerprints of the builders are deep and unweathered. Nothing has had time to wear down; the world is being born in a state of frantic, unfinished construction.

Space here is vast but sparse. Though everything is "built," the structures are separated by enormous, empty plains of flat, pounded earth. You might walk for miles across a perfectly level floor of baked mud, only to encounter a single, colossal pillar that spirals upward into the dim light, its surface covered in the crude marks of a primitive scraper. The scale is so immense that the atmosphere develops its own internal weather; hot, moist drafts whistle through the gaps in the unfinished walls, sounding like a low, mournful flute played by the wind.

But the reality of this place is unstable. At a 2.8 weirdness rating, the laws of physics are as soft as the wet clay. In the distance, you might see a massive wall simply *un-happen*—the bricks liquefying into a red mist that then re-solidifies into a floating bridge that leads to nowhere. The geometry is turbulent. A staircase might begin at your feet and, as you watch, stretch ten miles into the haze, its steps vibrating with the energy of a creation that doesn't know when to stop. 

Quiet dominates, but it is not a peaceful silence. It is the silence of a construction site where the workers have just stepped away, or perhaps the silence of a god holding its breath. Occasionally, there is a wet *thwack*—the sound of a ton of fresh mud falling from an impossible height to start a new foundation, or the grinding groan of a thousand-ton slab of terracotta shifting six inches to the left as the ground beneath it decides to change its mind.

Light comes from nowhere and everywhere, a diffused, dim glow that seems to emanate from the silt-heavy atmosphere itself. It casts no sharp shadows, only long, blurry smudges of gray against the red earth. In this half-light, the "total built" nature of the world feels like a trap. There is no "outdoors." Every horizon is just another wall, every sky is just a higher, vaulted ceiling of unbaked brick, and every breath is a lungful of hot, wet dust and the smell of ancient, scorched earth. It is a world being built by a frantic, primitive force that has mastered the scale of planets but has not yet learned how to make a curve or plant a seed.
