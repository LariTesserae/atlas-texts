This is the Gilded Throat, a pocket of violent activity hidden five miles beneath the floor of a dead ocean. It is a single, immense subterranean cistern, a localized rupture in the planet’s crust where the heavy, mineral-saturated sea has found a way down into the heat of the mantle. 

The environment is entirely liquid, but the water here is not the cold, thin brine of the surface. It is a thick, warm slurry, the temperature of a fresh fever, pressurized until it behaves with the viscosity of oil. There is no sunlight. The only illumination comes from the dull, orange-red glow of heat-stressed metal and the occasional flicker of a high-pressure arc-lamp, casting long, shivering shadows through a medium that is never still.

At this depth, the landscape is not geological, but architectural—or rather, a skeletal ghost of industry. The "city" is a vertical sprawl of rusted iron pylons, massive brass-bound turbines, and honeycombed hab-modules that cling to the cavern walls like barnacles. It is urban in its complexity but hollow in its soul. The density of the space is deceptively low; while the infrastructure is vast, it is mostly negative space—enormous, cavernous voids between the gargantuan intake pipes and the churning rotaries. You could swim for minutes through the dim, warm murk without touching a solid surface, surrounded only by the vibration of the water.

The sound is the defining feature of the Gilded Throat. It is a 2.9-scale roar, a physical weight that hammers against the ears and vibrates the marrow of the bone. It is the sound of a trillion gallons of water being forced through industrial narrowings—the scream of cavitation, the rhythmic, low-frequency thrum of tectonic pumps, and the grinding of massive, unlubricated gears. The noise is so constant and so loud that it has become a weather pattern; the sound waves create visible ripples in the silt, churning the water into a permanent state of turbulence.

Nothing grows here. There are no leaves, no stalks, no soft photosynthesis. The only biology is a crust of chemotrophic bacteria that forms slick, pale veils over the metal surfaces, and the occasional "long-body"—a rare, translucent scavenger, blind and eel-like, that drifts through the voids between the machines. These creatures are rare, survivors in a world built for extraction rather than life.

Everything in the Throat is established and worn. The edges of the great brass valves are rounded by centuries of high-pressure erosion. The iron catwalks are pitted and thin, smoothed by the relentless rush of the mineral-heavy water. It is a world in a state of violent equilibrium: the machines are breaking down, yet they are so massive that their decay takes an eternity. 

The dynamic of the space is one of chaotic instability. Huge, hot-water plumes erupt from cracked pipes, creating sudden, blinding thermal updrafts that can toss a heavy object like a piece of driftwood. The water is never "level"; it is a frantic, churning mess of eddies and pressure-claps. The scale is intimate—contained entirely within the walls of this one gargantuan throat—yet the emptiness within that containment makes it feel like an endless, industrial cathedral.

You are inside the gut of a planet-sized engine that has forgotten its purpose but cannot stop its movement. The warmth is oppressive, the noise is a wall, and the darkness is thick with the taste of copper and salt. You are a speck of dust inside a screaming, flooded clock.
