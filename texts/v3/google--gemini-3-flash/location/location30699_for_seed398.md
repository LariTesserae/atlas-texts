At the very zenith of the Zenith—a needle-thin spire of obsidian glass that pierces the upper ionosphere—there is a vertical fracture no wider than a human hand. Within this hairline split lies the city of Oure.

The scale is deceptive. To a traveler, it is a crack in a rock; to the laws of physics operating within, it is a sprawling, multi-layered metropolis of logic-lattices and silver-threaded circuitry. This is a pocket of urbanity tucked into the throat of the sky, where the elevation is so extreme that the atmosphere has thinned into a purple-black void. Yet, inside the fracture, the air is thick, stagnant, and oppressively hot.

The heat does not come from a sun. The light here is dim, a terminal twilight filtered through the translucent, jagged edges of the spire’s peak. Instead, the warmth is a byproduct of the city itself—a radiant, dry fever born from hyper-dense processing and the friction of subatomic currents. It is the heat of a machine that never sleeps, even though its creators are long absent.

The terrain of Oure is young and mercilessly sharp. Because the spire only recently thrust its way through the crust of this geologically volatile world, the obsidian walls of the fracture have not yet been smoothed by time. They are razor-edged, shimmering with a dark, oily luster. There is no soil here, no moss, no clinging lichen. Life, in the biological sense, never arrived at this height. The world is purely mineral and mathematical.

Water exists here as a ghost. It is a periodic intruder, arriving not as rain, but as gasping bursts of condensation. When the superheated air of the city-crack meets the freezing void of the exterior peak, thin veils of moisture bloom against the glass walls. The water beads into perfect, heavy spheres that roll down the obsidian slopes, momentarily cooling the humming silver filaments of the city before evaporating back into the humid haze. These "sweat-cycles" are the only weather Oure knows.

The architecture is an organized delirium. Spanning the gap between the obsidian walls are webs of a material that defies modern engineering—cables of solid light, structures built from folded space, and shimmering, translucent modules that seem to vibrate at a frequency just beyond the range of hearing. The city is empty, or nearly so. There is a vast, echoing distance between one structure and the next, a loneliness of scale that makes the urban density feel hollow. It is a ghost town of the future, a settlement built for a population of data-points or light-entities that have moved on.

Everything is quiet, save for a low-frequency thrum that vibrates in the marrow of the stone. It is the sound of a heart beating in an empty house. Occasionally, a sharp *tink* echoes through the fracture as a silver strut expands under the heat, or a droplet of condensed water shatters against a super-heated circuit-path.

The city is active, however. It is not dead, merely unoccupied. Automated maintenance-drones—small, geometric shapes of mercury-like fluid—crawl slowly along the sharp ridges of the obsidian, polishing surfaces and re-weaving broken light-paths. They move with a predatory precision, though there is no prey to be found. They are part of an ecosystem of pure utility, sustaining a cycle of construction and repair that serves no one but the architecture itself.

In Oure, gravity feels tentative. The extreme elevation and the strange technologies embedded in the rock pull at the senses, making the vertical walls feel like floors and the dim, distant sky feel like a crushing weight. It is a place of profound stillness and frantic, invisible motion—a hot, damp, crystalline hive at the top of the world, waiting for a purpose that has yet to be recalculated.
