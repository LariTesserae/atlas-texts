Deep within a lightless vein of the lower crust, tucked into a pocket of space no larger than a cathedral’s nave, lies the city of Oure. It is a place defined by the weight of the earth above it and the relentless, rhythmic percussion of water.

Here, the world is entirely mineral. There is no green, no soft leaf, no rot. The architecture is a vertical sprawl of porous limestone and slick basalt, a dense honeycomb of dwellings carved directly into the walls of a great, weeping rift. The tech is fundamental: hand-chipped basalt tools, heavy stone counterweights, and primitive pulleys made of braided mineral fibers. Every surface is smooth, not by design, but by the patient tongue of water that has licked these stones for millennia.

The atmosphere is a cool, heavy mist. Water is the primary inhabitant. It does not fall as rain; it seeps from the ceiling in a billion synchronized tears, slicking the walls until they shine like oiled silk. The ground is a shallow, permanent basin of standing water, perhaps ankle-deep, through which the narrow streets are cut as raised, slippery causeways. Because the elevation is so far below the surface, the air is dense and smells of wet flint and cold iron.

The scale is intensely intimate. To stand in the center of the main "plaza" is to be able to touch the ceiling with a long pole and see the flickering lights of the furthest dwellings just a few dozen yards away. It is a claustrophobe’s urbanism—a city of layers, where one person’s floor is another’s vaulted ceiling, all stacked in a tight, vertical crush. There are no open vistas, only the sight of more stone, more dampness, and more carved niches.

The sound is a constant, deafening roar—a 2.3 out of 3 on the scale of noise. It is the sound of the "Great Weep." The millions of drips striking the shallow pools and the stone balconies create a white noise so thick it is almost tactile. Residents here do not speak; they use a primitive sign language or sharp, percussive clacks of stone-on-stone to communicate over the watery din. The sound never changes. It has been the same volume for an epoch, a frozen equilibrium of acoustics.

Light is a rare luxury, 0.7 out of 3. There is no sun, only the dim, sourceless luminescence of phosphorescent bacteria clinging to the dampest cracks, and the occasional flicker of a tallow-pot rendered from the rare, sightless pale-crabs that scavenge the basin floor. The shadows are long and heavy, and the inhabitants move through them with a slow, practiced grace, feeling their way along the eroded balustrades.

Nothing here grows, and yet everything is "established." The erosion is profound; the stairs are worn into deep, U-shaped troughs by generations of bare feet and the steady drip of the ceiling. The city feels finished—a masterpiece of primitive survival that reached its peak a thousand years ago and has seen no reason to change since. There is no construction, only maintenance. 

Life is rare. Aside from the occasional translucent crustacean scuttling through the dark pools, the city is a monument to a static existence. The people who live here—if they can still be called people—are as pale and hard as the stone they inhabit. They are part of the mineral cycle, their skin damp with the same mist that coats the walls, their movements as rhythmic and inevitable as the dripping of the ceiling.

In Oure, time does not pass in a linear sense. There are no seasons, no day and night, no harvest. There is only the cool, dark wetness and the roar of the water, an eternal city caught in a stone throat, breathing the damp air of the deep earth in a perfect, unchanging stasis.
