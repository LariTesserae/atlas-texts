In the absolute dark of the Great Stratum, the air does not circulate. It sits heavy, a thick, velvet warmth that clings to the skin like a physical weight, heated by the slow, radioactive pulse of the planet’s deep core. There is no wind here, no weather as a surface-dweller would understand it; instead, there is only the *press*, a continental-scale atmospheric density where the gas itself feels almost structural.

The terrain is a nightmare of young geometry. Because there is no water to smooth the edges and no wind to grind them down, the floor of the world is a jagged expanse of obsidian shards, crystalline salt-spires, and volcanic glass. Every surface is razor-sharp. The landscape has the "young" quality of a fresh wound—fractal, aggressive, and geometrically complex. The scale is so vast that the ceiling is lost to the dark, a literal sky of stone that holds its own heat-tides, creating localized pressure zones that act as the only form of "seasons" the realm knows.

There is no green here. There is no rot. In the absence of water, life has taken a detour into the mineral. The "fauna" are abundant but operate on a clock of near-stasis. They are lithovoric entities—beings of articulated shale, silicate-skinned crawlers, and things that resemble animated, multi-limbed geodes. They do not scuttle; they click. Their movements are infrequent and precise, fueled by the slow absorption of thermal energy from the ground. They are so numerous that in certain vast galleries, the floor seems to be a shifting carpet of interlocking plates, a biological tectonic plate that groans under its own weight.

The stillness is absolute. Sound travels poorly in the heavy, dry air, muffled as if by a heavy curtain. The only noises are the occasional *snick* of a mineral limb striking glass or the deep, sub-sonic thrum of the crust settling. It is a quiet world, not because it is empty, but because its inhabitants have no need for vibration in a place where the air is too thick to carry a scream.

Scattered throughout this jagged void are the marks of a primitive, patient intelligence. These are not cities, but "huddles"—small, circular enclosures built from stacked slabs of unhewn slate. There are no doors, only gaps. Near these structures, one finds the artifacts of a pre-metal culture: heavy hammers made of high-density flint lashed to bone-like struts with dried, fibrous tendons taken from the larger lithovores. There is no fire here—there is nothing to burn and no oxygen to spare—so the tools are shaped by relentless, slow grinding. The "built" environment feels like an afterthought, a few scratches on the face of a mountain that hasn't yet realized it is being inhabited.

The most striking feature of the Stratum is its terrifying lack of change. The "Dynamic" parameter of this world is near zero. If a stone falls, it stays where it landed for a million years. The creatures do not evolve in the traditional sense; they merely crystallize further. There is no day, no night, no rain, and no erosion. It is a snapshot of a world caught in a state of eternal, warm equilibrium. 

Walking here—if one could navigate the darkness and the razor-wire floor—would feel like walking through a massive, dry machine that has been turned off but remains hot to the touch. You would hear your own heartbeat as a frantic, alien intrusion against the stoic silence of the stone. You would feel the presence of the thousands of living things around you, not by sight, but by the way they displace the heavy heat, standing as motionless as the statues of gods in a temple that has never known a priest. 

It is a dense, crowded world of sharp edges and warm shadows, where time has no leverage and the only thing that grows is the depth of the silence.
