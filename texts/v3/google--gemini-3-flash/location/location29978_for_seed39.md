The air at this altitude is less a gas and more a cold, saturated suspension. We are at the roof of a young world, where the peaks are not mountains in the traditional sense, but jagged, upward-thrusting splinters of obsidian and raw basalt, so new that the concept of "soil" has not yet been invented. There is no grit here, no soft dirt—only the razor-edged geometry of stone that has just arrived from the interior.

Because the erosion is so minimal, the landscape feels unfinished, like a sculpture still being hewn from the block. The ridges are terrifyingly sharp, rising into a dim, slate-gray sky that hangs heavy with moisture. It is a wet cold—the kind that settles into the marrow. Water does not fall as rain so much as it manifests; it condenses directly onto the dark stone, turning the vertical faces into weeping walls. Thin films of water slide over the non-porous rock, collecting in shallow, crystalline basins that have not yet had time to carve out proper drainage.

The scale is immense and terrifyingly empty. Between one jagged spire and the next, there is a gulf of gray mist that could hide a city, yet there are no cities here. There is nothing built. No roads, no walls, no foundations. The emptiness is a physical weight, a vastness where the eye finds nothing to rest upon but the repeating motif of sharp rock and drifting fog.

Yet, despite the lack of structures, the environment is saturated with a heavy, industrial presence. It is not an industry of factories, but an industry of the air. Massive, autonomous atmospheric harvesters—each the size of a cathedral, yet possessing no "architecture" other than their functional, utilitarian hulls—drift silently through the canyons. They are smooth, matte-black shapes, humming with a deep, subsonic vibration that vibrates in the chest. They do not land. They do not build. They simply exist as nomadic engines, processing the thick, wet atmosphere, trailing long plumes of supercooled exhaust that look like artificial clouds. They are the only technology here: transient, mechanical, and strangely biological in their wandering.

Beneath the hum of these machines, the land is surprisingly alive. In the narrow, damp fissures where the rock has slightly buckled, sparse clumps of bioluminescent moss cling to the wet stone. They glow with a faint, sickly green hue, providing the only color in a world of charcoal and silver. 

The fauna, however, is abundant. Creatures with long, spindly limbs and multi-layered fur move with terrifying grace across the sheer, slick faces of the peaks. They are "crag-striders," their hooves tipped with a natural adhesive that allows them to sleep vertically on the weeping rock. They follow the nomadic harvesters, feeding on the rich, mineral-heavy frost that forms on the machines' intake vents. In the sky, vast, leathery-winged flyers circle the thermal exhausts of the engines, their cries lost to the whistling wind.

It is a world of constant motion but little sound. The dripping of water, the low-frequency thrum of the wandering tech, and the distant, rhythmic clatter of stone-fall as the young peaks continue to settle. The atmosphere is an active cycle of condensation and sublimation, a restless, cold engine that never sleeps. You are standing on the edge of a jagged precipice, looking out over a valley that is actually a tectonic wound, watching a billion-ton machine glide through a cloud of its own making, while a dozen pale, furred eyes watch you from the shadows of a nearby cleft. It is a place of raw potential, where the geology is still waking up, and the only industry is the quiet, relentless processing of the void.
