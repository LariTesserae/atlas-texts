This is the interior of the Great Cisterns, a place where the concept of "nature" has been entirely replaced by the concept of "the craft." There is no soil here, no bedrock, no sky. Every surface—the floor, the arching ribs of the ceiling, the tiered basins—is composed of hand-fired terracotta, hammered copper plates, and rough-hewn blocks of salt-pitted limestone. It is a world built by hand, yet it feels as ancient and weary as a mountain range.

The scale is intimate, yet hollow. You are within a series of three interconnected chambers, each the size of a small cathedral, but the density of the space is remarkably low. There are no partitions, no furniture, and no clutter. The emptiness is palpable, a vast volume of cool, damp air held between walls that bear the rhythmic, thumb-pressed textures of a potter’s wheel. 

The temperature is a steady, bracing cool, the kind of chill that clings to damp fabric. Water is the primary actor here. It does not flow in rivers, but rather weeps from the seams where the copper meets the clay. It sweats from the ceiling in heavy, rhythmic droplets and gathers in shallow, hand-carved depressions in the floor. The environment is a "drip-marsh"; the ground is a mosaic of flooded tiles and slick, algae-filmed ceramic, never deeper than an ankle but never truly dry.

Erosion has been at work for centuries. The sharp edges of the primitive masonry have been rounded by the persistent transit of water. The copper plates are green with a thick, velvety patina, and in some places, the salt-spray from the weeping walls has eaten jagged, lace-like holes through the metal, revealing only more darkness behind them. It is a landscape in a state of "settled decay"—it is falling apart, but so slowly that the collapse has become its permanent form.

Vegetation is a ghost here. There are no trees, no grasses. Instead, a thin, translucent film of pale violet mold clings to the dampest curves of the terracotta. Occasionally, a spindly, white, thread-like fungus sprouts from the gaps in the masonry, shivering in the drafts, but it provides no cover and no color.

In contrast to the sparse plant life, the fauna is overwhelming. The silence one might expect from such a hollow place is murdered by the sound. The chambers are loud—a cacophony of clicking, chittering, and the wet slap of movement. Thousands of pale, soft-shelled crustaceans, the size of dinner plates, scuttle across the walls, their many legs tapping against the ceramic like rain on a tin roof. In the shallow pools, translucent rib-ribboned eels pulse with a faint, internal light, their splashing constant as they thrash in the low-oxygen water. Overhead, heavy-bodied insects with wings like parchment batter themselves against the ceiling, their buzzing an abrasive drone that vibrates in the marrow of your teeth.

The light is dim and bruised. It filters down from narrow, high-altitude slits in the structure, though what lies outside is invisible. The light that does reach the floor is grey and diffused by the constant mist rising from the water, casting long, wavering shadows that seem to move independent of their sources.

Everything here is active. The world is a primitive machine in mid-cycle. You can hear the heavy, slow thrum of something deep beneath the tiles—perhaps a manual bellows or a gravity-fed pump—moving water from the lower sumps back to the ceiling. The ecology is a frantic scramble; the crustaceans graze on the mineral deposits of the weeping walls, while the eels occasionally lunge from the puddles to snatch an insect from the air. 

It is a strange, hand-tooled void. It feels like the inside of a gargantuan, abandoned lung, one made of clay and copper, still breathing only because the water continues to fall and the creatures continue to eat. There is no sense of "up" or "out," only the repetition of the tiles, the cold kiss of the mist, and the relentless, clicking industry of the things that live in the dark.
