The world is a vertical ocean, a sky of warm, heavy brine held aloft by pressures and forces that defy terrestrial logic. Here, elevation does not mean thinner air, but rather a climb toward the "surface-that-isn't"—a shimmering, hazy boundary where the water grows hotter and more saturated with suspended minerals. We are high up, thousands of meters above an invisible seabed, perched on the jagged, obsidian tips of "Emergents."

The terrain consists of these Emergents: gargantuan, needle-sharp spires of volcanic glass and raw, unweathered basalt that are currently thrusting upward from the lightless depths. They have no softness to them. There is no sand, no silted plains, no rounded river-rocks. The geology is embryonic, a violent birth frozen in the slow-motion viscosity of the deep. Every edge is a razor; every surface is a fresh, cooling flow of igneous black, still slick with the primordial sheen of its own creation.

Woven around these jagged peaks is a city of immense, primitive density. It is an urban sprawl of the Neolithic deep. Without fire or forge, the architecture is a triumph of manual craft and biological engineering. The "buildings" are colossal baskets of woven sea-vine and braided kelp-trunk, weighted down by stones lashed in complex macramé nets. These structures are tiered, stacked one atop the other like the nests of cliff-dwelling birds, clinging to the vertical faces of the Emergents. Thousands of these bulbous, swaying chambers form a continuous, porous wall of habitation that stretches for miles across the thermal vents.

The vegetation is lush and aggressive, fueled by the intense warmth of the rising magma below and the nutrient-rich brine. Forests of giant, translucent bladder-ferns grow in thick, suffocating layers, their fronds glowing with a faint, bioluminescent amber. They don't just grow on the rocks; they grow on the houses, on the tethering ropes, and on each other, creating a dense, multi-layered canopy that chokes the dim light into a soft, golden twilight.

Everything here moves at the speed of a heartbeat in hibernation. The water is thick, nearly a gel, muting all sound into a low-frequency thrum. The occasional clink of a stone tool against volcanic glass carries for miles, a sharp, lonely note in the pervasive quiet. There is no wind, only the slow, rhythmic pulse of thermal convection—the "Updraft"—which gently tugs at the hair and the long, trailing silks of the vegetation.

Fauna is a rarity, a ghost in the machine. Occasionally, a massive, gelatinous filter-feeder, the size of a cathedral and nearly as silent, drifts through the canyons of the city, its cilia pulsing in a slow, hypnotic wave. These creatures are treated with a quiet reverence; they are the only things that move faster than the rock, yet even they seem to be dreaming as they pass.

The technology of the inhabitants is beautiful in its simplicity. There are no machines, only levers of bone and pulleys of woven hair. They communicate through rhythmic strikes on the hollowed bladders of giant kelp, sending vibrations through the dense water. They are a people of the knot and the weave, their entire civilization held together by millions of hand-tied intersections. 

In this place, the world is still being decided. The rock is too new to have a history, the water is too heavy to allow for haste, and the city hangs in the balance between the rising heat of the earth and the vast, dim silence of the high-water sky. It is a warm, crowded, and breathless womb of a world, where the act of building is a slow, tactile prayer against the sharp, new edges of the earth.
