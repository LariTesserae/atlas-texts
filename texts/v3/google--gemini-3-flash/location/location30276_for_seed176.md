This is the Hissing Rib, a single vertical fracture in a mountain of black, unweathered glass that thrusts six miles straight up from a sea of permanent white mist. 

The scale here is intimate but terrifyingly steep. You stand on a series of staggered, interconnected iron gratings—the "Catwalk Orchards"—bolted directly into a basalt cliff so fresh and sharp it looks as if it were shattered only hours ago. The rock face is a wall of obsidian mirrors, jagged and un-eroded, reflecting the glare of a high, bright sun that never quite penetrates the dense humidity.

Everything is wet. The air is a thick, warm soup of suspended droplets that condense on every surface. Water doesn't just fall here; it weeps, trickles, and gushes from every fissure. It runs down the black glass in thin, shimmering sheets, giving the stone a greasy, prehistoric sheen. The heat is steady and tropical, smelling of wet rust, ozone, and the sweet, cloying scent of crushed ferns.

The vegetation is a claustrophobic riot. Because the rock is too young to have soil, the plants have become structural. Massive, waxy-leafed bromeliads the size of industrial boilers are wedged into the obsidian cracks. Thick, hair-like mosses, vibrant emerald and pulsing with absorbed water, carpet the iron scaffolds, muffling the ring of boots on metal. Vines as thick as a human thigh wrap around the heavy industrial piping that snakes up the cliffside, their roots seeking the warmth of the steam-filled conduits. 

The industrial presence is scattered but massive. This is not a ruined city, but a functioning, solitary lung of a machine. Great brass flywheels, fifteen feet across, turn with an agonizing, patient slowness behind cages of verdigris-covered copper. Every few minutes, a titanic pressure-valve somewhere above vents a plume of scalding white vapor that screams into the damp air before dissipating into the mist. The sound is a constant, heavy roar—a combination of falling water, the rhythmic *thud-hiss* of hydraulic pistons, and the low-frequency thrum of moving parts so large they vibrate the very marrow of your bones.

The tech is heavy, electrified, and cumbersome. Thick, rubber-insulated cables, slick with algae, drape between the outcroppings like the webs of a mechanical spider. Small, glowing amber bulbs are housed in thick glass jars along the walkways, though they are hardly needed in the bright, diffused daylight. There are signs of a rare inhabitant—a heavy wrench left on a ledge, a ceramic mug sitting on a vibrating turbine casing, a small, weathered shrine made of brass shavings tucked into a niche in the rock—but the scale of the machinery dwarfs any human presence.

Fauna is nearly non-existent, save for the occasional "Glass-Glider"—small, translucent-winged insects that cling to the wet obsidian, vibrating their wings to shake off the condensation. They seem indifferent to the deafening roar of the steam.

The world here feels static despite the noise. The flywheels turn so slowly they appear still until you look away and return; the plants grow with a relentless, creeping speed that eventually swallows the iron, but in this moment, everything is locked in a humid, vibrating equilibrium. You are caught in a cathedral of wet glass and screaming brass, suspended thousands of feet above an unseen ground, where the only way forward is a rusted ladder disappearing into the verdant, dripping mist above.
