The descent ends in the Hallow-Works, a subterranean cathedral of rust and chlorophyll buried three hundred feet beneath the surface crust. Here, the air does not circulate; it sits, heavy and swollen with a temperature that clings to the skin like a hot, damp wool blanket. It is a steady, sweltering 110 degrees, a heat that feels not like an event, but like a fundamental property of the stone itself.

The space is small—perhaps the size of a provincial train station or a grand, vaulted cloister—but it is impossibly dense. The walls are not visible; they are obscured by a frantic, layered encroachment of vegetation that thrives on the margins of heat and electricity. These are not soft, green things. The flora here is waxy, translucent, and thick-limbed, resembling rubberized conduits more than stems. Broad, liver-colored leaves, the size of dinner plates, overlap in scales, dripping with a constant, slow condensation that beads like oily sweat. There is no standing water, only this pervasive dampness—an intermittent ticking of heavy droplets falling from a leaf-tip onto a rusted iron grate, or the hiss of a bead hitting a hot copper pipe.

The "ground" is a labyrinth of established industrial remains. Massive, weathered brass cylinders, the size of fallen redwoods, lie half-embedded in the floor, their surfaces etched by centuries of slow chemical oxidation. Piles of gear-teeth, each as large as a human head, are fused together by lime deposits and moss. This is not a ruin in the process of falling; it is a ruin that has finished falling and has settled into a permanent, calcified architecture. The machines are "industrial" in their DNA—great pistons, throttles, and ceramic insulators—but they have been claimed by the geology of the deep.

In the center of the chamber stands the Primary Core, a gargantuan, vertically oriented engine that still hums with a terrifying, monolithic energy. It is the source of the sound: a low-frequency, physical thrum that vibrates the marrow in one’s bones. It is a loud, rhythmic tectonic groan, a sound that seems to take minutes to complete a single cycle. It is the sound of a mountain trying to breathe through a throat of iron.

The light is a dim, bruised amber. It does not come from the sun, but from the plants themselves and the dying breath of the machines. Faint electrical arcs, slow and languid as drifting sparks, occasionally crawl across the surface of the "Fern-wire"—a species of moss that has evolved to mimic copper filaments, drawing a meager charge from the ambient static of the Core. The glow is intermittent, pulsed by the slow heartbeat of the engine, casting long, shivering shadows of jagged leaves against the salt-crusted metal.

There is a profound absence of movement other than the mechanical and the botanical. No insects skitter through the rust; no birds nest in the high, dark rafters. The ecology is entirely autotrophic and metallic. The plants do not sway, for there is no wind, only the heavy, vertical pressure of the heat. They grow with agonizing slowness, their roots literally welding themselves to the cooling fins of the ancient generators, drawing minerals directly from the corroding alloys.

The rules here are dictated by the balance of decay. The "Slow" dynamic of the Hallow-Works means that a dropped tool might take decades to rust through, but it will eventually become indistinguishable from the floor. The "weirdness" manifests in the way the biological and the mechanical have reached a stalemate. A vine does not just climb a pipe; it mimics the pipe’s ribbing, eventually pulsing with the same rhythmic vibration as the steam within. 

Everything is near-touching. The density creates a sense of claustrophobia despite the vaulted ceiling. To move through the space is to push aside heavy, tepid fronds that feel like wet leather and to step over humming conduits that thrum with a forgotten purpose. It is a place of stagnant, high-energy peace—a hot, loud, and lonely womb where the industrial age has been slowly digested by a patient, sightless garden.
