The altitude is felt first in the lungs—a thin, sharp intake of air that tastes of frozen chlorophyll and wet stone. Here, at the roof of this world, the ground is not earth but an endless, churning sea of specialized lignified matter. It is a landscape of "Industrial Botany," where the functions of a factory have been subsumed by the evolution of the vine and the root.

Everything is green, but it is a cold, bruised green—the color of hemlock needles and deep-sea kelp. The vegetation is total; it forms the cliffs, the valleys, and the very sky-scraping peaks. Enormous, hollowed trunks the size of cathedrals act as natural cooling towers, venting plumes of pale, freezing mist that never quite becomes rain. Instead, the moisture exists as a constant state of condensation, slicking the waxy surfaces of the world in a fine, shimmering glaze.

The most striking feature is the movement. Despite the absolute, heavy silence—a quiet so profound it feels like a weight against the eardrums—the landscape is in a state of violent, turbulent motion. Great organic pistons, grown from heartwood and lubricated by viscous, amber-colored saps, rhythmically plunge into deep moss-choked pits. Massive, gear-like wooden whorls, three hundred feet in diameter, grind slowly against one another to mill frozen spores into a fine dust. These are not machines built by hands; they are the natural morphology of the flora, evolved to harvest the kinetic energy of the high-altitude winds and the thermal gradients of the planet’s core.

The scale is regional, a vast valley of heaving timber. You can stand on a ridge of calcified ferns and look across ten miles of what appears to be a living, breathing engine. Huge sheets of leaf-matter, toughened into something resembling corrugated steel, slide over one another in a chaotic, perpetual rearrangement of the terrain. A hill might fold into a trench within an hour, only to be replaced by a rising spire of rapid-growth bamboo-analogues that pulse with a rhythmic, bioluminescent beat.

It is a world of "established erosion." The shapes are not new; the plants have been performing these mechanical cycles for eons. The grooves in the wood are deep, the "joints" of the world are polished smooth by millions of years of friction. Yet, there is no sound. The air is perhaps too thin, or the plants themselves are evolved to be acoustic sponges, their surfaces covered in a microscopic, velvet-like down that devours vibration. A massive timber-shaft might shatter under the tension of a thousand tons of torque, and it would happen in a ghost-like hush, the splinters falling like snow.

There are no animals here, or very few. Occasionally, one might see a spindly, chitinous creature, the color of frost, picking its way through the churning gears to lick the sweet, pressurized hydraulic sap from a leaking vine-joint. These rare scavengers are the only witnesses to the cold, industrial industry of the forest.

The light is steady and indifferent, filtering through the high-altitude haze to cast long, sharp shadows across the shifting floor. There are no buildings, no ruins, no evidence that anything has ever been "constructed." The complexity is entirely emergent. The forest is the factory; the factory is the forest. It produces nothing but its own continued, frantic, and silent existence, recycling its own cold moisture and turning the high winds into a frantic, mechanical dance of wood and leaf. 

You stand in the center of a storm of growth, surrounded by the violent motion of a billion tons of living matter, and hear nothing but the sound of your own blood rushing in your ears. The world is busy, industrial, and utterly indifferent to the concept of a creator. It simply grows, grinds, and endures in the freezing damp of the heights.
