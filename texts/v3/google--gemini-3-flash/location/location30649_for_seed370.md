At the apex of an unnamed peak, where the atmosphere is thin and dry as parchment, sits the Ossuary of Chimes. It is a place that has forgotten the concept of a "ground." There is no soil here, no exposed granite, no soft silt. Every square inch of the landscape has been meticulously fashioned by hand, a total construction of sun-baked terracotta, kiln-fired brick, and a mortar made from pulverized stone and resin.

The scale is intimate—a dense, vertical hamlet no larger than a cathedral—but its density is absolute. There are no plazas, no wide thoroughfares. It is a labyrinth of honeycombed cells, narrow crawlways, and spiraling stairs that lead from one claustrophobic chamber to the next. The architecture is primitive, reflecting a culture of manual labor and simple tools: thumbprints are visible in the dried clay of the walls, and the arches are supported by hand-hewn lintels of petrified wood. It is a masterpiece of the repetitive, the small, and the infinite.

The air is warm, holding a steady, dry heat that seems to radiate from the bricks themselves. There is no water; the atmosphere has been scrubbed of moisture by the altitude and the ancient, unchanging climate. In the absence of rain or wind-borne seeds, the only thing resembling life is a brittle, calcified lichen that encrusts the corners of the structures. It does not grow; it simply exists, a frozen mineral bloom that hasn't changed its shape in ten thousand years.

The light is a perpetual, dim amber. It filters down through thick, translucent tiles of shaved horn and mica set into the ceilings of the upper layers. There are no shadows that move, for the light source—whatever distant celestial body or atmospheric glow provides it—is as static as the architecture. In this dimness, the textures of the world are hyper-present: the grit of the dust, the smoothness of the worn floor-tiles, the rough, porous surface of the hand-molded pillars.

The most defining feature of this place, however, is the sound.

It is a roar that never falters. Because the Ossuary is a singular, porous block of architecture perched at a high-elevation wind-tunnel, the air is forced through millions of tiny, hand-carved flutes and apertures built into the exterior walls. The result is a deafening, harmonized scream—a chord played by a thousand pipes that has not changed its pitch or volume for eons. It is a sound so thick it feels like a physical weight against the skin. It vibrates through the soles of one's feet, humming in the marrow of the bones. Because the dynamic is zero, there is no rhythm to the noise, no rise and fall; it is a solid wall of acoustic pressure.

There is no fauna. Nothing with ears could survive the volume, and nothing with a metabolism could survive the lack of water. The place is an empty husk, a "built" mountain that has reached its final, most stable state. The erosion is ancient and complete; the sharp edges of the bricks have been rounded by the mere friction of the air over millions of years, turning the once-sharp stairs into smooth, flowing slides of terracotta.

Everything here is packed. Every room is filled with the artifacts of the builders—stacks of clay tablets, rows of empty urns, piles of stone needles—all left exactly where they were placed when the construction finally reached its limit. There is no room to breathe, no room to move without brushing against the history of the place. It is a tomb not for people, but for the act of building itself. It is a static, screaming monument to the primitive hand, suspended in the warm, dry dark at the top of the world.
