The light here is a physical weight. It is a blinding, white-gold radiance that pours from a sky so pale it looks like polished bone. Because the elevation is high, the atmosphere is thin but saturated, creating a paradox: the air is clear enough to see for leagues, yet it is thick with a heavy, shimmering humidity that never settles into clouds. 

Everything is wet. The ground—a vast, weathered expanse of dark, volcanic glass and buffed obsidian—glistens under a perpetual film of hot moisture. It is not rain that wets this world, but a constant, aggressive condensation. The heat is extreme, nearing the boiling point of water at this altitude, and the landscape responds by weeping. Droplets form on the underside of every overhang, crawling upward against gravity through capillary action before vanishing back into the haze.

There is no green. There is nothing that breathes carbon dioxide or reaches for the sun with leaves. The biology here is mineral and fluid. Giant, translucent slicks of jelly-like membranes—the fauna—drape themselves over the architecture like living stained glass. These creatures, the size of houses, pulse slowly, filtering the mineral-heavy steam. They are abundant, thousands of them drifting through the canyons of the city or clinging to the sides of great rusted conduits, yet they make no sound. They move with the excruciating grace of oil moving through water.

The "urban" element of this world is not a place for inhabitants, but a vast, sparse machine. It is a regional sprawl of industrial skeletons—massive, oxidised copper towers, interlocking pipes the size of stadiums, and gargantuan, silent turbines that bridge the gaps between basalt cliffs. This infrastructure is old, settled into the landscape as if it grew there. The metal is pitted and weeping orange streaks of rust that mingle with the white mineral crusts left behind by evaporating steam. 

Though the technology is clearly industrial, it operates in total near-silence. There is no clanging of metal or roar of combustion. Instead, there is only the faint, subsonic thrum of pressurized steam moving through miles of pipework, a vibration felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard in the ears. The occasional *hiss* of a pressure valve is the only sharp sound, and even that is muffled by the heavy, wet air.

The scale is immense and lonely. A single "street" might be a three-mile stretch of flat, wet obsidian flanked by two-hundred-foot tall cooling racks that hum with electrostatic energy. Between these structures, the distance is vast and empty, a void filled only by the blinding light and the drifting, silent fauna. 

In the low spots of the terrain, the water collects in shallow, boiling lagoons. These pools are not clear; they are saturated with dissolved salts and metallic oxides, turning them into mirrors of liquid silver or bruised purple. There are no waves, only the occasional bubble rising from a geothermal vent below, breaking the surface with a soft, wet *pop* that echoes off the silent metal towers.

The world feels like a factory that has achieved a state of meditation. It is active—the steam moves, the creatures pulse, the heat cycles through the massive heat-exchangers—but it has no urgency. It is an ecosystem of hot glass, wet metal, and brilliant, punishing light, where life has traded the noise of the hunt for the silence of the soak. The air tastes of copper and hot rain. You can stand on a high gantry, looking out over fifty miles of shimmering, industrial desert, and hear nothing but the sound of your own pulse, while a thousand translucent giants drift slowly through the white glare above you.
