This is a vertical world, defined by the absence of a horizon. The elevation is so extreme that the concept of "ground" is local and temporary; everything is a sheer face, a plunging chimney, or a jagged spire reaching upward into a thick, starless gloom. 

The air is warm, holding a dry, radiating heat that feels as though it emanates from the stones themselves rather than an overhead sun. It is a parched environment, devoid of moisture, where the atmosphere carries the faint, acrid scent of pulverized minerals and ozone. 

The geology is raw and violent. Because erosion is only just emerging, there are no soft curves, no sanded flats, and no talus slopes of weathered scree. Instead, the terrain is composed of obsidian-slick plates, crystalline lattices with razor-edges, and massive, blocky extrusions of basalt that look like they were thrust upward only moments ago. The scale is vast—vast enough that a single cliff face might disappear into the darkness both above and below, honeycombed with vents that breathe out heat but no steam.

The world is characterized by a "turbulent" dynamic, yet it is not a storm of wind or water. It is a turbulence of the earth itself. The landscape is in a state of hyper-active formation. Deep, sub-audible groans vibrate through the soles of the feet as the vertical pillars shift. Every few hours, the geometry of a region changes; a spire might suddenly undergo a rapid, solid-state growth, lengthening by dozens of meters with a sound like a thousand dry sticks breaking at once, while a neighboring shelf shears off and drops into the lightless depths. It is a slow-motion explosion of geometry.

In the shadows of these shifting monoliths, there is a sparse presence of life—though not as we usually recognize it. There are no leaves, no stems, no green. Instead, there are calcified fungal mats that cling to the warm rock like dry, brittle scabs. In the deeper cracks, pale, hair-like filaments sway in the heat-currents, harvesting dry chemical energy from the air. These are the only things that move besides the rocks themselves. 

There is a strange, unsettling quiet here, punctuated only by the tectonic activity. The sound is not a roar, but a series of discrete, sharp events: the *tink* of a crystalline flake falling, the grinding moan of two plates pressing together, and the occasional, thunderous boom of a mountain-sized block settling into a new position.

Scattered across the more stable ledges are the "built" elements—the only evidence of intent in this chaotic verticality. They are not cities or machines, but primitive, solitary markers. Large, flat slabs of a different, paler mineral have been leaned against one another to form crude, triangular shelters, or stacked into precarious cairns that stand three stories tall. There are no tools nearby, no carvings, and no light sources. These structures seem to be frantic attempts to mark a location, to claim a "here" in a landscape that refuses to stay the same. The markers are often found snapped in half or buried under new up-thrusts of obsidian, suggesting a long, losing battle between a primitive architect and the restless crust.

Light is a rare commodity. There is no sun, only a pervasive, dim luminescence that leaks out from the friction of the moving plates—a faint, brownish-red glow that outlines the terrifyingly sharp edges of the world. It provides just enough definition to see the void between the spires, but never enough to see what lies at the bottom. 

You move through this place by climbing, always climbing, feeling the warmth of the stone through your palms, listening to the world rearrange itself in the dark. It is a place that is perpetually being born, jagged and unfinished, where the very act of standing still is a gamble against the rising earth.
