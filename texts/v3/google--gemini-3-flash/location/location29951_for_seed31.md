Here, at the ceiling of the world, the air does not circulate. It sits heavy and warm, a thick, invisible sponge saturated with moisture that never quite turns to rain. The elevation is dizzying—a vast, jagged plateau of obsidian-dark rock pushed upward by a recent, violent geologic youth. The peaks are not rounded by time; they are needles and razors, their edges so fresh they seem to hum against the stillness of the sky.

Water is the primary resident. It does not flow in the traditional sense, for the world here is too static for the rush of rivers. Instead, the mountain weeps. Every vertical face of the dark, sharp stone is slick with a thin, constant film of moisture, shimmering under a steady, unchanging light. In the hollows between the crags, shallow basins of water sit like polished mirrors, so still that they appear to be sheets of glass. There are no ripples. Even as the atmosphere condenses and heavy droplets fall from the overhangs, they seem to be absorbed into the pools without disturbing the surface, a quiet communion of liquid.

The vegetation is lush but disciplined. Thick, velvet-textured mosses in shades of emerald and bruised purple coat the lower reaches of the stone spires. Large, broad-leafed ferns, their fronds heavy with dew, sprout from the deep fissures in the rock. Yet, despite the richness of the flora, the landscape feels empty. The plants do not compete or tangle; they sit in their allotted spaces, separated by wide stretches of clean, wet stone. There is a profound sense of distance between each green presence, a spatial rhythm that allows the eye to travel miles across the valley floor without being snagged by undergrowth.

Rising from this wet, jagged earth is a city of terrifyingly simple ambition. It is a sprawling settlement of heavy, hand-hewn stone, built with a precision that suggests immense labor but rudimentary tools. There are no arches here, no delicate carvings. The architecture consists of massive, cyclopean slabs stacked into stepped pyramids and long, low-slung galleries that follow the natural contours of the high ridges. The city is organized, possessing a clear infrastructure of wide, flat avenues paved in dark slate and deep, rectangular channels intended to catch the constant weep of the mountains.

It is an urban environment, yet it is utterly silent. There are no birds to call from the heights, no insects to drone in the moss, no scuttle of small feet in the shadows. The only sound is the rhythmic, occasional *tink* of water hitting stone—a sound so isolated it serves only to emphasize the absolute quiet.

The technology of the place is tactile and heavy. Great stone counterweights sit at the edges of plazas, perhaps used to lift the massive lintels now set in place. There are no wires, no pipes, no glowing lights. The inhabitants—if they are there at all—move in a world of friction and gravity, using simple levers and hempen ropes that rot slowly in the warm, damp air. 

Nothing changes. The sun, or whatever light source dominates this high place, does not seem to track across the sky. The shadows are fixed, pinned to the ground. The moss does not grow over the paths, and the water does not erode the sharp edges of the obsidian buildings. It is a city caught in a moment of eternal afternoon, a high-altitude marsh of stone and fern where the wetness is a permanent state of being rather than a passing weather event. The scale is vast—a regional sprawl of peaks and plazas—but the density is low, leaving the landscape feeling like a grand, empty stage waiting for a play that has either finished or has yet to begin.
