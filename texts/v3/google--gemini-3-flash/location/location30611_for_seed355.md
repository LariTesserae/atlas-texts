The heat is the first thing that registers—a thick, suffocating weight that carries the scent of ancient minerals and wet wool. It is a humid kiln, a low-lying basin where the air has grown heavy enough to feel like a physical presence against the skin. In this absolute darkness, the world is defined not by sight, but by the oppressive warmth and the constant, rhythmic sound of falling water.

This is a city of salt and obsidian, though no hand ever carved it. It is an urban sprawl of geological happenstance, a vast, organized landscape of "buildings" that have settled into an established, eroded equilibrium over eons. The scale is immense; the avenues are wide enough for armies that do not exist, stretching out into a regional expanse of black, glistening stone. Despite the architectural density, there is a profound sense of emptiness. The structures—soaring, windowless obelisks and tiered ziggurats—are spaced with a mathematical loneliness, separated by plazas of perfectly flat, flooded basalt.

The water here is a primary actor. It does not merely rain; the atmosphere itself seems to be in a state of perpetual collapse. The air is so saturated that water condenses in mid-air, forming heavy, warm spheres that drift downward with agonizing slowness, or weeping upward from the porous ground. Every surface is slick, covered in a thin, shimmering film. There are no oceans or rivers here, only a global, shallow flood that pools in the gutters of the stone streets, reflecting a light that isn't there.

There is no technology, no flickering lamps or humming wires. The "urban" nature of the place is biological or mineralogical. The towers are not built; they are grown from the steady deposition of calcium and silica, shaped by the weird, fluctuating gravity of a reality in breakdown. In some sectors, the towers do not stand upright but lean at impossible, nauseating angles, their foundations dissolved by the heat, yet they do not fall. They hang in a state of permanent, eroded collapse, held together by the sheer density of the humid air.

Vegetation is a rare, sickly intrusion. There are no leaves or flowers. Instead, in the crevices of the great obsidian blocks, sparse clusters of black, hair-like filaments shiver in the dark. They are more like mineral growths than plants, absorbing the heat and the salt, twitching rhythmically as if counting the seconds of an eternal night. There is no animal life—not even the hum of an insect or the scuttle of a lizard. The silence is profound, broken only by the *thip-thip-thip* of water striking stone and a low, subsonic vibration that suggests the entire basin is slowly, very slowly, rotating.

The world is active, but its cycles are alien. The "weather" consists of the heat intensifying until the water on the ground flash-boils into a scalding mist, rising to the ceiling of the dark vault, only to cool slightly and begin the long, weeping descent again. During these cycles, the geometry of the city shifts. A street that led to a central plaza may, hours later, lead into a blind canyon of weeping salt. The "built" environment is in a constant state of slow-motion flux—erosion and deposition occurring simultaneously, keeping the city in a state of ancient, weathered balance.

It is a place of profound architectural intent without an architect. You walk through a plaza that feels like the heart of a metropolis, surrounded by the silhouettes of impossible skyscrapers, yet you are the only heartbeat for a hundred miles. The heat is a constant embrace, the water a constant veil, and the darkness a solid wall that hides the fact that the horizon is not a line, but a curve that eventually loops back upon itself, trapping the hot, wet silence in a perfect, lightless circle.
