The world here is a vast, subterranean hollow, an ancient lung of the earth that has forgotten the sun. The ceiling is so distant it acts as a sky, a vaulted expanse of pale, self-luminous quartz that sheds a relentless, shadowless white glare across the floor. This is a bright basement, cold and clean, where the air tastes of wet flint and carries a perpetual, brisk chill.

The terrain is a study in extreme age. The floor of the hollow is not jagged or rocky, but has been worn down by unthinkable durations of time into a series of shallow, undulating basins and flat, polished plains. The stone is a mottled gray-blue, slicked with a thin, iridescent film of moisture. It feels like walking on the surface of a giant, weathered tooth. There is no soil here, only the fine, silty dust of ground-up minerals that gathers in the hollows. Water does not flow in rivers; it beads on the walls and drips from the luminous heights in a slow, rhythmic condensation, forming shallow, crystal-clear pools that evaporate before they can ever become deep.

There is no green. The ecology is entirely animal and mineral. In place of trees, there are spindly, calcified structures—spires of bone-ash and hardened mud that rise in clusters across the plain. These are the cities. Despite the primitive nature of the materials, the architecture is complex and urban in scale. These are not mere nests, but sprawling, organized settlements of beehive-style dwellings, interconnected by raised walkways of woven hair and pounded silt. 

The density of these settlements is low; they sit like lonely islands in a sea of empty, polished stone. You can look across a single valley and see three or four of these hive-cities, miles apart, with nothing but the shimmering, damp plains between them.

The most striking feature of this place is the contradiction of its energy. It is a world of frantic, hyperactive motion, yet it is almost perfectly silent. 

The fauna is abundant, a teeming population of pale, multi-limbed creatures with wide, lidless eyes and soft, padded feet. They move with a terrifying, liquid speed. These are the builders. They are in a state of constant, turbulent industry. At any given moment, a thousand of them are swarming over a single spire, tearing down a wall with their clawed hands only to rebuild it a few inches to the left. They work with primitive tools—sharpened flakes of volcanic glass and heavy stone mallets—but they move so fast they appear as a blur of white motion against the gray stone.

It is a bazaar of silent chaos. You might watch a bridge being woven across a gap, hundreds of creatures scurrying back and forth in a feverish, anthill-like trance, and yet the only sound you hear is the faint, rhythmic *tink-tink* of glass on stone and the occasional, soft splash of a foot hitting a puddle. There are no voices, no grinding of engines, no wind. The air is still, but the ground seems to vibrate with the kinetic energy of a million silent footfalls.

The rules of this world are dictated by this tireless, soundless labor. Nothing is ever finished. The city you see in the morning will have a different silhouette by evening. A tower may be erected, inhabited, and entirely dismantled within a single lunar cycle of the glowing ceiling. The materials are recycled endlessly; the bone-ash is ground down and remixed with condensation to form new mortar. It is a civilization in a state of high-speed, equilibrium-driven flux—an ancient, stable environment being constantly rearranged by a primitive, hyper-dynamic population.

In the gaps between the cities, you find the remnants of their "ancient" history, which is really just the rubble of last week. Small piles of discarded glass tools, polished smooth by the constant dampness, sit like cairns on the open plains. The light from above is so steady and bright that it flattens the perspective, making the distant hives look like tiny, intricate toys set upon a vast, damp tabletop. It is a cold, busy, and beautiful void, where life exerts itself with frantic intensity against a backdrop of primordial, unchanging stone.
