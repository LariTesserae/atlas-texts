The summit is not a peak, but a jagged, obsidian-sharp bowl perched at an impossible altitude. Here, the air is thin and tastes of copper and cold rain, perpetually heavy with a mist that never drifts, only vibrates. This is a cathedral of stone and steel-flesh, a small, enclosed amphitheater of mountainside that has not yet learned how to crumble. The rocks are raw, their edges so fresh they seem to slice the very light that struggles to reach the floor.

Water does not flow here so much as it perspires from the atmosphere. The black, unweathered cliffs weep a constant, viscous fluid that hums as it beads. It clings to the sharp geometry of the terrain, pooling in shallow, perfectly still basins that reflect a sky that never changes its bruised, twilight hue.

The most striking feature is the sound: a rhythmic, industrial thudding that rises from the bedrock itself. It is the sound of a massive, subterranean engine—not a machine built by hands, but a geological function. The mountain is a piston. The ground shudders with a deep, mechanical resonance, a low-frequency roar that suggests a factory the size of a continent churning just beneath the moss. Yet, there are no buildings, no ruins, no rusted scrap. The technology is intrinsic, biological, and mineral.

Lush, alien vegetation clings to the sparse patches of black soil. These are not soft plants. They are ferns with leaves like serrated foil, and vines that pulse with a rhythmic, electrical glow, mimicking the throb of the mountain’s heartbeat. Between these bursts of greenery, the space is wide and empty, creating a sense of profound loneliness despite the abundance of life. The density is low; each organism stands as a singular, monumental presence, separated by yards of weeping stone.

The fauna is everywhere, yet difficult to look at directly. Small, multi-limbed creatures with shells like burnished chrome skitter across the jagged rocks, their movements synchronized with the mountain’s mechanical pulse. Birds—or things that fly like them—hover in the air, their wings remaining perfectly still as they are held aloft by some localized defiance of gravity. They do not chirp; they emit bursts of high-speed data-burst static that echo off the sharp cliffs.

Reality here has reached a point of exhaustion. In the center of the bowl, water does not fall; it hangs in the air in perfect, basketball-sized spheres, revolving slowly around unseen axes. Time has ceased to be a linear progression and has become a frozen state of being. A leaf falling from a metallic fern may take a century to reach the ground, or it may simply vanish mid-air, reappearing at the top of the branch in a seamless loop. The light, dim and flickering, seems to originate from the air itself rather than a sun, casting shadows that point in conflicting directions.

Everything is cool to the touch—a damp, refrigerated chill that preserves the landscape in its state of perpetual "just-born." There is no decay here, only the relentless, loud, and unmoving operation of a natural world that has mistaken itself for a machine. You are standing inside a high-altitude engine room where the grease is moss, the fuel is cold water, and the operator is a law of physics that went beautifully, terrifyingly wrong.
