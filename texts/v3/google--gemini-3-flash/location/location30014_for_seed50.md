The sound is the first and final thing: a physical, chest-crushing roar that occupies every cubic inch of the space. It is not the sound of a storm, but the sound of the world being poured. Here, at an altitude where the air should be thin and freezing, the atmosphere has been replaced by the "Great Downward"—a vertical ocean falling from an unseen height. It is a sky made of heavy, rushing curtains of cold, aerated water, illuminated by a stark, indifferent light that turns the deluge into a blinding hall of mirrors.

In this colossal verticality, there is a pocket of near-void. It is a tiny, accidental room in the chaos, a "bubble-well" perhaps a hundred feet across, where the falling weight of the ocean parts for a moment around a singular, impossible geographic birth. 

Emerging from the center of this pocket is a spire of raw, black obsidian-glass. It is not an ancient peak; it is a geographic embryo. The rock is so new that its edges are razor-edged, lacking even a few days of erosion. It feels under construction, as if the pressure of the falling water is actively squeezing the mineral out of the ether, crystallizing it in real-time. The spire is a jagged, vertical needle, slick with spray but unweathered by time. It has no soil, no sediment, only the gleaming, cooling facets of its own birth.

The scale is intensely intimate, a micro-environment trapped within a global catastrophe. You can stand on a ledge of this spire and see the "walls" of the world—shifting, turbulent curtains of water that move with a violent, rhythmic instability. The pocket is never the same shape for more than a minute. The water-walls bulge inward and snap back, creating a frantic, hyperactive sense of claustrophobia within a vast, empty space.

Life is an afterthought here. In the crevices of the sharp glass, sparse clumps of a pale, rubbery lichen cling for survival. It has no leaves, only translucent bladders that vibrate in sympathy with the deafening roar, harvesting energy from the sheer kinetic force of the sound. Once, a creature might pass through—a "sky-ray," a flat, cartilaginous thing with no eyes, navigating the turbulence by sensing the pressure changes in the falling curtains. It is a rare, lonely sight, a ghost in the machine of the downpour.

There are marks of presence, though they are primitive and desperate. A few platforms of salvaged, water-logged timber—driftwood from some distant, flooded world—have been lashed to the obsidian spire with thick, hand-braided ropes of kelp-fiber. These structures are not houses, but perches. A single, small hut made of stretched fish-skins sits precariously on a jagged outcrop. It is held down by heavy stones. Near it, a primitive apparatus of hammered copper funnels the spray into a stone basin. There are no machines, no fires, no complex tools—only the manual craft of a soul trying to anchor itself to a needle in a waterfall.

The light is constant and cold. It filters down through miles of falling water, refracted into a million shimmering needles that dance across the black glass of the spire. It is bright enough to see every grain of the spray, yet it provides no warmth. The temperature sits at a steady, bone-aching chill, kept low by the eternal motion of the water.

Everything here is in a state of becoming or being undone. The spire grows upward, pushed by tectonic or fluid pressures from below, while the water-walls attempt to collapse the air-pocket. It is a place of high-energy emptiness, a room made of noise and falling glass, where the only rule is the weight of the water and the sharp, emerging reality of the stone. You are suspended in a high, hidden flaw of the universe, watching the world try to decide if it is a sea or a mountain.
