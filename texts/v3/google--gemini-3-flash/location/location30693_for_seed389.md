The world is a vertical wound of black glass and pressurized steam.

At an altitude where the air should be freezing, the heat is instead an intrusive, heavy weight—a sweltering 2.7 that radiates not from a sun, but from the marrow of the rock itself. The geology here is embryonic (0.5 erosion); the edges of the obsidian cliffs are razor-sharp, having never been softened by wind or significant rainfall. There is no soil, no silt, and no sand. There is only the raw, tectonic trauma of the up-thrust.

Moisture exists here as a pervasive, slick film (1.2 water). It is not a cycle of rain, but a constant, gasping condensation. The heat of the stone meets the thin, cold air of the heights, wringing water out of nothing. It clings to the undersides of metal gratings and beads on the surface of the glass cliffs, lubricating the world without ever truly washing it clean.

The landscape is defined by a staggering density of construction (2.3 built, 2.4 density). An industrial civilization has grafted itself onto these vertical shards. Massive, soot-stained iron conduits and brass-fitted pressure vessels are bolted directly into the unyielding basalt. The city does not sit on the land; it clutches it. Suspended walkways, tiered factories, and windowless housing blocks are stacked one atop the other in a dizzying, multi-layered sprawl that fills every crevice of the mountain range.

Despite the industrial scale (2.5 tech), the world is eerily quiet (0.9 sound). The machines here do not clatter or scream; they breathe. There is a deep, subsonic thrum of massive pistons moving with geological slowness (0.6 dynamic). Immense gears, three stories tall, turn perhaps once an hour, their movement so gradual it is nearly imperceptible to the naked eye. The work being done here—refining the heat of the planet, perhaps, or distilling the heavy vapors of the heights—is a patient, rhythmic process.

There is a total absence of green (0.1 vegetation). No moss clings to the damp metal; no lichen ventures onto the black glass. The only "ecology" is a mineral one. Occasionally, one might spot a scavenger (1.4 fauna)—a creature with a carapace of dull pewter or calcified bone, moving with the same agonizing slowness as the gears, clinging to the vertical pipes with multi-jointed limbs. These rare inhabitants seem less like animals and more like stray thoughts of the machines.

The light (1.5) is steady and unremarkable, filtering through a permanent haze of steam and industrial exhaust that hangs between the spires. It casts long, unmoving shadows across the dense infrastructure. 

The "weirdness" (2.4) manifest in the physics of the place. Because the erosion is so low and the elevation so high, the air behaves strangely; sound travels poorly, muffled as if by invisible wool, yet a drop of water falling from a high catwalk might take minutes to reach the depths, drifting sideways in defiance of expected gravity, caught in the slow, hot thermals rising from the industrial vents. The buildings themselves seem to be slowly being swallowed by the rock, not through decay, but because the mountains are still growing, pushing the iron city further into the sky with every passing century. 

To stand on a balcony in this place is to feel the intense, damp heat rising from below, to hear the muffled heartbeat of a million tons of slow-moving iron, and to look out over a horizon of black, knife-edged peaks that have never known the touch of a leaf or the sound of a bird. It is a city of stasis and pressure, built on the sharpest edge of the world.
