At the crest of the world, where the atmosphere thins into a piercing, crystalline clarity, lies the Sallow-Grange. It is a place of finality, an ancient plateau so deeply eroded by eons of wind that its surface has reached a state of glassy, undulating smoothness, like a frozen ocean of grey-blue siltstone. 

Though the air is frigid—a biting, sharp cold that makes the lungs ache—the light here is relentless and bright. The sun does not warm, but it illuminates every microscopic fissure in the stone with an unforgiving, silvered glare.

The Sallow-Grange is a city, yet it contains no masonry, no metal, and no hands to build them. Instead, the "urban" landscape is a biological phenomenon. Massive, calcified lichen structures have grown into precise, geometric towers, some rising sixty feet into the air, mirroring the density of a crowded port town. These structures are hollow, porous, and hard as bone. They are arranged in concentric rings around a central depression, creating streets and plazas of bare, polished rock. The architecture is a result of millions of years of competitive growth—the plants have learned to form walls to shield one another from the gale-force winds, creating a self-organizing maze of organic limestone.

The scale is intimate; one could walk from one end of this "city" to the other in twenty minutes. Beyond its perimeter, there is nothing but the vast, empty drop of the mountain and the void of the sky. The density is low—between the towering, bone-white structures, there is a vacuum of space, a sense of being in a cathedral whose roof has been ripped off.

It is never quiet. The Sallow-Grange is a percussion instrument. Because the lichen towers are riddled with tubular vents and hollow chambers, the constant, high-altitude wind creates a deafening, polyphonic roar. It is a choir of low groans and piercing whistles that vibrates in the marrow of one's teeth. The sound is active and shifting, changing pitch as the wind veers or as the intermittent rains—sudden, freezing squalls that turn the stone to a slick mirror—plug the vents with slush.

Water exists here in a state of nervous transition. It is damp, but rarely liquid for long. Condensation clings to the leathery, emerald-green mosses that carpet the interiors of the white towers, thick and lush despite the cold. When the sun hits a certain angle, the entire Grange begins to steam as the frost sublimates, wrapping the "city" in a brief, blinding shroud of white vapor.

Life is present but specialized. The vegetation is vigorous, a monoculture of architects: the white, bone-hard lichen and the soft, heat-trapping mosses that live inside them. Fauna is nearly non-existent, save for the Occasional-Drifter—massive, flat, paper-thin organisms like translucent kites that snag on the towers to feed on the mineral-rich mist before being swept back into the void.

There is a profound sense of "final form" here. The erosion has done its work; there are no more landslides to be had, no more jagged peaks to crumble. The Sallow-Grange is what remains when the world has finished changing its shape and has settled into a cold, loud, and beautiful stasis. It is a settlement of plants, built for the wind, standing at the edge of the sky.
