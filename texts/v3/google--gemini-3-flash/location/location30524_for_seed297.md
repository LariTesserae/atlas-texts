Here, the concept of "nature" has been rewritten by a logic that does not require chemistry.

The landscape is a vast, low-slung basin of fractured obsidian and synthetic silica. It is a dry world, devoid of even a trace of vapor; the air is a parched, super-heated gas that carries no scent of rot or growth. Yet, the horizon is obscured by a forest so dense it feels suffocating. This is a "lushness" of hardware—a biological mimicry executed in superconducting filaments and self-assembling carbon.

The terrain beneath is young, almost dangerously so. The ground consists of flat, geometric plates that seem to have snapped into existence only moments ago. Their edges are molecularly sharp, unweathered by wind or rain, for there is no weather here. The ground does not yield; it is a basement of dark, polished mineral that hums with a faint, sub-audible vibration.

Rising from this jagged floor are the "trees." They are tall, slender spires of matte-white ceramic, branching into millions of fractal needles that shimmer with a faint, internal gold light. They do not sway. There is no breeze to move them. They stand in a state of frozen, dense equilibrium, packed so closely together that moving between them requires a constant navigation of sharp angles and narrow gaps. They are not growing in the traditional sense; they are *extending*, pushing further into the sky by iterating their own geometry, yet they never seem to change. If you were to mark a spot on a trunk, that mark would remain, immutable, for a thousand years.

The light is pervasive and bright, as if the sky itself is a high-definition panel emitting a constant, shadowless noon. This light reveals the "glitches" in the geography. In the middle distance, a grove of these ceramic ferns might simply cease to obey perspective—they appear the same size whether you are ten feet or a mile away, creating a nauseating sense of flatness. Elsewhere, the landscape "tears," revealing glimpses of a void beneath the black floor that looks like raw source code or untextured white space. 

There is a profound lack of sound. The silence is not the absence of noise, but a heavy, pressurized thing. Occasionally, the silence is punctured by a sharp *clink*—the sound of a new fractal leaf snapping into existence—or a low, rhythmic thrumming that seems to come from the sky itself.

Fauna is nearly non-existent, but not entirely absent. Every few miles, one might encounter a "Lattice-Walker." These are not creatures of flesh, but drifting, polyhedral structures of light and wire. They move with a stuttering, non-linear motion, appearing in one spot and then another without crossing the space between. They do not eat; they seem to be auditing the landscape, their sensor-arrays clicking softly as they scan the immutable trees for errors in the geometry.

The atmosphere is warm, a dry, radiant heat that feels like the exhaust from a massive processor. It is a comfortable warmth, but one that offers no comfort. There is no water to drink, no fruit to eat, and no soft place to lie down. Every surface is hard, every edge is sharp, and every object is a masterpiece of post-physical engineering.

In this place, time has reached a stalemate with design. The world is finished, yet it is just beginning. It is a regional expanse of infinite complexity, a forest of static data where the only thing that changes is the observer's location. To walk here is to move through a graveyard of the future—a place where life was replaced by a perfect, dry, and terrifyingly beautiful simulation that forgot to include a way for things to die.
