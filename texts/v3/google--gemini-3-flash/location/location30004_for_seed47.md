The world of the Oul-Lattice is a study in impossible verticality and the profound silence of the vacuum. 

It is a landscape defined by the "Needles"—vast, slender pillars of bone-smooth, vitrified mineral that rise from a base so deep it is obscured by the curve of the planet. These spires reach upward for eighty miles, piercing the thin, cool atmosphere and entering the stillness of the exosphere. They are not mountains; they are the final, eroded remnants of a planetary core that has been scoured by a billion years of solar winds. The surfaces are polished to a mirror-sheen, reflecting the steady, normal light of a distant sun with a clarity that feels surgical.

There is no water here. The "seas" are basins of fine, white silica dust, miles deep, that behave like fluids under the influence of the world’s erratic gravitational tides. 

Despite the ancient, settled nature of the terrain—every stone having found its final, unchangeable seat eons ago—the environment is hyper-dynamic. The air, though thin and dry, is thick with a "phase-storm." Matter here does not sit still in its dimension. You might be looking across a hundred-mile gulf at a neighboring spire, only to see the entire horizon shiver and rewrite itself. The spires remain, but the space between them boils with flickering geometric shapes, ghost-lights, and sudden, violent shifts in the local gravitational constant. It is a bazaar of physical laws, changing every second, yet it makes no sound. A mountain-sized block of crystallized gas might shatter into a million shards a mile away, and you would hear only the faint, rhythmic pulse of your own blood in your ears.

The density of the world is vanishingly low. It is a world of "between." Between the spires, there is mostly nothing—vast, yawning chasms of clear, cool air. Traveling from one spire to another is an exercise in traversing a void. 

Vegetation is sparse and alien. It takes the form of "Lace-Glass"—biological structures that look like delicate, frost-covered webs stretched across the shadowed leeward sides of the Needles. These organisms do not drink; they feed on the high-energy particles flung off by the phase-storms. They are brittle, clicking softly if touched, though they rarely move. Fauna is rarer still: occasional "Drifters," translucent, kite-like organisms the size of city blocks, float on the solar winds, their bodies so thin they are nearly two-dimensional. They navigate the turbulent gravitational pockets with a grace that suggests they are tuned to the planet's erratic heartbeat.

Scattered across the peaks of the Needles are the remains of a civilization that transcended the need for biology. These are not ruins in the traditional sense; they do not crumble. They are artifacts of "Hard-Light" and "Folded-Space." A single building might appear as a floating ring of black metal that, when viewed from the side, disappears entirely. These structures hum with a technology that manipulates the very fabric of the landscape to keep the Needles from snapping under their own impossible height. There are no doors, no windows—only smooth, humming surfaces that seem to be calculating the chaos of the phase-storms in real-time.

The light is steady, a pale, cool yellow that casts shadows of terrifying length. Because the air is so clean and the moisture so non-existent, there is no atmospheric haze. You can see a pebble on a ledge three miles away as if it were in your hand. This clarity, combined with the extreme elevation, creates a permanent sense of vertigo.

The rules of this world are dictated by the "Flux." While the ground is the oldest, most stable matter in the universe—eroded down to its indestructible essence—the space *around* the ground is in a state of constant, high-speed evolution. A bridge of solid light might blink into existence between two spires, hold for a century, and then vanish in a nanosecond, leaving whatever was crossing it to fall for a literal hour before hitting the silica dust below.

It is a place of profound, lonely beauty—a cathedral of stone needles and silent storms, where the technology of gods holds up the skeleton of a dead world, and the only thing that moves is the light and the shivering air.
