This is the Great Hydrosphere, a planetary-scale volume of super-cooled, high-pressure water that exists in a state of terminal equilibrium. It is not an ocean as a surface-dweller would understand it; there is no shore, no tide, and no sky. It is a three-dimensional abyss, three times the diameter of a standard terrestrial world, held together by a gravitational core that generates a terrifyingly high elevation relative to the void of space.

The landscape is defined by its emptiness. Because the density is so low—a mere 0.1 on the scale of occupancy—one can drift through the cold, sapphire-dark medium for hundreds of leagues without encountering a single solid object. When a presence finally emerges from the gloom, it is always a shock: a "Young Peak." These are colossal, jagged spires of un-eroded volcanic glass and raw basalt that thrust upward from a core so deep it is never seen. Because the world is static and the water does not move in currents, there is no erosion. The edges of these mountains are razor-sharp, as fresh as the moment they were forged, cutting through the water like knives.

There is no green here. The vegetation is non-existent, replaced by a mineral ecology. The peaks are occasionally dusted with a fine, silicate "snow"—precipitated minerals that settle in the still water.

Within this vast, silent void, the sound is the first thing that breaks the mind. It is a deafening, 2.9-magnitude roar that never ceases. This is not the sound of weather or waves, but the sound of the Machines. Scattered across the Hydrosphere, separated by thousands of miles of empty water, are the Industrial Anchors. These are gargantuan, blackened steel structures the size of provinces, bolted directly into the young, sharp peaks. They are engines of incomprehensible purpose. Huge, thrumming turbines and massive, reciprocating pistons churn the water, creating a vibration so intense it can shatter glass. The sound travels perfectly through the cold, dense medium, overlapping from thousands of miles away to create a permanent, bone-shaking cacophony that feels like a solid weight against the skin.

Despite the noise and the cold, life is abundant, though it is a strange, non-botanical life. Huge, translucent leviathans, shaped like hollow needles and spanning half a mile in length, drift through the void. They have no eyes; they navigate by the deafening industrial hum, their bodies vibrating in resonance with the distant engines. Clouds of silver, plate-like fish congregate near the Industrial Anchors, feeding on the heat signatures and the microscopic mineral grit shed by the grinding gears. These creatures do not evolve, and they do not die off; they exist in a frozen biological equilibrium, much like the world itself.

The light is normal—a diffused, sourceless luminescence that permeates the water, as if the molecules themselves were faintly glowing. It provides no warmth. The temperature is a constant, shivering 0.2, just a fraction above the point where the pressure would force the water into exotic ice.

Nothing ever changes. There are no seasons, no storms, and no decay. The machines have been running for an eternity, their metal surfaces untouched by rust in the oxygen-free depths. A traveler could leave a mark on a basalt cliff and return a thousand years later to find it exactly as it was, the jagged edge still sharp enough to draw blood. It is a world of monumental scale and industrial permanence, a loud, cold, and empty cathedral of water where the only thing that moves is the rhythmic, mechanical pulse of the hidden engines.
