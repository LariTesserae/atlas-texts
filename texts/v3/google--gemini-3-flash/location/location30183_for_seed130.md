The ground is not earth, but a vast, low-lying pediment of overlapping iron plates, worn to the thickness of parchment by a billion years of friction. This is a basin of total construction, a landscape where the concept of "natural substrate" has been erased by successive epochs of building. Here, the floor of the world is a rusted, water-slicked plane that stretches toward a horizon lost in shadow. It is ancient—not in the way of mountains, but in the way of a machine that has been running since the stars were young, its edges rounded and its fine details buffed away by the endless weeping of the atmosphere.

It is cool here, a damp chill that settles in the marrow. The air is heavy, a suspended soup of industrial condensation that tastes of copper and cold grease. Water does not fall as rain so much as it manifests; it beads on every surface, weeping from the undersides of gargantuan, overhead conduits and pooling in the shallow depressions of the metal floor. These pools are dark, iridescent with thin films of ancient lubricants, reflecting nothing but the dim, intermittent flicker of failing indicator lights miles above.

The scale is oppressive yet hollow. The landscape is an open expanse, a valley-sized floor of machinery, but the structures are spaced with a lonely, sparse density. A mile-wide piston, rusted to a deep umber, might stand alone in a plain of flat plating, rising into the dark ceiling like the trunk of a dead god. There is a profound sense of emptiness between these mechanical giants, a regional loneliness where the only company is the rhythm of the works.

And the sound is absolute. It is a deafening, structural roar—the groan of tectonic-scale gears grinding against silted bearings, the shriek of high-pressure steam venting into the cold dark, and the rhythmic, subsonic thrum of a heart that beats once every hour, shaking the very plates of the floor. You do not hear the sound so much as you are inhabited by it; it vibrates the fluid in your eyes and the marrow in your teeth. It is a constant, violent presence that makes silence seem like a forgotten myth.

Vegetation is a desperate, sparse afterthought. In the seams where the floor-plates meet, a translucent, hair-like moss clings to the moisture, feeding on the heat of the friction below. It is pale, lacking chlorophyll, shivering with every vibration of the ground. There are no animals. Nothing with blood could survive the relentless vibration or the chemical bite of the air. The only "ecology" is the cycle of the machines themselves—the way the condensation drips into catch-basins, is pumped through screaming pipes to some unseen heights, and falls again as a fine, metallic mist.

The world is active, but its activity is geological in pace and industrial in nature. Huge, slow-moving gantry systems, miles long, crawl across the ceiling on tracks so worn they are almost smooth, their movement marked by the shower of orange sparks that drift down like dying stars. The light is nearly non-existent, a permanent 0.5-scale gloom where the only illumination comes from these sparks, the dull red glow of overheating regulators, or the occasional, violet arc of a short-circuiting cable dancing in the mist.

This is a world that has reached its final, stable state. It is a derelict that cannot stop working. The erosion is visible in the way the great pillars have been thinned by the constant drip of acidic water, and how the stairs—built for someone who vanished eons ago—have been worn down into smooth, slippery ramps. It is a place of total artifice, yet it feels more primordial than a forest, a testament to a civilization that built so much, for so long, that the planet beneath them simply ceased to be, replaced by this shivering, weeping, screaming monument to the machine.
