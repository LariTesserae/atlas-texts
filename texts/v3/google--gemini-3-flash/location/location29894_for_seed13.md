The sky here is not a theater of movement; it is a solid, unblinking vault of pale cerulean. The sun hangs at a perpetual forty-five-degree angle, a fixed weight in the heavens that never matures into noon or softens into dusk. Because the light never moves, the shadows are permanent residents, etched into the landscape like black ink spilled on gray silk. 

We are high up, where the air is thin and crisp, carrying a constant, bracing chill that never fluctuates. The terrain is a violent, young geography—a forest of stone needles and razor-edged ridges that have not yet known the softening hand of time. There are no rounded pebbles here, only shards; no rolling hills, only vertical ruptures of basalt and obsidian that pierce the atmosphere. 

Woven through this jagged wilderness is a city that seems to have grown as a secondary crust over the rock. It is a vast, sprawling settlement of hand-hewn stone and heavy timber, an urban expanse that stretches across the valley floor and climbs the surrounding spires. Despite its scale, it is built with a primitive, muscular logic. There are no machines, no humming wires. Instead, there are massive aqueducts held up by dry-stack masonry, and colossal stairs carved directly into the mountain’s face. The architecture is a feat of geometry and manual labor, an endless repetition of plazas, granaries, and tiered dwellings that seem to have been placed by a civilization with a thousand years of patience and no concept of the wheel.

The city is strangely quiet, its density deceptive. While the buildings are many and the infrastructure is grand, the spaces between them are wide and airy. It is a place of grand plazas where only a few figures might stand at once, and immense bridges that span kilometer-wide chasms, used by only a handful of travelers. 

Life here is defined by the Stillness. Because the sun never moves, the world is divided into two distinct biomes: the Lit and the Shadowed. In the Lit zones, the stone is dry and warm to the touch, home to brittle, sun-bleached lichens. But in the permanent shadows of the great towers and the deep ravines, the moisture of the world accumulates. Here, the "damp" is a constant state. Intermittent mists, born from the condensation of high-altitude clouds against the cold stone, drift through the streets like ghosts. These mists never dissipate; they simply hover, feeding a lush, vertical ecology. 

Mosses, three feet thick and vibrant emerald, coat the shaded sides of every building. Ferns with fronds as long as oars erupt from the mortar-less joints of the city walls. In these cool, dark pockets, the vegetation is so aggressive it threatens to swallow the primitive masonry, creating hanging gardens that drip a slow, rhythmic pulse of water into stone basins below.

The fauna are as quiet as the stone. Large, flightless birds with plumage the color of wet slate move through the plazas with a slow, deliberate gait. Their feet are padded, making no sound on the cobbles. In the lush, shadowed districts, translucent arboreal slugs the size of hounds slide over the mossy facades, their bioluminescence competing with the steady, unmoving daylight. There is a sense of immense biological activity, yet it produces no cacophony—only the occasional soft scrape of a claw on stone or the muffled drip of condensation.

Nothing changes. The wind does not blow because the temperature gradients are locked in place. The water in the aqueducts does not rush; it glides in a glassy, laminar flow, its surface unbroken by ripples. The inhabitants, too, seem to exist in a state of frozen equilibrium. They work with stone and fiber, their tools simple and unchanging, their movements mirroring the glacial pace of their world. A tapestry started a century ago might still be on the same loom, the weaver’s descendants continuing the work with the same tension and the same thread.

It is a world of sharp edges and soft coverings, a place where the primitive majesty of human labor is fused to a landscape that refused to erode. You can stand on a high balcony and look out over miles of stone towers, verdant moss-walls, and jagged peaks, knowing that this exact tableau—this precise arrangement of shadow and light—has not shifted by a single inch in an eternity, and never will.
