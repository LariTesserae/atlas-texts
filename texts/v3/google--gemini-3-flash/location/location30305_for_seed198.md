The world is a tiered abyss of iron and masonry, a mountain range forged by the hands of industry rather than the tectonic shifts of earth. Here, the concept of "ground" has been forgotten; there is only the substrate of the Machine. At this elevation, the air is thin, cool, and saturated with a pervasive, clinging dampness. It is not raining, precisely, but the atmosphere is in a constant state of collapse, weeping a fine, cool mist that slickens every surface.

Everything is constructed. The ravines are narrow alleys between vertical foundries; the cliffs are the sheer, windowless walls of monolithic heat-exchanges. Yet, despite the total absence of natural earth, the landscape is violently green. Vegetation has claimed the industrial corpse with a desperate, suffocating fervor. Thick, dark moss carpets the rusted walkways like velvet, muting the ring of boots on metal. Ferns with fronds the size of sails erupt from the joints of massive, weeping pipes, their roots finding purchase in the lime and mineral deposits left by centuries of leaking coolant. 

The scale is staggering. Looking out from a high catwalk, the eye travels across a valley of smog and structure that stretches for leagues. There are no gaps in this vista. The density is absolute; where one building ends, another begins, connected by a frenetic web of suspension cables, steam-tunnels, and oscillating conveyor systems. It is a packed, airless geography, a labyrinthine hive where every cubic meter of space is occupied by a gear, a vine, or a dripping vent.

Despite the monumental scale of the machinery, the world is eerily, profoundly quiet. The noise of the great works has settled into a sub-audible vibration, a low-frequency hum that one feels in the marrow rather than hears in the ear. The occasional sound that does break through is soft—the rhythmic *clack-hiss* of a distant piston, the heavy splash of water falling from a high overflow pipe into a dark cistern, the rustle of waxy leaves in the updraft. 

It is a place of frantic, silent motion. The "turbulent" nature of the landscape is not one of wind or storm, but of a ceaseless, mechanical metabolism. The entire horizon is shifting. Towers on massive, slow-moving screw-jacks rise and fall to meet changing pressure demands. Huge, automated cranes swing through the mist, rearranging modular housing blocks like a giant playing a game of solitaire. Scaffolding is built and dismantled in the span of an hour by swarms of shadowed, unseen mechanisms. The landscape is never the same twice; a path taken in the morning may be blocked by a newly-grown cooling vent by evening.

The light is a permanent, bruised twilight. The sun is a pale, diffused glow behind a thick ceiling of industrial smog and low-hanging clouds. It casts no sharp shadows, only a pervasive, monochromatic dimness that turns the copper-colored rust and the deep emerald moss into shades of charcoal and slate. 

Life, other than the relentless flora, is a rarity. There are no birds in the sky, only the occasional heavy-bodied moth fluttering around a flickering sodium lamp, or a pale, damp crustacean scuttling through the plumbing. The humans, if they remain, are hidden deep within the pressurized interiors of the great shells, leaving the exterior to the plants and the rain.

This is a world that has reached a strange, industrial equilibrium. It is weathered and ancient, the metal pitted by oxidation and smoothed by the constant flow of mineral-rich water, yet it remains hyperactive. It is a garden of iron, a forest of pistons, a place where the distinction between the "built" and the "grown" has dissolved into a single, humid, and endlessly moving complexity.
