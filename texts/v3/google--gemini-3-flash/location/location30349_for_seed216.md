Here, the sky is not above the world, but within it. 

You stand upon the **Great Weave**, an architectural continent of living fiber suspended in a warm, high-altitude void. The scale is impossible to reconcile with a terrestrial mind; the "ground" beneath you is a braided lattice of gargantuan, petrified vines, each the width of a mountain range, woven together in a complex, intentional geometry that stretches toward a horizon that never curves down, only fades into a charcoal-colored haze.

It is a world entirely **built**, yet entirely **biological**. There is no stone here, no silt, no ore. The substrate is a multi-layered crust of moss, fern, and interlocking root-systems that have been guided, knotted, and fused over eons. It feels like a city designed by a god of basketry, a planet-sized nest constructed with the meticulous, primitive grace of a weaver bird, using only the tensile strength of wood and the glue of resin. 

The **density** is unsettlingly low. Between the massive, continent-sized braided arches are gulfs of nothingness—hollows so vast that whole weather systems circulate within them. You can look through a gap in the floor and see a second layer of the Weave five hundred miles below, a dim silhouette against the dark, shimmering mists of the planet’s interior. Because the elevation is so high, the air is thin but strangely heavy with humidity, a paradoxical "wet thinness" that clings to the lungs.

**Water** does not flow in rivers; it weeps. The humidity of the warm, dim atmosphere condenses against the gargantuan stalks of the overhead arches, forming droplets the size of houses. These globes of water slide slowly down the vertical vines, trembling with surface tension, until they shatter against the lower moss-plains in a silent, slow-motion explosion of spray. Everything is perpetually damp. The air smells of crushed mint, wet peat, and the sweet, cloying scent of active decay. 

The **light** is a bruised, perpetual twilight. There is no sun, or perhaps the sun is blocked by the outer layers of the Weave. The illumination comes from the plants themselves—a dull, rhythmic pulse of bioluminescence that travels through the root-networks like a slow nervous system. The glow is a deep, oceanic teal, casting long, soft shadows that seem to have a weight of their own.

**Technology** here is a matter of bone, sinew, and sap. There are structures—dwellings like massive, hollowed-out gourds or rib-cages of bent willow—lashed together with vines that are still growing. There are no machines, only complex pulleys made of spider-silk and counterweights of compressed peat. It is a pre-industrial masterpiece, a civilization that mastered the knot before the wheel.

**Fauna** is abundant but ghosts the periphery. You see the movement of things that are half-glider, half-crustacean, drifting through the voids between the braids on gossamer wings. They make no sound. The world is defined by a profound **silence**. The atmosphere is too vast and the substrate too soft to carry the sharp cracks of a solid world. Even the falling water is muted, a low thud rather than a splash. The only noise is the occasional, tectonic *groan* of the Weave as a trillion tons of living wood shifts against the wind.

The **weirdness** manifests in the way space and biology interact. Gravity is not a constant pull toward a center, but a local property of the vines. If you walk along the side of a mile-thick stalk, "down" becomes the center of that stalk. Time, too, seems to be a biological process here. In some pockets of the Weave, plants grow so fast you can hear the cellulostic fibers screaming as they stretch; in others, the air is so thick with ancient spores that memories seem to leak out of your mind and take root in the moss around your feet. You might pass a flower that is blooming with the face of a person you knew, or find a section of the floor that is woven not from vines, but from the calcified hair of some creature that died a million years ago.

The world is **active**, but at the pace of a forest rather than a machine. You can feel the heartbeat of the ecosystem in the soles of your feet—a slow, hydraulic thrumming of sap being pumped through the continental arteries. It is a balanced, weathered place, where the erosion of wind and water has smoothed the gargantuan braids into soft, velvet-green highways. It is an old world, a settled world, a hollow world that has forgotten what it means to be solid.
