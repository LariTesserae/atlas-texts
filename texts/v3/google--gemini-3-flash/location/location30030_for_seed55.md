The world is a silent, lightless ocean that has long since claimed a planet-sized monument. There is no natural ground here; there is only the Work.

Imagine a structural expanse that stretches for thousands of miles in every direction, a submerged megacity or a world-spanning temple complex crafted entirely from hand-hewn basalt, heavy ceramics, and interlocking greenstone. Every inch of the floor is paved; every vertical rise is a staircase or a carved relief. Because the tech level is primitive, there are no sleek alloys or glowing conduits. Instead, the architecture is defined by the weight of megalithic masonry—cyclopean blocks the size of cathedrals, fitted together with such ancient precision that even after eons of immersion, the seams remain watertight.

The water is abyssal and freezing, a heavy, motionless cold that leeches the heat from the stone. It is not a violent sea. There are no waves, only the imperceptible, continental-scale shifting of deep-sea currents that move with the patience of glaciers. 

Over this total construction, the vegetation has taken hold with a slow, suffocating ferocity. In the near-total darkness, the flora does not rely on photosynthesis. Instead, it is a lush, pale jungle of chemosynthetic mosses and ropey, translucent vines that cling to the carvings. These plants are thick and rubbery, their leaves as large as sails, swaying in the slow-motion push of the water. They grow in dense, layered canopies that choke the narrow alleys between the great stone towers, creating a labyrinth of wet, fibrous green. The vegetation is so thick in places that it mimics the terrain, forming false floors of woven kelp-equivalent that can support the weight of a falling stone.

Erosion has performed a final, softening ritual on the world. The sharp edges of the primitive masonry—the corners of the ziggurats, the features of the gargantuan statues, the lips of the great basins—have all been rubbed smooth by millions of years of water flow. The world feels "finished," as if it has reached its terminal state. There is no more rubble, only the smooth, undulating curves of ancient stone beneath a coat of slime and moss.

The scale is impossible to hold in the mind at once. You could travel for a century across a single plaza and never see the end of the paving stones. The "low" elevation means there are no mountains, only the tiered plateaus of the buildings themselves, rising like flat-topped mesas above the sunken streets. 

There is a profound absence of life that moves. No fish dart through the kelp; no crustaceans scuttle over the masonry. The fauna is effectively non-existent, leaving the world to the slow, creeping metabolism of the plants. The sound is a heavy, pressurized silence, broken only by the occasional low-frequency groan of the structure settling under its own inconceivable weight—a sound that vibrates through the bones rather than hitting the ears.

It is a world of "Total Built" where the builders are gone, and their tools—simple hammers, bone chisels, and hempen ropes—have long since dissolved. What remains is a dark, frozen garden of stone and vine, a planet-sized basement filled with the quiet, slow-motion breathing of a forest that has never seen the sun. Everything is wet, everything is old, and everything is still.
