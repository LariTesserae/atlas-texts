This is the summit of the world, though the word "summit" implies a peak that no longer exists. Eons of erosion have ground the once-jagged heights into a vast, undulating plateau of ancient, saturated peat. At this elevation, the air is thin and perpetually frigid, a biting 0.5-degree chill that smells of ozone and wet iron.

The ground is not earth. There is no stone here, no sand, no mineral soil. The substrate is entirely, aggressively biological. You walk upon a mile-thick layer of compressed, prehistoric mosses and interwoven root-mats that have reached a terminal state of evolution. It is a "total vegetation" environment, but it is not a forest. It is a shivering, wet prairie of hyper-specialized flora that has assumed the roles of geography and geology alike.

Underfoot, the surface is a literal sponge. Every step draws black, freezing water to the surface, which immediately begins to steam in the low-pressure atmosphere. The "wetness" here is constant; the air is a suspension of freezing mist and heavy, low-hanging clouds that never quite break, keeping the light in a state of permanent, bruised twilight.

The scale is immense and hauntingly empty. Despite the total coverage of plant life, the landscape feels sparse. There are no thickets, no crowding canopies. Instead, the plateau is dotted with solitary, gargantuan structures that push up through the moss like the rusted hulks of sunken ships.

These are the "industrial" organisms—the source of the rhythmic, mechanical cacophony that defines the soundscape.

Every few miles, a "Piston-Lily" rises forty feet into the dim air. These are not soft flowers; they are composed of iron-hard, lignin-plated bark and hydraulic vacuoles. They operate with a violent, rhythmic motion, their central columns slamming up and down with the sound of a steam hammer, pumping nutrient-rich silt from the lightless depths of the peat-sea. They vent plumes of hot, sulfurous vapor that hiss into the cold air, creating localized pockets of turbulence. 

Nearby, "Capacitor-Vines" stretch across the moss like high-tension wires, humming with a low-frequency vibration that makes your teeth ache. They harvest the static electricity from the constant high-altitude storms, their translucent leaves flickering with captured lightning. There are no buildings, no ruins of civilization, yet the landscape sounds like a factory at the height of a shift—clanking, hissing, and thrumming with a mindless, mechanized intent.

The ecology is abundant but alien. Fauna here are not distinct from the "industry." Translucent, multi-limbed scavengers—looking like a cross between a shrimp and a glass clock—scuttle between the Piston-Lilies, lubricating the moving parts with their own secretions. Massive, slow-moving "Atmospheric Whales" drift through the mist, their bodies nothing more than gas-filled bladders of woven kelp, trailing long, conductive tendrils that spark when they touch the Capacitor-Vines.

The reality of this place is brittle. Because the ecosystem is so ancient and has reached such a strange, terminal equilibrium, the laws of physics seem to have grown tired. In the troughs between the slow-rolling hills of moss, gravity occasionally falters. You might see a pool of water hovering three feet off the ground, rotating slowly like a liquid planetoid, or find patches of the plateau where time flows in stutters—where the falling mist freezes in mid-air and then shatters like glass before dissolving back into vapor.

The dynamic of the place is one of "turbulent stability." It is chaotic, loud, and violently active, yet it never changes. A Piston-Lily may explode under its own pressure, sending shrapnel of wood and sap across the plain, only for a new one to begin twitching upward from the muck within minutes. It is a Solaris-like engine of mindless production, a cold, wet, high-altitude factory that produces nothing but its own continued, roaring existence.

You stand in the center of a vast, dim theater of biological machinery, shivering in the cold, listening to the rhythmic *thump-hiss* of a world that has forgotten how to be natural and has instead become a machine made of sap and cell-walls. The horizon is a flat, grey line where the saturated earth meets the weeping sky, broken only by the distant, lonely silhouettes of the great bio-engines, working tirelessly in the dark.
