The world here has been ground down to its final, most stable posture. There are no mountains left, only the "stumps" of a forgotten geography—low, hump-backed mounds of polished basalt and obsidian that rise like the spines of half-submerged beasts from a sea of grey silt. This is a landscape of maximum erosion, a regional basin where the wind and water have finished their work, leaving behind a flat, open expanse that stretches into a dim, perpetual twilight.

The air is a heavy, cold soup. It is not merely humid; it is saturating. Water does not just fall as rain; it weeps from the very pores of the stone. The atmosphere is a thick, condensing veil that turns distant objects into ghosts. Everywhere, there is the sound of trickling, splashing, and the deep, resonant *thrum* of water moving through ancient, subterranean channels that sit just inches below the surface. 

But the dominant sound is a physical weight: a deafening, low-frequency roar. It is the product of a massive, atmospheric engine—the wind rushing through the "Organ Pipes," thousands of naturally hollowed-out mineral spires that are all that remain of a vanished mountain range. These spires, riddled with holes by eons of acidic mist, turn the breeze into a discordant, bone-shaking hum that never ceases. It is a sound you feel in your teeth, a constant acoustic pressure that defines the boundaries of the world.

There is no green here. The concept of a leaf or a stem is alien to this mineral reality. Instead, the ground is draped in "Slime-Veils"—vast, translucent sheets of non-cellular biology that pulse with a faint, internal phosphorescence. These mats are neither plant nor fungus; they are colonial organisms that harvest the mineral runoff from the weeping stones. They are slick, rubbery to the touch, and smell faintly of ozone and wet copper. 

Despite the lack of flora, the basin is teeming with motion. Great herds of "Shell-Backs"—six-legged, soft-bodied creatures the size of small houses—lumber across the silt flats. They possess no eyes, navigating instead by the deafening vibrations of the Organ Pipes, their carapaces vibrating in sympathy with the world’s roar. They graze on the Slime-Veils, leaving behind wide, glistening trails in the grey mud. Smaller, spindly scavengers dart between their legs, their movements frantic and jerky in the dim light.

Human presence is a whisper against this tectonic scale. Scattered across the basin are the "Hollows"—primitive dwellings carved directly into the sides of the larger basalt mounds. There is no wood for fire, no ore for smelting. The inhabitants use tools of sharpened bone and polished glass, lashed together with strips of dried Slime-Veil. Their architecture is one of necessity and silence: small, squat structures built to deflect the wind and dampen the roar. They are hunters and gatherers of the mineral-mats, their lives dictated by the slow, seasonal migration of the Shell-Backs. 

A walk through this landscape is a lesson in patience and endurance. The ground is a patchwork of shallow, cold pools and slippery mineral shelves. The dim light makes distances hard to judge; a cluster of Organ Pipes might be a mile away or five, their silhouettes flickering in the shifting mist. Everything is in motion—the slow crawl of the herds, the weeping of the rocks, the constant vibration of the air. It is a world that has reached its end and found, in that stillness, a strange and thunderous vitality. There is a profound sense of peace in the erosion, as if the planet itself has finally exhaled and settled into its true, final shape.
