The sky does not exist here; there is only the Ceiling, a weight of absolute, lightless black that feels as though it sits mere inches above the highest spires. We are at a great altitude, perched on the jagged, embryonic teeth of a mountain range that is still being born. The geology is raw, unweathered, and violent. There are no rounded pebbles or smooth riverbeds; every edge is a razor, every surface a fresh fracture of obsidian and dark, conductive ore. The peaks have not yet learned how to crumble.

It is profoundly, piercingly cold—a temperature that turns breath into immediate frost, yet the world is impossibly wet. This is not the wetness of rain, but of a constant, aggressive condensation. The air is a saturated soup of supercooled vapor that clings to every surface, coating the raw mineral edges in a thick, gelatinous sheath of "heavy water" that refuses to freeze solid despite the temperature. It weeps from the stone in slow, viscous tears, dripping into bottomless chasms with a heavy, rhythmic *thud*.

Amidst this jagged, frozen nursery sits a city that defies every law of classical architecture. It is a dense, vertical sprawl of hyper-advanced infrastructure, woven directly into the unformed rock. The buildings are not constructed; they seem to have been extruded or precipitated into existence. They are composed of a matte-black material that absorbs what little stray energy exists, forming impossible geometries—towers that narrow to a single atom’s width, bridges held in place by shimmering gravitational tethers rather than bolts or beams. There are no lights. The city operates in a spectrum invisible to the eye, a silent, bustling hive of post-material engineering.

The density is claustrophobic. The spires are packed so tightly that the "streets" are merely narrow, vertical capillaries, slick with the omnipresent moisture. There is no horizontal plane; to move is to ascend or descend through a forest of cold, wet metal and stone.

And it is loud. The sound is a monolithic, unchanging roar—the sound of a planetary-scale engine idling at a frequency that vibrates the marrow. It is the sound of the tech, the hum of the mountains being pulled upward by artificial tectonics, and the relentless dripping of the heavy water. It is a sound so loud it eventually becomes a form of silence, a pressure against the eardrums that never relents.

There is no green here. Not a blade of moss, not a single fungal spore. The world is strictly mineral and synthetic. Yet, the environment is teeming. The fauna is overwhelming, a biomass that seems to outweigh the city itself. These are not creatures of flesh and bone, but entities of clicking chitin, translucent membranes, and shivering filaments. They are everywhere—clinging to the undersides of the gravity-bridges, swarming in the dark capillaries of the city, carpeting the raw rock faces. They move with agonizing slowness, their lifespans perhaps measured in centuries, their metabolisms tuned to the glacial pace of the mountains’ growth. They do not hunt so much as they absorb; they are scavengers of vibration and heat-leaks from the city’s core. They resemble clusters of glass needles or slow-pulsing lungs, stacked one on top of the other in a shivering, wet carpet of life that covers every available inch of the "built" world.

Nothing changes quickly. A single drip of water may take minutes to fall. A creature may take a day to lift a single limb. The city’s lights never flicker because they never shine. It is a place of static, high-energy tension—a wet, dark, crowded cathedral of emerging stone and impossible machines, humming a deafening note into the void, while a billion slow-moving things huddle together for a warmth that never comes.
