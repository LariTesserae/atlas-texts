This is the Plateau of the Weeping Pelt, a continental expanse that sits so high in the atmosphere that the air is thin but perpetually saturated. Here, the concept of "geology" has been surrendered. The ancient stone foundations of the world have long since been ground down by eons of slow, hydraulic pressure, replaced entirely by a miles-deep mantle of living and semi-decayed vegetable matter. You are standing not on soil, but on the skin of a world-sized organism.

The terrain is a series of gargantuan, soft-edged undulations—vast, rolling hills of moss and liverwort that stretch for thousands of miles. Because the erosion is so advanced, there are no jagged peaks or sharp ravines. The landscape has settled into a "final form," a rhythmic, heaving plain of green and bruised purple. 

The air is warm, thick, and dim. The sun is a pale, diffused glow behind a permanent ceiling of low-hanging, moisture-heavy clouds that move with agonizing slowness. Water does not fall as rain so much as it simply exists as part of the air. It condenses on every surface, forming heavy, fat beads that cling to the translucent hairs of the giant mosses before sliding down into the dark, spongy depths of the substrate. The world is constantly weeping. Every indentation in the landscape is a pool of black, tea-colored water, filtered through a billion layers of peat.

The vegetation is total. There is no bare earth, no exposed rock. The "trees" here are not woody, but are instead colossal, pressurized stalks of succulent tissue, resembling ribbed columns of jade or obsidian. They do not have leaves; they have vast, drooping veils of capillary-rich lace that pull moisture directly from the mist. These pillars are spaced widely but are connected by a dense, multi-layered carpet of ferns and broad, waxy succulents that groan softly underfoot, shifting their weight like a mattress.

The scale is so vast that the plateau generates its own internal weather systems. You can stand on a high ridge of compressed peat and look out over a horizon that seems to curve upward, an endless sea of undulating green fluff, punctuated by the slow-moving shadows of local cloud-drifts.

In the distance, there are "ruins," though the term is imprecise. They are scattered, singular monoliths—towers of a calcified, bone-like material that seems to have grown out of the ground rather than being built upon it. These structures are ancient, their surfaces smoothed by millions of years of mist-erosion until they resemble melted wax. They are empty, windowless, and silent, serving perhaps as anchors for the shifting sea of vegetation or as vents for the deep, warm gases trapped beneath the peat. There is no sign of tools, no metal, no artifice—only the suggestion of a biological intent that has long since passed into dormancy.

The fauna is abundant but subterranean or slow-moving. Beneath the waxy leaves, things are stirring. You hear the rhythmic, wet pulse of giant gastropods—creatures the size of boulders that slide through the mossy tunnels with a sound like a heavy boot pulling out of deep mud. Soft-bodied, many-limbed grazers with translucent skin drift through the dim light, their movements languid and deliberate, as if they are swimming through the thick air.

The sound of the world is a low-frequency hum, a combination of the wind passing over the lacework canopies and the internal gurgle of water moving through millions of vascular channels. It is a quiet place, muffled by the sheer density of the living mass. Any sharp noise is swallowed instantly by the moss.

Time here feels stalled. The dynamic is one of patient, glacial cycles. A single leaf may take a decade to unfurl; a pool may take a century to fill and drain. The landscape is a closed loop of birth, saturation, and slow decay, a warm, dim nursery that has forgotten the sky and the stone, settling into a permanent, wet twilight at the top of the world.
