The air at this altitude is thin, sharp, and blindingly bright. Here, the sun does not merely shine; it dominates, a scouring white weight that reflects off every surface with surgical intensity. Despite this brilliance, the air remains stubbornly cool, holding a chill that smells of crushed mint and wet iron.

The terrain is a nightmare of adolescence. These are young mountains, jagged and violent, their ridges as thin and sharp as the blades of a flint knife. There has not been enough time for wind or water to soften them. The peaks rise in frantic, vertical lunges, creating a landscape of deep, narrow clefts and sudden, sheer drop-offs that overlook a valley floor miles wide. 

Water exists here primarily as a memory or a secret. It does not flow in rivers; instead, it manifests as a persistent, heavy dampness that clings to the undersides of things. Every morning, the industrial husks that dot the landscape are slick with thick beads of condensation—a cold, metallic sweat. Pockets of dark, standing water hide in the shadows of the jagged rocks, their surfaces so still they look like polished obsidian.

In this high-contrast world of white light and black shadow, the vegetation is unnervingly lush. Thick, fleshy succulents with leaves the size of shields carpet the vertical slopes. They are a deep, bruised purple and a vibrant, waxy emerald, their surfaces glistening with the ambient moisture. Long, ropey vines, thick as an arm and stiff to the touch, bridge the gaps between the rock spires. They do not sway. There is no wind here. The plants grow with a slow, crushing deliberation, their roots finding purchase in the microscopic cracks of the unweathered stone, physically prying the mountains apart over decades of silent effort.

Scattered across this verdant, jagged expanse are the marks of a heavy, silent industry. These are not ruins, but they are not inhabited. Huge, cast-iron pistons, half-buried in the moss, cycle once every hour with a vibration that is felt in the teeth rather than heard in the ears. Massive copper cables, green with oxidation, are strung across the valleys, tensioned so high they hum at a frequency just below the threshold of human hearing. Occasionally, a small, windowless blockhouse of reinforced concrete sits perched on a knife-edge ridge, its surface smooth and defiant against the chaotic geometry of the rock.

There is a profound, pressurized silence. No birds call; no insects drone. The only sound is the occasional, tectonic *clack* of a cooling stone or the rhythmic, muffled thud of a distant machine completing a cycle. The fauna is nearly non-existent, save for the occasional sight of a pale, leathery creature that moves with the same agonizing slowness as the plants, drifting across the rock faces like a stray cloud.

The weirdness of the place lies in its stillness. It feels like a massive, automated clockwork mechanism that has been overgrown by a jungle before it could finish its task. The machines—massive, rusted, and electrified—seem to be waiting for a signal that has been traveling through the void for a thousand years. The light is so bright it threatens to bleach the world into two dimensions, yet the shadows remain cold and damp, sheltering plants that seem to feed on the hum of the buried engines. Everything is high-definition, motionless, and heavy with the patient tension of a spring that has been wound too tight and left in a silent room.
