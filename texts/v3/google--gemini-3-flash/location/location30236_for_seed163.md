At this altitude, the air is thin but heavy with a suspension of liquid light. The world is a vast, high-altitude shelf, an emerald plateau that seems to have been birthed only moments ago in geologic time. There are no valleys carved by eons of runoff, no jagged peaks worn smooth by wind. The terrain is a series of massive, embryonic undulations—smooth, rounded mounds of raw, unweathered matter that suggest a planet still in the process of cooling or congealing. 

Yet, this raw geology is entirely encased in life. 

There is no stone to be seen. The substrate of the world is a singular, continuous organism: a moss-flesh so dense and deep that it functions as both soil and bedrock. It is a vibrant, neon-lit green that seems to drink the blindingly bright sunlight. The vegetation is not merely present; it is the architecture of the landscape. It grows in thick, rubbery sheets and towering, spongy stalks that reach hundreds of feet into the chilled air, yet these features are spaced far apart. The scale is open and lonely; one can look for miles across the weeping mounds and see only three or four of these giant botanical sentinels, separated by wide, glistening plains of emerald velvet.

The moisture is absolute. Water does not behave as it does in lower, older worlds. It does not flow in rivers; it exudes. The entire landscape sweats. Droplets the size of citrus fruits cling to the underside of every leaf, tension-heavy and shimmering, before falling with rhythmic slowness into the moss below. The atmosphere is a permanent state of post-rain clarity, a cold, condensing mist that turns the sun’s rays into solid bars of white gold. Everything is slick, silver-edged, and dripping.

Dominating this pristine, embryonic wild are the machines. 

They are scattered across the plateau like abandoned toys of a giant, industrial god. These are not delicate electronics, but heavy, iron-clad monuments of industry—massive, soot-stained pumping stations and slow-turning gears the size of city blocks. They are embedded directly into the green flesh of the world, their foundations disappearing into the moss. They hum with a terrifying, rhythmic power. 

The sound is the defining characteristic of the place. It is a deafening, oceanic roar that never wavers. It is the composite sound of a thousand massive waterfalls spilling from the lips of the plateau into the unseen abyss below, layered with the deep, tectonic thrumming of the industrial engines. The noise is so loud it becomes a physical pressure, a vibration in the marrow of the bone, yet it is strangely steady. It does not suggest chaos, but a massive, slow-motion production—the planet-scale extraction of water or the steady churning of the atmosphere itself.

Despite the industrial roar, the pace of the world is agonizingly slow. The great gears of the stations may take a full day to complete a single rotation. The water falls in long, graceful ribbons that seem to defy gravity in the thin air. There is no fauna to break the stillness—no birds to brave the deafening hum, no insects in the cold, wet air. It is a lonely, bright, and thunderous nursery. 

You stand on a ridge of un-eroded moss, looking out across a five-mile span of weeping green. In the distance, a single iron tower vents a vertical plume of white steam that stands perfectly straight in the windless, freezing sky. The sun is a blinding white disc, turning the wet world into a hall of mirrors. Everything is emerging, everything is heavy, and everything is screaming with the sound of its own creation.
