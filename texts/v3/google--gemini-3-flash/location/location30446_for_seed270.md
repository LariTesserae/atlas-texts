This is the interior of the Great Casing, a space no larger than a steamer trunk, wedged between two immobile slabs of tectonic slate. It is a world of absolute dryness, a desiccated pocket where the concept of a drop of liquid is biologically impossible. The floor is not soil, but a deep, fine silt composed of pulverized mica and iron filings—the result of eons of slow, grinding erosion that has smoothed every internal surface into a dull, matte finish. There are no hills here, only the slight, undulating dunes created by the vibration of the machinery below.

The air is cool and tastes of ozone and static. It is perfectly clear, illuminated by a steady, ambient glow that bleeds through the microscopic fissures in the ceiling slabs—a "normal" daylight that feels strangely clinical in this subterranean hollow. 

Life here is not green. There is a total absence of chlorophyll or cellulose. Instead, the landscape is defined by the overwhelming presence of the *Carapace-Swarm*. They are everywhere. They are the topography. Small, beetle-like organisms with shells the color of tarnished copper and burnished lead crawl over one another in layers three or four deep. They do not compete for space so much as they exist as a fluid mass, a living carpet that undulates across the mineral flats. Their numbers are so vast that the sound of their movement—the dry clicking of billions of chitinous legs against the mica dust—creates a constant, percussive roar, like the sound of heavy rain on a tin roof, despite the lack of weather.

Embedded in this shifting sea of carapaces are the ruins of a miniature industrial age. These are not grand cities, but scattered, functional skeletons: brass piston-rods the size of sewing needles that still pump rhythmically into the dust; tiny, rusted flywheels that spin with a low, mechanical hum; and networks of copper piping that vent puffs of warm, dry air from deeper chambers. These structures are ancient, their edges rounded by the constant scouring of the silt and the passage of the Swarm. The technology is not "smart"; it is mechanical, heavy, and persistent. A gear might take a century to complete a single rotation, yet it continues, driven by some hidden, subterranean clockwork.

The ecology is a closed loop of kinetic energy. The Swarm does not eat plants; they huddle around the vibrating industrial vents, drawing warmth and vibration from the dying machines. They are scavengers of motion. When a machine part finally seizes, the Swarm descends upon it, their tiny mandibles vibrating at frequencies high enough to shave off microscopic flakes of metal, which they ingest to reinforce their shells. 

Everything is in motion. The landscape is a dynamic, roiling crust of activity. You cannot look at a single spot for more than a second without seeing a change—a new layer of beetles surfacing, a rhythmic puff of dust from a buried exhaust port, the slow, grinding tilt of a copper strut settling further into the silt. It is a dense, crowded, and incredibly loud miniature universe, polished by time into a final, stable state of frantic, mechanical survival. There is no growth here, only the endless, busy maintenance of what remains.
