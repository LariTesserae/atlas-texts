The world is a drowned cathedral of stone, a singular, manufactured continent sinking forever into a cold, sunless sea. There is no sand here, no silt, and no soil. Every inch of the terrain—the soaring plateaus, the spiraling trenches, and the vast, flat plains—is constructed. It is a landscape of interlocking megaliths, carved from a substance harder than granite and smoother than glass, though eons of rushing water have rounded every sharp edge into a nub.

The water is heavy and hyper-saline, a pressurized medium that feels more like a gel than a liquid. It is dim, lit only by a faint, ghostly luminescence that seems to bleed out of the very stones of the world. Because the elevation is high—a plateau perched atop the spine of a submerged world—the water does not sit still. It roars in silent, invisible rivers. These are the Great Tides: massive, turbulent currents that whip through the gaps between the artificial mountains. They carry no heat, only the abrasive force of ancient erosion.

There is no green. Not a single blade of seagrass or speck of algae clings to the masonry. The world is a monochrome study in greys, deep indigos, and the bone-white of weathered mineral. In place of plants, the landscape is populated by "The Looming." These are massive, non-technological constructs—perhaps once pillars or supports—that have been worn down into smooth, bulbous shapes like giant, petrified mushrooms, miles apart from one another. 

Life here is frantic and blind. In the spaces between the monumental ruins, the fauna is abundant but scattered. There are "shiver-wraiths"—translucent, ribbon-like creatures the size of ships that ride the violent currents, their bodies snapping like whips in the turbulence. They feed not on sunlight or plants, but on the kinetic energy of the water, vibrating with a low, sub-sonic hum that can be felt in the marrow of one’s bones. Smaller, hard-shelled scavengers scuttle across the etched patterns of the floor, their many legs clicking against the ancient masonry in a rhythm that is lost to the roar of the tides.

The rules of this place are defined by weight and flow. Because the entire world is a "built" environment, there are no caves, only "vaults." There are no hills, only "mounds of debris." The scale is so vast that one can travel for days across a single, perfectly flat plaza, watching the distant silhouettes of megalithic towers dance in the refraction of the churning water. 

It is a lonely, busy place. The sound is a constant, muffled thrum—the voice of a million tons of water being forced through the narrow apertures of a dead civilization’s architecture. The air—if there were air—would be frozen. Here, the water saps heat with a predatory efficiency. Everything is in motion; the currents are so strong they occasionally pick up boulders of ancient masonry and toss them like pebbles, grinding the world down further, millimeter by century.

The weirdness lies in the intent. Every curve of the landscape feels deliberate, as if the entire region was once a single, gargantuan machine or a piece of jewelry for a god, now stripped of its purpose and left to drown. There are no tools here, no wires, no glowing screens—only the raw, physical presence of a shaped world enduring the infinite violence of the sea. It is a stable apocalypse; the end of the world happened so long ago that the ruins have become the new geology. The turbulence is the only thing that changes, a chaotic, thrashing energy that ensures nothing—not even the most abundant life—ever truly finds rest.
