The ground is not earth, but a petrified crust of ancient, interlocking root-systems that have hardened into a substance indistinguishable from granite. This is a world where the botanical has become the geological. The landscape is a series of staggering, vertical plateaus—gigantic, calcified stumps the size of small continents—separated by mile-deep chasms of empty, shimmering air. Here, "total vegetation" does not mean a dense forest, but rather that the entire planetary shell is a biological construct, weathered by aeons into a stable, eroded ruin of xylem and cellulose.

It is a hot, high place. The air is thin but heavy with the scent of fermented nectar and scorched wood. Because the scale is so vast, these wooden plateaus generate their own localized weather; a storm might brew in the shadow of a single, sun-facing ridge, dumping brief, violent torrents of water into the deep crevices before the heat turns it back into a clinging, humid mist. There are no oceans, only "riverine veins"—troughs in the bark-stone where condensation collects and flows in narrow, silver ribbons toward the dark abysses below.

The "sparse" nature of the world is felt in the terrifying distances between the landmasses. You can stand on the edge of a jagged, leafy precipice and look out across fifty miles of nothingness to see the next green-crowned pillar rising through the haze. These gaps are not empty, however; they are filled with the sound.

The noise is a physical weight. It is a deafening, multi-layered roar produced by a fauna population that has reached a terminal saturation point. Trillions of iridescent, chitinous wings beat against the hot air; colossal, soft-bodied sky-polyps bellow to one another across the voids; and burrowing arthropods the size of ships grind their mandibles against the heartwood deep within the plateaus. The sound never stops. It is a vibrating, screaming hum that shakes the marrow in one’s bones, a constant reminder that life here is not just present, but aggressive and total.

Dotted along the weathered ridges are the faint marks of a primitive presence. These are not cities, but "scabs" on the landscape—small, precarious habitations woven from living vines and reinforced with the sun-dried husks of dead insects. The builders have no metal. Their tools are scrapers made of sharpened obsidian-leaf and hammers of heavy, fossilized seed-pods. Long, swaying bridges of braided fiber span the smaller cracks in the bark, looking like spiderwebs against the gargantuan scale of the wooden cliffs. There is an occasional monolith—a single, smooth pillar of white wood carved with spiraling grooves to catch rainwater—standing as the only sign of a coordinated effort.

The movement of the world is slow but relentless. You can see the dynamic nature of the ecosystem in the "creep" of the vegetation. Even though the plateaus are established and eroded, the living layer on top is in constant flux. A massive, succulent flower might bloom over the course of a week, swelling to the size of a cathedral, only to be devoured in hours by a swarm of shrieking, neon-colored avians. The heat creates shimmering thermals that lift debris—miles of gossamer silk, drifting pollen-clouds, and the shed skins of sky-serpents—up into the light, where they circulate like slow-motion snow.

Everything is strange, yet perfectly balanced. The water is scarce enough to be precious but frequent enough to sustain the riotous life. The tech is primitive because the environment provides everything and requires nothing that fire or iron could improve. You are a small, quiet speck in a roaring, vertical garden that spans the horizon, standing on the back of an ancient, wooden god that has long since forgotten it was ever a tree. The light of the sun, filtered through the perpetual haze of spores and moisture, turns the entire atmosphere into a glowing, golden amber, casting long, vibrating shadows over a world that is far too loud and far too large to ever truly know.
