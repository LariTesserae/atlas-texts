Below the crust of the world lies the Sub-Basin, a regional expanse of industrial geometry that has never seen a sun. Here, the concept of "nature" has been entirely replaced by "infrastructure." There is no stone, no silt, no loam; the terrain is a staggered, mile-wide valley of corrugated alloy plates, massive structural beams, and vast, interlocking cooling fins that stretch toward a ceiling lost in the dark.

The air is bitterly cold, a sharp 0.3 chill that bites at the lungs. Because the temperature is so low and the humidity so high, the atmosphere is in a constant state of collapse. A fine, pervasive mist clings to everything, and the great metal bulkheads "weep." Rivulets of frigid condensation crawl down the vertical faces of the machines like slow-motion veins, pooling in the depressions of the floor.

This has created a strange, synthetic marshland. In the low-lying areas of the basin, the "water" — a mixture of pure condensation, escaped lubricants, and chemical coolants — sits several inches deep over diamond-tread plating. It does not flow so much as it vibrates, rippling in sync with the subterranean thrum of the world.

The erosion here is young and aggressive. The metal surfaces are not yet rusted through, but they are beginning to pit and flower with the white crust of oxidation. The edges of the stairs and the lips of the massive drainage pipes remain razor-sharp, recently forged or perhaps self-assembled by some deep-layer manufacturing process that has only just finished its work. There is a raw, unfinished quality to the landscape, as if the basin were an engine started before its housing was complete.

Vegetation is a lonely presence. It consists primarily of "copper-moss" — thin, fibrous filaments of conductive wire and bio-synthetic lichen that cling to the warmest parts of the machinery. These pale, wiry patches glow with a faint, sickly phosphorescence, feeding on the stray electromagnetic currents of the pipes. They don't wave in a breeze; they shiver.

The scale is immense. From a high vantage point on a maintenance gantry, the basin looks like a city built for giants, but a city without inhabitants. There are no houses, only primary functions. Miles of pipe-forests give way to vast, open squares of humming transformers. The density is low; these monumental structures are separated by wide, echoing voids of dark air and flooded plazas.

The sound is a physical weight. It is not a single noise, but a polyphonic roar of industry. The rhythmic *thud-hiss* of massive pneumatic pistons miles away provides a heartbeat, while the closer environment is filled with the frantic, high-pitched whine of turbines and the constant, wet *drip-clack* of water hitting hollow metal. It is a loud, busy place, perpetually active. Things are moving: great overhead cranes glide silently along tracks in the ceiling, their red warning lights cutting through the dim, grey fog; pistons cycle; valves release bursts of steam that vanish into the cold.

Fauna is nearly non-existent, though occasionally, one might see a "Scourer" — a small, multi-legged maintenance drone with a chassis of dull brass, scurrying across a vertical wall to tighten a bolt or clear a drain. They are skittish and mechanical, the only predators in an ecology of maintenance.

It is a world of hard angles and soft mists, a freezing, rhythmic womb of steel. There is a sense of immense purpose here, but no witness to it. The lights are dim, reduced to the amber glow of status lamps and the occasional flash of a short-circuit, casting long, jumping shadows across a landscape that is forever damp, forever cold, and forever working.
