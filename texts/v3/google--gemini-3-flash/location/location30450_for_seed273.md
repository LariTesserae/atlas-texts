This is the Verticality, a soaring, manufactured range where the concept of "ground" has been forgotten for centuries. It is an environment of extreme height, a jagged geography of rusted iron, pitted bronze, and molded ceramic, reaching so far upward that the air at the summits is thin and shimmering with solar heat. There is no natural substrate here; every cliff-face is a vertical wall of riveted plates, every cavern is a hollowed-out engine room, and every plateau is a sprawling, suspended deck.

The climate is one of heavy, golden warmth. The light is aggressive and bright, reflecting off millions of metallic surfaces to create a pervasive, shadowless glare. Despite the heat, the world is perpetually damp. It is not a place of lakes or seas, but of "sweating" infrastructure. Huge, slow-churning industrial condensers draw moisture from the rising thermals, coating the metal walls in a permanent sheen of condensation. Water beads on every rivet, trickling down in rhythmic, intermittent drips that feed the explosion of life clinging to the structure.

Vegetation is relentless and opportunistic. It is a lush, vertical jungle. Thick, waxy vines—green as emeralds and heavy with moisture—wrap themselves around massive, cooling pipes, their root systems prying into the seams of the metalwork. Great hanging ferns drape from the undersides of gantries, catching the mist. Moss, thick and soft as a carpet, covers the rusted walkways, muffling the footsteps of anything that moves. In the sun-drenched upper reaches, flowering epiphytes burst from the ventilation grates, their blooms smelling of fermented sugar and machine oil.

The technology here is heavy and industrial, though it feels ancient and settled. It is a world of massive, slow-moving pistons the size of cathedrals, and gears that turn with such glacial deliberation they seem like part of the landscape’s tectonic plate system. The machines are not sleek; they are blackened by soot, caked in mineral deposits from the water, and hum with a deep, vibrating power. The sound is a constant, loud layering: the rhythmic *thrum-thrum* of deep-core engines, the high-pitched hiss of escaping steam, and the shrieking calls of abundant fauna.

Life teems in the crevices. Metallic-winged insects, the size of a hand, drone between the flowering pipes. Large, opportunistic birds with hooked talons nest in the dormant fan-shrouds of giant air-scrubbers. There are climbing mammals with multi-jointed limbs that navigate the vertical lattice with terrifying speed, their hides camouflaged to match the mottled patterns of rust and lichen. The ecology has reached a state of balance with the machine; the animals use the heat of the engines to incubate eggs, and the plants provide the filtration that keeps the air breathable in the lower, denser sectors.

Everything here happens on a scale that dwarfs the individual. Looking out from a catwalk, one sees not a horizon of trees, but a valley of shimmering towers and suspended refineries, all interconnected by a web of frayed cables and bridges. The erosion is visible not in worn stone, but in the "petrifaction" of the build. The iron has oxidized into deep oranges and purples, and the copper has turned a chalky sea-foam green. The structure is so old and so massive that it has its own version of geology—sections of the city have slumped under their own weight, creating new "slopes" and "canyons" within the artificial mountain.

The world is characterized by a strange, patient slowness. The great cranes may take a decade to complete a single rotation. The structural shifts—the settling of the massive metal plates—occur over generations. It is a dense, crowded, loud, and vibrant world, trapped in a permanent state of high-noon humidity, where the distinction between the machine and the forest has finally, completely dissolved.
