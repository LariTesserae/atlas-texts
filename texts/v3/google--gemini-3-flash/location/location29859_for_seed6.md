This is the Interior of the Gantry-Cistern, a high-altitude pressure-vessel suspended between two jagged, unweathered peaks. It is a cathedral of warm, stagnant industrial fluid, a hamlet-sized chamber where the concept of "dryness" has been engineered out of existence.

The environment is entirely synthetic. There is no sand, no silt, and no stone that was not poured into a mold. The floor is a grid of heavy, polished brass plates, their edges still sharp and lethal, showing none of the rounding one expects from a submerged world. Huge, hexagonal copper pillars rise from the floor, etched with fresh, deep-grooved serial numbers and cooling fins that have not yet gathered a single speck of grit. This is a young place, a machine recently switched on and filled, its surfaces still smelling faintly of machine oil and hot solder beneath the weight of the water.

The water here is not like the sea; it is a heavy, viscous medium, tea-warm and saturated with minerals. It presses against the skin with a comforting, womb-like weight. Because of the elevation—perched thousands of feet above the clouds—the light that enters through the thick, reinforced glass ceiling is crystalline and direct, slicing through the golden liquid in solid, unmoving beams.

The density of the space is absolute. There is no "open water." Every cubic meter is occupied. If it is not a bundle of insulated copper cabling or a slow-turning turbine blade, it is the vegetation. The plants here are engineered marvels of lush, terrifying efficiency. They are "Chloroplast-Engines"—great, broad-leafed ferns with translucent, emerald fronds that wrap themselves tightly around the hot industrial piping. They don't sway so much as they pulse, their root systems weaving into the gaps between the floor-plates, drawing warmth directly from the humming machinery. The greenery is so thick it creates a green twilight in the corners, a vertical jungle where the vines are indistinguishable from the hydraulic hoses.

There is a profound, heavy silence. The water muffles everything. The only sounds are the occasional, deep-register *thrum* of a massive piston moving somewhere beneath the floor—a sound felt in the chest more than heard in the ears—and the slow, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* of metal expanding under the heat. There are no fish, no insects, no darting shadows. The ecology is purely vegetable and mechanical, a closed-loop system of growth and generation.

Movement in the Gantry-Cistern is glacial. The great brass gears, ten feet across and notched with razor-sharp teeth, turn so slowly that their rotation is invisible to the casual eye. You might return after an hour to find a gear has moved only a single tooth. The plants grow with a similar, patient intent, their tendrils creeping over the rivets of the walls in a slow-motion conquest of the metal. 

The air—or rather, the fluid—is rich and sweet, tasting of ozone and nectar. Above, through the glass, one can see the thin, freezing atmosphere of the mountain peaks and the jagged, un-eroded teeth of the range, but inside, the world is a hot, crowded greenhouse of brass and chlorophyll. It is a place of total construction, a high-pressure garden where the machines provide the heat, the water provides the life, and the silence provides the law. The sharp edges of the new world are being slowly, very slowly, upholstered in an unending tide of green.
