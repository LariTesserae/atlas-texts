The horizon does not curve so much as it recedes into a shimmering, heat-distorted line where the bone-white ground meets a pale, bleached sky. This is the Floor—a continental expanse of deliberate construction, yet one devoid of any mechanism, wire, or gear. Every square inch of the landscape has been laid down, secreted, or grown into a vast tessellation of calcified plates, each the size of a city block.

The plates are raw and sharp-edged. There is no sand here, no dust, and no soil. The world is too young for the indignity of grit; the process of erosion has barely begun its work on these massive, extruded surfaces. The material of the Floor feels like a hybrid of unglazed porcelain and sun-bleached heartwood—cool to the touch in the shadows, but now, under the vertical weight of a 2.7-intensity sun, it radiates a dry, punishing heat.

Everything here is a product of architecture, yet it feels entirely biological. The ridges that rise from the flatlands are not mountains, but monumental buttresses, miles long and only a few hundred feet high, resembling the vaulted ribs of a titanic, ground-hugging organism. They follow a geometry that is too regular to be accidental but too organic to be industrial. 

Water exists as a secondary, seasonal ghost within this stone-flesh grid. It does not rain often, but the atmosphere is heavy with a stagnant, humid weight. In the deep seams between the plates, where the shadows linger, condensation gathers in thick, oily beads. Every few miles, these seams widen into shallow, perfectly circular basins—riverine pockets where warm, mineral-rich water pulses slowly, driven by some deep, subterranean rhythm. These are not springs; they are circulatory vents. The water is brackish and still, reflecting the sun with a metallic glare.

Life is a lonely, scattered presence. There are no animals to skitter across the plates or birds to divide the air. The silence is profound, broken only by the occasional, tectonic *clack* of a plate expanding in the heat or the faint, wet hiss of steam escaping a distant seam. 

The vegetation is sparse and alien to the concept of soft earth. Small, waxy succulents with the texture of cured leather sprout directly from the hairline fractures in the Floor. They do not have roots so much as they have anchors, fusing their cellular structure to the built substrate. They grow in long, thin lines, tracing the invisible veins of moisture beneath the surface. Occasionally, one might find a "forest"—a cluster of three or four pillar-like stalks, thirty feet tall, translucent and hollow, vibrating slightly as they pull humidity from the baking air.

The scale of this place creates its own internal physics. Because the land is so flat and the structures so vast, the weather operates in predictable, rhythmic cycles. In the mid-afternoon, the heat reaches a breaking point, and the moisture in the seams flash-evaporates, creating a low-lying, localized fog that hugs the ground but never rises more than ten feet. For an hour, the world becomes a white-on-white labyrinth of mist and heat, where the only landmarks are the tops of the massive rib-ridges.

There is a sense of immense potential energy here, an ecosystem that is "active" but not "busy." The world is breathing, but its breaths are measured in hours, and its growth is measured in centuries. It is a total environment, a planet-sized cradle built from the substance of whatever came before, now waiting for the first grain of dust to be worn from its pristine, manufactured skin. It is a cathedral with no worshippers, a machine with no parts, a body with no soul—only the sun, the stone, and the slow, rhythmic pulse of the warm, hidden water.
