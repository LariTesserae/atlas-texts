The air here is a thick, velvet weight, pressing against the skin with the humid insistence of a tropical greenhouse at midnight. There is no sun, no moon, no stars. The darkness is absolute, a 0.0 baseline that swallows depth and distance, yet the environment feels crowded, teeming, and wet.

The ground is young—violently young. It consists of jagged, unweathered shelves of obsidian-slick rock and sharp-edged silicate plates that seem to have buckled upward from the depths only hours ago. There is no soil, only the grit of pulverized glass and the constant, rhythmic weeping of the stone. Water does not fall as rain; it simply manifests on every surface, sweating out of the pores of the rock and condensing from the warm, stagnant air. It pools in shallow, razor-lined basins, creating a vast, interconnected marsh of tepid, mineral-heavy liquid that tastes of salt and copper.

In this lightless expanse, scale is a deceptive thing. The horizon feels miles away, an open regional basin of flat, jagged lowlands, yet the space is packed with a claustrophobic density of presences. Objects and entities occupy the same coordinates without colliding. You might step toward a looming industrial shape only to find yourself passing through a layer of reality that smells of ozone and ancient kelp, while the shape remains exactly as far away as it was before.

The flora is minimal—stiff, translucent stalks of glass-fiber reeds that grow in the cracks of the obsidian, and patches of rubbery, liver-colored moss that pulse slowly underfoot. But the fauna is overwhelming. The darkness is alive with the sound of soft, wet movement. Thousands of unseen creatures, perhaps blind or sensing the world through heat, glide through the shallow waters and skitter over the sharp ridges. Their sounds are muted: a thousand tiny clicks, the slithering of bellies over wet glass, and the occasional low, resonant vibration of something much larger breathing in the distance.

Rising out of the marsh at irregular intervals are the machines. They are massive, industrial, and seemingly abandoned, though they thrum with a latent, subterranean power. Great rusted turbines, half-submerged in the mineral pools, rotate so slowly that their movement is only perceptible if you watch a single bolt for an hour. Thick, lead-shielded cables, the size of redwood trunks, snake across the landscape, disappearing into fissures in the sharp rock. Occasionally, a spark of blue electricity—the only light in this world—crawls lazily along a wire, illuminating for a fraction of a second the sheer scale of the derelict infrastructure: cooling towers that reach into the black void, and vast, windowless factories that seem to have been grown from the earth rather than built upon it.

Reality here is frayed. In the spaces between the machines, the geometry of the basin fails. A pool of water may ripple upward into the air, forming a standing column of liquid that refuses to fall. Shadows cast by the rare electric sparks sometimes detach from their objects and linger for several minutes, drifting like ghosts through the reeds. You might see the same rusted engine block repeated twelve times in a perfect, overlapping row, each one vibrating at a slightly different frequency.

Everything is slow. The water drips with a sluggish, syrupy cadence. The geological plates groan as they settle, a sound that takes minutes to travel from a low rumble to a sharp crack. The industrial ruins are not decaying so much as they are being absorbed into the young, wet stone. It is a place of heavy, warm gestation, where the laws of physics have not yet decided to settle, and the silence is merely a veil over a billion tiny, breathing lives hidden in the dark.
