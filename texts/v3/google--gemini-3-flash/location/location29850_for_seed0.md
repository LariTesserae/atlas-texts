In the Black-Rib Basin, the world is not seen so much as it is felt against the skin and heard as a physical pressure against the eardrums.

The terrain is a jagged, adolescent landscape of obsidian shards and upthrust basalt. Because the erosion is so young, the earth has not yet been softened by time; every ridge is a razor, every slope a precarious stack of glass-sharp plates that chime like breaking crystal when stepped upon. The elevation is low—a vast, sunken amphitheater the size of a regional capital—where the air pools heavy and warm, trapped by towering perimeter walls that block out all but the faintest, bruised purple gloam of a sky.

Light is a rare commodity here, rated at a near-total darkness, yet the basin is far from invisible. It glows with the frantic, sickly neon of hyper-active biology.

The vegetation is a dense, suffocating carpet of light-sensitive mosses and towering, fleshy macro-fungi that pulse with internal heat. There are no trees in the traditional sense, but rather "gall-towers"—vast, vertical growths of spiraling, charcoal-colored fiber that leak a thick, phosphorescent sap. These towers are draped in "weeping veils," a species of hanging succulent that doesn't need soil, drawing its intermittent moisture from the thick condensation that rolls off the basin walls every few hours. This water doesn't flow in rivers; it beads, drips, and pools in the hollows of the sharp rocks, creating small, temporary mirrors that reflect the chaotic flickering of the life above them.

The scale of the place is deceptive. While it is a singular basin, the sheer density of presences makes it feel like an infinite, layered city of meat and fiber. Everything is touching. Leaves rub against carapaces; roots entwine with the discarded molts of giant insects.

The sound is the basin’s most violent feature. It is deafening—a constant, tectonic roar composed of millions of overlapping clicks, hums, and shrieks. This is the "Fauna," an overwhelming tide of life that outnumbers the plants. The air is thick with "chatter-moths" the size of dinner plates, their wings beating so fast they create a low-frequency thrum that vibrates in the chest. Crawling things with too many legs scuttle over the obsidian ridges, their chitinous shells grinding against the stone. There is no silence, only different textures of noise.

The ecology is governed by a turbulent, hyperactive dynamic. This is not a stable forest; it is a riot. The "gall-towers" grow several feet in a day, only to be devoured by swarms of acid-blooded larvae or toppled by the frequent, minor tremors of the young earth. It is a landscape of Solaris-like creation and destruction, where a thicket of bioluminescent ferns might sprout, bloom into a blinding display of orange sparks, and be trampled into a mulch of organic decay within a single afternoon.

In the midst of this chaotic churn, there are signs of a primitive intelligence, though they have built nothing that lasts. You might find a cluster of obsidian shards lashed together with braided fungus-silk to form a heavy, serrated cleaver, or a series of rhythmic carvings etched into a basalt cliff-face, fresh and unweathered. These are tools of survival, meant for hacking through the aggressive undergrowth or harvesting the fatty organs of the swarm-beasts. There are no houses, no shelters, no permanent marks. To build a wall here would be to invite the turbulence to knock it down. The inhabitants—if they can be called that—are nomads of the immediate, moving with the swarms, using simple bone-and-stone tech to carve a momentary space for themselves in the dark.

The atmosphere is a damp, humid pressure, smelling of ozone, wet stone, and the sweet, cloying scent of over-ripe fermentation. It is a warm, dark, shrieking cradle, a place where the earth is still sharp and the blood of the world is moving too fast to ever settle into peace.
