The air here is a dry, warm vacuum, smelling faintly of ozone and scorched silica. There is no moisture—not in the ground, not in the atmosphere, not in the biology. In place of a water cycle, there is a heat cycle, a slow and silent exchange of thermal energy conducted through the razor-edged terrain.

We are at a great height, a massive continental shelf of raw, unweathered basalt and obsidian that seems to have been thrust upward only moments ago in geologic time. Because there is no wind to speak of and no rain to scour the surfaces, the mountains are terrifyingly sharp. The ridges are like broken glass, rising in jagged, geometric zig-zags toward a sky of unwavering, brilliant clarity. The light is steady and golden, illuminating a landscape that is both embryonic and ancient, caught in the act of becoming.

Despite the total absence of water, the land is lush. This vegetation is not carbon-based as we understand it, but a form of "industrial flora." Across the high plateaus, massive thickets of copper-wire vines and brass-petaled succulent-forms erupt from the cracks in the obsidian. They do not sway; they are rigid, held in a frozen equilibrium. These plants are mechanized in their internal logic: their stems are telescoping pistons of burnished chrome; their leaves are broad, photovoltaic membranes that thrum with a suppressed, sub-audible electrical charge. 

This is a world of scaled production without a producer. The "plants" function like biological factories, pulling minerals from the deep crust and refining them into complex metallic shapes. You might see a grove of tall, silver-white cylinders that look like cooling towers, but they are organic to this place—living chimneys that vent nothing but heat. There are no buildings, no ruins, no roads. The industrial nature of the world is innate, a property of its evolution rather than the result of a builder’s hand.

The scale is vast and lonely. While the thickets of metal-growth are dense and intricate, they are separated by miles of flat, empty void-space. You can stand on a high, needle-like peak and look across a hundred miles of absolute stillness, seeing only three or four other clusters of growth, each a solitary island of complexity in an ocean of raw, unworked stone. 

There is no sound. No wind to whistle through the copper vines, no insects to buzz, no birds to cry. The only noise is the occasional, tectonic *clink* of a metal leaf expanding in the warmth, a sound like a cooling engine block. 

Nothing moves. The landscape is locked in a 0.5/3 dynamic—a state of permanent stasis. The plants do not appear to grow, yet they are mature. The mountains do not crumble, yet they are new. It is as if the universe began in this exact configuration and decided it was finished. The light never fades, the heat never dissipates, and the metallic forest continues its silent, pointless manufacturing, producing nothing but its own beautiful, terrifyingly sharp existence. 

Here, the "industrial" is the "natural." The gears are the seeds. The silence is the only law. It is a high, dry cathedral of static electricity and unbreaking glass, waiting for a wind that will never come.
