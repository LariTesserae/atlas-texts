This is the Interior of the Great Braided Shell, a space no larger than a village square, yet appearing infinite due to the way its geometry refuses to close. 

There is no dirt here. There is no stone. Every surface, from the undulating floor to the vaulted ribs above, has been manufactured. It is a work of total craft, a masterwork of primitive labor. The ground is a vast, interlocking mosaic of hand-fired terracotta tiles, each the size of a human palm, pressed with the fingerprints of those who laid them. The walls are not walls but colossal, braided ropes of sun-bleached fiber—thick as tree trunks—woven into a complex, non-Euclidean lattice. 

The air is parched, holding a heat that feels like the breath of a kiln. There is no water; the concept of liquidity seems absent from the physics of this place. Instead of moisture, a fine, golden dust hangs suspended in the air, caught in the perpendicular beams of light that enter through the gaps in the woven ceiling. 

Despite the dryness, the environment is lush. It is thick with a specialized, non-aqueous ecology. "Plants" made of brittle, calcified lace erupt from the seams between the floor tiles. They are the color of dried apricots and scorched bone. Some resemble ferns but are composed of fine, sharp needles of glass; others are like giant, petrified sponges that pulse with a slow, internal glow. These growths do not wilt; they are rigid and sharp-edged, having emerged recently in a frantic burst of geological-speed growth, leaving their silhouettes crisp and un-weathered. 

The density is stifling. The "vegetation" grows so closely together that to move through the space requires a constant, careful sidestepping around serrated fronds. Layer upon layer of woven mats and hanging fiber-gardens create a canopy that obscures the true height of the shell. 

Reality here is thin and fraying. In the corners of the eye, the braided ropes of the walls seem to untwist and re-knit themselves into new patterns. Gravity is not a constant downward pull but a gentle suggestion; if one stares too long at the ceiling, the perspective shifts, and the ceiling becomes a floor, though the body remains anchored to the terracotta. There are "soft spots" in the air where the light bends into impossible angles, showing glimpses of the same room from a different time, or perhaps a different version of the same room where the tiles are blue instead of red.

Fauna is abundant but subtle. Small, multi-legged creatures carved from obsidian and pale wood scuttle along the braided ropes. They have no mouths, for there is nothing to drink. They seem to feed on the static electricity generated by the dry air rubbing against the fibers. They move with a clicking, mechanical gait, though they are clearly organic—or as organic as a creature of dry pith and mineral can be. Occasionally, large, flat ribbons of something like translucent leather drift through the upper reaches of the lattice, moving with the agonizing slowness of a cloud.

The sound is a heavy, muffled quiet. The density of the fibers swallows noise. There is no wind, only the occasional *tink* of a ceramic tile settling or the dry, rasping sound of one glass leaf brushing against another. It is the silence of a tomb that is nonetheless brimming with life.

Time moves with a patient, rhythmic pulse. The light never fades, but it shifts in hue—from a pale straw yellow to a deep, bruised ochre—over cycles that feel like they span years. The growth of the lace-plants is so slow it is invisible to the eye, yet you can feel the pressure of their expansion against the tiles, a constant, silent force of a world still building itself by hand. This is a young place, a fresh creation of a primitive god who works only with clay, fiber, and the absence of water.
