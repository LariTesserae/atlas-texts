The peaks of the Aethel-Grange do not taper; they shatter. At this elevation, the air is thin enough to reveal the true, blinding ferocity of the sun, a white-hot glare that reflects off the razor-sharp, unweathered edges of the mountains. This is a young landscape, a continental upheaval of obsidian and basalt that has not yet learned how to crumble. The ridges are jagged, like the teeth of a fresh-cut saw, rising thousands of feet into a sky that is a deep, bruised violet.

Despite the biting cold—a temperature that turns breath into immediate crystals—the world is not frozen. It is lubricated. Intermittent bursts of thick, grey condensation erupt from vents in the rock, coating the landscape in a fine, slick film. This moisture does not gather into lakes; it travels through the mountain’s veins, pumped by the rhythmic, subterranean thrumming of a landscape that functions as a singular, planetary engine.

There are no buildings here, no architecture, yet the environment is undeniably industrial. The "vegetation" is a dense, packed forest of bio-mechanical conduits. Massive, tubular stalks the color of scorched copper rise from the crevices, pulsing with a slow, hydraulic rhythm. They are not wood; they are a composite of silicate and pressurized fiber, acting as organic pistons that grind against the stone with a deafening, metallic shriek. Every square inch of the surface is occupied. There is no open ground. If you are not standing on a sharp ridge, you are standing on a bed of vibrating, mossy cables or the shuddering plates of a "leaf" that feels like cold, galvanized steel.

The scale of this operation is terrifying. From the highest precipice, you can see the engine-forest stretching across the horizon, a sea of rhythmic, pumping stalks that manipulate the very atmosphere. They are breathing for the planet, their collective exhaust forming clouds of heavy, metallic mist that settle in the valleys.

The fauna is not merely present; it is a saturation. The air is thick with "aerosols"—creatures the size of whales that drift on thermal vents, their hides shimmering with bioluminescent warnings. Beneath them, the forest floor (if it can be called that) is a writhing carpet of hard-shelled organisms that act as the lubricant and the maintenance crew for the mountain-machine. They resemble silver-plated beetles the size of shields, millions of them, clicking and grinding in a ceaseless, ear-splitting cacophony. They consume the frost and excrete a thick, conductive oil that keeps the organic pistons from seizing in the cold.

The sound is a wall. It is the sound of a factory the size of a continent: the hiss of high-pressure steam, the deep, infrasonic groan of shifting tectonic plates being held in place by root-cables, and the constant, shrill chirping of a trillion metallic insects. It is a roar that you do not just hear, but feel in the marrow of your bones.

Everything here moves with a deliberate, agonizing slowness. The great pistons may only complete a single stroke over the course of a week. The fauna, despite their numbers, move with the patient lethality of glaciers, shifting their positions in a slow-motion dance of consumption and excretion. The landscape is changing, but on a timeline that ignores the frantic ticking of a human heart. The mountains are still growing, pushed upward by the immense biological pressure of the roots, gaining inches every decade, screaming as the fresh rock cracks against the sky.

It is a place of blinding light and freezing shadow, a crowded, thumping laboratory of evolution where biology has skipped the fragility of soft tissue and gone straight to the durability of the machine. There is no silence here, and there is no room to breathe. There is only the Work—the slow, massive, and deafeningly loud process of the planet building itself.
