Whoever or whatever lives in this jagged, high-altitude plateau is a being—or a collective of beings—shaped by the raw, otherworldly forces of their environment. They are not immediately visible, not bound to forms we might easily recognize, and their presence is felt more as an undercurrent, a pulse woven into the heat, the moisture, and the strange hum of the alien technology scattered across this searing, nascent landscape. Let me draw them out slowly, allowing their essence to emerge as the words unfold, reflecting the plateau’s own balance of sparsity and intensity, of weirdness and nascent life.

I sense at first a consciousness that is not singular but distributed, fragmented yet cohesive, like the rivulets of water that seep through the spongy ground. These beings—I’ll call them the Shimmerkin for now, though naming feels inadequate—do not inhabit the plateau in a fixed, corporeal way. They are semi-ephemeral, their forms fluctuating between states of matter and energy, perhaps a byproduct of the warped physics of this place, where gravity tugs sideways and time stretches or contracts unpredictably. Imagine a shimmer in the air, like heatwaves rising from the raw, unweathered stone, but with intent—a deliberate ripple that moves against the wind, darting between the waxy ferns and translucent berries, coalescing briefly into shapes that suggest life before dissolving again. One moment, they might resemble the sinewy, multi-limbed reptilian predators that stalk the plateau’s edges, their bodies refracting light as if woven from the violet-edged shadows of this world. The next, they are nothing more than a flicker, a distortion in the humid air, leaving behind only the faint scent of ozone or the metallic tang that lingers after the hourly rains.

Their physicality, when it manifests, is mutable, adapted to the harsh extremes of heat and thin air. If you could pin one down to observe—an impossible task, as they recoil from direct scrutiny—their "skin" might appear as a prismatic lattice, akin to the half-formed constructs scattered across the plateau, refracting the bruised indigo of the sky into fleeting rainbows. Their limbs, if they can be called that, are not fixed in number or shape; they extend and retract like pseudopods or tendrils, mimicking the coiled, pulsing vines of the vegetation, perhaps out of camouflage or symbiosis. Their movement is sinuous, almost liquid, flowing over the jagged terrain with a grace that defies its sharpness, as if they are not bound by the same spatial rules as we are. They leave no footprints on the spongy marsh-edge ground, only a faint shimmer of displaced moisture that evaporates in seconds under the relentless heat.

But the Shimmerkin are not merely physical, or even primarily so. Their essence seems tied to the plateau’s strangeness, to the flickering discharges of the hexagonal dome at its center and the shifting glyphs on the scattered artifacts. I sense they are linked to this technology, not as creators or users in the way we might understand, but as extensions of it—a living manifestation of its purpose, or perhaps its memory. When the dome hums its low, resonant tone, the air around it thickens with their presence; the Shimmerkin gather, invisible but felt, as if drawn to commune with the structure in a language of vibration and light. Touching an artifact, with its glyphs that respond to thought, might summon a fleeting interaction—a ripple of warmth, a momentary vision of fractal patterns unfolding in the mind, as if they are trying to speak through the tech, or through us. Their connection to these objects suggests a history, or a future, tied to a civilization that operates on principles beyond our grasp, where the boundaries between biology, energy, and machine blur into irrelevance.

Their behavior, like their form, is elusive yet purposeful. They are not predators in the raw sense, nor are they prey; they exist outside the typical ecological dance of the plateau’s sparse fauna, though they seem to observe it with detached curiosity. The translucent insects and many-limbed reptilians do not react to their presence, as if the Shimmerkin occupy a parallel layer of reality, intersecting only when they choose. They might hover near a cluster of glowing berries, their shimmer intensifying as the bioluminescent spores released by the plants dissolve into the air—perhaps feeding on the energy, or guiding the spores’ dispersal in some mutualistic bond we cannot fathom. During the sudden, violent rains, their presence spikes; the air crackles with their energy, and the reflections on the wet stone sometimes reveal fleeting outlines, as if the water momentarily anchors them to our perception. They seem to thrive in the dynamic shifts of this place, drawing strength from the electric gusts of wind and the rapid cycles of wet and dry.

What drives them? I sense no emotion as we might define it—no fear, no aggression, no joy—but there is a palpable intent, a watchfulness that permeates the plateau’s stillness. They are guardians, or perhaps witnesses, tied to the plateau’s existence as a testing ground or crucible. The way they cluster near the dome during its hums, or linger by the cliffs where gravity warps, suggests they are waiting—either for something to arrive, or for the plateau itself to reach some critical state of becoming. They do not interact with outsiders directly; attempts to approach them result in a subtle retreat, a fading of their shimmer, as if they are bound by rules that forbid engagement. Yet their presence lingers in the mind, a sensation of being seen, evaluated, through a lens we cannot comprehend. Stare too long at the horizon’s mirage, and you might feel their gaze in return, a prickling on the skin that is not hostile but simply other.

Their relationship with time and space reinforces their alien nature. Just as hours can feel like minutes during the rainstorms, the Shimmerkin seem to operate on a temporal scale that is not linear. A single flicker of their form might replay itself in memory, looping with slight variations, as if they exist in multiple moments at once. Near the cliffs, where the sideways pull of gravity is strongest, their shimmer distorts further, stretching or compressing as if they are navigating unseen dimensions layered over our own. They might be native to this warping, born of the plateau’s nascent rawness and the collision of forces beyond understanding, or they might be interlopers from elsewhere, drawn to this fragment of reality for reasons tied to the forgotten—or not-yet-arrived—purpose of the technology here.

As I linger on them, the Shimmerkin become more themselves, entities of heat and light and flux, embodying the plateau’s tense equilibrium. They are not beings of flesh and blood, nor of pure energy, but something interstitial, a bridge between the raw stone and the incomprehensible tech, between the lush, determined patches of life and the bruised, streaking sky. They are the plateau’s memory, or its dream, a living echo of whatever forces carved this place from the mountain’s bones and imbued it with such strangeness. To live here, among the sweating rocks and fleeting rains, is to be part of a process still unfolding, and the Shimmerkin are both participants and observers in that slow, alien becoming. They do not welcome or reject; they simply are, shimmering in the heat, waiting in the quiet, as much a part of this place as the hum of the dome or the metallic tang after the rain. If they have a name for themselves, it is not spoken in sound but in the flicker of glyphs, the bend of light, the subtle pull of a reality not quite ours.
