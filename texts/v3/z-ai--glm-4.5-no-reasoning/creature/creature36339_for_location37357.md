The people who live here are not inhabitants in the way one might think of a town’s citizens. They do not reside in the weeping architecture so much as they are *held* by it, absorbed into its slow, damp metabolism. They are a handful of stoic, quiet figures—perhaps three dozen, though no one counts, for counting implies a separation between self and surroundings that does not exist here. They have the look of things that have been steeped: their skin is pale, not with the pallor of illness, but with the translucent, waterlogged whiteness of the gripe-worms, faintly mapped with the blue-grey shadows of the corridors. Their hands are perpetually soft, the nails worn down to rubbery crescents from the constant friction of wet stone. They move with a specific, deliberate gait—a slow, wide-footed stride that presses the slick flagstones to test their grip, their shoulders slightly hunched beneath the tons of stone that hang above them.

They do not have names that travel beyond the ridge. Among themselves, communication is largely subsonic and tactile. The acoustic engine of the settlement—the drips, the whispers, the wet clicking of the worms—makes spoken language a strain. Instead, they have developed a vocabulary of knocks and scrapes, tapping their knuckles or their stone-scraping tools against the shared walls to signal a blocked drain, a weakening hinge, or a shift in the ridge. They can read the pressure of the hill in the vibrations of the stone. They know when the western face is preparing to shear not by sight, but by the subtle change in the pitch of the water running down the gutters, a flattening of the hum that tells them the fractures are growing. 

Their inner lives are as minimal and dense as their settlement. They do not tell stories. Storytelling requires a past that is dry enough to be preserved, and here, memory petrifies into meaningless mineral lumps. They do not dream of elsewhere; the concept of "dryness" is as abstract and unimaginable to them as the vacuum of space is to a deep-sea creature. Their mental landscape is entirely localized, governed by an intimate, anxious awareness of weight and water. They can feel the bulging of the shared walls in their sleep; they register the accumulation of condensation on their resting shelves the way a sailor registers the shift of the wind. Their dominant emotion is not sadness, but a taut, low-grade vigilance—a constant, muscle-tensing anticipation of the crush. Yet there is no panic in this. It has simply become the baseline of their existence, the atmosphere of their minds.

Their labor is their sole ritual, and it binds them into a single, pulsing entity. The work is endless and Sisyphean, but they do not resent it; resentment requires a belief that things could be otherwise. To scrape the yellow-grey lichen from the doorways is to perform the settlement's exhalation; to pitch the rawhide hinges is to flex its joints; to clear the gripe-worms from the drains is to clear its veins. When they work together to lever a massive roof slab back into place as the walls beneath it bow, they move with a synchronous, breathless grace, their bare feet finding purchase in the same microscopic crevices, their combined strength acting as a temporary counterweight to the hill’s crushing inertia. In these moments, they are not individuals; they are the sinew of the ridge itself, holding the stone together.

They are not young, and they do not age as dry-landers do. The petrification that claims dropped tools claims them too, in a slower, subtler way. Their joints calcify with the same mineral sediment that crusts the puddles, their spines curving to match the inward bulge of the walls. They do not die so much as they slow down, their body heat failing to push back the dampness of their sleeping shelves. When one of them ceases to wake, they are not buried. The settlement does not have the space or the dry earth for it. Instead, the others move the cooling body to the westernmost edge, where the stone is already shearing away. They lay them in the cleft of the fresh fracture, and within days, the slow perspiration of the hill has encased them in a fine, damp shell of mudstone and lime. They become part of the ridge's weeping architecture, a new layer of pressure for the remaining few to endure.

They are the antibodies of this sickly, transitional place, fighting a quiet, unwinnable war against the gravity of the young ridge and the saturation of the air. They are a people who have looked at the slow, crushing weight of a waterlogged world and decided not to flee, but to become the thing that scrapes it clean, watches it fracture, and tightens the belt one more notch, living inside the geology until the geology lives inside them.
