# What Lives in the Green Veil

---

## The Reapers

They are the largest things that move through the reed-forest, and you will almost certainly never see one whole.

You will see the passage. A channel closed behind them, stalks bending and springing back, water shaken loose in a cascade that reverberates for long seconds after they have gone. You will feel the displacement in the rootmass — a slow rolling pressure, as though the ground itself were a muscle contracting. And if you are very still, very patient, you will catch a glimpse through the green wall: a flank, a curve, something dark and wet and massive that is already moving away.

The Reapers are built for the vertical. Their bodies are long, low, sinuous — six limbs spaced along a torso that can be four meters end to end, each limb a gripping mechanism: two jointed toes in front, two behind, able to close around a reed-stalk or spread flat against the rootmass. They move like climbers traversing a cliff made of grass, pulling themselves forward in a hand-over-hand rhythm that becomes eerily fast once they reach their stride. Their tails are not tails as you would think of them — they are prehensile, muscled, as long as the body again, and they carry the last quarter of their weight, acting as a fifth limb, a rudder, a brake, a grip on the world when the world is nothing but vertical surfaces and soft ground.

Their heads are narrow. Their eyes are set wide and high, positioned not for forward stereoscopic vision but for lateral awareness — they watch the reeds around them, not what is ahead. What they hunt is not seen. It is heard. It is felt. Their skulls carry resonant chambers, hollowed bone behind the jaw, and the low vibrations of the water-percussion and the rootmass-voices reach them amplified, sorted, interpreted. They know where prey moves by the sound it makes moving through water-soaked vegetation. They know where other Reapers are by the subsonic hum they produce — a constant territorial broadcast, felt rather than heard, that you perceive only as a vague pressure change when one passes near.

They are not pack animals, but they are not solitary either. They exist in a network of spacing — each Reaper holding territory that overlaps at its edges with two or three others, contesting those borders with vocalizations and occasional combat but never pursuing the losers beyond the boundary line. It is a system that works because the prey is abundant and the space is vast and because, in the Green Veil, exhaustion kills more surely than teeth.

Their teeth. You find the skulls sometimes, grown into the rootmass, the bone already serving as substrate for moss and the not-fungus. The jaws are long, narrow, crowded with teeth that interlock when the mouth closes — not fangs, not grinding molars, but a cage of points, a trap. Whatever goes in does not come out. The Reaper bites and holds and waits for the struggling to stop, and then it retracts its head into its own neck-folds — a thick ruff of overlapping skin that protects the throat while it feeds — and it swallows in the slow, patient way of something that has never learned to hurry.

They are dark. Dark green, dark brown, dark grey — the color of wet things in shadow. Their skin is not smooth; it carries growths, excrescences, small pockets where water collects and moss takes root. The oldest Reapers are half-vegetable, their backs becoming gardens of cup-leaf and drip-catcher, their tails trailing streamers of the not-fungus. They do not groom these growths away. They use them. In a world where camouflage is the difference between eating and being eaten, to carry a garden on your back is to disappear.

## The Pale Swimmers

They are what the Reapers hunt. And they are what hunts the Reapers, when the season turns and the young are born and hunger becomes imperative.

The Pale Swimmers are smaller — a meter, a meter and a half of body, plus a tail that adds another half. They are pale. Not white, not albino, but the grey-pink-translucent color of things that have never needed pigment, that have lived for generations in the green twilight where color is meaningless and only pattern matters. Their skin shows the dark branching of their veins, the slower pulse of their blood. You can see the shadows of their organs when they pass close.

They are compressed laterally, thin from side to side, so they can slip between the stalks without displacing them, without bending, without sound. Their limbs are small, tucked against their bodies when they move, used only for gripping when they stop or for scrambling when they are caught. Their primary locomotion is serpentine — a sinusoidal wave that runs from head to tail, pressing against the stalks on either side, propulsion by lateral resistance.

They are fast. Faster than anything else in the green dark. And they are silent in a way that seems deliberate, seems like a skill honed over evolutionary time against the Reaper's vibration-sense. They do not splash. They do not compress the rootmass audibly. They choose paths where the substrate is firmest, where their weight will not produce the low rumble that carries for meters. And when they must cross open ground — one of the rare basalt ridges, a place where the reeds thin and the mineral bone of the plateau shows — they cross in bursts, quick and low and exposed for only seconds, their pale bodies flashing against the dark rock before they vanish again.

They eat the reed-grass. Not the living stalks — those are too tough, too silica-laden — but the dead fall, the collapsed and rotting vegetation that accumulates at the base of every clump. They eat the mosses. They eat the not-fungus when they can find it in its fruiting stage, small pale buttons of photosynthetic tissue that emerge from the rootmass after particularly heavy condensation. And they eat the insects — the larvae, the skaters, the whole micro-ecologies that live in the cup-leaf water. They are generalists, survivors, the kind of omnivore that thrives in a young ecosystem because it will try anything once and eat it again if it does not die.

They breed constantly. This is their strategy against predation. The Pale Swimmers are born in litters of six to eight, tiny and translucent and mobile within hours, and they reach sexual maturity in less than a local year. The Reapers take them in numbers, but the numbers are always replaced. In the boom times — when the condensation is heavy and the vegetation grows fastest and the food is abundant — the Swimmers reach a population density that is almost plague-like, and then the Reapers boom in turn, and the cycle swings, and the Green Veil absorbs the excess in the usual way: death, rot, rootmass, new growth.

You hear them at night. Or what passes for night — the darkening, the deep twilight when the cloud-ceiling lowers and the reed-forest becomes black rather than green. They call to each other then, a soft clicking sound made by snapping their jaw-hinges, a sound that carries through the vegetation in a way their body-movement does not. Click-click-click. Contact calls. Mate-location calls. Warning calls, when a Reaper's subsonic hum enters their perceptual range. The night is full of clicking, layered and overlapping, a conversation in a language you will never decode.

## The Drinkers

There is a third kind of large fauna, and it is the one you are most likely to see, because it does not flee, because it has no reason to flee, because nothing in the Green Veil hunts it regularly enough to make fear worthwhile.

The Drinkers are heavy. Not long, not sinuous, but *dense* — barrel-bodied, low to the ground, four thick limbs that end in broad splayed feet, each foot a spreading mesh of toes connected by webbing that acts as both paddle and snowshoe on the wet rootmass. They weigh what a small horse would weigh, but they carry it in half the height, and they move with a deliberation that makes each step a decision.

They eat the cup-leaves. This is their name and their niche and their evolutionary compact with the Green Veil. The cup-leaves — those shaped vessels that collect water and detritus and small dead things — are everywhere, growing on every surface, and the Drinkers move through the reed-forest methodically, grazing them. They do not eat the leaf itself. They eat what is *in* the leaf — the pooled water, the insect larvae, the fallen seeds, the small corpses, the nutrient slurry that accumulates in every cup. They have a tongue like a sponge, long and mobile and covered in fine papillae, and they insert it into each cup in turn, drain it, and move on.

The vegetation has adapted to them. The cup-leaves in areas where Drinkers frequent are larger, deeper, shaped to accommodate the tongue's width. They grow at the height of the Drinker's mouth, not by coincidence but by selection — the cups that are too high go unharvested, their contents rotting and returning to the plant; the cups that are too low are trampled, crushed, selected against. What remains is a mutualism: the Drinker feeds, and in feeding, it cleans the cups, removes the anoxic sludge that would otherwise drown the leaf's own tissues, and it deposits fertilizer — its waste, spread along its grazing route, nitrogen and phosphate returned to the rootmass in a form the plants can uptake immediately.

They are brown. A wet brown, dark and glossy, the color of saturated wood. Their skin is loose, folded, hanging in dewlaps and creases that collect moisture and harbor communities of small organisms — mites, springtails, things without names that live nowhere else. A Drinker is an ecosystem. A walking, grazing, defecating ecosystem that carries a subset of the Green Veil's biodiversity on its skin and in its gut and leaves a portion of itself behind wherever it goes.

They are not social. They tolerate each other at distances of ten, fifteen meters, grazing the same area in parallel without interaction, without aggression. They communicate, if they communicate, through scent — a thick, mulchy odor that marks their trails, that identifies individuals, that signals reproductive readiness or its absence. The smell is not unpleasant. It is the smell of the Green Veil concentrated: wet green, cold mineral, slow life.

They have no natural predators as adults. The Reapers will take a sick one, a slow one, a juvenile separated from its mother. But a healthy adult Drinker is too large, too solid, too difficult to kill in a world where pursuit is exhausting and wounds fester in the constant wet. They die of age, of disease, of the slow failure of their joints in the cold. And when they die, they do not stand. They sink. The rootmass receives them, and the green takes them, and within a season there is nothing left but a slight depression in the substrate where the body went in, and a flush of new growth — richer, greener, taller — marking the place where all that concentrated nutrient returned to the system.

## The Small Life

It is everywhere. You cannot escape it. You cannot take a step without killing something, without disrupting something, without entering the territory of something smaller than your fingernail that considers your foot an environmental catastrophe.

The cup-leaf ecosystems are the most accessible. Each cup holds water — fresh, slightly acidic, enriched by whatever has fallen in. And in that water: larvae. Dipteran analogues, wriggling and filter-feeding, dense enough that the water in older cups is more larva than liquid. Predators among them — larger larvae with hooked mouthparts that take the smaller larvae from behind, slowly, legs-first. And on the surface, the skaters — things like water-striders, walking on the meniscus, hunting the larvae that come up to breathe. And above the skaters, the flyers — tiny things with iridescent wings that dip to the surface, lay eggs, take off again, living their entire adult lives in the space between one cup and the next.

Each cup is a world. Each reed-stalk is a continent of cups, and the Green Veil is a galaxy of reed-stalks, and the small life fills all of it, occupying every niche, exploiting every surface, existing at a scale where a droplet is an ocean and a leaf is a continent and a season is a generation and a generation is an eternity.

In the rootmass, other things. The worms — pale, blind, segmented, processing. They are the substrate's digestive system, consuming the dead vegetation that falls from above, breaking it down, mixing it, aerating it with their passages. Without them, the rootmass would compact into something impermeable, and the water would pool, and the anaerobic zones would expand, and the system would suffocate. The worms keep it alive. They are unglamorous and essential and present in numbers that would sicken you if you could see them — if you could peel back the rootmass like a carpet and see the pale writhing beneath.

And in the not-fungus, things that are not quite worms and not quite insects and not quite anything you have a category for. The not-fungus is not quite fungus, and the things that live in it are not quite anything else, and this is the pattern of the Green Veil: the categories break down. The young ecosystem is still experimenting. It has not settled on forms. It is trying everything, and what works persists, and what does not is absorbed, and the boundaries between plant and animal and fungus are suggestions, not rules.

There are mites that photosynthesize. They carry chloroplasts — stolen, inherited, it is unclear — in their translucent bodies, and they cluster on the surfaces of leaves where the light is strongest, and they are green, and they feed on light, and they are animals. This should not work. It works. The Green Veil does not care about your shoulds.

There are things that are both predator and plant. They root in the substrate, they extend pale tendrils upward into the reed-forest, and when something small brushes against a tendril, it triggers a closing response — slow, not dramatic, nothing like the snap of a terrestrial Venus flytrap — but inexorable. The tendril folds, the prey is folded with it, and over the course of hours, the prey is digested through the tendril's surface, absorbed, and the tendril extends again, patient, waiting. They are not common. But you find them if you look carefully, and when you find one, you find many — they cluster, they clone, they spread through the rootmass in connected networks that may cover meters of territory, all of them descended from a single individual that found this spot and stayed.

## The Callers

The things you hear in the high reeds, the things that might be birds.

They are not birds. They are something else — something that evolved from a different lineage, that found flight in a world where flight is almost useless and made of it something different.

The Callers are small. Fifteen centimeters, twenty, lightweight and hollow-boned, warm-blooded, active. They live in the upper reaches of the reed-forest where the stalks thin and the light — what there is of it — enters. They cannot fly in the way you mean when you say flight. The reeds are too dense, the spaces too narrow, the risk of collision too high. What they do instead is *climb and glide*. Their wings are not broad; they are long, narrow, adapted for short bursts of controlled descent from one stalk-top to another. Their feet are zygodactyl — two toes forward, two back — and they grip the reed-stalks with an ease that seems almost contemptuous, moving up and down the vertical surfaces as if gravity were a suggestion they have chosen to ignore.

They call. God, they call. The sound is sharp, piercing, repetitive — a three-note phrase that varies by individual and is repeated thirty, forty, a hundred times in succession. They are territorial. Each Caller holds a volume of space — a cylinder of reed-forest ten meters in diameter and twenty meters high — and it defends that volume against all others with sound. The Green Veil's acoustic properties serve them; the stalks transmit and amplify their calls, and in the early darkening, when the condensation lets up slightly and the cloud-ceiling lifts, the chorus of Callers rings through the wet architecture like bells in a cathedral of grass.

They mate for the season. The males call; the females select; the pair bonds for the length of one reproductive cycle. They build nests — woven cups of dead reed-fiber and moss, lined with the not-fungus's pale threads, attached to stalks at the junction of leaf and stem. The nests collect water. Everything collects water here. The Callers have solved this problem by building their nests at a slight angle, so the water drains from one edge, and by weaving the lining so densely that the chicks — two, usually, sometimes three — rest above the damp on a platform of their own shed down.

They eat the insects of the upper reeds. They eat the small flyers that navigate between cups. They eat each other's eggs, when they can, when the neighbor's territory is unguarded for a moment. They are aggressive, territorial, constantly vocal, and they are the closest thing the Green Veil has to beauty — their calls are musical, their movements are precise, their small bright eyes are the only points of color in a world that has otherwise committed to green and grey and the dark end of the spectrum.

## The People

There are people. Or there were. Or there are still, deeper in, further than you have walked, in the part of the Green Veil that no one has mapped and no one has reached and no one has returned from.

You have found their marks. The worn path. The woven shelter. The circle of stones on the basalt ridge. These are not the marks of visitors. These are the marks of residents — of people who came and stayed and learned and lived, for a time, within the Green Veil's logic.

They were small. You know this because the shelter is small — a dome two meters across, built for someone who could sleep curled in a space that would cramp you. The path is narrow, the stones in the circle are small, the tool-marks on the bent stalks suggest hands no larger than a child's. But they were not children. They were something else. Something that evolved small, or that was always small, or that became small over generations of living in a world where food is scarce and warmth is scarce and every calorie saved by a smaller body is a calorie that keeps you alive.

They moved through the reed-forest differently than you do. You push and shove and break and make noise. They slipped. They knew the paths — not the paths you can see, but the paths that exist at the interface of stalk and air, the gaps that open and close with the wind, the channels that require timing and body-knowledge and the ability to read the green as a language of space. They were swimmers in the reeds the way the Pale Swimmers are swimmers in the reeds, and this may not be coincidence. This may be convergence. The same problem — movement through dense vertical vegetation on soft wet ground — may have only so many solutions, and the people found the same ones that the Swimmers found, or the Swimmers found the same ones that the people found, or they found them together, over time, influencing each other, co-evolving in the way that predator and prey co-evolve, in the way that mutualists co-evolve, in the way that everything in the Green Veil co-evolves because nothing here exists alone.

They drank from the cup-leaves. This is how you survive when the sky offers only mist and the ground offers only seepage. You find the cups that are clean — the high cups, the ones the Drinkers cannot reach, the ones where the water has just condensed and nothing has fallen in yet — and you drink, and you are careful, because the wrong cup will make you sick. The cups harbor not just larvae but parasites, pathogens, things that will colonize your gut and take what you eat and leave you weaker than you started. The people knew which cups to drink from. They had to. The knowledge was survival, and survival was the only teacher the Green Veil recognizes.

They ate what the Pale Swimmers ate. The dead vegetation, the moss, the fruiting not-fungus, the insects. And what the Swimmers did not eat — the small hunters, the slow worms, the Callers when they could catch them, the eggs from the nests, the larvae from the cups. They were omnivores in an ecosystem of omnivores, and they were not the apex, and they were not the bottom, and they were somewhere in the middle where everything is both eater and eaten, and this is the most dangerous place to be.

They had tools. You know this from the marks — sharp cuts on the woven shelter's stalks, deliberate and angled, made by something held in a hand and swung with force. Stone, probably. The basalt ridges provide material — dark, fine-grained, fracture-sharp. You imagine them sitting on those ridges, striking stone against stone, making the edges they needed, and then leaving the ridges and descending into the green with their sharp things and their small bodies and their knowledge of the cups and the paths and the season's rhythm.

You do not know if they are still there.

The Green Veil is vast. You have walked for a week and crossed nothing, reached no edge, found no limit. The plateau extends beyond your capacity to traverse it, and the vegetation covers all of it, and the people — if they remain — could be anywhere, could be everywhere, could be living in their small way in their small shelters, drinking from their chosen cups, eating their chosen foods, calling to each other in a language you have never heard and would not recognize if you did.

The shelter you found is old. The path is overgrown. The circle of stones sits undisturbed. But the Green Veil absorbs history in a single season, erases it, grows over it. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, not here, not in a place that devours the past with such efficiency that even bone becomes substrate within the turn of a year.

You think about them when you are cold. You think about them when the darkening comes and the water-noise rises and the Callers begin their chorus and something large moves in the green five meters away and you cannot see it and cannot identify it and can only stand still and wait for it to pass. You think about how they survived, and whether they survived, and what it would mean — what it would *cost* — to live in a place that does not notice you, that does not accommodate you, that does not provide any comfort that you do not first identify and then claim and then defend against everything else that is trying to claim it too.

## What Lives Here

Everything. Too much. An excess of life so great that life becomes the medium, the substrate, the condition of existence. You do not live *in* the Green Veil the way you live in a forest or a desert or a city. You live *within* it, surrounded, penetrated, colonized. The mites on your skin. The spores in your lungs. The water in your clothes that is not yours and was never yours, that belongs to the Green Veil and is merely passing through you on its way from cloud to rootmass to river to cloud again.

What lives here is a system. A single vast slow system of water and green and cold and hunger and growth and death and absorption, and all the individual lives — the Reapers, the Swimmers, the Drinkers, the Callers, the worms, the mites, the people, all the things with names and all the things without — are expressions of that system, moments when it becomes visible, when it takes a form you can recognize as *alive*.

But the system itself is what lives here. The Green Veil is what lives here. And it is patient, and it is cold, and it is growing, and it does not care about you at all.
