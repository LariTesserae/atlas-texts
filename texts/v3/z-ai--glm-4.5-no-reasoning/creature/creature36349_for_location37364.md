To live here is to live at the pace of stone. The people of the plateau are not distinct from the city; they are its mobile, short-lived tissue, moving over the bones of the metropolis like a slow, warm pulse. They are stoneworkers, climbers, and keepers of the condensation-traps, their lives organized entirely around the slow maintenance of the city and the harvesting of the sparse, stubborn life that shares the plateau.

Physically, they are compacted by the altitude and whittled by the cold. Generations of breathing the thin, silica-laced air have broadened their chests and shortened their stature, while the relentless anesthetizing cold has pressed them into a dense, heat-conserving form. Their skin is thick, wind-cracked, and wind-carved—taking on the same grey-brown patina as the basalts they handle, creased deeply around the eyes and mouth like the frost-shattered joints of the bedrock. Their lungs are vast, dark bellows, adapted to pull oxygen from air that barely qualifies as breath, and their blood runs heavy and slow, thick with the cold, carrying warmth not to the extremities, but to the core, prioritizing the survival of the whole over the comfort of the parts. Their fingers are broad, calloused slabs, permanently crooked from a lifetime of gripping jagged handholds and working bone chisels, their nails worn down to hard, horn-like crescents.

They move like a glacier flows—imperceptibly, by inches, with no wasted motion. Hurry is a foreign concept, burned out of them by the cold. To rush is to sweat, and to sweat is to invite the freeze, which kills as surely as a fall from the plateau's edge. When they walk, they lean into the wind, their weight pitched forward, feet placed with the deliberate, testing care of a climber feeling for a fracture. When they rest, they rest entirely, becoming inert and still, sinking into the cold stone like another boulder, conserving heat with a discipline that is entirely involuntary. Their eyes are pale, narrowed against the glare of the hazeless sky and the endless grey-brown expanse, the irises washed out to a frost-blue or a pale, granite grey. They do not look at the horizon; they look at the rock immediately before them, reading the grain, the fracture lines, the subtle discolorations that indicate a handhold or a weak seam.

They are wrapped in heavy, rough-woven hides—layers upon layers of stitched leather and fur from the cliff-dwelling birds and the small, furred predators. The garments are not tailored so much as aggregated, draped and bound with hide thongs, creating bulky, asymmetrical silhouettes that blend into the shadows of the deep alleys. The cold forces a constant, subtle tension in their bodies, a permanent clench of the jaw and hunch of the shoulders. Over time, this tension becomes structural; the oldest among them are bowed, their spines curved like the sagging doorways of the tenements, their bodies having slowly settled into the same postures as the architecture, shaped by the same forces.

They are a people of profound, specialized ignorance and profound, specialized knowledge. They know nothing of metal, of mortar, of the wheel. They have no agriculture, no writing, no astronomy. The sky to them is merely a source of light and wind, not a map or a mystery. But they know stone with an intimacy that transcends language. They can read the memory of the rock—where it will split, how it will weather, which face will hold the condensation and which will shed it. They know the city not as a maze, but as a body, knowing its veins (the corridors), its pores (the cisterns), and its breath (the frost that blooms and recedes). They are born into a quarter of the city, and they rarely leave it. Their world is defined by the reach of their hand and the depth of their alley; beyond that, the city continues, but it is largely theoretical, a hum of activity felt through the shared walls rather than seen.

Their lives are a closed circuit of extraction and maintenance. They harvest the lithophytic lichens with bone scrapers, prying the pale, frost-resistant crusts from the rock to eat raw or to steep in the cold cistern water until they soften into a bitter, nutritious gruel. They hunt the hairless, cold-blooded grazers, plucking them from the vertical faces with quick, sure hands, and they set silent traps of stacked stone for the small, furred predators that descend at night. They collect the birds' eggs from the high rookeries, climbing the sheer, guano-streaked cliffs with a terrifying, methodical grace, moving hand-over-hand on ledges no wider than a finger, their leathery wings—no, that is the bird; they have no wings, only the memory of flight in the way they navigate the vertical city. They climb as if they are falling upward, their sucker-like feet—no, that is the grazer. The people climb with their hands and feet, pressing their bodies into the cold stone, finding friction where none should exist, driven by a deep, spinal understanding of gravity and grip.

The condensation-traps are their genius and their obsession. They have learned to shape the stone so that the freezing damp beads and runs, guided by tiny, hand-carved channels into the natural cisterns. They spend generations modifying a single wall, tilting it a fraction of a degree to catch more moisture, carving grooves that direct the meltwater away from the foundations and toward the pools. This is their highest art and their most sacred duty: the shaping of the city to harvest the sky's thin, cold sweat.

Their social structure is minimal, nearly invisible. They do not gather in crowds; the alleys are too narrow, and the cold too pressing, for communal life. They exist in small, kin-bound clusters, sharing body heat in the deep interiors of the tenements, huddled in rooms that are never fully dark or fully warm, lit by the faint, bioluminescent glow of the microbial slimes they cultivate in stone bowls, heated by the slow combustion of dried grazer dung and bird down. They speak in low, gravelly murmurs, a language of clicks and stops that mimics the sound of stone fracturing, efficient and stripped of ornament. They do not sing. They do not pray. They do not tell stories of a time before the cold, because there was no time before the cold; the cold is the primary condition of existence, as fundamental as the rock.

Children are born in the dark, damp interiors, and they learn to climb before they learn to walk properly, because the city is more vertical than horizontal. They are put to work early, their small fingers useful for cleaning the condensation channels and plucking the tiny grazers from the deep crevices. The old, when they can no longer climb or work stone, simply stop. They find a quiet corner, a sheltered alcove, and they sit down against the rock. They grow colder, and stiller, and eventually they become part of the city's sediment, their bodies mummified by the cold and the dry, their forms slowly absorbed into the masonry, another layer of human intent pressed into the stone.

There is no despair here, because despair requires the expectation of something better. They do not hope for warmth or plenty; they expect the freeze, the damp, the slow, grinding patience of the rock. They are not unhappy. They are not happy. They are present, with the absolute, severe presence of a mountain or a cliff face. They have found their place in the logic of altitude and cold, and they fill it completely, a dense, slow-moving layer of life pressed tight against the bone of the world, waiting out the freeze, one century at a time.
