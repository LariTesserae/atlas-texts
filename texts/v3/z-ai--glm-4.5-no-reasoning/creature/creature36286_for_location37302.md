The inhabitants of this place are not what you would see if you could see. They are what you would become if you stayed.

They are called—by themselves, by nothing, by the pressure of the warm dark—the Congregation. Not a species. A phenomenon. A condition of this place, as intrinsic as the heat or the mineral saturation or the slow, circulatory patience of the water. They do not have a history; they have a duration. They do not have a origin; they emerged, as minerals precipitate, as glazes crack, as channels wear smooth—gradually, inevitably, from the conditions themselves. The builders did not put them here. The builders built the machine—channels, basins, thermal gradients—and left it running, and the Congregation is what the machine produced when it ran long enough. They are the rust on the gears, if rust could swim. They are the mold on the stone, if mold had bodies and those bodies had given up on every sense except the ones that matter here.

Start with what they are not. They are not fish. Fish are too specific, too designed, too *shaped* by light and current and the particular pressures of open water. Fish have fins oriented for direction, eyes oriented for sight, bodies built to chase or flee. The Congregation has no direction and no pursuit. They are not vertebrate. They are not invertebrate in any familiar sense—not the organized complexity of a cephalopod, not the armored simplicity of a crustacean. They are something that happened when the blueprint for animal life was left in a warm bath for a million years and all the instructions that required light or season or dryness faded from the genome like writing from worn stone, leaving only the fundamental: *be warm, be near, feed, continue.*

Their bodies—if bodies is the word—are soft. Not the softness of flesh over bone, but the softness of water held in a membrane, the softness of a bag of warm fluid in warm fluid. They have no rigid structures. No skeleton, internal or external. No chitin, no shell, no cartilage. When they press against you in the dark, they feel like nothing distinct—like a thickening of the water, a region of slightly different density, a momentary resistance that yields immediately and flows around your hand. They are gelatinous in the true sense: colloidal, amorphous, their tissues a slow gel of proteins and mineral compounds held in suspension. If you could hold one—and you could, they would not resist, would not struggle, would simply rest in your palm like a warm, heavy sponge without a skeleton—you would feel its weight (they are denser than the water, slightly, just enough to sink when they are still) and its texture (smooth, slick, poreless, like worn ceramic, like something that has been eroded into its final shape). They do not bruise. They do not break. They compress and reform. They are, in their physical constitution, as patient as the architecture that houses them.

Their size varies, but not widely. The parameter of abundance is high, and this is a small space; they fill it densely, and density constrains volume. The largest are perhaps the length of a human forearm, the diameter of a closed fist—slow, massive by the standards of this ecology, pulsing in the deeper basins where the warm water pools and the circulation is slowest. The smallest are fingertip-sized, even smaller, occupying the narrow channels, the cracks between fitted blocks, the spaces too small for their elders. Between these extremes, a continuous gradation of size, no discrete cohorts, no generations, just the slow growth of bodies that never stop growing and never stop being consumed—a continuum of being from the smallest to the largest, each feeding on those below, each food for those above. They are, all of them, both predator and prey. They are, all of them, eating and being eaten at the same time. This is not violence. This is circulation. The builders made channels for water; the Congregation makes channels for biomass, moving energy through the system the same way heat moves through the stone—slowly, uniformly, without urgency.

They do not see. The parameter for light is 0.4 out of 3—darkness, for all practical purposes, and they have been here so long that the very capacity for vision has been selected away, worn smooth like the edges of the channels. There are no eyes. No eye spots, no photoreceptive cells, no residual organs that once saw and now do not. Vision was not something they lost; it was something they never developed, or developed and abandoned so long ago that no trace remains. They do not miss it. There is nothing to see. The dark is total and eternal and it is their medium, their condition, as fundamental to their existence as the warmth or the water. To imagine them seeing is to imagine a fish breathing air—it is not an enhancement, it is a category error.

Instead, they feel. Their bodies are one large sensory surface, a membrane attuned to pressure and temperature and chemical gradient. Every square centimeter of their skin (if skin is the word—surface, boundary, the place where they end and the water begins) is receptive to the displacement of water around them, the subtle shifts in current that indicate the passage of another body, the faint thermal gradients that mark the boundaries of basins and channels, the chemical signatures that signal food or mate or the proximity of something larger and hungrier. They navigate by touch in the broadest sense—not contact, but nearness, the pressure of the warm dark itself, the way the water feels different in a narrow channel (compressed, directed, urgent) than in a broad basin (still, pooling, patient). They know this place as a topology of pressure and heat. They have mapped it with their bodies over a million generations, and the map is written into their chemistry, inherited not as knowledge but as instinct, as the way their cells respond to gradients, the way they drift toward warmth and away from cold, toward concentration and away from absence.

And they feel each other. This is the center of their existence: the constant, near-touching presence of others. The density is high. They are packed into this architecture like sediment into a riverbed, occupying every volume, filling every niche. A single organism in this place would be lost—not merely disoriented, but *wrong*, a datum that does not fit the parameter. They exist in congregation; it is their natural state, their ecological imperative. When they move, they move together—not in coordinated schools, not with the purposeful alignment of fish, but in loose aggregations, drifts, slow currents of bodies that happen to be moving the same direction at the same time because the water is moving that direction and they are moving with the water. They do not lead. They do not follow. They occupy. They fill space. They are, in their collective being, a organ of the larger system—a way for the warm water to be processed, for the mineral saturation to be converted, for the geochemical energy to be transformed into motion and growth and the faint, collective vibration of ten thousand small bodies in close proximity.

What do they do? They persist. They feed. They reproduce. They die.

Feeding first. The water is thick with mineral saturation—dense, silty, almost viscous with dissolved compounds. The warmth sustains a slow bacterial chemosynthesis at the limits of detection, microorganisms that derive energy from the geochemical gradients the builders' architecture creates. These microorganisms are the base of the chain, the primary production, the reason anything larger exists. The smallest members of the Congestion filter-feed, drawing water through their porous bodies (they have no mouths, no digestive tracts in the familiar sense—just apertures, pores, a slow osmotic exchange of water and nutrient across their boundary membranes), extracting bacteria and mineral compounds, converting the dissolved into the embodied. The larger ones feed on the smaller—not by predation in any active sense, but by engulfment, by pressing their larger bodies against smaller ones and absorbing them through the same porous membranes, a slow chemical digestion that takes hours and leaves no trace. It is not violent. It is not even intentional, in any way that intention can be meaningfully ascribed to them. A large organism drifts through a volume of water saturated with smaller organisms; the smaller ones are pressed against its surface; some are absorbed; the larger one moves on. The smaller ones do not flee. They cannot, and there is no reason to—fleeing implies a future in which one is not absorbed, and for the smallest, there is no such future. They will be absorbed, or they will grow, and if they grow, they will absorb others. It is the way of the place. It is circulation.

Reproduction. They do not mate. They divide. Not the sharp, binary fission of bacteria, but a slower, more gradual process—a budding, a swelling, a separation that takes days or weeks and leaves both parent and offspring slightly smaller, slightly different, still drifting in the same warm water. A large organism in a basin will, over the course of a long period, develop a constriction, a narrowing that deepens until the body is two bodies, loosely connected, then barely connected, then not connected at all. The two new organisms drift apart. They are not identical to the parent. The division is not symmetrical; one receives slightly more mass, slightly different concentrations of the mineral compounds that structure their internal chemistry. They are, in a loose sense, the parent's children—derived from it, carrying its chemistry, but already diverging, already becoming their own configuration of the same fundamental template. There is no courtship, no competition, no selection based on fitness in any active sense. The slow, glacial reproduction produces variation; the stable environment selects for stability; the Congregation changes at the rate the architecture changes, which is to say, imperceptibly. They are as finished as the stonework, as worn smooth as the channels, as settled into their final form as everything else in this place.

Death. They do not die in any dramatic sense—no predation events, no sudden collapses, no struggle and cessation. They dwindle. An organism that has divided is slightly smaller; an organism that cannot find enough dissolved nutrient in the water shrinks, its body reabsorbing its own mass, its membranes folding inward. The smallest organisms—the ones at the limit of viability, the ones too small to filter-feed efficiently, too small to be absorbed by others—simply dissolve. Their membranes thin; their chemistry disperses; they become water again, mineral saturation, bacterial substrate. They return to the base of the chain. It is not death as an event but death as a gradient, a slow transition from organism to medium, from the organized to the dissolved. There are no corpses in this place. There is no waste. Everything is consumed, everything is circulated, everything returns. The Congregation is a closed system, like the water, like the architecture, like the slow geochemical processes that sustain it all. Nothing leaves. Nothing enters. Nothing changes.

And yet—they are not nothing. They are not simple. They are not automatons, mindlessly cycling through chemical processes in the dark. They have a presence, a being, a way of existing that is more than the sum of their biochemistry. When they brush against your hand in the warm dark—and they will, because they are everywhere, because the density is high, because they occupy every volume the architecture allows—you will feel something that is not merely physical. You will feel the weight of their duration, the million generations they have spent in this place, evolving toward and arriving at this form. You will feel the patience of their lives, the slow pulse of their existence, the way they fill the channels and basins not as water fills them—passively, by gravity—but as life fills them: actively, densely, with the specific gravity of being. They are warm, as the water is warm, and their warmth is indistinguishable from the water's warmth, and yet it is different—it is the warmth of metabolism, of slow chemical fires burning inside each soft body, of the geochemical energy extracted and converted and embodied. They are the warmth that does not move, made flesh. They are the darkness that does not lift, made alive.

They are aware of you. Not in any way that implies cognition, recognition, selfhood—but they are aware, in the way that all life is aware of disruption in its medium. Your body is warmer than the water, or cooler; your movement creates displacement, pressure waves that propagate through the dense, warm fluid; your chemistry is alien, a concentration of compounds and gradients that none of them have encountered in their million-year occupancy of this place. They will investigate you. Not with curiosity—curiosity implies a mind that can be curious—but with the fundamental response of organisms to novelty in their environment: approach, contact, assessment. They will drift toward you, drawn by the gradients you create. They will press against your skin, testing your surface with their surfaces, seeking apertures, pores, ways to absorb or be absorbed. The larger ones will be more insistent, their slow bulk pressing against you with the weight of warm water, their porous membranes seeking contact with your less-porous skin. The smaller ones will flutter against you in passing, a thousand near-touches, a precipitation of contact like silt in the current. For a moment—for a long, slow moment, measured in the patient time of this place—you will be part of the Congregation. You will be a novel object in their world, a gradient, a presence, a warmth and a displacement and a chemistry. They will know you as they know each other: by touch, by pressure, by the way you feel in the dark.

And then you will leave, or you will stay, and if you stay, you will become like them. Not quickly—nothing happens quickly here—but gradually, inevitably, as the warm water works on your body, as the mineral saturation seeps into your tissues, as the darkness and the patience and the slow circulation of heat through worn-smooth channels erodes your edges and rounds your transitions and dissolves your intentions into topology. This is what the Congregation is: the shape that life takes when it has been here long enough. They are not the first inhabitants of this place. They are not the last. They are simply the current ones, the stable ones, the ones that emerged when the machine ran long enough and will persist until the machine stops or the warmth fails or something from outside—something with light and sharp edges and the impatience of change—intervenes.

But nothing will intervene. The weirdness is low. The dynamic is minimal. This is not a place of events. It is a place of durations, of processes so slow they are nearly static, of cycles so long they have no visible beginning or end. The Congregation will continue. They will circulate through the channels, breed in the basins, feed in the dark, divide and grow and dwindle and dissolve, ten thousand or a hundred thousand or a million soft bodies in close proximity, feeling each other, pressing against each other, existing in the warm dark together. They will do this for another million years, or ten million, or however long the warmth holds. They will outlast everything that knows them. They are already older than any language that could name them. They will be here when the names are gone, when the languages are gone, when the very concept of naming is as worn away as the glaze on the ceramic walls.

This is who lives here. Not a species—a condition. Not inhabitants—a congregation. Not life—a mode of persistence, a way of being warm in the dark, a way of filling the architecture with motion and metabolism and the faint, collective vibration of existing. They are the final form of life in a finished place, the stable shape that emerges when all the pressures have had their say and all the changes have worn smooth. They are what remains when everything else is gone. They are the bottom of time, made soft and warm and alive, drifting in the dark, waiting for nothing, wanting nothing, being nothing except what they are: the fullness of the empty place, the motion in the stillness, the warmth that does not move.
