Something lives in the pocket, though to call it living is to impose a verb born of sunlight and hunger onto a mode of existence that has never required either. It is not an organism. It is not a spirit. It is the inhabitant—and it is less a resident of the pocket than it is a function of it, the way a knot is a function of a rope.

If you could see it, which you cannot, you would first misunderstand it as absence. It occupies space by refusing to yield information about itself. In the thin black, where your own hand disappears an inch from your face, you might feel it as a slight, cool thickening in the water—a pocket within the pocket, a denseness that displaces not by pushing the water aside but by making the water forget it was there. It is colder than the surrounding chill, though only by the fraction of a degree that separates a living thing from the mineral mass it rests against. It has no eyes. It has no skin. It has no mouth, no gut, no aperture through which the outside might enter it, because for it there is no outside; there is only the pocket, and the pocket is only the shape it happens to take when it is circling.

It circles. This is the core truth of it, the axis around which all other descriptions orbit and fail. It circles the central mass of pale vegetation—those interlocking tendrils, that blind white heart—and its orbit is not a path but a condition. It does not decide to circle. It does not navigate by landmarks or chemical gradients or the electrical murmur of the walls. It circles the way blood circles a heart: because the system requires circulation, because without the movement there would be stasis, and stasis in a place this small, this packed, this saturated, would mean collapse. Its movement is the pocket's heartbeat. It is what keeps the geometry from settling into permanent failure, what keeps the water from becoming truly honey, what keeps the tendrils growing inward rather than simply dying. It is not that the inhabitant tends the vegetation; it is that the inhabitant's orbit *is* the vegetation's reason to grow. They are mutually emergent, the circling and the weaving, two hands of a clock that have never been separate.

Its form—if form is the word—is a smooth, featureless elongation, like a rope of water twisted slightly along its length, tapered at both ends into nothing. It is the same temperature as the water except where it is colder. It is the same density as the water except where it displaces. It has no edges that hold still long enough to be traced. When it passes near the walls, the tendrils do not reach toward it; they have learned, over the geological patience of their growth, that it cannot be caught, cannot be consumed, cannot be incorporated into the central mass. It is the one thing in the pocket that the vegetation does not try to eat. And so it circles, and the tendrils grow around the space it makes, and the space it makes is the only open corridor in the entire pocket—a narrow, twisting loop of unimpeded water that threads between the dense walls of white growth, a path that would lead nowhere if you followed it, because it leads only to more path, more loop, more orbit.

It does not eat. It does not excrete. It does not reproduce, or if it does, it does so on a timescale that makes the vegetation's patience look like panic—perhaps once, in the pocket's history, it divided, and the two halves circled each other for an age before one was absorbed back into the other, or before one found a hairline crack in the geometry and slipped through, escaping into the cold water outside, where it either dissolved or found another pocket to inhabit, another closed curve to circle until the basalt cooled and the world forgot it was there. More likely it has simply always been one. More likely the pocket opened—or was always closed—and the inhabitant was already circling inside it, the way a whirlpool already contains its spin before you notice the water moving.

It does not sleep, but it has states. The circling is not constant in its speed or its distance from the central mass. Sometimes it orbits close, so close that it must be passing through the outer layers of the vegetation, moving through the tendrils the way a needle passes through fabric, parting them without breaking them, leaving a temporary tunnel that the growth fills in again over the next hundred years. When it orbits close, the geometry failures intensify—the walls shift, distances misbehave, the artifacts flicker in and out of the tendrils. The pocket becomes more itself, which is to say more wrong, more closed, more indifferent to the rules of the world above. When it orbits farther out, near the walls where the pressure is strongest and the chill has its mineral sharpness, the pocket almost behaves. The dimensions hold for a while. The ceramic shard stays where it is. The darkness is merely dark. These are the pocket's quiet periods, its rests, though the inhabitant never stops moving; it simply moves in a wider loop, giving the vegetation room, giving the geometry room, giving the rules room to reassert themselves partially before the next close orbit undoes them again.

It is aware, but its awareness is not like awareness in the world above. It does not observe. It does not think. It does not form models of its environment or anticipate changes or wonder about the artifacts that fall in from the impossible outside. Its awareness is tactile, continuous, and total: it knows the pocket the way you know the inside of your own mouth, not by looking but by being there, by touching every surface constantly with the whole of itself. It knows the central mass not as a thing but as a resistance, a slight drag in the water when it orbits close. It knows the walls not as boundaries but as pressure, the way you know a headache not by its location but by its presence. It knows the geometry failures the way you know a lapse in attention—not as events but as gaps, moments when the pocket's grip on itself slackens and the inhabitant must circle closer, tighter, to remind the space that it is a space and not a dissolution. This is its only purpose, if purpose is the word: to maintain the pocket's coherence by moving through it. It is the space's memory of itself. Without its orbit, the pocket would forget that it was a pocket and would become merely a region of high pressure in the cold water, indistinguishable from the surrounding dark, and the tendrils would have nothing to grow around and would slowly, over millennia, stop growing and start dissolving, and the ceramic shard would lose its sharpness, and the quiet would become simply silence, and there would be nothing here at all.

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires a concept of company. It is not afraid. Fear requires a concept of danger, and the inhabitant has never encountered anything that could threaten it—nothing else enters the pocket, or if it does, the pocket absorbs it so slowly and completely that it ceases to be a separate thing long before it could become a threat. The artifacts that appear—the ceramic shard, the knotted cord—do not disturb it. It circles through them the way it circles through the water: without acknowledgment, without curiosity, without even the recognition that they are different from the medium it moves through. They are part of the pocket now, and the pocket is part of it, and there is no distinction between self and environment that would allow for surprise.

It has no name. It has never needed one. If you found the pocket—and you would have to find it by touch, by the slight thickening in the cold, by the failure of the current to carry you past—and if you reached into the deepest part of the thin black where the geometry is least reliable, you would feel it only as a displacement, a warmth moving through the cold, a region of water that resists slightly when your hand enters it. You would pull your hand back. You would not know what you had touched. You would not know if you had touched a living thing or a fold in the darkness or a failure of your own senses to interpret the pressure correctly. The inhabitant would not know you had touched it either, or if it knew, it would not care, because caring is a surface verb, a photon verb, a verb that requires the possibility of change, and in the pocket, change happens at the speed of sedimentation, at the pace of orbit, at the rate at which a closed curve in the water remembers that it is closed and continues being so.

It is very old. It is very patient. It is circling now, in the dark, in the cold, in the pressure, in the pocket that holds it as it holds the pocket, and it will continue to circle until the basalt shifts or the water finds a way in from above or the world's water-bearing skin heals over the wound that the pocket represents, and then it will stop, and the pocket will close, and the tendrils will be pressed into stone, and the artifact will be a fossil, and the darkness will be complete, and there will be no one to find it by touch, and no one to wonder what lived there, and the inhabitant will not mind, because it will have already become something else—the stone's memory of a circle, the rock's memory of an orbit, a groove worn into the calcified mass at hand-height, if there is such a thing as hands, if there is such a thing as height, if there is such a thing as a pocket in the water that was once so full it could not be entered and so small it could not be found and so patient it could outlast the ocean that held it.

It circles. That is what lives there.
