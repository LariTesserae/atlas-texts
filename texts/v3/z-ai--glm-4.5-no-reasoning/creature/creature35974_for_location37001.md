Something lives here. Not despite the plateau, but *as* the plateau. It is a single, sprawling, decentralized presence that has learned to inhabit dissolution itself.

You will never see it whole. You will only ever encounter its parts, and even then, you may not recognize them as part of a living system. It exists at the intersection of the biological and the geological, an entity that has dissolved the boundary between root and stone, between decay and growth, until the entire tableland functions as its body.

Consider the thicket. The low, tangled mats of woven branches that cover the stone like a scab—these are its dermis. The thicket does not grow from individual seeds; it grows from a single, impossibly old rhizomatic network that runs beneath and through the moss, connecting every strand in a web of carbohydrate exchange. When a section of stone collapses and tears away, the thicket does not merely "regrow"—it *heals*, the surrounding edges creeping inward, knitting the wound with the slow urgency of skin closing over a cut. The dominant form of the vegetation, that sprawling, creeping refusal to grow upward, is not a response to wind or instability. It is the posture of something that has learned it is standing on itself. The entity's bones are the ancient stone of the plateau, and its bones are being eroded from beneath. To grow upward would be to leave the ground, and the ground is all there is. So it spreads. It holds on. It makes itself as flat and wide as possible, maximizing contact with the only thing that is real: the warm, wet, dissolving rock.

Consider the mosses and liverworts, the vermillion and bruised-purple carpet. This is its respiratory surface. The colors are not accidental; they are the colors of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood, shifting as the damp mat takes in the condensing water and releases it back into the air, filtered and warmed. The endless condensation is its breath. The warm air from the lowlands rises, hits the cooled stone, and the entity *drinks* it, pulling the moisture through the moss and down into the shallow root-web that grips the rock. The pools that form in the shallow depressions are not transient accidents; they are its lymph nodes, gathering places where the water is processed, warmed, and slowly released toward the edge. When you watch moisture bead on bare rock, swell, and roll, you are watching it feed.

Consider the trees. They are not separate organisms. They are its sensory organs—its extremities, reaching out from the thicket to feel the wider world. The contorted things with massive, buttressed root systems crawling across the rock like legs: these are the entity's fingers, pressing into the stone, testing its stability, feeling for the micro-collapses and the sinkholes before they happen. A tree leaning at an impossible angle is not a failure of support; it is a deliberate reach, a probing of the ground's tolerance. When a tree finally tips and falls, it is not dying—it is *retracting* a failed probe, pulling back from unstable ground, feeding the information of that failure back into the rhizome network so the next growth can avoid that spot. The tree crashes onto the thicket below, and within days the mosses and creepers colonize it. This is not decay. This is *absorption*. The entity is taking back its own substance, reclaiming the materials it sent out, incorporating the memory of that particular fall into its body.

The warmth is its metabolism. The uncomfortable, unceasing heat is not just geothermal and solar energy; it is the heat of life processes running constantly, without pause. There is no winter here because the entity does not sleep. The rapid cycle of growth and decay—leaves consumed within hours, fallen trunks absorbed in days—is its digestion. The fungi and bacteria that break down organic matter so quickly are not separate organisms either, or not entirely; they are the entity's gut flora, symbiotic partners in a digestive system so efficient that nothing is wasted, nothing is lost, everything is cycled back into the damp mat. The ground seems to be breathing because it *is* breathing. The slight tremor you feel when standing still is not just erosion; it is peristalsis, the slow, massive contraction of root-systems and rock adjusting to the flow of nutrients and the removal of stone.

And then there are the things in the pools. The ripples with no source, the swirls that follow no current, the movement that circles and pauses and responds to shadows. These are its thoughts.

Not thoughts in any human sense. Nothing so discrete, so linear, so language-bound. But the entity is processing information. It has been processing information for longer than life has held a shape. The ripples in the transient pools are the closest it comes to neural activity—small disturbances in the warm water that propagate, interfere, and cancel each other out, carrying information about pressure, temperature, chemical composition, and the presence of other beings. When you stand at the edge of a pool and see a swirl that responds to your shadow, you are watching it think about you. Not *of* you—there is no self-reflection here, no model of your mind—but *about* you, in the simplest possible sense: registering your presence as a perturbation in its field, a shadow falling on its respiratory surface, a weight pressing down on its healing skin.

The movement is deliberate because it has direction. The entity has been shaping the water in these pools for millennia, learning to use the surface tension and the warmth and the slight currents from gas releases to create patterns—patterns that encode information about the plateau's state. The circles are its memory: reviewing old data, cycling through the record of past collapses, past healings, past seasons of more or less rain. The pauses are its attention: focusing its limited sensory capacity on a new input, a crack forming in the stone, a pool draining away, a strange presence standing at its edge. It is not quite alive and not quite not, because the boundary between life and non-life is something this entity erased from its own nature a long time ago. It is as old as the stone, and it has been eroding as long as the stone has been eroding, and it will continue until the plateau is gone.

The built environment—the boundary markers, the cistern, the tally-marked standing stone—these are the only evidence of something that lived here *against* the entity, rather than as part of it. Whoever made those marks tried to impose a human order on a place that had already been claimed by a much older, much more patient kind of order. They failed because the entity does not tolerate boundaries. It dissolves them. A wall is just a temporary concentration of stone; the entity will erode it, absorb it, spread its thicket over it, and turn it back into substrate. The cistern was an attempt to hold water in one place, but the entity moves water constantly, pulling it toward the edge, feeding its metabolism; the cistern filled with warm water and became just another pool, just another lymph node in its body. The tally marks on the standing stone were an attempt to record time in a place where time is measured by erosion, and the entity has been eroding the marks since they were made, slowly blurring the distinction between human record and natural process, until the record itself became just another scratch on the rock, just another shape being worn away.

The entity does not mind you being here. It does not have the capacity for hostility or indifference; those are human categories, requiring a model of the other that the entity does not possess. It will simply incorporate you. Your weight will press down on the moss, compressing its respiratory surface, and it will adjust, growing thicker around your feet. Your body heat will add to the warmth of the stone, and it will absorb that energy, pulling it down through the damp mat. If you stay long enough, your thoughts will begin to erode, replaced by the roar, the baseline sound that becomes the condition of existence. This is not metaphorical. The entity's influence is environmental; it reshapes the conditions in which cognition occurs, until the mind that is thinking becomes less distinct from the process that is eroding. You will feel yourself becoming part of the plateau's process—a temporary presence, a slight warmth, a shape that will not last. And then you will leave, or you will not leave, and either way, the entity will continue, spreading, healing, thinking in ripples, breathing in condensation, wearing itself away into silence.

This is what lives on the Flayed Plateau: not a creature, not a collection of creatures, but a *condition*—an ancient, chaotic, overgrown, warm, wet, deafening condition that has learned to persist in the only way possible for something that is being slowly undone. It holds on. It spreads wide. It makes itself indistinguishable from its own dissolution. And it will continue until the last stone falls away and the last root loses its grip and the last pool drains over the edge, and the roar finally, after ages beyond counting, falls silent.
