The people of the Underbazaar are not a tribe, nor a clan, nor a family in any way the sunlit world would recognize. They are an accumulation. They are the sediment of human presence, settling in the low places where the damp pools, layering themselves into the niches and worn grooves of the stone until they are as much a feature of the architecture as the petrified mortar or the bone-colored bracket-fungus. They have lived here long enough that the boundary between inhabitant and habitat has blurred; they are shaped by this place as absolutely as the pale geckos are shaped by it, and if you removed them to open air and open sky, they would dry and crack and fail, like deep-sea things dragged to the surface.

---

**Their bodies** tell the story first.

They are not tall. Generations of low ceilings—the places where a raised hand brushes stone—have selected for compactness, or perhaps it is simply that the tall do not stay, do not fit, do not find the space habitable. The ones who remain move with a particular geometry: shoulders turned just so to pass through the narrow aisles, heads ducked by habit rather than necessity, a gait that is half-walk, half-controlled slide on the wetted terraces. They do not lift their feet high. The floor is uneven, slick, terraced; a high step is a risk, and so they shuffle, they glide, they move with the floor rather than across it. To watch them traverse the main chamber is to watch water find its path—not fast, not slow, but inevitable, responsive, flowing around the columns and the hanging fabrics and the abandoned baskets with a fluidity that comes from knowing every surface variation by feel.

Their skin is the color of the stone. Not by birth—whatever ancestries they carried have been written over by the environment—but by long immersion. The light here is grey-amber, diffuse, never direct, and the melanin of their skin has adjusted to a world where the sun is a rumor filtered through ceiling-shafts. They are pale, but it is not the pallor of illness or confinement. It is the pallor of adaptation: a skin tone that disappears against the wet walls, that makes them hard to see in the lower galleries where the light fails. Their hands and forearms are darker, stained by the trade-goods they handle—the dyes that mark the drainage channels have marked them too, leaving their fingertips and the creases of their palms colored with the permanent residue of whatever they have touched longest. An older woman who trades in the pale fungi has hands the color of old bone, the spores worked into her skin like a second pigment. A man who shapes the hand-fired bricks has forearms rust-orange with clay dust that has bonded with the oils of his skin until it will never wash free.

The damp is in them. Their hair hangs lank unless they bind it, and they do bind it—with cord, with strip of fabric, with the thin rootless vines cut from the ceiling joints, which are flexible when fresh and dry to a pale rigidity that holds all day. Their clothes are not dry and have not been dry in living memory. Fabric in the Underbazaar absorbs the moisture of the air and holds it against the skin, and the occupants wear this as a condition, not a discomfort. They do not seek dryness. They would not trust it if they found it. Dryness here would mean a failure of the ventilation, a closing of the shafts, a disruption of the breath of the place—and they are attuned to the breath, the hourly shifts of temperature, the condensation that beads and runs. They feel the changes in their joints, in the old injuries that never fully healed, and they read the weeping walls as others read the sky: for weather, for portent, for the regular heartbeat of the complex.

Their lungs are vast. Not visibly, not in the way of barrel-chested highlanders, but in capacity, in the slow deep rhythm of their breathing. The air here is thick—with spores, with insect-song vibration, with the organic residue of centuries of occupation. The cricket-thrum is not just sound; it is particulate, the air so charged with the physical exhalation of tens of thousands of insects that each breath carries a faint dust of chitin and song. The occupants breathe through it. Their respiratory systems have adapted, have developed whatever quiet immunities and filters and tolerances allow them to sleep while the air hums and the geckos drop wetly to the floor and the bats flicker through the shafts of grey-amber light. A visitor would cough. A visitor would wheeze and rub their eyes and feel the vibration in their chest as an intrusion. The occupants do not. They pull the thick air in as naturally as the rootless vines draw moisture, and their bodies use what the air provides and discard the rest without comment.

Their hearing is both acute and selective. They hear the crickets—of course they hear the crickets; the sound is inescapable—but they hear *through* them, the way a sailor hears the ocean as background and catches the specific slap of a loose line atop the roar. They hear the scrabbling of rats in the walls. They hear the particular click of a gecko detaching from stone. They hear the shift in the cricket-chorus that signals a pressure change from below—the moment when the larger thing moves and the insects pause and the silence opens like a wound. They hear one another's footsteps and can identify, by footfall alone, who approaches from behind the fabric wall. Speech here is raised but not loud, and the words blur at their edges as the description notes, but the occupants have compensated: they speak in a dialect of half-words and gestures, of significant pauses and positional language, of communications that rely as much on where you stand and what you carry as on what you say. They have learned to read the sound-silences the way a field-fox reads the gaps between bird-calls: for information, for danger, for the movement of prey.

---

**Their faces** are the faces of people who have never seen themselves clearly.

There are no mirrors in the Underbazaar. The still half-inch of water in the deepest hollows will reflect a face if you lean close, but the reflection is grey and distorted, a fun-house version of yourself that tells you nothing useful. The occupants do not lean close. They know their faces by touch—by the way their hands map the planes of cheek and brow in the dark, by the way their features fit against the worn stone of the sleeping-alcoves. The child who scratched the wall scratched a representation of something, but whether it was themselves or a parent or a creature from below, no outsider could say. The scratch marks are abstract, repeated, the visual vocabulary of someone whose visual vocabulary is limited to stone and fabric and the pale things that grow in damp.

Their eyes are adapted to the grey-amber light, which means they see well in it and poorly in anything brighter. A shaft of direct sunlight falling through a ceiling-joint is painful to them; they flinch, they turn away, they raise a hand as if to ward off a blow. In the lower galleries, where the light fails entirely, they navigate by sound and touch and the subtle variations in air-pressure that indicate a passage versus a wall. Their pupils are large, their irises pale, and in the dimness their eyes catch what light there is and reflect it back—a faint shine, like the geckos' eyes, like the wet film on the floor. They do not see color as the sunlit world sees it. The reds and blues of whatever goods they trade are muted to them, absorbed into the grey-green-bone spectrum of their world. They see texture instead. They see variation in darkness. They see the difference between a wall that is merely dark and a wall that is a passage into further dark, and they read this difference the way a navigator reads the sea.

Their expressions are subdued. Not because they do not feel—there is humor in the Underbazaar, and affection, and the particular sharp alertness that passes for fear in a place where fear is a constant low-grade condition, not an event—but because expression is wasted here. In a space where speech blurs and faces are half-visible, the economy of communication favors the minimal. A raised eyebrow. A shift of weight. The particular way someone sets down a basket, or does not set it down, or sets it down and leaves their hand resting on the rim. These are the expressions of the Underbazaar: small, precise, communicative to those who have learned the language and invisible to those who have not. A stranger watching a transaction between two occupants would see two stone-faced figures exchanging goods with no conversation, no eye contact, no visible affect. They would not see the negotiation that occurred in the timing, in the placement, in the half-second pause before the hand released. The occupants are not stoic. They are *encoded*. Their emotional lives are as rich and complex as anyone's, but the bandwidth of expression is narrow, and so they have learned to compress: to pack significance into gesture and silence and the precise angle at which a body stands in an alcove.

---

**Their trade** is not commerce as the sunlit world practices it.

They trade in small things—this is true—but "small" is a word that measures by sunlit standards. In the Underbazaar, a handful of pale fungi harvested from the lower galleries is not small. It is a medicine, or a delicacy, or a material with properties that the occupants know and do not name. The insects in woven cages are not small; they are a protein source that requires no grazing land, no feed, no water beyond the ambient damp, and they reproduce in the walls without encouragement, a self-sustaining larder that the occupants tap with the casual expertise of people who have eaten the same thing for generations and have developed the techniques—the preparation methods, the spice-substitutes, the ways of processing that render the palatable more palatable still—to make the practice sustainable.

The tools they trade are not manufactured in any centralized way. They are found, modified, adapted. A hammer-head made from a chunk of fallen column, a handle made from dried rootless-vine wrapped and re-wrapped until it forms a grip. A blade of hammered scrap-metal, rusted to the texture of stone, sharp enough for the work required and not sharp enough to be dangerous in any way that matters. The occupants are not smiths. They are not craftspeople in the sunlit sense, because the sunlit sense requires surplus, requires raw material in quantity, requires the luxury of specialization. Here, specialization is a vulnerability. Everyone does a little of everything, and the tools they use reflect this: generalized, multi-purpose, worn to the specific shape of the work they do but always capable of being repurposed, turned to the hand in a new orientation, made to serve a function they were not shaped for.

The pale fungi are their currency and their medicine and their mystery. The fungi that grow on the upper walls—pale shelves bracketing the column bases, delicate stalks rising from mortar cracks—are common, harvested regularly, traded openly. But the fungi brought up from the lower galleries are different. The occupants handle them with care. They do not speak of where they grow, or what they grow on, or what the lower galleries are like. They trade them to one another, not to outsiders—or if they trade them to outsiders, they trade them under a framing that is not quite false and not quite true: *medicine*, they say, or *a delicacy*, or *for the ceremonies*, and the outsider can accept this or not, but the truth behind the words is not available. The pale fungi from below are the material expression of the arrangement. They are what comes back from the deep place. They are the proof that whatever lives there will trade, will tolerate, will maintain the boundary between the inhabited levels and the deeper galleries in exchange for something—perhaps the occasional marked stone, perhaps the presence of the occupants themselves as a barrier between the below and the above, perhaps nothing that the occupants can name because the arrangement predates them and they inherited it from the sediment of their predecessors.

---

**Their children** are born here, and they are native in a way their parents may not be.

A child born in the Underbazaar has never known dry air. They have never heard silence. They take the cricket-thrum as the baseline condition of existence, and when they sleep—and they sleep deeply, as children do, wrapped in the constant sound as others wrap in blankets—their dreams are shaped by it. They dream of vibration. They dream of hum. They do not dream of fields or forests or open sky unless someone has told them stories of such places, and the stories they are told are not the stories of the sunlit world. They are told stories of the deeper galleries, of the thing that moves below, of the arrangement that keeps the boundary. They are told these stories not as warnings but as *instructions*. This is the place. This is how we live in it. This is what we do not do, and what we do, and what we carry when we go below, and what we bring back, and what we do not speak of to the people who come from above.

The child's scratches in the wall—mentioned in the description, a small detail among many—are the artifact of a childhood spent learning the architecture by touch. The children climb. They climb the columns, the spiral-wound stone, the petrified mortar harder than the stone itself, and they learn the handholds the way sunlit children learn trees. They climb to the ceiling and touch the rootless vines and sometimes pull them down and weave them into the structures they build in the alcoves—small structures, play-structures, miniature versions of the fabric walls and hanging baskets that divide the main chamber, learning by doing the work of partitioning, of claiming space, of making a place within a place. They catch geckos and let them go. They watch the insects in their cages and learn to distinguish the kinds by the sound they make, by the depth of their thrum, by the specific frequency that resonates in this column or that. They learn to read the walls. They learn to read the damp.

A child born in the Underbazaar is not afraid of the dark. The dark is a place, not an absence, and they move into it with the same confidence they move through the lit chambers—slower, more attentive, a hand on the wall, an ear cocked for the shift in sound that means a passage or a column or a drop. They are not afraid of the thing below because they have been taught the arrangement, and the arrangement is older than fear, older than the current occupants, a compact between the human and the whatever-else that has held so long it has become part of the architecture, part of the place, as foundational as the fitted stone and the petrified mortar. The children do not test the arrangement. They do not go below without the marked stone, without the tool, without the light-source. They do not go below alone. They learn this the way they learn not to step on the slick terraces: not through punishment, not through warning, but through the simple observation that this is how it is done, and it is done this way because it works, and it has worked for longer than anyone can say.

---

**Their old people** are the keepers of the arrangement, and they are different from the young.

Time in the Underbazaar is measured not in years but in surfaces worn. An old person here is not old by sunlit standards—sixty, perhaps, or seventy, the span compressed by the damp and the dark and the particular stresses of underground life—but they carry their age in their bodies in ways that make them seem ancient. Their joints are swollen with the damp that has worked into them over decades. Their spines are curved—not from the low ceilings, which they navigate without stooping, but from the particular posture of someone who spends their life looking down: at the floor, at the goods, at the drainage channels, at the pale fungi that grow at their feet. Their hands are the most telling. The hands of an old Underbazaar occupant are maps of the place: fingers worn flat at the tips from handling stone, palms crossed with lines that are not fate-lines but rope-lines, the creases stained and re-stained with dyes and spores and the residue of trade-goods until the skin is a palimpsest of occupation, every color of the Underbazaar represented in the geography of the hand.

They sit in the worn stone seats—grooves worn for buttocks, worn over generations, not by one person but by the accumulated weight of all the people who have sat in this spot to watch this alcove, to guard this passage, to sleep in this particular warmth near this particular vent. The seats are not assigned. They are claimed by habit, by the body's knowledge of where it fits, and an old person returning to the main chamber will go to their seat without thinking, will settle into the groove as if into a mold, and the stone will receive them as it received the generation before and will receive the generation after, because the stone is patient and the bodies are soft and this is what soft things do to hard places over time: they wear them, in their image, in their exact shape, leaving the negative of their presence for the next body to fill.

The old people are the ones who go below. Not often—but when the fungi are needed, when the arrangement requires acknowledgment, when something has shifted in the deep galleries and the compact must be renewed, it is the old who go. They carry the marked stone. They carry the light-source—some kind of oil-lamp, hand-fired, burning the rendered fat of the insects or some other fuel that the Underbazaar provides, a yellow flame that makes the deeper darkness reel back and show its textures for a moment before closing in again. They go, and they are gone for intervals that vary, and they return with the pale fungi and the holed stones and the living things they do not explain, and their faces when they return are the faces of people who have been somewhere that cannot be described in the language of the upper galleries, and they do not try. They settle into their worn seats and they say nothing, or they say something oblique—*it holds*, or *the same*, or *not yet*—and the others accept this and the life of the Underbazaar continues.

---

**Their relationship to the thing below** is the defining feature of their culture, and it is defined by its undefinition.

They do not name it. They do not personify it. They do not worship it or fear it or seek to understand it, because understanding implies a framework, and the thing below does not fit any framework the human mind can construct. It is larger than a person. It displaces air when it moves. It makes the floor-films ripple and the cricket-song pause and the pressure change in ways that the occupants feel in their ears and their sinuses and the fine hairs on their arms. It lives in the deeper galleries where the construction is older and rougher, where the mortar has crumbled to sand, where the air smells of ammonia and sweet rot and the concentrated waste of biological processes operating at a scale the upper galleries do not support. It is there. The occupants know it is there. They have always known it is there, because the arrangement has always been in place, and the arrangement is between *them*—or between whatever they were before they were what they are—and *it*—or whatever it was before it was what it is—and the arrangement holds because it has held, because holding is what it does, because the alternative to holding is not a possibility that the occupants entertain.

They do not speak of it to outsiders. This is not a rule, not a prohibition, not a secret in the way that secrets are kept in the sunlit world—with locks and oaths and the threat of punishment. It is simply not done. It does not occur to them to do it. The arrangement is so foundational to their existence that explaining it would be like explaining breathing—possible, technically, but why would you, and where would you begin, and what words would you use that would convey the experience of a thing you have done every moment of your life without reflection? The outsiders who come to trade—few, occasional, seeking the specific goods that the Underbazaar provides—ask no questions about the deeper galleries. They see the marked stones and the pale fungi from below and the old people who go and return with the particular silence of those who have witnessed something they will not name, and the outsiders accept this, because the outsiders are there to trade, and the trade is good, and the price of not asking is low, and the price of asking is unknown.

The arrangement is a boundary. The inhabited levels are the boundary. The occupants, by living where they live, by maintaining the market and the cooking-fire and the fabric walls and the sleeping-alcoves, are performing the arrangement. They are the membrane between the thing below and the world above, and this is their function, and it is a function they perform whether they intend to or not, because the arrangement is structural, architectural, as inherent to the complex as the fitted stone and the petrified mortar and the ceiling-shafts that admit the grey-amber light. Someone built this place. Someone built it all the way down, with no natural floor, no raw stone, no substrate—just made space descending into made space, and at some point in the descent the human scale ends and the other scale begins, and the transition is marked by the change in construction quality, by the ammonia smell, by the cricket-silence, by the movement of air that signals the passage of something large in a space not made for human passage. The occupants live at the transition. They are the transition. They are the seam where the human-made meets the other-made, and they hold it by existing, by occupying, by continuing the trade and the life and the arrangement that has held since before memory.

---

**They do not wonder** about what was here before the here was built.

This is perhaps the most alien thing about them, the thing that a visitor from the sunlit world would struggle with most. The visitor would look at the fitted stone and the petrified mortar and the construction that goes all the way down and they would ask: *who built this? Why? When? What was here before? What is below the below? What is the thing in the deep galleries, and who made the arrangement, and what does it mean that a market sits above a mystery and the people who live in the market do not seem to care?*

The occupants do not care. Not because they are incurious, but because their curiosity operates within the bounds of their world, and their world is the Underbazaar: the main chamber and the side aisles and the lower terraces down to the point where the light fails and the arrangement begins. Beyond that point, curiosity does not apply. Beyond that point, the architecture continues, and the thing moves, and the arrangement holds, and the questions the visitor asks are questions that have no answers because the place has no memory of a time when it was not this, and the occupants have no memory of a time when they were not here, and the thing below has no memory—or has a memory so different from human memory that the word *memory* does not apply—and the fitted stone goes down and down and down, made all the way through, and at no point does it become ground, and at no point does it become natural, and at no point does it become anything other than what it is: a construction, deliberate and thorough and old, older than the occupants, older than the arrangement, older perhaps than the thing below, or perhaps not, or perhaps the distinction between older and not-older is meaningless in a place where time is measured in surfaces worn and cricket-song shifts and the slow chemical etching of a bracket-fungus that has been in its spot so long it has shaped the stone beneath it.

The occupants live in this. They live in the completeness, in the made-ness, in the unsettling implication that someone built a volume the size of a cathedral and then filled it with chambers and columns and drainage channels and ventilation shafts and then gave it over to time and damp and insects and geckos and bats and fungi and people, and the people came and they stayed and they adapted and they built their fabric walls and their hand-fired ovens and they taught their children the arrangement and they grew old in the worn stone seats and they traded their pale fungi and their live insects and they did not wonder, because wondering is a luxury of those who live in places that have explanations, and the Underbazaar does not have explanations. It has the arrangement. It has the cricket-song. It has the grey-amber light and the weeping walls and the constant damp and the thing that moves below and the people who move above, and this is enough. This has always been enough. This will, in all likelihood, continue to be enough for as long as the fitted stone holds and the mortar remains petrified and the arrangement endures and the occupants persist in their occupation, wearing the stone in their image, groove by groove, generation by generation, until the seats are deep enough to hold anyone and the drainage channels are black enough to be permanent and the Underbazaar is more theirs than it ever was whoever-built-it's, because occupancy is ownership, and adaptation is creation, and the people of the Underbazaar have adapted so thoroughly to this place that they are, in the end, what lives here most truly: not the insects, not the geckos, not the bats, not the thing below, but *them*, the human sediment, the slow accumulation of presence, the living mechanism within the living mechanism, singing the cricket-song back to the crickets in the language of their own footsteps on the wetted stone.
