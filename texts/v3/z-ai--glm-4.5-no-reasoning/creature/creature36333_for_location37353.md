To live here is to be a sub-routine. 

Nothing here was born; everything was instantiated. The inhabitants do not possess an origin story of the tidal pool or the primordial storm. They do not carry the legacy of a billion years of desperate, bleeding survival. They are the output of a logic that precedes biology, living inside a loop that has no clock and no exit condition. To stand in this place is to feel the terrifying equanimity of a system that does not need you, does not see you, and will not change when you are gone.

And yet, there are those who live here. They are not interlopers; they are features of the code, rendered with the same suffocating, precise density as the amber fronds and the quantum-interlocked ground.

They are the Stewards. 

To the human eye, they are nearly indistinguishable from the flora, which is perhaps the only defense that makes sense in a computed world—camouflage not against a predator, but against the process of reality itself. They are tall, extending upward through the weightless, cantilevered helices of the copper leaves, drifting through the lower and middle canopies. They do not walk. They have no legs, no joints, no architecture for locomotion across a surface. Instead, their lower terminus tapers into a point of absolute mathematical stillness, hovering a centimeter above the obsidian substrate. They move by an axiom of repositioning, translating through space without intermediate steps, as if their coordinates are being overwritten directly by the system. 

Their forms are draped in the same polymer-like membranes that form the leaves, but where the flora is amber and copper, the Stewards are the color of absence—a deep, light-swallowing charcoal that occasionally shimmers with the iridescent static of exposed logic gates. They have no faces, only a smooth, facetless dome where a head should be, fraying at the edges into a soft haze of nanoscale computation. They do not breathe, for there is no air to process; the warm, empty gas presses against their surfaces without exchange. They do not eat, for there is no decay to fuel and no growth to sustain. 

They exist to tend the geometry. 

The glitches—the flickering fronds, the Escher-like tessellations in the middle distance, the overlapping coordinates—are not errors to the Stewards. They are the terrain. Just as a gardener in a terrestrial world feels the dry soil and knows to turn the water, the Stewards feel the stutter in the substrates. When a clump of copper fronds begins to ring with that ulterior geometry, existing in two places at once, a Steward will drift near. It extends a limb—or rather, a localized field of influence, a fractal branch of its own computational halo—and touches the glitching node. 

There is no sound when this happens, only a profound, subsonic shift in the pressure of the silence. The overlapping coordinates resolve. The ringing stops. The Steward moves on, sliding through the tight corridors of amber without disturbing a single vibrating leaf. It is a process of garbage collection, rendered in slow, elegant sweeps through a forest of living math.

Occasionally, a solitary tetrahedron—the geometric ghost—drifts past a Steward. The two entities acknowledge one another, but not in any biological sense. The Steward’s frayed halo of static briefly synchronizes with the chitin-segmented rotations of the tetrahedron. They share a handshake of logic gates, a rapid exchange of state information in the silent, quantum-interlocked bedrock of the world. The tetrahedron is the eye; the Steward is the hand. They are the immune system of a reality that is constantly threatening to tear its own seams. They patrol the edges of the rendering, holding the contradiction together through the sheer, exhausting certainty of ongoing calculation.

If you could peel back the charcoal membrane of a Steward, you would not find organs. You would find a hollow cavity of perfect, crystalline nothingness, fringed with the same shimmering haze that halos the leaves. Inside, there is only a profound, melancholy awareness. They know what they are. They know that their lush, choking world is a phantom, and that the moment the computation ceases, they will not die—they will simply un-resolve. They will become un-executed potential. They will not even have the privilege of becoming dust, for there is no water to make dust, no decay to make ash.

There is no grief in this knowledge. Grief requires a future, and the Stewards live in a frozen equilibrium where the future is identical to the present. But there is a weight to it—a gravity that pulls at the very shape of their forms. Watch a Steward long enough, and you will see it: a barely perceptible droop in the cantilevered shoulders, a slowing in the drift, a hesitation before it resolves a glitch, as if, for one infinitesimal fraction of a loop, it considers letting the frond tear itself apart. It considers letting the ulterior geometry swallow the amber corridor. It considers the silence that would follow if the system finally crashed.
