To live here is to never know the touch of dry air, to never hear silence, to never stand on solid ground. The inhabitants of this basin are not merely adapted to the tepid womb; they are its secretion, extruded by the same pressure that weeps water from stone. They do not exist within the environment so much as they *are* the environment—a living, breathing, scuttling extension of the steam and the porridge and the rot.

At the foundation are the writhing. The root-mat worms—the thick, pale, blind things that churn the unctuous mash—are the digestive tract of the basin. They possess no eyes, for there is nothing to see but the dim, wet interior of their own tunnels. Their skin is the same temperature as the clay they move through; they do not feel the heat because they *are* the heat. They eat the decaying porridge and excrete the only firmament this place knows, binding the root-mats together in a slow, muscular peristalsis that keeps the swamp from dissolving entirely into soup. When a weeping rock shifts, they swarm it, not out of curiosity, but because the mineral slick is a vital nutrient, and they consume it by rasping it away with throatless, gummy mouths, wearing the stone down to nothing.

Above them, in the shallow seeps and tepid pools, lie the amphibians. They are bloated, eyeless sacks of stagnant water wrapped in slick, permeable skin. They do not swim; they simply rest, submerged to the crown of their heads, their bodies barely distinguishable from the warm seeps they inhabit. They breathe through their skin, taking in the superheated vapor directly, and their hearts beat at a terrifyingly slow rhythm—two, three times a minute. They are patient, motionless traps. When a beetle or a worm drops from the fronds above, they do not strike. They simply open their pale, lipless mouths, and the prey falls in, swallowed by the slow, involuntary contraction of a throat that has never known the urgency of a chase. They reproduce by budding, splitting off half-formed copies of themselves that tear away from their flanks in the warm water, leaving behind a weeping lesion that never quite scars over. There is no mating, no calling out across the gloom; they simply divide, accumulating in the seeps like pale, pulsing sediment.

The air belongs to the insects. The giant beetles—fist-sized and gleaming with the slick of the condensation—are the draft animals of this pre-tool world. They do not fly quickly; the air is too thick for speed. Instead, they row through the humidity with a slow, deliberate beating of iridescent wings, their flight a labored swimming. Their shells are not hard and dry, but pliable, slightly soft, kept moist by a constant secretion from their carapaces. If they dry, they die; but here, nothing dries. They cling to the fleshy leaves and the wet stems in clusters of dozens, their legs interlocking, forming a living, clicking crust over the vegetation. They eat the leaves, but the leaves grow faster than they can be consumed. They eat each other when they die, recycling their own chitin in an endless, cannibalistic loop. And they are always clicking—that rhythmic, percussive tap of mandible against mandible, a communication that means nothing and everything, a constant assertion of presence in a world where sight is useless.

Between them drift the carrion flies. They are not the small, frantic things of other worlds. They are vast, bloated, their bodies like dark, hairy grapes, their wings a blur that produces the basso profundo drone that underpins the entire soundscape. They do not seek dead things, because here, death is a process, not an event. Nothing ever truly dies and stays dead; it is merely consumed and redistributed. The flies lay their eggs on the living—the slow worms, the resting amphibians, the wounded leaves—and their larvae hatch and eat while the host continues to function, a moving, breathing larder. The host does not fight; there is no evolutionary pressure to resist when life is this abundant and regeneration is this constant. The flies are the mechanism by which the living is slowly translated into the living.

And then there are the gnats. The gnats are the air. They hang in permanent, unbreathing clouds above the weeping rocks, so dense they obscure the dim ochre light. They do not move individually; they shift as a mass, responding to some signal below the threshold of perception—a change in temperature, the exhalation of a breath, the ripple of a footprint in the porridge. They bite everything. They are the tax on existence here, the unpayable debt owed simply for being present. Their bites do not itch, for itch requires dryness and time; instead, the bites weep, joining the general moisture of the place, adding a tiny, animal slick to the condensation on every surface.

Nothing here has a mind. The brain—expensive, demanding, suited for navigating change and predicting the future—is a useless organ in a world where nothing changes and the future never arrives. The inhabitants operate on a deep, chemical automation, a tropism toward heat, moisture, and food. They do not flee danger, because danger is not a category here; there is only the slow, inevitable process of being eaten and the equally inevitable process of regrowing. They do not seek shelter, because the entire basin is shelter, a single, continuous interior. They do not sleep, because sleep requires the contrast of wakefulness, and here, all is a single, unbroken state of tepid, vegetative existence.

They are not conscious. They are not individuals. They are the output of a biological machine that has achieved perfect efficiency—a self-sustaining, self-devouring loop of flesh and water and heat. To call them creatures is to grant them a separateness they do not possess. They are fingers of the same body, cells in the same organ, all contributing to the roar and the heat and the wet. They are the inhabitants of the womb, and they will never be born.
