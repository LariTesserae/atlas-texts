To live here is to be native to a throat.

The people of this city—if people they remain—have been shaped by the slow oven and the immense pressure until they are something adjacent to human, something mineral in its patience. They are the fauna of an unfinished engine, and they bear the marks of a world where nothing has been rounded by time. Their edges, too, are sharp.

Consider their hands. The grease that slicks the elevator shafts is also under their fingernails, worked so deep into the whorls of their skin that it has become a kind of carapace. Their palms are calloused not from soft, repeated friction, but from the razor-edged railings and the unyielding basalt, the micro-abrasions of a world that has never learned to be gentle. Their skin has taken on a tone to match the city: a gray-brown pallor, slicked with the constant film of condensation and soot, plated with a greasy carbon residue that no amount of scrubbing will lift. They are waterproofed by their own filth. When the anomalous, warm drizzle falls from the city's upper exhaust, it beads off their shoulders like water off a newly bladed prow.

They do not speak much, these dwellers. Speech is a gradient, a movement of air, and this is a place of weak gradients. Their vocal cords, subjected to the constant sub-audible thrum that vibrates in the molars and breastbone, have adapted into something lower and more resonant. When they do communicate across the ravine-streets—barely wide enough to pass shoulder-to-shoulder—they do so in a series of low clicks, guttural stops, and harmonic hums that carry through the iron and stone better than through the thin, superheated air. They feel each other's footsteps through the cantilevered walkways before they see each other. They are becoming part of the city's circulatory tangle, another set of vibrations running through the pipework.

Their eyes are an exercise in redundancy and adaptation. The ambient gray-white light filtering through the thermal haze is diffuse, casting no sharp shadows, but it is ever-present. There is no night here, only the varying thickness of metallic overhangs. Pupils that once dilated and contracted have learned to exist in a state of permanent narrow slit, guarded by heavy, lowered lids. But in the deep guts of the city, where the sun never reaches and the only illumination is the threatening cherry-red of dormant blast furnaces and the blue-white crack of arcing junctions, the eye surrenders its reliance on light. The dwellers of the lower tenement stacks navigate by the thermal differential itself, sensing the weeping, mineral-heavy walls through the subtle shifts in air pressure and the radiating heat on the skin of their cheeks. They have not gone blind, but they have evolved a new sense, a kind of thermoreception that maps the city's slow breathing. They can feel a coolant pipe sweating from three meters away. They know when a blast furnace is cycling up by the way the iron floorplates pull at the hairs of their arms.

They are not builders, not in the way we understand it. They are maintainers, inhabitants of an embryonic organ grown in a vat of iron and heat. The giant bore-machines clinging like ticks to the rock faces were not operated by them; they were found, or inherited, or perhaps simply encountered, already mid-process, already embedded. The dwellers do not question the unfinished districts, the scaffolding reaching up hundreds of meters to nowhere, the empty window frames looking out onto raw cliff faces. This is not abandonment to them, nor is it a promise. It is simply the state of things. The city is still being born, and they are the white blood cells in its slow, tectonic circulation, keeping it alive long enough to reach whatever final shape it is meant to take over a timescale no human lifespan will see completed.

And so their labor is quiet, nearly motionless, a patient process. They tend to the condenser coils humming beneath the walkways. They monitor the low, static thrum of electricity, making minor adjustments with wrenches as long as their arms, tightening a valve here, releasing a pressure build-up there. They watch the cherry-red glow of the furnaces, ensuring it does not flare into urgency, ensuring the city continues to burn without roaring. Their movements are economical to the point of stillness. To move quickly is to sweat, and to sweat is to lose water, and water is a sporadic, almost clandestine presence. They drink the dark, mineral-heavy drip that pools in the hollows of unused valve-casings, the oily trickles that run down the stairwells. It tastes of rust and basalt and the deep thermal bleed of the planet. It sustains them, though it makes them slightly magnetic; sometimes, in the lower galleries, a dweller's hair will slowly rise and cling to an overhead steam main, drawn by the ambient charge, and they will have to gently peel themselves away before moving on.

They do not sleep in beds. The heat makes horizontal rest an oppression, a pressing into the metal that feels like being cooked. Instead, they recline in the narrow crevices between the vibrating pipes, propped at angles where the condensation drips onto their chests and the thermal differential creates a small, precious pocket of cooler air. They do not sleep as we do, falling into unconsciousness; they enter a kind of idle mode, a low-power state where their breathing synchronizes with the tick-tick-tick of cooling ducts contracting in the shade and the hollow plink of water in a steel drum. Their eyes remain half-open, watching the cherry-red dark. They are part of the city's dream.

There are no children here. Or at least, none that an outsider would recognize. The dwellers do not reproduce in the midst of this sterile, absolute mineral world; there is no carbon to spare, no soft tissue to offer. But neither do they seem to age in the way of soft creatures subject to time's rounding hand. They emerge, fully formed, from the deepest, hottest sections of the city, pulled like ingots from the weeping walls, coated in the same greasy soot, knowing by instinct the paths through the honeycomb and the frequency of the tectonic groan. Perhaps they are manufactured, though no one sees the factories that produce them. Perhaps they bud, slowly, calcifying in the heat over long decades until they crack free from the condensation-slicked stone. Or perhaps they simply *are*, an intrinsic byproduct of the city's own continuation, precipitating out of the superheated air and the mineral water like the pale, eyeless drakes precipitate out of the slag.

And like the drakes, they are subsumed by the city's function. They crawl with agonizing slowness from one heat gradient to another, tending to the slow-motion eruption of architecture. They are not its masters. They are its microbiome, its necessary flora, keeping the throat of this vast, mechanical volcano clear so that it can continue to barely hold its breath.

To look upon them is to see the city looking back. They are iron and heat and patience, the living equivalent of the razor-sharp basalt. They are what lives in a place that does not sleep, yet does not wake: a people who are not waiting, for there is nothing to wait for, but who are simply *enduring*, one slow, tectonic heartbeat at a time, in the sweating darkness of the unfinished world.
