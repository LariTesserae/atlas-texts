To live here is to have never known the sun, the sky, or the swift movement of a heartbeat. Whatever inhabits this place is not merely resident; it is the final, sluggish consciousness of a world that has outlived its own dynamics. Life here has been distilled down to a single, pervasive vegetative mind—a pale, fleshy omnipresence that knows nothing of sight or sound, only the patient, chemical hum of existence against the stone.

They are the great, blind coilers. Without eyes, without the capacity for light, they have become bodies of pure tactile sensation. They feel the world as an unyielding geometry of smooth, eroded masonry pressing against their slick, cold flesh. The ribbony kelp-analogs that wrap the cyclopean pillars are their extended sensory tendrils, reading the slow, million-year heartbeat of the ocean through the faint vibrations of settling stone. They do not reach toward a sun; they reach inward, threading their rhizomes through the interlocking megalithic blocks, binding the primitive architecture together with their own calcified roots. They are the mortar now, holding the drowned city in its frozen equilibrium.

They are the bloated, translucent feeders. In the staggering, pressurized dark, they hang from the low, flat ceilings and choke the step-wells in overlapping shelves. They are lethargy incarnate. To exist just above the freezing point, where molecular motion crawls, is to live in a state of near-suspension. Their metabolisms are glacial; they bleed their viscous sap upward in the sluggish currents, and they grow a centimeter a century. They do not think in thoughts; they think in chemical gradients, tasting the slow leaching of the hand-carved rock, drinking the mineral richness of the primordial syrup. They are patient beyond the comprehension of surface dwellers, enduring the infinite epoch by simply refusing to die, expanding cell by cell in the crushing hydrostatic embrace.

They are the suffocating, beautiful necrosis. Layered six deep in the passageways, they are a single, continuous organism of reclamation—a pale jungle of the deep caldron that has swallowed the hand-made earth. They remember the builders, not as architects, but as a distant, chemical echo. The crude monolithic pillars and terraces are their only substrate, their naked rock. They have smothered the obsession of a million lifetimes of chisel and haul under a cold, slick canopy of sponge and frilled frond. They are the grave flowers for a fauna that vanished eons ago, blooming in a darkness where chlorophyll is a forgotten myth and bioluminescence never evolved, for there is nothing here to signal, nothing to see.

To be alive in this realm is to be a monolith of flesh. The inhabitants do not dart or dash or sing; they merely persist, locked in a viscous cycle of slow growth and imperceptible decay. They are as dense and packed as the architecture they consume, filling every square meter of the drowned world. They feel the unimaginable weight of the atmospheric ocean pressing down upon them, and they yield to it, flattening, calcifying, becoming dense and heavy. When the faint, low-frequency groan of a settling megalith reverberates through the water, they collectively stir—a subtle, rustling exhalation of frilled edges brushing against rounded stone, the immense, patient waters breathing through their bodies.

They are the final inhabitants of a place at the end of time. There is no evolution left for them; they have reached the oldest stable state. They are the pale, blind watchers in the velvet dark, waiting out the final epoch with the stoic patience of stone and the slow, insistent hunger of the deep.
