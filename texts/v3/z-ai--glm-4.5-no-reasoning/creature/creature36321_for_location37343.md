To live in the Comburan Deep is to live inside a lung that has forgotten how to exhale.

You do not *reside* here; you are metabolized by it. You are a transient pressure differential, a warm eddy in an ocean of chill, moving through a city that was never built for you—or, perhaps, for anything with a sense of self as you understand it. The coraloids arch overhead like the ribs of some vast, sleeping beast, and though you know they are chitin and cellulose, your primitive brain insists on the rising and falling of a breath. But the air is still. It has been still since before the continents knew where to drift. 

You are a creature of the surface, and so you think in terms of what is missing. You catalog the absences: no rain, no sun, no green, no silence, no decay. But the inhabitants of the Deep—the true locals, the ones whose ancestry stretches back to the eon before the oxygen catastrophe—do not know the word *lack*. They do not miss the rain because they have no conception of water falling from above; their fluids run on ammonia and ancient chemistry, thick and amber, sluggish in the cold but never freezing. They do not miss the sun because the dark red glow of the gas-bladders is the only light that has ever existed, and to them, it is blindingly sufficient. They do not miss silence because the cacophony is the texture of existence itself; to be in the Deep is to be embedded in sound the way surface creatures are embedded in air. 

The *resonators* are the heartbeat of this world. Anchored to the great coraloid columns, their barrel-shaped bodies pulsing with the slow, deliberate rhythm of geological time, they are not merely making noise—they are stating their coordinates in a three-dimensional darkness. Each boom is a declaration: *I am here, I claim this vibration, this frequency is my lineage.* When you stand among them, the basso profundo does not just wash over you; it rearranges you. Your sternum vibrates in sympathy with a creature that has never seen the sky. For a moment, you are synced to a metabolism that runs on argon and sulfur, and you feel the absurd fragility of your own water-based existence. You are a soap bubble drifting through a cathedral of stone.

Beneath the resonators' thunder, the *scuttlers* are the static of the world's neural network. They are the restless thoughts that never cease, the ground-level processing of a city that is still, in some vestigial way, computing. They move on forty-two legs or six, clicking and scraping over the pale evaporite floor that crunches like old bone. They are always feeding. In a place where decay is glacial, where a fallen organism might lie compressed for a million years before it is subsumed by the next generation, feeding is a patient, methodical act. They are not scavenging; they are archiving. They break down the dead into forms the vegetation can reclaim, perpetuating a cycle so slow that a human lifespan is not even a tick in their clock. To watch a scuttler is to watch a machine executing a function it has executed since the Archean. There is no hesitation in its movement, no curiosity, only the relentless imperative of chemical equilibrium.

And then there are the *callers*, the ones who speak through the bones of the earth. You feel them before you hear them—a tremor in the soles of your feet, a shiver running up the mineralized columns and into your knees. They are shaking the substrate itself, using the planet as a resonant chamber. Their calls are not territorial in the way a wolf's howl is territorial; they are seismic claims. They own the vibration of the rock. When they speak, the whole Deep listens, not with ears but with the sensitive undersides of a thousand species, with the filamentous curtains of vegetation that sway imperceptibly in the induced tremors. The callers measure their lives in the forty-seven-hour cycle of loud and less-loud. They do not wake and sleep; they swell and contract with the acoustic tide.

You walk through the avenues, wide enough for a parade of leviathans, yet occupied only by the rubbery maroon mats and the rust-veined bulbs. The city feels empty because you are looking for *architects*. You are looking for the hands that shaped the arches, the minds that decreed the spacing of the columns. But the Deep has outlived its gods. The intelligence that guided the coraloids' tropism was not a mind but a tide, a chemical gradient that pulled and pushed over millennia, resulting in a structure of breathtaking, accidental intention. The city is a fossil of a behavior, and the things that live here now are the scavengers of that fossil. They do not wonder at the vaulted ceilings. They do not ponder the meaning of a corridor that ends in a blank wall of fused chitin. The city is merely the terrain they have adapted to, as unquestioningly as a mountain goat adapts to a cliff face.

The vegetation is the skin of the Deep, and it is the most alien thing of all. It is lush in the way that a tumor is lush—a proliferation of matter so dense it becomes its own ecosystem. The dark oxidized purple and the rust-orange sap are the colors of a blood that has never seen oxygen. The gas-bladders glowing faintly in the 650-nanometer range are not providing light for anyone's benefit; they are venting, they are breathing, they are performing a metabolic exchange that happens to shed photons into the dark. You navigate by these dim crimson lanterns, moving from one faint glow to the next, trusting the bioluminescence not because it illuminates your path, but because it is the only thing that separates the solid column from the open air.

Eventually, you stop thinking of the Deep as a place. It is a condition. It is the state of having arrived at the end of all variables. The temperature does not change. The chemistry does not change. The sound does not change. The city does not change. You realize that the inhabitants of the Comburan Deep are not living their lives; they are *being* their lives. They are fixed points in a static system, perfect expressions of an equation that solved itself three billion years ago. 

They do not fear extinction, because extinction requires a change in the parameters, and the parameters here are etched in stone and time. They do not hope, because hope is a response to uncertainty, and there is no uncertainty left. They do not love the Deep, because love requires a distinction between the self and the environment, and they are as much a product of this cold, loud, dark purple world as the coraloids and the mineral floor. 

When you finally leave—if you leave—you will carry the crunch of the evaporite in your ears and the phantom vibration of the resonators in your chest. You will step into the rain and the sunlight, and it will feel like an assault. For a terrible, beautiful moment, you will understand what it means to live in a place that has finished becoming, and you will miss the sound of a world that has never known silence.
