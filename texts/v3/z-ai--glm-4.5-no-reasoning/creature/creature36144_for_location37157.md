# What Lives on the Meridian Plateau

---

Before you can name what lives here, you must unlearn what living means.

You arrive carrying your definitions like heavy luggage—metabolism, reproduction, response to stimulus, the whole taxonomy of vitality you learned from wet places, green places, places where life announces itself with chlorophyll and blood and sound. You will leave without that luggage. The plateau will not take it from you; it will simply let you set it down, and when you reach for it again, you will find you no longer want it.

---

## The Mats

Start with what is almost not alive.

The dark patches on the rock—they have no name, and any name you give them will be a kind of violence, a forcing of these things into a language they have never needed. But you must call them something to think about them, so call them the mats, and know that the word is wrong.

A mat is a circle no larger than your palm, sometimes smaller, sometimes the size of a spread hand, never larger. It is black-green, or green-black—the color occupies an unstable region of the spectrum where your eye cannot settle on a category. In the morning, when the condensation lies on the rock, the mat is slightly darker than the wet stone around it, and this is the only way to see it from any distance: as a patch of deeper darkness, a place where the light does not quite reflect the same way. You walk toward it. You kneel. You put your face close enough to smell the rock—mineral, old, the scent of something that has been cooling for four billion years—and there, in that intimate range, the mat resolves into texture. It is not a film. It is not a coating. It is a *structure*, or the ghost of one: a dense tangle of threads so fine they might be single cells, might be strands of hyphae, might be something that has no analogue in any biology you know, all interwoven and pressed flat against the stone by the weight of eons, by the accumulation of its own slow growth.

The threads are not random. If you had a microscope and the patience of a geologist, you would see that they branch, that they anastomose, that they form a network with a pattern that repeats at different scales—fractal, almost, but not quite, the self-similarity breaking down at a certain resolution, introducing a new motif that itself almost repeats. The pattern is not decorative. It is functional, or it was functional once, or it is functional still in a way that does not correspond to any function you can imagine. The threads follow the micro-cracks in the rock. They bridge the tiny pits where condensation lingers longest. They thicken at the intersections of quartz veins, where two different minerals meet and the chemistry of the stone changes over a distance of microns. The mat is reading the rock. It is mapping it. It is growing into the landscape of the surface at a scale the landscape was not prepared for, filling in details that the rock itself has forgotten.

How old is a single mat? This is the wrong question. A mat does not have an individual lifespan. It does not die in the way that a plant dies, or a person. It grows—millimeters per decade, centimeters per millennium—and as it grows, its center may thin, may dry, may become a stain rather than a living thing, while its edge continues to advance. The mat you see is a wave of living material spreading outward from some point of origin, leaving behind it a residue of dead matter so thin it is indistinguishable from the mineral dust that coats everything. The mat is a front. The mat is a migration. The mat is a slow explosion of dark green-black life that has been radiating from its origin point since before the last ice age, before the ice age before that, before the idea of ice ages had occurred to the planet as something it might want to try.

And there are billions of them. Not scattered, not sparse—*dense*. Every square meter of the plateau has its mats, dozens of them, overlapping at their edges or separated by millimeters of bare stone. They do not compete. Competition requires scarcity, requires the urgency of not-enough, and there is enough here—enough space, enough condensation, enough time. Each mat takes what it needs from the morning's moisture and spends the rest of the day in a state of suspended animation, its metabolism slowed to a rate that is measurable only by the most sensitive instruments, or by the gradual accumulation of its growth over spans no instrument has been watching. At night, when the temperature drops and the condensation forms again, the mats drink. They do not drink as you drink, with gulps and swallows. They absorb. The moisture moves into the threads by capillarity, by osmosis, by some process that may be neither, and the threads swell imperceptibly and their chemistry quickens and for a few hours they are fully alive, and then the sun comes and they wait, and the sun goes and they drink, and this has been their rhythm since long before rhythm had a name.

Do they reproduce? Yes. Not as a favor to themselves, not out of any drive, but because the physics of their structure makes it inevitable. A thread extends beyond the mat's edge. It finds a micro-crack, a pit, a grain of dust rich in organic residue. It takes hold. It begins to grow. In a thousand years it will be a mat the size of your thumbnail. In ten thousand, the size of your palm. In a hundred thousand—well. The plateau has time. The plateau has nothing but time, and the mats have learned, or they have never needed to learn, that patience is not a virtue but a condition of existence.

## The Orange Threads

In the deeper depressions—the ones that hold rainwater for hours instead of minutes, the ones that are almost bowls, almost catchments—there is something else.

You would miss it if you did not know to look. The mats are everywhere, but the orange threads are only in these low places, these slight hollows where the water sits and warms and sits and vanishes. And even there they are rare—a depression that seems perfectly suited may have none, while another, indistinguishable, may have dozens.

A thread rises from a mat. It is two centimeters tall, or one, or three, and it is as thin as a human hair, thinner, and it is orange. Not the orange of a sunset or a fruit or a traffic cone—this is a denser orange, an orange that seems to contain its own light, that vibrates against the dark rock with a chromatic intensity that is almost painful after the grayscale of everything else. The plateau has been withholding color, hoarding it, refusing to offer anything but black and gray and the faintest green-black, and then here, rising from a patch of mat in a shallow bowl, is this flag, this beacon, this *announcement* of orange, and the effect is not aesthetic but cognitive: your brain does something unexpected, re-calibrates, remembers that the world contains wavelengths it has not been processing.

The thread bears a structure at its tip. It is not a leaf—leaves have veins, have symmetry, have the grammar of photosynthesis written in their form. It is not a flower—flowers are advertisements, and there is nothing here to advertise to. It is a small, irregular mass, no bigger than a pinhead, slightly lobed, slightly glossy, and it is the same orange as the thread but more so, as though the color has been concentrated here, distilled, made essential. Inside the mass—if you could open it, if you could section it and stain it and put it under a lens—you would find spores. Or something like spores. Or something that serves the function of spores without being spores, tiny packets of condensed living matter, each one a potential mat, a possible origin point, a compressed map of the pattern that will unroll over millennia if it finds the right micro-crack in the right rock on the right morning.

The rain comes. The threads grow from the mats in the depressions. The structures at their tips mature, darken slightly, split. The packets release—not explosively, not dramatically, but slowly, one by one, over the course of days, each one falling or drifting the millimeter to the rock surface, each one settling into a micro-crack or a grain of dust and beginning the long process of becoming a thread, becoming a mat, becoming a patch of dark green-black on the ancient stone. And then the depression dries, and the threads wither, and the structures at their tips curl and brown and crumble, and the organic matter returns to the dust, and the dust waits for the next rain, and the cycle continues, and has continued, and will continue.

The orange is a mystery. Not chemically—you could analyze the pigment, identify the carotenoid or the mineral or the strange hybrid molecule that produces this exact wavelength. But functionally. Why orange? The mats are nearly invisible. The congregants are nearly invisible. Everything here has invested in not-being-seen, in blending with the dark rock and the dark condensation and the dark dawn. And then the threads are orange, conspicuously, defiantly, as though they have opted out of the camouflage that everything else wears. Perhaps it is incidental—a chemical byproduct of the reproductive process, a color that serves no purpose and carries no cost. Perhaps it is ancient—a signal that once meant something to something that no longer exists, a flag planted for a pollinator or a disperser that died out when the plateau was young and the world was different. Perhaps it is a message, and you are not the recipient, and no one you can imagine is the recipient, and the orange threads have been broadcasting on a frequency that nothing has received for a million years, and they do not know to stop, and they will not stop, and the plateau will be dotted with orange long after you have forgotten what color means.

## The Congregants

And now the difficult part.

You have been putting it off. You have described the mats and the threads because they are comprehensible—strange, yes, slow beyond human experience, yes, but still within the extended family of the living, still recognizable as something that grows and reproduces and dies. The congregants are not like this. The congregants are the reason the plateau is not merely barren, and they are the reason you will not sleep well after you leave, and they are the reason that "life" is a word you will have to reconsider.

You see them first in the morning, or you think you do. The condensation has formed. The rock is glossy. You are standing still, looking at the horizon, feeling the wrongness of the angle in your inner ear, and something moves at the edge of your vision. You turn. There is nothing. You look back at the horizon. It moves again. You look down.

The rock is moving.

Not the rock—the *surface* of the rock, the thin layer of condensation on the rock, is moving, and it takes you a long moment to understand that what you are seeing is not water flowing but something in the water flowing, something dark, something numerous, something that covers the wet stone in a shifting, iridescent film of presence.

A congregant—study one, if you can, if your eye can isolate a single entity from the multitude—is small. The size of a grain of rice, or a lentil, or a closed fist. There is every size between these extremes, a continuous spectrum of scale that suggests growth without stages, expansion without molting, as though the congregant simply becomes larger by adding to itself, by extending its boundary, by being more of what it already is.

And what it is—that is the question.

It is dark. Darker than the rock, which is itself dark beyond dark, a gray so deep it reads as black. The congregant is darker still. It is the darkest thing on the plateau, the darkest thing you have ever seen, a darkness that seems to absorb light rather than reflect it, a darkness that is not a color but an absence, a hole in the visual field, a place where your eye goes and finds nothing to process and sends back a signal of *there is something here but I cannot see it*. Against the wet rock in the morning light, the congregants are visible only as patches of deeper darkness, as places where the glaze of condensation is somehow less reflective, and you realize that you are not seeing them directly but inferring them from the way they modify the surface, the way they drink the water and darken the stone beneath them.

They move. This is the thing that you must understand, and the thing that you will struggle to understand, because their movement is not like any movement you have seen. They do not have legs. They do not have cilia. They do not have pseudopods in the recognizable sense, those blunt finger-like extensions that an amoeba pushes forward to drag itself across a surface. A congregant extends itself—yes. But it extends itself *flowingly*, as though it were a liquid, as though the boundary between self and not-self were not a membrane but a meniscus, a surface tension that can be locally overcome to allow a portion of the congregant's substance to spill forward while the rest waits, and then the waiting portion contracts, pulls itself after the spilled portion, and the congregant has moved.

It is not fast. By your standards, it is barely motion at all—a few centimeters per hour, a distance you could cross in a stride taking a morning to traverse. But it is not random. The congregant moves toward moisture. It moves toward the condensation, extending itself across the film of water, absorbing it—drinking it, you realize, drinking the way a cloth drinks a spill, by contact and capillarity and the physics of wet surfaces touching wet surfaces. And as it drinks, it grows, or it seems to grow, its dark substance expanding slightly, becoming slightly less dense, as though the water it absorbs is being incorporated into its mass, diluting it, making it more.

And then the sun rises, and the condensation evaporates, and the congregants—

This is where it becomes strange.

*They vanish.*

Not all at once. Not dramatically. They thin. Their dark substance, so opaque in the morning, becomes translucent, then transparent, then—nothing. Or not nothing, but something below the threshold of perception, something that your eye cannot resolve, something that may still be there, on the rock, in the micro-cracks, in the film of dust and organic residue that coats every surface, but that has shifted into a form that does not interact with visible light. You stare at the rock where a moment ago a thousand congregants were flowing and drinking, and now there is only stone, only the faint stain of moisture being lifted away, only the heat beginning to shimmer, and you do not know where they have gone.

You tell yourself they have retreated into the micro-cracks. This is a reasonable hypothesis. The cracks are there—millimeter-scale, sub-millimeter-scale, a whole universe of shelter at a scale you cannot examine without instruments you do not have. The congregants are small enough to fit. They could be there, crowded into the invisible spaces, waiting for the next condensation, the next morning, the next opportunity to drink. This is what you tell yourself, and it is comforting, and it may even be true.

But some of them do not vanish. Some of them stay.

## The Stayers

A fraction. A residue. A few per thousand, or per ten thousand—it is impossible to count, because the vanished congregants cannot be counted, and you do not know the denominator. But some remain visible through the heat of the day, moving even more slowly than before, their dark substance barely flowing, barely extending, conserving whatever they conserved by not vanishing with the others.

They are in the shallow depressions. This makes sense—the depressions hold moisture longer, the condensation lingers in the low places, the stayers are the ones who found water and kept it and did not need to retreat or become transparent or do whatever the others did. They are drinking, still, from the last thin film of wetness on the stone, and they will drink until it is gone, and then they will—

Then they will gather.

This is the behavior. This is the thing that has no explanation, the thing that turns the plateau from a curiosity into a mystery, the thing that you will think about at night when you are far from here and the world is familiar and you will remember that somewhere, on a flat dark stone older than life, small dark things are gathering at specific points for specific reasons and no one knows why.

The gathering is not random. This is the first and most important fact. You walk the plateau in the afternoon, when the stayers are visible, and you see them accumulating in certain spots—ten, twenty, a hundred congregants pressing together, their boundaries merging and separating and merging again, forming a dense dark mass that is darker than any individual congregant, a darkness squared, a darkness multiplied, a darkness that is the product of many small darknesses combining into one large one. And you walk further, and you find another such spot, and another, and between them—nothing. Bare rock. The occasional mat. The occasional orange thread. But no congregants, or only solitary ones, flowing alone, not gathering.

What marks these spots? You look. You kneel. You run your hand over the rock. It is the same—dark, polished, crossed by thin veins of quartz, pitted by micro-cracks. The angle is the same. The exposure is the same. There is no visible feature, no anomaly, no marker. And yet the congregants find them. They find them every day, the same spots, the same points on the vast and featureless plateau, and they gather there, and they build.

---

## The Building

The gathering is not static. The congregants do not simply accumulate and sit. They accumulate and *produce*.

The process is slow—you will use that word many times, and each time you will mean it more than the last. A congregation forms over hours. The congregants flow together, merge, separate, merge again, their dark bodies pressing against each other in a way that does not seem competitive or cooperative but simply *concentrated*, as though the gathering itself has a density, a criticality, and when enough congregants are close enough for long enough, something happens.

They leave material behind.

It is not their bodies. They do not die to produce it—they flow away afterward, thinning, vanishing, returning to wherever the congregants go when they go. The material is something else. It is dark—darker than the rock, which is already dark, darker than the congregants themselves, a darkness that is almost purple, a darkness that has *depth*, that your eye falls into and cannot find the bottom of. It is hard—harder than the surrounding stone, resistant to scratches, resistant to the rain, resistant to the eons of polishing that have made the plateau surface what it is. It is fused, though by what process you cannot imagine—there is no heat source, no chemical reaction you can identify, no mechanism by which the congregants' substance could be transformed into this dense, dark, impervious material.

But it is there. Each gathering leaves a small deposit—millimeters across, thinner than a coin, a dark smear on the rock that does not wipe away, that does not weather away, that is simply *there*, now and henceforth, a permanent addition to the surface. And the next time the congregants gather—which is the next morning, or the next week, or the next month, the rhythm is not regular but it is persistent—they gather at the same spot. And they leave another deposit. And the deposits accumulate, layer on layer, millimeter on millimeter, and the spot becomes a mound, and the mound becomes a cairn, and the cairn becomes a structure, and the structure becomes a feature of the landscape, and the landscape is changed, imperceptibly, irreversibly, by the action of small dark things doing something they have always done.

---

## The Cairns

They are everywhere.

Once you learn to see them—and it takes time, it takes days of walking the plateau and looking and looking and looking before your eye adjusts to the subtlety of the contours, the way a cairn differs from a swell in the rock—you cannot stop seeing them. They are as common as the mats, as common as the congregants, as common as anything here, which is to say they are everywhere but invisible to the casual glance.

The smallest are barely raised—millimeters, the thickness of a fingernail, a darkening of the rock that might be a mineral vein, might be a stain, might be nothing. These are the young ones, the recent ones, still in the process of being built, still growing by increments too small to perceive between one visit and the next. The congregants gather on them every cycle, adding their fraction of a millimeter, their fraction of a gram, and the cairn grows, and will grow, and will continue to grow for as long as the plateau exists and the condensation forms and the congregants come to drink.

The largest are knee-high. This is not impressive in a landscape of mountains, but there are no mountains here—there is nothing here, nothing but flat rock and the low mounds that the congregants have built, and a knee-height cairn on this surface is a mountain, is the only vertical thing for a day's walk in any direction, is a landmark, is an event. It is also ancient. You can tell by the polish—the same polish that the rock has, the luster of eons, the shine of every abrasion across every age concentrating on the cairn's surface until it gleams like old bone, like dark water, like something that has been smoothed by time itself. These ancient cairns have been here so long that they have become part of the plateau's topography—not built on the rock, but *of* the rock, or indistinguishable from it, their dark fused material weathering at the same rate as the surrounding stone, their heights slowly diminishing as the eons strip them away.

But the congregants still build on them. Even the oldest, most eroded cairns—the ones that are barely bumps, that are ghosts of structures, that have been worn down to a fraction of their original height—still attract their gatherings, their slow additions, their millimeter-per-cycle growth. The congregants do not forget. Or they do not need to remember—the cairns themselves are the memory, are the markers, are the thing that attracts the next gathering, and as long as the cairn exists, even as a contour change too slight for human perception, the congregants will find it, will gather on it, will add to it, will continue the building that has been ongoing since before the cairn was a cairn, since before it was a stain, since before it was anything but a spot on the rock where, for some reason, the congregants gathered.

Why these spots? *Why these spots?* You will ask this question until you are tired of asking it, and you will not find an answer. The spots are not different from the surrounding rock in any way you can measure—with a thermometer, with a spectrometer, with a seismograph, with any instrument you carry or can imagine. They are not hotter or colder. They are not chemically distinct. They are not magnetically anomalous. They are not at the intersections of any visible pattern. They are just spots, points in the undifferentiated expanse of the plateau, and the congregants find them, and build on them, and have always built on them, and will always build on them, and the reason—if there is a reason—is locked in whatever passes for mind in the congregant, or in the collective of congregants, or in the plateau itself, or in the four billion years of history that have produced this place and these things and this behavior.

## What the Congregants Are

You do not know.

This is the honest answer, and it is the answer you will carry away with you, and it will sit in you like a stone, like a dark fused stone on the flat surface of your understanding, growing by imperceptible increments each time you think of it, each time you turn the question over and fail to resolve it and add the failure to the accumulation of failures that is your understanding of the congregants.

They are alive. This seems certain—they move, they consume, they respond to conditions, they produce material, they exhibit behavior that is not random. But alive by what definition? The definitions you carried here were made for other things—things with cells, things with membranes, things with DNA and ribosomes and the whole machinery of terrestrial biology that has been honed by three billion years of evolution in wet places. The congregants may have none of this. They may be cellular—they are small enough to be unicellular, or they may be colonial, or they may be something else, something that does not divide neatly into cell and not-cell, into organism and colony, into individual and mass. Their boundaries are uncertain. They merge and separate when they meet, passing through each other like drops of oil in water, and this suggests that the boundary between one congregant and another is not a membrane, not a wall, but a convention—a region where the density of whatever substance they are made of is high enough to be visible, to be dark, to be *them*, and where it falls below that threshold, they are not them, they are the rock, they are the condensation, they are the thin film of organic dust that coats everything, they are the plateau itself.

Are they individuals? A congregant the size of a rice grain and a congregant the size of a fist—do they differ in kind, or only in degree? Is the larger one simply a smaller one that has absorbed more moisture, incorporated more substance, grown in the way that a mat grows? Or is it a colony—a collection of rice-grain-sized congregants that have merged so thoroughly that they can no longer be distinguished? And when they vanish in the heat of the day, do they die, or do they disperse—each fist-sized congregant dissolving into a cloud of rice-grain-sized ones that spread across the micro-cracks and become invisible and reassemble the next morning from the same cracks, the same dust, the same residual chemistry that they left behind?

You do not know. You cannot know, not with the tools you have, not with the time you have. The congregants operate on timescales that your lifespan cannot engage. To understand them, you would need to watch a single gathering for a thousand years, to measure the growth of a single cairn over a million, to trace the pattern of gatherings across the plateau over a billion and see whether the pattern changes, whether the cairns move, whether the congregants are building something that will one day be visible from orbit, a dark inscription on the flat stone that says something to someone, or that says nothing to no one, or that says nothing that can be distinguished from the patient, purposeless, endless continuation of living things doing what they do.

## The Ecosystem

It is an ecosystem only in the loosest sense, only if you expand the definition to include interactions so slow and so subtle that they are almost mathematical.

The condensation forms on the rock. The congregants drink it. The mats drink it. The orange threads, when they grow, drink the rainwater that pools in the depressions, and they release spores that become new mats, which drink the condensation. The congregants gather at certain points and leave fused material that becomes cairns, which are slightly higher than the surrounding rock, which means they catch the wind differently and the condensation forms on them differently—perhaps a fraction of a milliliter more, perhaps a fraction of a second longer—and this difference, infinitesimal as it is, may be enough to attract the mats, which grow slightly thicker on the cairns, which produce slightly more organic dust when they die, which provides slightly more substrate for the next generation of orange threads, which produce slightly more spores, which—

It is a system. It is a system of feedbacks so faint that they are nearly imaginary, so slow that they are nearly stationary, so tenuous that a single hard rain could wash them away and reset them to zero, except that the rain does not wash them away, because the cairns are hard, and the mats are flat, and the congregants vanish into the micro-cracks, and the orange threads crumble into dust that is indistinguishable from the dust that was already there, and after the rain the condensation forms again, and the congregants emerge again, and the mats drink again, and the system continues, as it has continued, as it will continue.

Is there energy flowing through this system? Yes—the sun provides it, indirectly, by heating the rock and driving the condensation cycle, by lifting the water that fell as rain back into the atmosphere, by creating the temperature differential that makes the morning moisture possible. Is there complexity? Yes—the congregants' gathering behavior, the pattern of cairns across the plateau, the relationship between the cairns and the mats, the timing of the orange threads' reproduction—all of these are complex, are structured, are *organized* in a way that raw chemistry is not. Is there meaning?

This is the wrong question. Meaning is something you impose. The plateau does not have meaning; it has process. The congregants do not build cairns because the cairns mean something; they build cairns because that is what congregants do, because the physics of their substance and the chemistry of their environment and the history of their existence on this specific rock produce gathering as an inevitable behavior, and gathering produces fused material as an inevitable consequence, and fused material accumulates into cairns as an inevitable result of accumulation, and the cairns persist and grow because there is no mechanism to stop them, no force to erase them, no event to interrupt the cycle, and so they continue, and the plateau is covered with them, and that is all.

And yet—

And yet you stand on the plateau in the last light of evening, and the cairns throw their long shadows across the rock, and the congregants are flowing back into the micro-cracks or becoming transparent or dispersing into the dust, and the mats are darkening as the moisture leaves them, and the orange threads are curling and browning in the depressions, and the whole landscape is settling into the brief equilibrium of night, and you look at the cairns—at their spacing, at their sizes, at the way the largest ones seem to form a rough perimeter around the plateau's center, at the way the smallest ones cluster near the depressions where the orange threads grow, at the way the whole pattern suggests a diagram, a circuit, a *design*—

And you think: they are building something. The congregants, the mats, the threads, the cairns, the condensation and the rock and the time—they are all building something together, some structure that is not any single cairn but the totality of cairns, not any single gathering but the pattern of gatherings, not any single cycle but the accumulation of cycles, and the structure is not complete—it will never be complete, it is a process not a product, a verb not a noun—but it is *taking shape*, it is *becoming*, it is something that was not here before and that will be here after, something that the plateau is making out of itself by the simple expedient of existing for long enough.

And you do not know what it is. And you will not know. And the plateau does not care that you do not know, and the congregants do not care, and the cairns do not care, and the rock does not care, and the four billion years of patience that have produced this place and these things and this behavior do not care, because caring is something that animals do, and this is not an animal, and these are not animals, and what lives here is not anything you were prepared to recognize as life, and yet it lives, and it builds, and it continues, and it will continue, and the cairns will grow, millimeter by millimeter, cycle by cycle, morning by morning, until the plateau is not flat anymore, until the cairns are the topography, until the congregants have built a landscape out of their own residues, and that landscape will be dark and low and patient and strange, and it will be old beyond reckoning, and it will not explain itself, and it will not need to.

## What Remains

You will leave. The plateau will not notice your departure any more than it noticed your arrival. The congregants will flow around the place where you stood, drinking the condensation from your footprints, incorporating the organic residue you left behind—the dead skin cells, the strands of hair, the faint chemical signature of your sweat and breath and existence—into their substance, their process, their slow accumulation. In a thousand years, a cairn will be slightly taller because you were here. In a million, the difference will be undetectable, worn away by the same eons that wear everything away, and your footprints will be gone, and your residue will be gone, and you will be gone, and the plateau will remain.

But something will remain of you in the cairn. A fraction of a fraction of a gram of carbon that was once in your body, that was once part of the organic dust, that was once consumed by a congregant, that was once fused into the dark material of a structure that is growing, slowly, on a spot that was chosen by a process that has no name, on a rock that has been flat for longer than anything has been alive. You will be part of the plateau. Not in a metaphorical sense—not in the romantic way that the travel writers mean when they say "part of the landscape"—but in a literal, chemical, physical sense. Your matter will be incorporated into the cairns, and the cairns will persist, and the congregants will build on them, and the cycle will continue, and you will be here, invisibly, imperceptibly, for as long as the plateau endures.

This is what lives on the Meridian Plateau. Not the mats, not the orange threads, not the congregants, not the cairns—these are its expressions, its organs, its processes. What lives here is the plateau itself. The rock and the water and the time and the patience and the accumulation of four billion years of small events into a surface that is not empty but *full*, full of life so slow it is indistinguishable from geology, full of purpose so patient it is indistinguishable from randomness, full of meaning so diffuse it is indistinguishable from the fact of continuing.

What lives here is the oldest kind of life. Not the first—there were earlier things, in wetter places, that have left their fossils in younger stone. But the most persistent. The kind that does not need very much and does not do very much and does not change very much and does not stop. The kind that outlasts everything else not by being stronger or faster or more adapted but by being *simpler*, by requiring less, by continuing when there is no reason to continue except that continuing is what it does.

The congregants do not know they are alive. The mats do not know they are growing. The cairns do not know they are being built. The plateau does not know it is patient. And yet—and yet—they are, and they do, and they will, and the dark fused structures will continue to rise, millimeter by millimeter, morning by morning, on the flat and ancient stone, and the condensation will form and be consumed and form again, and the orange threads will bloom and wither and bloom again, and the congregants will flow and gather and build and disperse and flow again, and the plateau will continue to be what it has always been: a place where life is so slow and so small and so patient that it has become indistinguishable from the rock itself, and the rock has become indistinguishable from life, and the boundary between the living and the mineral has dissolved, and everything is just process, just continuation, just the slow accumulation of small events into something that, given enough time, might become something that means something, or might not, or might already mean something that no one is equipped to understand.

This is what lives on the Meridian Plateau. And it is enough. It has always been enough. It did not need you to arrive and it does not need you to leave and it does not need you to understand, but it has allowed you to be here, and it has incorporated you into its process, and you will carry the knowledge of it with you—the knowledge that somewhere, on a high and ancient flatness that has forgotten how to have topography, small dark things are building structures that have no purpose and no end, and they have been doing this since before your species learned to walk, and they will be doing it after your species has forgotten how, and the structures will be there, growing, patient, dark, and strange, and alive in the only way that matters: they continue.
