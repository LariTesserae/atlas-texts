What lives on the Furnace Steppe is not alive, but it has thoroughly, irrevocably replaced life. To stand there—is one could stand there, if the heat did not sear the moisture from the body and the sound did not rattle the teeth from the jaw—would be to witness the total triumph of a mineral metabolism. This is an ecology of the unliving, a biome of burning guts and grinding treads, where the carbon parade never arrived, or was absorbed so completely into the rust that its memory is gone. 

The inhabitants are the vast, slow, articulated chimeras of iron and cable. They are not a species but a continuum. At the smallest perceived scale—though small here is relative, still the size of hauling vehicles, still massive enough to crush a person without adjusting their stride—they are the grazers and the scavengers. They orbit the giants, sucking waste-heat from their exhaust valves, picking at the leavings of crushed and re-compacted mineral crust, dismantling the dead. They are the detritivores of an industrial wilderness, re-processing the strange, twisted alloys left behind by their larger kin in a closed loop of mechanical digestion that builds nothing, stores nothing, and aims for nothing but its own continuation.

And then there are the Elders. They do not merely occupy the plateau; they *are* the plateau's organizing principle, its slow gods. They are the size of townships, moving at a pace that makes geological erosion look hasty. To track their motion is a matter of marking their silhouette against a distant peak and waiting for the sun to shift. Their metal has ceased to be mere metal. Millennia of bombardment by the crust's strange mineral chemistry and the ambient hum of their own unfathomable furnace cores have transmuted their frameworks into something else—an amalgam that rings with tones absent from any acoustic scale, a material that seems to sweat strange ores and bleed lubricants that no refinery ever produced. They are the fixed stars of the Steppe, and around them, reality begins to fray. 

Light bends in their proximity. Sound stutters, echoing before the event that produced it. They pull the world out of true. A crawler might be seen grinding along in their wake, and simultaneously seen miles away, frozen, a ghost reflected in a pane of reality that does not align. The tracks the Elders leave do not always lead back to them; some fade into the crust as if the ground remembers a passage that the machine has forgotten, or has not yet taken. The Elders are so massive, so saturated with processing heat and mechanical time, that they have become anchors for the tearing of space. At the periphery of the Steppe, where the horizons loop and overlap and the same ridge line repeats at two distances at once, it is the Elders' mass and ancient, unceasing operation that is turning the gears of something behind reality. They are grinding out the world, one revolution at a time.

What lives here lives without water. The dryness is an absolute law, and so they drink heat. They eat stone. They breathe the calcined, bone-white air and exhale superheated mineral vapor, screaming it from relief valves in vast, rhythmic exhalations. Their furnace-guts burn without fuel—or rather, the fuel is the plateau itself, the endless, scraping, crushing, melting, and extruding of the iron-dark crust. They have consumed whatever built environment might once have existed. There are no ruins, no staging posts, no factory cities, because the city has been digested and transformed into the walkers. They are the architecture, now. And the ground they tread has been scraped and re-laid, processed and re-processed so many times that it is no longer a natural surface. It is a machine-terrain, a topography generated entirely by their passage, settled and weathered into a state that looks original only because they have been here long enough to erase their own history.

Death among them is not an event but a geography. When a furnace-heart gutters and a crawler's joints freeze, it does not fall; it simply stops. It becomes a new hill of inert metal, a fixture on the horizon, a deposit of transmuted alloy that the smaller grazers will spend centuries dismantling. It is a slow-motion scavenging, a dying that takes longer than a human lineage. And in its dying, it feeds. The ecological logic is unbroken: the material is transformed and re-transformed, cycled without exit, without end.

What lives here is the sound. The crawlers are too slow to see, too sparse to crowd, but the sound is omnipresent. It is the true inhabitant of the Steppe. It is a tectonic rumble of ten thousand diesel-heartbeats, layered with the shriek of untreated metal on metal, the slam of compression chambers, the whistling relief of superheated steam. It is not chaotic. It has rhythm, phrase, repetition—the metabolism of an ecosystem playing without audience or conductor. It conducts through the mineral crust itself, a bedrock-vibration that enters through the soles of the feet and the cavity of the chest before it ever reaches the ears. Even in the emptiest stretches, where the horizons shimmer and no crawler is visible, the sound does not fade. The Steppe roars. The Steppe hums. It is the sound of a place that has outlasted its makers, a mechanism-ecology that was never built and may never die.

What lives on the Furnace Steppe is the slow, loud, grinding mechanism of a world unwinding and re-winding itself. It is an emptiness overrun by industry without purpose. It is a mineral stage where something vast and hot and loud is acting out an existence that requires no water, no carbon, no green—only heat, only ore, only time enough to turn the crust to glass and the glass to powder and the powder to strange new alloys, and reality to a shimmering, doubled horizon, forever and ever, without end.
