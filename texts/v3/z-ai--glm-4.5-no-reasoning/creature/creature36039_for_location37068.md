Something lives here.

Not in the way that life insists elsewhere—not as a heartbeat, not as breath, not as the soft animal urgency of blood and water. That kind of life was foreclosed long ago, when the air became a weight and the water a memory. What lives here is older, or newer, than the distinction between the organic and the mineral. What lives here has never seen the sun, and does not know it is supposed to be dead.

---

The mountain range is the organism. Not metaphorically. The sprawling, regional-scale tumult of raw geology—this is its body, still embryonic, still folding itself into existence. Those vast ragged dorsals of granite pushing out of the deep earth are its spine, its limbs, the slow articulation of a skeletal structure that has not yet decided how it wants to lie. The fresh ravines are its joints, still fusing. The vertical shards of obsidian are the sharp, unfledged growths of a body forming faster than it can integrate its own edges—a child whose bones are outpacing its skin.

Consider how it feeds. Every few minutes, a new fissure detonates open, spewing hot gravel. This is not chaos. This is metabolism. The planet's stomach is digesting itself, pulling deep material up, converting pressure into form, heat into structure. The continuous low-frequency thrum is not mere tectonic grinding—it is peristalsis, the deep intestinal churn of a body eating its own interior to grow an exterior. The magma is its blood, and the wound won't stop bleeding because the body does not yet know how to clot, does not yet want to. It is ravenous. It is adolescent. The mountains are growing faster than gravity can pull them apart because gravity is a law for finished things, and this thing is not finished.

It is being born, and birth is violence.

---

And it is not alone in its own flesh.

Down in the hottest crevices, the lithocrepe spreads. The pale, fibrous substance that exudes from fresh cracks—this too is part of the organism, or rather, it is the organism's first attempt at an immune response. The crystalline lattice growing in branching mycelial patterns across raw stone: call it a scab, call it a fungus, call it a mineral metabolism. It is the body trying to seal itself, producing a crude integument over its own wounds. It crunches to powder underfoot because it is not yet robust; it is a provisional tissue, a first draft of skin. The faint sweetish gas it releases when crushed is its chemical exhalation—a waste product, a breath, a sigh from something that does not have lungs but knows, in some rudimentary way, that it must exchange something with the world outside itself.

The lithocrepe is not alive in any recognized sense. But recognition is a failure of imagination, not a measure of reality. It grows in patterns. It responds to pressure and heat. It colonizes new fractures. It tries. And trying—reaching, spreading, attempting to close what keeps opening—that is the bare minimum of life, the threshold below which even rocks do not bother.

---

And there is the darkness itself.

The way shadows lag a second behind the shifting of glowing rock, as if they have a viscosity—that is not a trick of the failing light. That is a presence. The darkness here is not an absence but a substance, a medium, a fluid in which the birthing mountains float and push. It pools in the crevices with a weight that exceeds mere shadow. It has a delayed reaction to the rearrangement of the world because it is not passive; it is engaged, clinging, reluctant to release the forms it has settled into. The darkness is the amniotic fluid of this place, the embryonic sea in which a world is gestating. It has no water, but it has this—a thick, particulate obscurity that buffers and resists, that slows light and swallows sound.

You could almost call it alive, if you stretched the word far enough. But it is more accurate to say that it is *alive-adjacent*—a property of the environment rather than an organism within it. The darkness is the mood of the place, and the mood is oppressive, and the oppressiveness is a kind of agency. It does things. It suppresses the light to 0.4. It muffles sound into those long, weighted silences. It shapes the conditions under which the mountains grow and the lithocrepe spreads. It is the medium, and the medium is the message, and the message is: *this world is not for you.*

---

But something was here before. Something that thought this world *could* be for them.

The tilted pillar of synthetic polymer. The shattered habitat module, cracked open like an egg, its interior exposed. These are fossils—not of life, but of hope. Someone built a research station here, or a waymarker, when this was a stable, quiet plateau. They stood on ground they assumed was dead. They placed structures with smooth, curved edges in a world that had not yet learned to produce them. They brought technology, purpose, the assumption that geology is slow and patient and can be trusted to stay.

They were wrong. The ground decided to live.

And when it did, it did not politely rearrange itself around their structures. It consumed them. The obsidian engulfs the pillar's base. The ground beneath the habitat module crests upward, carrying the broken shell like a fragment of bone pushed through skin. The artifacts are being swallowed—not destroyed, but *incorporated*, the way the body incorporates a splinter, surrounding it with tissue, trying to reject it but ultimately accepting it as a feature of the landscape. In a million years, if this place ever cools, those synthetic remnants will be embedded in the stone like imprints in shale—the only evidence that something soft and brief and hopeful ever stood here.

They are the graves of those who built here before the ground decided to live. They are also the ground's first foreign bodies, its first irritations, its first lessons in the fact that not everything that exists is rock. In some small, chemical way, those polymers may be teaching the lithocrepe what shape a smooth edge is, what a curve looks like, what it means to be something other than a fracture. The body is learning from its infections.

---

So what lives here?

A mountain range that is also an organism, still unborn, still screaming its way into existence through the slow violence of tectonic force. A fungal-mineral scab that is its first skin, its first attempt at self-healing. A darkness that is not merely the absence of light but the presence of a suffocating, amniotic medium. The ghosts of builders who thought the earth was dead and found it was only sleeping. And beneath it all, the planet itself—not the whole planet, but this wounded, hyperactive region of it—trying to shake itself apart while simultaneously building itself anew, which is the definition of a living thing: that it destroys and creates itself in the same motion, that it cannot stop changing, that it is never the same from one moment to the next.

The world is being skinned alive. And what lives in a place that is being skinned alive is the thing doing the skinning, and the thing being skinned, and the skin itself, and the void where the skin used to be.

They are all the same thing. And it does not know its own name. And it will not stop growing. And it will not apologize for what it has become.
