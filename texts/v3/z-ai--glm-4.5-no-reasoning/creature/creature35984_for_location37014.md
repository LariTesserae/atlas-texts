To live here is to be a furnace oneself.

They are not flesh. Flesh is a water-story, a tale of cells swimming in saline, of osmosis and evaporation. There is no water here to write that story. Instead, they are kiln-fired things—ceramic endoskeletons wrapped in a mesh of mineral sinew, articulated by joints of expanding talc and cooled basalt. If you stood one in a downpour, it would not drown; it would dissolve, the water attacking its silicate bonds like acid. But there is no downpour here. There is only the heat, and in the heat, they are indurate. Immortal, by any metric that matters.

They are few. You could walk the crushing garnet streets for an epoch and not see another, yet you are never alone. They are an intimate population, their numbers folded into the city's recursive geography. The settlement is a labyrinth of thermal echoes; a left turn brings you to a street identical to the one you left, but older, its stones more deeply eroded, its arches sunk lower toward the ground. The residents do not walk these streets so much as they phase through them, stepping along gradients of temperature rather than lines of distance. They navigate by the heartbeat of the stone. They know that a corridor radiating at exactly 2.81 leads to the habitation blocks, while a cool spot—a mere 2.65—is the threshold to the fungal groves. To a creature of water, the city is a disorienting loop; to them, it is a house with many rooms, all of them warm.

Their shapes are dense, stooped, and deliberate. They move with the grinding patience of tectonic plates, because quick motion is a waste of energy, and energy is sacred. They are broad across the shoulder—necessary to radiate the immense thermal load of their own internal chemistry—and their heads are low, slung forward beneath a ridge of heat-shedding ceramic laminate. They have no eyes. In a city lit only by the sickly amber glow of rust-palms and the infrared shimmer of superheated air, visible light is a useless novelty. Instead, their faces are smooth, shallow concavities of porous stone, riddled with micro-capillaries. They do not see the city; they *feel* it. They read the thermoluminescent glow of the vegetation as a blinding flare, they track the subterranean boil of siloxane in the walls as a deep, resonant hum in their bones. Your own body, leaking moisture and radiating a feverish 98.6 degrees against the oppressive 2.8-out-of-3 heat, would blare across their senses like a beacon. If you walked the streets, the blind faces of the flora and the blind faces of the residents would both tilt toward you, drawn by the same anomalous heat.

They do not eat. They do not drink. They *absorb*. 

Several times a cycle, they root themselves. They find a place where the siloxane solvent sweats heavily from the weeping walls, or where the rust-palms grow thickest, and they press their flat, porous feet and the palms of their broad hands against the stone. They become indistinguishable from the vegetation. Their internal chemistry is a slow fire of oxidation and silicate recombination; they pull the heated atmospheric gases through their capillaries, metabolizing the mineral sweat directly. It is a communion that takes hours. While rooted, they are part of the architecture. The dry vines sometimes crawl over them, mistaking their ceramic hides for stable ground, and the residents allow it. There is a symbiosis here: the flora provides the concentrated chemical byproducts they need to repair their own micro-fractures, and they provide a stable, radiating scaffold for the glassy-noduled branches to climb toward the low ceiling. 

They are born from the city’s death. When one of their kind finally fractures—when the cumulative thermal stress of millennia creates a fault line their chemistry can no longer seal—they do not fall. They calcify, just like the rust-palms. They become permanent mineral scaffolding. It is the only way they reproduce. From the dead, heat-stressed husk of an elder, a new one slowly accretes. Over centuries, the mineral sweat deposits new layers of silicate within the hollows of the old skeleton. The fractal cascade of the flora weaves through the growing form, feeding it raw compounds, shaping it. The newborn is not carved or born; it is *precipitated*. When the heat cycle reaches a crescendo and the siloxane boils over, the new resident fractures away from its elder's remains, a smaller, smoother echo of the one that came before, already reaching out to root itself against the vibrating wall.

Their lives are measured in the slow crawl of erosion. They built this place with simple tools—obsidian wedges, bone levers, immense patience—and they maintain it the same way. When the thermal expansion shifts a megalith out of true, they wait for the cooldown micro-fluctuation, lever it back with a shaft of carved bone, and pack the seam with crushed garnet and mineral fiber. There are no beasts of burden because nothing else could survive the bearing of this weight. They carry the stone themselves, moving it inch by inch across the roaring, breathless streets. 

They have no written language, because there is no medium that survives here unaltered; everything is eventually consumed by the heat, subsumed into the ceramic and the stone. Their history is acoustic. They remember in the throb of the leviathan heartbeat, in the groaning expansion of the arches. The elders—those whose ceramic hides are deeply pitted and laced with veins of deposited copper—can emit a resonant tone from deep within their hollow thoraxes. When they gather in the deep, shadowed streets, they harmonize with the city. They strike their obsidian blades against the basalt columns, not to cut, but to speak, sending ringing messages through the fitted stone that vibrate through the thermal gradients, felt by the porous faces of their kin streets away. 

They are the quiet guardians of the wound. They are the intelligence embedded in the geological itself. They do not wonder about the surface, about water, about the cold. They have no concept of rain, of ocean, of the sky. To them, a world without the crushing pressure of the stone above, without the steady roar of sublimating sap and the searing kiss of the absolute dry heat, would be a vacuum. They are the inhabitants of the furnace, the children of the crack in the world, thriving on nothing but the earth’s own deep warmth, as permanent, as patient, and as unforgiving as the basalt they walk upon.
