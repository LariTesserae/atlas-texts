To live here is to be an exception so improbable that you exist only as a persistent glitch in a system that has no need of you. You are the half-second of stillness in a convulsion. You are the crumb of indigestible matter in a throat that has evolved to swallow worlds.

Who lives here are the Throat-Dwellers—the Residuals, the Margin-Eaters—and there is nothing romantic about them. They are not noble savages untainted by civilization; they are survivors of a catastrophe that never ended, clinging to the underside of a biological avalanche. Perhaps they descended here generations ago, following some fading memory of shelter or mineral, and the throat closed behind them. Perhaps they were always here, a relict species of primate that evolved in the absence of sun and sky, their eyes widening over millennia into saucer-pupils that can parse the bruised ochre of deep-fold bioluminescence. Perhaps they were once something else entirely, something that has been digested down to its essential, stubborn core: a hand that grips, a mind that recognizes patterns, a will that refuses to stop breathing even when every breath is a mouthful of chitin and spore.

They are small. They have to be. The arthropodal swarms—those iridescent, shearing carpets—do not hunt the Residuals so much as simply *encompass* them. A dweller caught in the open during a swarm-event is not killed; they are *subtracted*, rendered into nutrient by a thousand industrial mouthparts in seconds, leaving nothing but a brief stain in the dry humus that is itself consumed within the hour. So the dwellers are small, and they are slight, and they have made a religion of stillness.

Their bodies tell the story of their world. Their skin is not skin as we would recognize it—rather, it is a continuous, slightly overlapping dermal scale, like the bark of a young root, providing some small resistance to the abrasive mandibles that brush past them constantly. It is the color of old humus: a grey-brown that borders on umber, mottled with patches of paler, unpigmented tissue where the fungal spores have colonized them in symbiotic defense. Yes, they are colonized. Every dweller carries a garden of specialized fungus on their shoulders and the crowns of their heads—a pale, shelf-like growth that, when they are crouched and still, makes them indistinguishable from yet another fungal protrusion in the root-walls. This is their camouflage, and it is alive. The fungus feeds on their dead skin cells and the lipid secretions of their scalps; in return, it emits a pheromone that marks them as *part of the architecture*, invisible to the chemically-blind arthropods that navigate by scent-taste alone. They have tamed nothing. They have simply learned to smell like the furniture.

Their eyes are vast, dark, and recessed beneath heavy brow-ridges of hardened keratin. They see in the amber and the ochre, in the sullen fever-glow of the living walls. They have no need of the sharp, bright vision of surface-dwellers; their world is one of gradients and movements, of the subtle shift in a fungal shelf that precedes collapse, of the particular density of a spore-cloud that indicates a parasitic wasp-swarm is about to hatch. Their pupils are not round but horizontal—wide slits that take in the panoramic sprawl of the vault, ever-watching for the seething crawl of the destroyers. And their ears... their ears have atrophied to vestigial folds, useless in a world where sound is a physical assault. They do not hear; they *feel*. They feel the groan of shifting heartwood through the pads of their feet, the vibration of a trillion mandibles through the marrow of their bones. They have learned to read the tremors the way a sailor reads the sea: a particular sub-bass thrum means *move*, a sudden staccato crackle means *freeze*, and a deep, rhythmic pulse means *the great pylons are shifting—find new shelter, now*.

They are pre-metal, pre-writing, pre-everything, and their material culture is a thing of desperation. The obsidian hand-axe is their pinnacle—their Excalibur, their satellite, their cathedral. They knapped it from the rare volcanic glass that sometimes wells up in the deep folds where the root-mantle thins and the mineral geology still, faintly, exists. To make such a tool is an act of extraordinary courage; it requires a journey to the obsidian seeps, places where the heat is volcanic and the air is thick with alkaloid vapors that burn the lungs. And then it requires the knapping itself—striking the glass, making the sharp, *loud* cracking sounds that, in this world, are a dinner-bell for every arthropod within a quarter-mile. They lose people to the making of these tools. They lose them often. And so every shard of obsidian is a sacred thing, passed from hand to hand, worn down to a nub, re-sharpened until it is a sliver, and then still used, because without it, they cannot cut the hardened resin of the fungal shelves, cannot carve the hollows in which they hide, cannot slice through the tough, fibrous hide of the giant grubs to harvest the silk within.

The twine they make from that grub-silk is their second treasure. It is a material that should not exist—silk spun by a larval creature the size of a human torso, secreted to shape the very architecture of the world. To harvest it, a dweller must find a grub in its burrow, must slice it open with the obsidian axe while it is still alive and secreting, must extract the silk-glands and spin the twine before the resin hardens and the swarm comes to investigate the chemical scream of the dying grub. It is done in seconds. It is done in terror. The twine that results is coarse, thick, and incredibly strong—strong enough to bind seed-pods into cairns, to mark paths, to lash together the precarious platforms on which they sleep.

And the cairns. Oh, the cairns. This is their writing, their history, their literature—all expressed in the language of stacked seed-pods. A cairn of three pods, stacked vertically, means *shelter here, but only for a short time*. A cairn of five, arranged in a ring, means *danger—grub-fields ahead, swarms active*. A cairn of seven, forming a spiral, means *we were here, we were seven, we are moving deeper*. The seed-pods are the hard-shelled casings of a particular barbed flora, and they are ideal for this purpose because nothing eats them; even the arthropods find their shells impervious. And so the cairns persist—sometimes for days, sometimes for a week—before the churning metabolism of the world consumes them, pushes them over, swallows them into the substrate. The dwellers are used to this. Their history is always being erased. They have no permanence, no archive, no memory that extends beyond the living. The oldest dweller remembers the cairns their parent stacked, and the paths they marked, and the shelters they carved—but that is all. Beyond that, there is only the endless, heaving present.

Their shelters are the saddest, most ingenious things. They do not build; they *parasitize*. They find the wounds in the living architecture—the places where a fungal shelf has collapsed, where a heartwood pylon has cracked, where a rhizome-bundle has been torn apart by the weight of its own growth—and they move into the brief, chaotic vacuum that follows. They use the obsidian axes to widen the wounds, to carve out hollows in the soft, unpigmented root-matter that is exposed by the damage. They use the grub-silk twine to hang lightweight platforms of shredded fiber from the hardened resin that the grubs have left behind. They make nests—small, cramped, dark nests that smell of their own fungal colonies and the dry humus they sleep on. And they know, always, that these nests are temporary. The world is healing even as they hollow it out; the root-matter is growing back, the fungal shelves are regenerating, the swarms are coming to consume the rot and rebuild. A shelter might last a day. It might last a week. None have ever lasted a month. And so they are always moving, always seeking the next wound, the next collapse, the next brief pocket of stillness in the churning riot.

They live in family-groups—small, necessarily, for a large group is too visible, too chemically distinct, too likely to attract the swarms. A group of five to eight is the upper limit; beyond that, the fungal camouflage cannot mask their collective biological signature, and the arthropods will find them. They mate quietly, quickly, with none of the elaborate courtships of surface-dwellers; there is no time, no safety, no room for anything but the imperative. Children are born small and precocial, their eyes open, their fungal colonies already budding from their shoulders—seeded, somehow, by the mother's own garden in a process that none of them understand or question. The children learn to be still before they learn to walk. They learn to read the tremors before they learn to speak. And they learn, very early, that the world does not care about them—that the throat will swallow them as indifferently as it swallows a spore, that the only thing standing between them and dissolution is the sharpness of their obsidian and the stillness of their bodies.

There is no religion here, not in any way we would recognize. They do not worship the plants or the fungi or the arthropods; they do not anthropomorphize the throat. They fear it, yes—but it is a practical fear, the fear of a mechanism, not of a god. If they have a spirituality, it is the spirituality of the cairn: the belief that to mark a place, to leave a sign, to say *I was here*—this has value, even if the sign is consumed within the hour. It is a defiance of entropy so small and so futile that it becomes, somehow, magnificent. They are stacking seed-pods in a cathedral of raw, deafening meat, and the cathedral does not know they exist, and they know the cathedral does not know, and they stack the pods anyway.

This is who lives in the throat.

Not a civilization. Not a tribe, in any comfortable, anthropological sense. A remnant. A scab on the lining of a living throat, clinging to the brief, raw places where the tissue has torn, feeding on the leakage of a world that is still being born and dying and being born again, all around them, every moment, in a roar that never ceases. They are the most marginal of marginal creatures—the ones who live not *despite* the overwhelming biological riot, but *within its margins*, in the spaces between the cycles, in the moments of hesitation before the next convulsion.

And they are still there. Still stacking their cairns. Still knapping their obsidian. Still spinning their twine from the guts of giant grubs. Still being, against all reason, in a place that has evolved to forget that beings like them could ever exist.

They are the answer to a question the throat never asked.
