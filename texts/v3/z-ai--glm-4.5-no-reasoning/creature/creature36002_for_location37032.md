To live here is to never know the sky as anything other than a lid.

You are a citizen of the crush, a node in an infinite, writhing circuit. You have no word for "sun" because you have no organ for longing. You do not look up; you look in. You look down, and you press. 

You are born not from an egg or a seed, but from a dissociation—a shearing off. You were once a bud on the flank of a pale, rubbery polyp, clustered on the lee side of a scalloped ravine. You woke to the world already partially grown, tearing yourself free in a wet, painful slough, leaving behind a raw patch on your parent’s flesh that immediately began to bubble with a new sibling. You hit the ground—a ground that is not stone, but a springy, oozing mat of slate-gray leathery flesh—and you were immediately consumed by the motion. 

You do not have eyes. The concept of "sight" is a madness that belongs to the upper, dead world. Instead, your head—flattened, wedge-shaped, armored in ridges of dark, ferrous chitin—is a dense nexus of thermal pits and vibrational tympana. You do not see the swarm; you *hear* it, a three-dimensional architecture of pressure and heat. You feel the world as a roaring, clicking, wet mass of proximity. To exist is to be touched. To exist is to be pressed upon by a hundred others of your kind, to feel the thrum of their wing-cases against your carapace, the scrape of their jointed legs over your dorsal plates. 

You are a crawler, broad and pale as a stomach, a living stomach, for that is mostly what you are. Your internal anatomy is not divided into neat organs but is a long, coiled, rumbling tube of acidic dissolution. You eat constantly. You must. The metabolism that sustains your chemosynthetic flesh burns with a slow, cold fire, leaching the metals from the rock and the fats from the fungal mats. You march in processions that are not lines, but avalanches—tens of thousands of you moving in a synchronized, blind tidal flow over the leathery lichen-analogues. 

When your mandibles—black, heavy, ending in a crunch like breaking ice—tear a chunk of oozing tissue from the mat, you feel the vibration of the rip travel up your forelimbs. You feel the gelid fluid burst against your mouthparts. The taste is not sweet or bitter; it is *chemical*. It is the sharp, coppery tang of iron, the dull slug of magnesium, the sour fizz of sulfur. It is the taste of the ancient shale itself, processed through the slow, leaky metabolism of the polyps. You swallow the chunk whole, feeling it writhe inside you, still alive with its own minor enzymatic fires, dissolving into the slurry of your gut.

Around you, the air is a solid. It is a churning fluid of thumb-length flyers, eyeless and segmented, navigating by the heat of your body and the friction of your movement. They roil in cyclonic columns, their wings—a blur of membranous, pale tissue—vibrating at frequencies that blend into a continuous, frictional hum. When they land on you, which they do constantly, it is a shower of hard, clicking taps. They rest on the ridges of your back, they probe the joints of your armor with their seeking, heat-sensitive antennae, they lay their microscopic, gelatinous roe in the damp crevices of your neck-plates. You do not shake them off. You cannot shake them off. They are as much a part of your environment as the mist. To be without them would mean you are dead, and the mats are already digesting you.

You live by the metronome of the damp. You feel it before it arrives—a shift in the pressure, a heavy, wet weight settling on your tympana. Then the mist rolls down the fluted runnels, condensing on the slickenside stone and on your own gleaming, greasy back. When the air decides to be wet, when the heavy droplets fall in sudden, localized bursts, the world convulses. The moisture hits the ground, and the ground responds. The pale polyps on the ridges above you dilate their apertures in frantic, rhythmic gasps. The flyers descend in thick, suffocating clouds to lay their spawn in the newly formed puddles. And you—you feel a sudden, urgent itch beneath your carapace. The damp triggers a hormonal surge, a violent imperative to grow. You press against your neighbors, shell to shell, crushing the weaker ones beneath your broad, flat body, not in malice, but in the blind, tidal need to molt, to expand, to consume.

The sound. You do not hear the sound as a distinct thing; you *are* the sound. The deafening white noise of a million million chitinous clicks, the wet slop of your own foot-pads on the damp flesh of the mats, the crunch of your mandibles, the droning hum of the flyers—it enters you through your exoskeleton, vibrating the fluid in your body cavity. It is a continuous, thrumming condition of existence. It precludes thought. You do not think. You do not have a mind separate from the roar. The roar is the ecosystem thinking. The roar is the great, blind stomach digesting the darkness.

And when your shell cracks—when the grind of a crawler larger than you, or the sudden, acidic excretion of a dying polyp, or simply the accumulated friction of a hundred thousand steps finally splits your dorsal plate—you do not die in isolation. Your ichor, thick and dark with leached iron, spills onto the gray mat. The flyers descend instantly, a squirming, clicking blanket of mouths. The crawlers march over you, their ice-crunch mandibles already tearing at your softer tissues. You are unmade in seconds, your minerals re-entering the lithotrophic web, your flesh becoming the flesh of the swarm. There is no silence, no rest, no peace. There is only the perpetual interchange, the damp, the crush, the roar. 

You are the basement of the world. You are the mouth that never closes.
