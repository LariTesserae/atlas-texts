Something lives here.

Not despite the roar, but *within* it. The sound is so total, so physically crushing, that it has birthed a new kind of silence—a negative space in the vibration where existence can hide. To survive the Drowned Manufactory, you must become a creature of the margin, an outlier in the algorithm of decay.

They are called the Stilt-Walkers, though they have no name for themselves. Language requires air untouched by the shriek of tearing steam, and such air does not exist here. They are the only fauna because they have ceased to be flesh in any recognizable sense. They are vertebrate, vaguely—possessing a long, articulated spine of dense, ossified cartilage—but their bodies have surrendered to the environment. They are eyeless, of course. In a world where light is a 0.5 and the dark is an active, disorienting presence, the eye is a liability, a wet vulnerability waiting to be ruined by toxic drizzle. Instead, their heads are wide, dish-like concavities of bone, swept back and grooved like the rusted parabolic dishes that top the silent telemetry towers. They do not see the Manufactory; they *listen* to its geometry.

Their hearing is not auditory in the way we understand it. It is a sub-dermal array of tensioned fibers and micro-vibrissae, running down their spines and threading into the pads of their feet. They feel the world as a topographic map of pressure. When a cracked pressure valve releases its screaming tear three hundred meters away, the Stilt-Walker feels the shockwave refract off a block-long pumping station, painting a temporary, vivid silhouette of the obstacle in their mind. They navigate the flat, wet expanse of ferrocrete not by sight, but by the rhythmic, skull-rattling thump of the sub-level turbines. To them, that apocalyptic roar is a lighthouse. It is the heartbeat of a dead god, and they sync their own slowed pulses to it.

They are immense and painfully thin. To move through the shallows without stirring the mineral-choked water, they have evolved legs like the ruined piles of the Manufactory itself—spindly, jointed things of hyper-dense bone, lifting their trunks a full two meters above the plated ground. They step with a glacial, deliberate patience, placing each foot upon a seam or a rivet, feeling for the integrity of the plate below. A misstep means plunging through a corroded grate into the pressurized flooded earth beneath, a death sentence with no one to witness it. Their feet are broad, chitinous plates, sensitive as tongues, tasting the water for the chemical signatures of the micro-storms, avoiding the scalding vapor that rolls down the avenues.

Their hides are not skin. They are a living accretion of the Manufactory’s decay—a carapace of rust-pigmented keratin, pitted and weeping with condensation. Pale, rootless lichen grows on the undersides of their arms and the arches of their backs, feeding on the chemical efflorescence that sweats from their own pores. They do not groom it; it is their camouflage. When they stand motionless between the skeletal silos—which they do for hours, sometimes days, conserving energy—they are indistinguishable from the stalled, titanic presses or the corroded stanchions. They are columns of rust and moisture in a forest of columns. Their metabolism is a miserly trick of the damp. They do not eat. They have no digestive tract. Instead, within their hollow, barrel-like chests, they harbor a dense, microbial furnace—a chemosynthetic stomach that synthesizes the metallic mist and the trace minerals weeping from the floor plates into a dark, sluggish nutrient. They drink the iron water, and the iron water makes them more like the iron world.

They are solitary by necessity. The density of the Manufactory is near zero, and so is theirs. They cross paths only as a function of the Machine's endless, pointless cycles, drawn to the same rare events: the localized, toxic drizzle that pools in the divots of the hexagonal plates, or the brief, sickly amber pulse of a warning beacon that briefly warms a section of the floor. When they do meet, there is no sound, no display. They press their dish-like heads against one another, spine to spine, and for a moment, the vibrations of the world are shared. They feel the other's weight in the roar, a brief, localized shift in the pressure gradients, a shadow in the sound. Then they separate, stepping back into the charcoal gray dark, their long legs carrying them away on paths only they can feel.

They are not the operators. They do not tend the Machine, and the Machine does not acknowledge them. They are simply the final, inevitable product of its exhaust—a biological accident born of planned obsolescence, living off the leavings of a tomb. They will die only when the roar finally ceases, when the last sub-level turbine grinds to a halt and the true, absolute silence finally floods the halls. Until then, they walk the Drowned Manufactory, blind and deaf to everything but the vibration in the marrow, monument to a world that forgot it was still alive.
