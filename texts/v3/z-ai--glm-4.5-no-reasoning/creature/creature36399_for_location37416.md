The cirque does not have inhabitants so much as it has participants—beings who have surrendered so thoroughly to the terms of the place that they have become expressions of it, indistinguishable in their patience from the stone and the seep.

---

**The amphibians** are the only ones who move with any visibility, and even they seem less like animals than like autonomous droplets—small, wet bodies that have chosen to be conscious. They are nearly black, these newt-salamander things, but that darkness is a kind of camouflage: in a place where the water is tea-dark and the ground is decomposition-black, being invisible is simply being honest about your situation. The gold undertone only shows when they move quickly, which is to say it almost never shows; they keep their gleam private, a hidden warmth that surfaces only in alarm or transit. Their skin has thinned to translucence in the oxygen-rich water, so that in the right light you can see the slow pulse of their blood, the shadow of their simple organs. They are voluntary creatures who have chosen the minimal life. They eat what the decay offers. They breathe what the water holds. They go nowhere that the wet does not take them. When you find one under a stone, and the two of you regard each other, what passes between you is not fear or curiosity but a mutual recognition of strangeness—two different solutions to the problem of staying alive, each incomprehensible to the other.

There may be many of them. There may be only a few. They are difficult to count because they are difficult to find, and because finding one does not mean you have found another. They exist in the shallows, in the saturated ground, in the root-maze beneath the broad-leaved plants—anywhere the wet goes, they go, which is everywhere, but they distribute themselves like a mineral in solution, present in each part but not concentrated anywhere. They have no territory, no displays, no urgency. When they move, they move with a deliberateness that makes the ferns seem hasty—hours to go a few feet, each step a commitment, the body pulled forward by an intention so slow it barely exists. And yet they persist. They have been here longer than the ferns. They will be here after the moss-slough changes the walls again. They are the cirque's only acknowledgment that animal life is possible here, and they make that acknowledgment quietly, without demonstration, as if to avoid drawing attention to the fact.

---

**The insects** are the cirque's least committed residents—present because the world produces them, not because the cirque asked for them. Small flies at the springs, midges in the shallows during whatever season triggers their breeding, and other tiny things that live in the moss-pelt and the leaf-litter and the fungal lace, too small to see, too numerous to ignore. They are suppressed here—dampened, slowed, kept from the abundance they would reach in warmer lower places. The wet weighs on them. The altitude thins the air their wings push against. They exist in a state of perpetual reservation, holding back, never reaching the hum and buzz and skitter that marks insect life elsewhere. The cirque tolerates them. It does not encourage them. They are the fauna of compromise, life that could be more but has accepted less because this is where they found themselves.

---

**The moss** is harder to classify as resident because it does not move, but it is the cirque's dominant consciousness if consciousness can be granted to something that thinks only in centuries and feels only in gradients of moisture. The moss on the lower walls has been here long enough to have memory—not memory as recall, but memory as pattern, as the accumulation of decisions made about where to grow and where to yield, which fractures to colonize and which to leave to the lichens. It grows in a pelt so thick it is almost animal—a fur of green that holds water and releases it grudgingly and calves when it becomes too heavy, sloughing off sections in a slow-motion collapse that takes hours and leaves the stone beneath exposed and pale and already beginning to film over again. The moss is always doing this—building up, breaking off, rebuilding. It is a skin that the cirque grows and sheds and regrows, a living covering that turns the walls from bare rock into something wet and yielding and responsive.

There are several species of moss here, though you cannot tell them apart by looking. They know each other. They compete in the slow war of boundaries, fronts moving centimeters per year, the map of green redrawn on a timescale that makes human lifetimes feel like eyeblinks. Darker patches yielding to lighter ones; bare stone colonized by pioneers that prepare the way for successors; curtains forming where the water-flow is constant, reaching outward under their own accumulated weight until they can no longer hold and break away. The moss is the cirque's closest thing to a mind—distributed, unconscious, processing information about moisture and light and time through the medium of growth itself, making decisions that no individual cell makes but the collective makes, the organism as a whole choosing where to be and how to be there.

---

**The lichens** on the higher walls are older than the moss by orders of magnitude, and they have the quality of beings who have seen enough time to be unimpressed by any particular moment within it. Some of them may be thousands of years old—singular organisms that have been spreading across their centimeters of rock since before the cirque finished forming, since before the mountain's sculpting hand paused, since before the categories of "young" and "old" had any meaning at this scale. They are pale gray and sulphur yellow, mapping the stone in continents of slow growth, each one a map of its own history—where it has been, where it is going, where the rock was slightly more favorable and the growth slightly faster, recorded in the shape and extent of the thallus. They do not ask for much. A little light, a little moisture from the air, and the patience that the cirque provides in infinite measure. They are the place's oldest agreements, life-and-stone contracts that have been honored since before any living thing could witness them.

---

**The broad-leaved plants** on the floor are the cirque's pragmatists—less poetic than the moss, less ancient than the lichen, but efficient in a way that the others are not. Each plant is a self-contained water system: broad dark leaves cupping condensation and rain and dripping it down to its own roots, glossy surfaces capturing what light enters the bowl, thick stems holding position in the saturated ground. They grow in stands, not thickets—each plant separated from its neighbors by negotiated distances, as if they have reached some accord about what each needs and what each will yield. Where a stand establishes itself, nothing else grows; the broad-leaved plants hold territory with a quiet effectiveness that leaves no room for dispute. They are the cirque's landlords, claiming space and managing it, determining what lives in their shadow and what does not. Under their leaves, the amphibians shelter. Around their roots, the fungal networks lace. They are not generous, but they are consistent, and in the cirque's economy of patience, consistency is its own kind of virtue.

---

**The ferns** are the youngest substantial residents, and they have the quality of explorers—still testing, still finding where they can reach, still pushing into niches that the moss and the broad-leaved plants have not claimed or cannot reach. They emerge from clefts in the rock and from the seam where stone meets soil, each frond uncoiling with a deliberateness that makes the act of growth visible—a performance of becoming that the older, established plants no longer need to enact. In the driest weeks, they curl tight, protecting their moisture; when the condensation returns, they unfurl, taking days, making the process so slow you can almost see it happening if you sit and watch. They have nowhere else to be, and no urgency to be somewhere else. Some have rooted in the weeping walls themselves, rhizomes wedged into fractures, drinking directly from the stone's slow bleeding—a commitment so thorough that they have become part of the wall's water system, dependent on the weeping as the moss is, as the lichens almost are not.

---

**The fungal networks** are the cirque's substrate residents—present everywhere, visible nowhere, lacing the ground with pale threads that connect root to root and decompose what needs decomposing and redistribute what needs redistributing. They are the communication system of the place, the infrastructure through which information about nutrients and moisture and chemical signals passes from one being to another. Without them, the broad-leaved plants could not hold their territory; without them, the moss could not colonize new stone so quickly; without them, the dead matter of fallen leaves and sloughed moss would accumulate without breaking down, and the cirque would choke on its own production. They are the cirque's hidden workers, present in every handful of dark yielding ground, threading the decomposition with pale lace, doing the work that makes all other work possible.

---

**The gnarled shrubs** at the rim are boundary-keepers—the cirque's concession to the hard world outside. They are wind-twisted, stunted, their leaves thick and waxy against the thin air and cold nights, and they mark the transition from this wet hollow to the open slope beyond. They are not really of the cirque; they are of the place where the cirque ends, where the walls lower and the protection ceases and the world becomes difficult again. They tolerate the cirque's influence—some moisture, some warmth, the sound that wraps around even them—but they do not depend on it. They are hardy in the way that the cirque's residents are not, adapted to exposure and wind and the cold that the hollow keeps at bay. They are the threshold, the last easy thing before the hard world resumes.

---

And then there is **the cirque itself**, which is not a resident but is the condition of residence—the agreement that all these beings are honoring, the terms under which they exist. The cirque is alive in the way that a body is alive—not conscious, not deliberate, but maintained, homeostatic, regulating itself through processes so slow and distributed that no single component is responsible. The water level that holds constant despite variations in input. The moss-patterns that shift in ways that stabilize rather than disrupt. The sound that persists as a standing wave, the place's own voice, harmonic and slow. The sub-audible pulse that may be springs or settling stone or deep water or the mountain's heartbeat, felt in the jaw and sternum, a vibration that has been going on since before counting and will continue after.

The cirque is an organism of organisms, a living system composed of living systems, each one so thoroughly adapted to the terms of the place that it has become a feature of the place rather than an inhabitant of it. The amphibians are as much a quality of the shallows as the water-darkness is. The moss is as much a quality of the walls as the stone is. The lichens are as much a quality of the time as the rock is. Nothing here is visiting. Nothing here is temporary. Even you, standing in the center at the quietest point, feeling the sound approach from all sides and cancel into pressure—even you are absorbed, for a moment, into the cirque's slow life, your heartbeat joining the sub-audible pulse, your warmth joining the thermal mass, your attention joining the place's own attention to itself.

This is who lives here: the amphibians, the insects, the moss, the lichens, the broad-leaved plants, the ferns, the fungi, the shrubs, and the cirque itself—a community so integrated that it is closer to a single organism than a collection of them, each part so dependent on the whole that none could survive alone, and the whole so constituted by its parts that it would not exist without them. They are not living in the cirque. They are the cirque living.
