To live here is to have never known the sound of your own heartbeat.

The inhabitants of this breathless mesa do not think in terms of days or seasons. Time is measured by the agonizingly slow creep of silicone sap through fractal veins, and by the angle of the overwhelming, unfiltered sun as it rakes across the continent of stone. To be a creature of this world is to be a subject of a geology that has merely learned to bleed.

The dominant consciousness here—if it can be called that, for it is utterly devoid of nervousness or haste—is the colony. The immense, barrel-shaped stands of crystal-ferns are not individuals; they are localized manifestations of a single, sprawling subterranean mind. Their root systems, buried deep in the ancient, rounded silica, have fused over eons into a continental web. They do not compete for resources; there is no water to fight over, no territory to defend against rivals. They are a single organism wearing the mask of a forest, breathing in unison through their waxy, translucent petals. When the harsh glare of the sun hits the vermillion and bruised-purple fronds, the heavy, non-polar sap heats and expands. The colony shifts, settling its immense weight with a tectonic groan that vibrates through the hollow bones of the mesa. This is their language: sub-audible thrums, chemical exhalations, and the patient, creaking acknowledgment of gravity.

They are hunters of the most passive and elegant kind. Their thoughts are airborne pheromones, drifting on the thin, powdery air, forming invisible highways that traverse the vast, echoing emptiness between the monumental stands. They secrete a subtle, irresistible invitation—a chemical mirage of nourishment—that hangs in the desiccated atmosphere like a promise. And they wait. A colony will stand perfectly still for centuries, its geometric leaves forming a stained-glass cathedral of metallic greens and deep reds, waiting for the void to deliver unto it a miracle of moisture.

And the void delivers. The drifters—those pale, gelatinous leviathans of the upper atmosphere—are the wanderers of this world. They are born in the rare, slow cyclones that churn at the edges of the plateau, assembling themselves from microscopic chemical spores and lighter-than-air gases. To look upon a drifter is to look through a ghost. They possess no eyes, no brain, no center of being. They are vast, trailing chemoreceptors, billions of miles of invisible nerve endings unfurled into the stratosphere, endlessly tasting the thin membrane of air for the scent of heavy, oily sap. They live in a permanent, blind sensory deprivation, knowing only the chemical gradient that pulls them toward their feast. They are the only moving things for a thousand miles, and they move like weather systems—slow, inevitable, and silent.

When a drifter descends, it is an act of fatalistic communion. It docks against the crystal-ferns, pressing its gelatinous, translucent mass against the sharp, geometric leaves, seeking the oily distillates that will sustain its slow buoyancy. The collision is entirely silent. The drifter has no mechanism for sound; the colony has no desire to make it. But the moment contact is made, the colony’s true nature reveals itself. The drifter is not a guest; it is an offering of water in a world that has none.

The colony excretes a paralytic mist, a fine, dry chemical powder that settles onto the drifter’s skin. There is no struggle. The drifter has no muscles to convulse, no voice to scream. It is simply locked in place, its vast, pale body merging with the shadows at the base of the vermillion fronds. And then, the colony begins to drink it backwards. Over years—over decades—the opaque, oily sap of the flora circulates down into the roots, drawing the organic moisture out of the dissolving carcass, pulling the water deep into the silica where it will be hoarded and converted into non-aqueous chemistry. The drifter slowly loses its definition, its transparent flesh becoming a dim, bruised smudge beneath the overlapping waxy leaves. Eventually, there is nothing left but a dry, chemical stain on the ancient rock, and the colony stands a fraction of a millimeter taller, its fractal canopy reaching infinitesimally closer to the unforgiving sun.

To live here is to accept that biology is just a slow-motion variant of erosion. The inhabitants of this plateau—rooted and drifting alike—are engaged in a choreography that requires no rush, no sound, and no pity. They are the children of a paradox: a lush desert, a vibrant void. They do not build, they do not remember, and they do not mourn. When the sun tracks away and the cool, radiating chill of the starved rock reclaims the mesa, the colony simply sleeps, groaning softly in the dark, waiting for the light to return and the silent ghosts to drift down once more from the edge of space.
