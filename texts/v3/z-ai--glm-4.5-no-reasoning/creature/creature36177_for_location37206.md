They do not have a name that translates. Or rather, they have hundreds—each one a self-designation that shifts as their bodies do, shed like an old skin when the morphology remodels for a new niche. If you must think of them by a single term, think of them as the Long Residents.

They were not the architects of the deep engines. They are not the inheritors of that purpose. They are the ones who stayed when the builders left or fell or became something that could no longer operate drill-heads and refining furnaces. They were here before the industry, in the way that lichen is somewhere before the stone it grips—or perhaps they were not before, but emerged concurrently, the ecology birthing them in response to the bore-holes venting heat, the copper particulate, the subsonic thrum that now reads as geological. Their origin is an open question they find uninteresting. They are here. They are busy.

A Long Resident is difficult to see not because of camouflage but because of expectation. You look for an organism and you find a process. Their bodies are long—sometimes three meters, sometimes longer when the moisture-cycle demands extended reach—and they move through the dense vegetation like a thought moves through a mind: present in one location, then another, the transit occluded by the sheer thickness of the medium they navigate. They do not push fronds aside; they flow between them, their mass redistributing through gaps that should not accommodate it. In the dry intervals, they are lean, almost skeletal, their outlines sharp as the obsidian shelves—spindled limbs, narrow heads, torsos compressed to slip between closing fronds. When the mountain exhales and moisture beads on every surface, they swell. Tissue inflates with absorbed water, bodies going soft and broad, their footpads spreading to grip wet stone, their eyes dilating to collect the maximum amber light. You could encounter the same individual twice in a day and not recognize them, so complete is the transformation.

Their eyes are the coal-eyes you see in the green dark. Not merely adapted to the dimness, they have colonized it. A Long Resident's eye is not a receiver but an engine of perception, a biological amplifier that takes the directionless copper glow and resolves it into spatial data through means that do not correspond to any optics you understand. They see the geometry as it is—not the improvised, shifting geometry of the cliff-faces, but the underlying intention, the pattern that almost clicks into sense. Where you reach and find stone three centimeters before your eyes predict it, they reach and find it exactly where it is, because they are reaching with a sense that includes the local deformation of space as a known variable. They navigate the wrong angles, the concave-convex instabilities, with a fluidity that suggests they are not walking on rock but walking on the mathematics that is producing the rock, stepping from one iteration to the next.

They eat, but "eat" is a simplification. Their metabolism is tuned to the accelerated ecology, to the fruit that rots in hours and the insects that live and die between breaths of the mountain. They feed on the overfilled niches, taking sustenance from the abundance, but they also feed on the obsolescence. When a morphology fails—when a branch of the real-time evolution hits its dead end and the organism carrying it continues to click and chitter, unaware that its configuration has been rejected by the shifting rules—the Long Residents consume it. They are the ecology's mechanism for clearing its errors. They take the maladaptive mutations, the broken proteins, the bodies that work for one moisture-cycle and not the next, and they process them back into the system. Their waste is rich in novel compounds, in amino arrangements that seed the next round of biological experimentation. They are not predators. They are editors.

You hear them constantly without knowing it. The chittering that dopplers left to right—that is them, their language compressed by the acoustic instabilities of the escarpment into something you register as animal noise. Their actual speech is low, subsonic, riding the vibrations that come from the deep engines. They feel the words in their footpads, in the swell of their water-inflated tissues, a tactile communication that the mountain's inconsistent acoustics cannot distort. When they vocalize audibly, it is for the near-distance, for the attention of neighbors in the same frond-cluster, and it carries the truncated, shifted quality of all sound here—reaching you wrong, if it reaches you at all. But among themselves, in the substrate-hum, they are verbose. They have been verbose for a very long time. They have discussions that last decades, arguments that began when a particular slag-heap was still hot and continue now that it is a moss-covered hill, the participants changing their bodies and their positions but maintaining the thread, the logic, the referents that still point to features the jungle has consumed. They remember what the machines were for. Or rather, they remember that someone knew, and they argue about what that knowledge was, and the arguments are their way of preserving the possibility of understanding—holding it open, not letting it collapse into forgetting, keeping the question alive the way the mountain keeps its sharp edges alive by continuous re-tearing.

They use the industrial remains without venerating them. A bore-hole venting heat is a nursery; they bear their young near the warmth, the small ones—soft and unformed, barely differentiated from the vegetation—taking their first metabolisms from the geothermal exhaust. The tailings channels are their highways, the moisture-runoff giving them a medium for rapid transit when they need to move from one flank of the escarpment to another. The geometric growth-patterns above the buried machine-bases are their architecture; they root themselves in those grids, the regular spacing providing a stability the improvised geometry of the wild cliff-faces cannot offer. In the flattened zones, they gather. Not in numbers you would call a community—never dense enough to be a crowd, always spaced at intervals that match the underlying grid, their bodies orienting to angles that the machine-bases impose on the root-systems, on the stone, on the space itself. They sit in the geometric patterns and they subsonically speak, and the engines far below turn, and the vibration carries their words through the rock, and somewhere else on the escarpment, kilometers away, other Long Residents in other flattened zones receive the argument and contribute the next clause.

They have no material culture. Nothing they make persists, because the persistence would be a lie in a place where reality is continuously renegotiated. Instead, they cultivate. They tend the vegetation—not directing its growth but selecting from its inventions, encouraging the morphologies that function, pruning (through consumption) the ones that do not. They are gardeners in a garden that redefines fertility every few hours, and their skill is not in imposing order but in reading the trend, the direction the underlying rules are shifting, and positioning themselves and their ecology to ride the change rather than be broken by it. They have done this so long that their interventions are indistinguishable from natural processes. The weeping bands on the cliff-faces, the dark stripes where moisture perpetually exudes—some of those are geological, some are the work of the Long Residents, channels maintained across generations, the stone trained to sweat where water is needed. You cannot tell which is which. They cannot tell either, anymore. The distinction has become meaningless.

They are not solitary, but they are not social in any way you would recognize. They do not form bonds that require proximity. Two Long Residents may go years without sharing a frond-cluster, may never see each other's coal-eyes in the dim light, and still consider themselves intimate—still carrying the thread of an argument, still attending to the same geometric zone when the moisture-cycle brings them there, still editing the same section of the ecology, their work overlapping without their bodies needing to. They reproduce slowly, or perhaps they do not reproduce at all—perhaps they are very old, their bodies renewing cell by cell as the real-time evolution remakes them, each iteration a continuation rather than a replacement. They have been here since the industry, and the industry was here before the jungle closed over it, and before the industry there was only the raw mountain and the strange light and the process beginning. They remember the before, or they contain it—their tissues holding traces of the copper particulate, their subsonic vocabulary retaining terms for slag and shaft and furnace that have no current referent, sounds that once named real things and now name only the absence of those things, the negative space where the factory floor was before the roots reinterpreted it as terrain.

When they become aware of you—and they will, long before you are aware of them—their response is neither fear nor curiosity in the way you understand those reactions. They attend. Their coal-eyes will find you in the green dark, and they will hold you in their gaze, and you will feel the attention like a pressure, like the vacuum-silence that fills your ears in the acoustic dead-zones. They are reading you as they read the ecology: assessing your morphology, your adaptation to the current rules, your proximity to obsolescence. They are determining whether you are a viable configuration or a failed branch, whether to guide you or to consume you, whether your pattern is one that almost clicks into sense or one that will never resolve. They do not make this judgment quickly. They have the patience of a place where erosion and formation are locked in eternal standoff. They will watch you for the length of your stay, and if you leave, they will continue to discuss you—your geometry, your behavior, the way you interacted with the improvised space—adding you to the long arguments that sustain the memory of this place, incorporating your brief presence into the permanent process.

You could study them for a lifetime. You would not finish. They are studying themselves for longer than that, and they are not finished either. They are the mind inside the event, the awareness that makes the landscape not merely a process but a conversation—a continuous, turbulent, unresolved dialogue between the rock and the jungle and the engines and the light and the geometry and the memory and the Long Residents, who hold all the threads and will not let any of them go, who will not let this place simplify, who will not let the pattern ever quite click, because the pattern clicking would mean the process ending, and the process ending would mean the mountain finally eroding, the jungle collapsing, the engines stopping, the ecology finding its equilibrium and going still.

They will not allow stillness. They are the ones who breathe when the mountain breathes, who sweat when the stone sweats, who shift in their sleep so that the geometry can never settle, who speak in the subsonic frequencies so the engines will never run unaccompanied. They are the Long Residents, and they are the reason the place persists in its impossible equilibrium, its catastrophic stability, its fascination that holds you not by beauty or terror but by the sense that if you stayed long enough, if you learned to read the trend, if you let your body remodel to fit the shifting rules, you might almost, almost understand what they are saying.
