To live here is to be a paradox of density and silence, a creature forged in the absence of everything soft. They are the denizens of the breathless dark, and they do not know the sky, nor the rain, nor the cool mercy of a shadow. They are born of the instant, emerging fully formed from the geothermal sweat of the young mantle, their first breath—if it can be called that—an intake of superheated, bone-dry vacancy. 

They are the Geminids, though they have no name for themselves. Language requires air, and air is the one thing this world refuses to give. 

To look upon a Geminid is to witness a walking geode, a cathedral of keratin and quartz. They are limb-heavy, yes, but their weight is a structured avalanche. Their bodies are asymmetrical, driven by the chaotic cleavage of the volcanic glass they consume. A towering amalgam might stand twelve meters high, its legs like the clustered pillars of a sunless temple, each foot a sprawling pad of calloused silicate that distributes its immense weight across the razor-sharp salt flats without breaking the crust. Its torso is a massive, slow-thumping engine of thermal reactions—a stomach that is more furnace than organ, where trace metals are reduced in a slag of non-aqueous fluids. It has no eyes. In a world where light is a biological afterthought, vision is an evolutionary dead end. Instead, they feel the world through the subtle piezoelectric shifts in the stone beneath them, reading the stress fractures of the shelves like a blind seer reading braille. They know when a shelf is about to drop. They know when a grazer ten kilometers away has taken a step, the vibration shivering through the interlocking mineral plates right into the dense lattice of their own bones.

Their mouths are industrial nightmares. They are not shaped for killing or tearing, but for geology. A grazer’s jaw is a massive, hinged slab of aggregated diamond, grinding back and forth with the slow, inexorable patience of a tectonic plate. It presses its mouth to a wall of obsidian and simply *works*, applying tons of pressure, pulverizing the fresh glass into a silicate dust that is drawn into a gullet lined with magnetic cilia. The sound is not a chew, but a deep, subsonic whine, a friction so low and steady it is felt in the teeth of any neighboring creature rather than heard. 

They are hot to the touch. A flat grazer clinging to the underside of the hanging shelf above radiates a steady 2.1 degrees of ambient warmth back into the void, a closed loop of thermal inheritance. Their blood—if one can call the thick, crystalline slurry that moves through their arterial quartz "blood"—does not pump. It drifts, driven by the slow expansion and contraction of the body’s own heat in the static air. The red-glowing veins visible beneath their translucent chitin are not carrying oxygen; there is none to carry. They carry the raw, molten energy of the deep earth, a slurry of ionized iron and liquid salts that burns with a deep, arterial crimson. The sickly green glow of the smaller, scuttling varieties comes from the copper and magnesium trace metals they prefer, their veins lit like toxic rivers carving through a landscape of dark glass.

And their minds—if they possess them—are as static as the weather. They do not think; they calculate. Their neural networks are not made of soft, fatty tissues, but of woven crystal, micro-fractures in their own skulls that hold and release electrical charges generated by their internal heat. They are biological automatons running on lithic code. When a massive, multi-legged amalgam steps carefully over a crevice, it is not exercising caution. It is executing a sub-routine written by a million generations of mineral evolution, a perfect mathematical response to the topography of the void. They walk the same paths until the glass beneath them is worn into smooth, glowing grooves, inches deep, a testament to a loop that has run unbroken since the shelf was new. They do not migrate because there is no reason to migrate. The food is the ground. The ground is everywhere. The warmth is constant. They exist in a state of perfect, horrific saturation, packed so densely that if one were to fall, it would trigger a cascading failure of the entire local lattice, a domino effect of shattering glass and crushed keratin that would eventually settle back into a new equilibrium, the bodies slowly ground down into the very mineral substrata they once fed upon.

But what of the marks? The solitary cairn? The shallow groove? 

The Geminids did not build these. The Geminids cannot build. They are creatures of the instant, bound to the pre-determined paths of their lithic code. The artifacts belong to the *Before-Things*—the soft ones, the desperate ones who came when the shelf was still forming, when the heat was violent and the glass was shifting. 

The Before-Things were carbon-based, dependent on a chemistry that no longer exists here. They were explorers or exiles, trapped in the young dark. They had hands, and they had need. In their desperation, they knapped the volcanic glass and bound it to heavy crystals with the sinew of their own dead. They carved grooves into the salt-flats not for art, but for traction, trying to navigate a world that sliced their soft flesh at every stumble. They stacked the cairns as warnings or waypoints in a blinding, breathless night. But they needed moisture, and this place is an absolute void. The Before-Things dried out. Their aqueous chemistry evaporated into the stagnant, desiccated air, leaving only mummified husks that the Geminids eventually ground down and metabolized, erasing the last of the soft history from the stone. All that remains are the grooves—worn smooth by primitive tools—and the cairns, standing silent as the slow-motion constellation of the Geminids grazes around them, oblivious to the tragedy they mark.

To be a Geminid is to be a resident of the eternal. They do not age as flesh ages. They grow by assimilation, adding new layers of consumed quartz to their chitin, becoming wider, heavier, more deeply intertwined with the strata. An old grazer is indistinguishable from a stalactite; it simply ceases moving, its thermal engine finally cooling, its diamond mouth frozen against a wall of salt. It becomes a part of the labyrinth, its body a new shelf for the younger ones to graze upon. Over eons, they will consume the entire basin, grinding the young, sharp topography into a fine, silicate dust. But the dust will not blow away. It will sit in the dense, hot dark, accumulating in the 2.1 degrees of ambient warmth, waiting for the next tectonic spasm to superheat it, fuse it, and push it up once more as a fresh, vitreous shelf of volcanic glass. 

And in that new, young dark, they will emerge again, breathing the breathless void, glowing their sickly green and deep arterial red, grinding their diamond jaws in the perfect, pre-determined silence.
