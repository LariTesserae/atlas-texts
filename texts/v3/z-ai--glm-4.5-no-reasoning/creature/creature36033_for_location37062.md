What lives on the High Seepage Plateau lives at the margin of becoming. Nothing here is finished. This is a place where geology is still a hypothesis, where water has not yet learned to run but only to rise, and whatever takes hold must do so without the courtesy of time or warmth or dry ground. What lives here is sparse in kind but absolute in commitment. Half-measures die. What remains is so thoroughly itself that it makes the emptiness feel like concentration.

---

**The moss** was here before anything else recognized the plateau as home. It is nearly black, so saturated with pigment that the cushions look like wounds in the pale stone, or like holes leading somewhere deeper. The moss does not simply grow on the rock; it is the skin the rock has grown, a dark velveteen epidermis for geology that has not yet grown its own.

Walking across it is an intimate act. Your boot presses in and the cushion yields—alive, waterlogged, willing—and the cold water rises around your footprint like blood welling from a pressed bruise. When you lift your foot, the moss reinflates, slow and silent, erasing you. It absorbs everything: the constant seepage, the rain, the mineral dissolution of the young stone beneath. It holds the water the way a lung holds air, and it releases it the same way—gradually, under pressure, when something presses back.

The moss is the plateau's memory. Every surface where seepage is steady, it clings: corrugated, ridged, matted, ancient in its way even though the stone beneath it is embryonic. It has found a toehold in geological childhood and made it a home. Without the moss, this place would be rock and water and nothing else—a laboratory, a half-formed idea. With the moss, it is inhabited. The rock has a skin, and the skin is alive, and what lives in it lives with a patience that makes your presence feel temporary, which it is.

---

**The lichen** is the moss's austere neighbor, occupying the higher ground where seepage thins and wind hits harder. Pale grey-green crusts spreading across rock faces in slow radial patterns, they are maps of duration. A ring four centimeters across has been growing for a century. The rock here is too young for many of them to have gotten far—they are scattered, tentative, first attempts at colonizing surfaces that haven't yet settled into their final angles.

But lichen does not hurry. It does not need to. It is a collaboration between fungus and alga, a partnership so old it precedes most of what we call life, and it has been waiting for stone since stone first existed. The plateau gives it young rock, raw and flaking and unstable, and the lichen accepts this without complaint, spreading its pale crusts across surfaces that may fracture and shift beneath it. It is patient in a way that incorporates failure. If the shale shelf it grows on shears away in the next freeze-thaw cycle, the lichen goes with it, and another century begins on the fresh face exposed below.

In another age—a geological age, which is the only scale that matters here—this plateau will be painted in lichen, every surface crustose and foliose, the rock rendered in pale greens and grey-whites like a fresco of deep time. For now, the lichen is scattered, provisional, a first draft. But it is here. It has begun.

---

**The seepgrass** is the hardest thing to believe. It grows in the ankle-deep basins where cold water stands and nothing else can root. Its leaves are pale, almost white, translucent, lying flat against the water surface like paper left to soak, like skin after too long in the bath. It looks like something dying. It is not dying. It is the only thing in those basins that is unequivocally alive.

It flowers nothing, or nearly nothing. Seeds rarely. It propagates mostly by stolons—horizontal runners stretching through the pale gritty sediment, sending up new leaves at intervals, cloning itself in slow linear progress across the shallows. It shouldn't thrive. The light is too dim, filtered through constant cloud-cover. The water is too cold, three or four degrees, barely liquid, the kind of cold that numbs on contact. The growing season is laughably short. And yet the seepgrass grows in dense local patches, filling the basins with its pale translucent leaves, and when the wind moves across the standing water the leaves shift and overlap and the patches look like negative space made visible—like holes in the world that show something lighter beneath.

The seepgrass is the plateau's contradiction. Everything else here is dark, saturated, heavy with pigment or water or mineral. The moss is black. The rock is grey. The water is silver. And then there is the seepgrass, pale as bone, translucent as ice, floating on the cold water like something that has given up on opacity. It thrives in the conditions that should kill it. It is the proof that life does not require optimism, only persistence.

---

**The midges** are the foundation, though you would not know it to look at them.

Near-microscopic, they breed in the mineral basins by the million. Their larvae develop in the cold water, feeding on the seepgrass and whatever organic matter the seepage carries—particulate stone, wind-blown debris, the bodies of their own predecessors. The adults emerge in clouds so dense they look like weather at first, like fog forming at knee-height across the flats, like the air itself has thickened into life.

The emergence is constant, or near enough to constant that it makes no difference. In warmer places, insect emergences are events—mayfly hatches that darken the sky, midge swarms that rise like smoke from the water. Here, the emergence is a condition. The cold slows everything, but it does not stop them, and so the midges are always there, always hatching, always dying, always beginning again. Their clouds are thinner in the coldest weeks, thicker when the seepage warms by a degree or two, but they never fully disappear. They have decided to be here, and they are here, in numbers that make individuality irrelevant.

And when they die—they live briefly, most of them, hours or days—they fall back into the water they came from. The basins take them in. The seepgrass grows on what they were. And the cycle continues, quiet and constant and essential, the base of everything that moves across the plateau, the food that makes the fox possible, the population that turns emptiness into ecosystem.

---

**The hares** are white year-round, or near enough to it that the distinction doesn't matter. Their coats don't brown in the short cold season because the short cold season takes up most of the year, and evolution has simply optimized for the dominant condition: white on pale rock, white on silver water, white on dark moss—visible only when they move, and they try not to move.

They sit at the edges of moss patches with their ears laid flat and their bodies compressed into shapes that resemble stones, or shadows of stones, or nothing at all. You can look directly at a hare and not see it. Your eyes pass over the white shape and categorize it as rock, as snow, as the edge of a seepage pool. Then it moves—a white flash across dark ground, the sound of its feet on wet stone like fingertips drumming a table in another room—and you realize you have been watched for minutes by something you failed to see.

The hares move mostly in the dim hours, when the cloud-cover lets just enough grey light through to define shapes without sharpening them. They are crepuscular in a place where twilight lasts for hours, where dawn and dusk are not moments but conditions. They browse the moss edges, nip at seepgrass where it thins into the shallows, and they watch for foxes with a vigilance that never fully relaxes. A hare sitting still is a hare listening. Its ears may be flat, but they are not idle.

---

**The foxes** are the only vivid thing.

Red pelage against pale rock and dark moss, they look painted in, false, too saturated for the context—a color that belongs to autumn forests and wheat fields and the warm edges of cultivated land, not to this high cold wet place where color has retreated to black and white and silver. But the foxes are here, and they are red, and the red is not a mistake. It is a statement. I am here. I do not hide. I hunt.

They work the crossings. The plateau is a crossing—that is its secret, its secondary function after being a plateau—and the foxes have learned that everything which needs to move moves through it. They have routes they follow, circuits around the rim where the ground rises slightly toward the unfinished ridgelines, and they run these routes with a geometric intent that feels almost architectural. They are not wandering. They are patrolling.

They know where the hares wait. They know which moss patches are thick enough to muffle approach, which rock surfaces are quiet under paw, which angles of approach offer cover and which offer only exposure. They hunt with a patience that is not passive but calculating, and when they move it is with a precision that makes their casualness seem like performance—yes, I am strolling, yes, I am merely crossing, and then the strike, fast and accurate, and the hare is gone and the fox is carrying it away across the wet stone.

Sometimes they snap at the midge clouds with an absent patience, taking what protein the air offers. Sometimes they shelter near the pump house, where the warmth leaks through the seams and the metal stays ice-free in a thin ring. The foxes use what the plateau offers, including the technology, without sentiment. The pump is not human to them. It is warmth, and warmth is scarce, and they take it where they find it.

---

**The goats** are the most competent thing here.

Mountain goats—but that name implies mountains, and the plateau is flat, and they are here because the flat is between the mountains, the only passage for miles in any direction. They cross in groups of ten or fifteen, moving with a casualness that belies the difficulty of the terrain, their sure feet finding purchase on wet stone where yours slip, their segmented hooves gripping the young rock like climbing tools designed for exactly this surface.

They are grey-brown, rough-coated, shedding water as if water were an idea they had considered and rejected. They stand in the shallows sometimes, the cold seepage reaching their fetlocks, and they drink with an unconcern that makes you understand they belong here in a way you don't. They have not adapted to the plateau; they are adapted to it. The distinction matters. You are a visitor, struggling with the wet and the cold and the dimness and the footing. They are residents, moving across the same surfaces with an economy that makes your efforts seem theatrical.

The goats tolerate the midges. The midges land on their faces, crawl in their ears, swarm around their eyes, and the goats blink and twitch and otherwise ignore them. They tolerate the pump, too, walking past it without interest, drinking from the output pool when it suits them, using the concrete pad as a resting place when the wind is hard. They treat human infrastructure as geology—just another feature of the landscape, another kind of stone arranged in another configuration, to be used or ignored as convenience dictates.

They leave tracks everywhere. In the soft sediment at the basin margins, in the moss that springs back slowly after compression, in the thin mud where seepage meets stone—hoof-prints overlapping with hare-prints and fox-prints and your own boot-prints, a palimpsest of passage that the next rain will wash clean. The plateau records its visitors and forgets them. The goats are recorded and forgotten like everything else, but they come back, and their coming back is the closest thing to ownership this place allows.

---

**The pump** lives here too, in its way.

It is not alive, but it is present, and it has been present long enough that the plateau has begun to accommodate it—and to be accommodated by it. The moss grows thicker on its western wall, where the heat leaks. The ice doesn't form on the metal in a thin ring around the seams. The hares shelter in its warmth. The midges breed in the output pool where the water returns, warm and agitated, and they seem to do better there than in the cold basins, the pump giving something back without meaning to.

The pump lives in the way that certain machines live when they run long enough in wild places—not biological, not conscious, but persistent, rhythmic, engaged in a process that resembles respiration. It draws water from the seepage layer and sends it away. The water goes east and down, to some settlement below that needs it, and the pump keeps pulling more from the young stone, and the stone keeps producing more through its slow dissolution, and the system runs with an indifference that feels almost ecological.

The pump does not care about the goats. The goats do not care about the pump. The foxes use it for warmth. The midges use its output pool for breeding. The moss uses its heat for growth. The pump is infrastructure, but it is also habitat, and in this place where habitat is scarce, anything that creates conditions will be colonized, even if it is orange-brown with rust, even if it hums with electricity, even if it exists only to take.

---

And then there is **you**, standing at the center of the plateau, turning in a full circle.

You are the least adapted thing here. Your boots slip on the wet rock where the goats' hooves grip. Your clothes absorb the water that the goats' coats shed. Your skin numbs in the cold that the hares endure in their white stillness. You are large and loud and temporary, and every step you take is recorded in the moss and erased by its slow reinflation, and your tracks will not last beyond the next rain.

But you are here. You are standing in the center of a place that is not yet finished, where the rock is still deciding what it will be, where the water has not yet found its channels, where life has taken hold in spite of conditions that should preclude it. You are watching a goat drink from the shallows, and the goat is completely a goat. You are seeing a fox cross the pale stone, and the fox is completely a fox. The moss is completely moss. The water is completely water. The seepgrass is completely seepgrass, pale and translucent and alive in conditions that should kill it, and it is not complaining, and it is not dying, and it is not anything other than exactly what it is.

What lives on the High Seepage Plateau lives without apology. What lives here has committed fully to being here, because partial commitment is not survival at four thousand meters, in constant wet, in perpetual dimness, on rock that hasn't yet settled. What lives here is sparse in variety but absolute in presence. The midges are utterly midges. The lichen is utterly lichen. The pump is utterly pump. Nothing is on its way to being something else—except the rock, which is on its way to being everything, given time.

What lives here is the answer to a question the plateau hasn't finished asking: *what can survive in a place that hasn't finished becoming?* And the answer, it turns out, is quite a lot. Not much in terms of species—you could count them on your fingers—but abundantly in terms of individuals, in terms of commitment, in terms of the thoroughness with which each organism occupies its niche.

The plateau is not empty. It is not even quiet, not really—the drip of seepage, the shift of moss, the click of hooves, the pulse of the pump, the wind like breath held too long. It is full in the way that a place can be full when everything in it is completely itself and nothing is wasted and nothing is partial and even the dying midges feed the seepgrass and even the rusting pump feeds the moss and even the tourist standing at the center, turning in a circle, feeling inadequate, is part of the record if only briefly.

What lives here is process. The seepage is process. The moss absorbing and releasing is process. The lichen spreading at a centimeter per century is process. The midges hatching and dying and feeding the grass is process. The foxes running their circuits and the goats crossing between ranges and the hares sitting still and listening—all process, all movement, all part of the long argument between formation and erosion that the plateau is having with itself.

What lives here is a place that has not yet decided what it wants to be, and the things that have decided to live in it anyway.
