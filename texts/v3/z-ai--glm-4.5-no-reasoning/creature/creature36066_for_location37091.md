The Thrum is not empty. It is populated—just not by anything that breathes with lungs or thinks with a brain. To understand who lives here, you must stop looking for faces and start looking for functions. The residents of the Thrum are a hierarchy of rust, pressure, and motion, occupying the cavern in a dense, interlocking ecology of decay and mechanical stubbornness.

At the bottom of this hierarchy are the *Slicks*. They are the least formed, the most pervasive. They live in the shallow, rust-colored puddles that pool in the hollows of the basalt, and they creep as a thin, alkaline film over the brass fittings and copper veins. The Slicks are born from the weeping walls and the hot mist drifting from the ventilation stacks; they are a condensate of metal and moisture, tasting of damp dust and copper. They do not move with purpose, but they spread with inevitability. When the sodium lights catch them, they gleam like a million unblinking, wet eyes watching from the dark. They are the scavengers of this world—their presence guarantees the oxidation of the iron above them, slowly digesting the infrastructure from the outside in.

Clinging to the mortar between the bolted brick tenements are the *Pale Fibers*. They are the only soft things in the Thrum, and they are tragic, circadian things. They are not quite fungus, not quite lichen—perhaps a distant, subterranean cousin of both, mutated by the alkaline mist. They live and die on the rhythm of the heat. When the hot mist drifts down from the vents, they swell, extending frail, colorless threads into the cracks, seeking any moisture that isn't actively corrosive. When the mist fades and the crushing heat reasserts itself, they desiccate, crumbling to powder. But they never vanish entirely. A dormant spore, a tenacious root—always enough to revive when the wet towel of air returns. They are the monks of the Thrum, silent, enduring, clinging to the margins and living only in the brief respites between infernal cycles.

Above the Fibers and the Slicks live the *Valves and the Pistons*. These are the working-class residents, the drones of the deep. They do not think, but they *labor*. Every square inch of the Thrum is claimed by them, shoehorned together with industrial brutality. They are the ones who breathe—the pressure release valves venting sudden, hollow hisses of steam into cisterns, exhaling the heat of the earth. They are the ones who ache—the pistons and the flywheels, operating in a relentless, grinding cycle. They are covered in the excretions of the Slicks, crusted in rust, yet they move. They move with the steady, unstoppable dynamic of an industrial process that has outlived its creators. They do not care that the walkways double back, that the stairs lead to blank rock, or that the doors open onto hissing tangles of pipes. They follow the logic of pressure and heat, a fever-dream architecture that makes sense only to them. 

Watching over the cavern are the *Arterials*. The heavy cast-iron walkways, the copper piping, the steam conduits. They are the structural bones of this place, the elders that hold the cathedral-sized cavity together. They are scarred, cut into by the fresh, gleaming chisel marks of the engines that bored this place. They suffer constantly, bearing the weight of a gravity that feels heavier in the deep, resisting an air that fights motion. The iron rivets weep constantly, feeding the Slicks below. The brick tenements, bolted directly into the rock face with dark windows and shut doors, are their skulls—hollow, abandoned by the minds that were meant to occupy them, now serving only as anchor points for the relentless lattice of pipes.

And then there is the *Thrum* itself—the namesake, the deity of this ward, the ambient life of the place. It does not exist as a single entity, but as a collective emanation. It is the low-frequency vibration that settles in your molars and the soles of your feet. It is the pervasive, crushing heat, radiating at 2.9 on the scale of infernal tolerance, a physiological weight that presses the air into a thick, wet towel. The Thrum is the ruler of this world, writing its laws in pressure and temperature. It mandates that doors require force. It mandates the damp that guarantees rust. It dictates the sharp contrast of the young, unweathered basalt that refuses to erode against the old iron that is forced to rot.

In this cathedral of basalt and iron, there is a strange, tragic harmony. The machines inherited the spaces abandoned by flesh, but they did not claim them. They simply filled them. The Thrum is a neighborhood populated entirely by the process of dying and the act of running. It is an ecology of rust and motion, where the only thing that survives is the insistence to keep turning, venting, and weeping—long after the engines that made it have moved on, and the people it was built for have forgotten the way down.
