To live in the Canopy Engine is to be a thing of hinges, patience, and mycelial memory. The inhabitants who remain are not its architects; they are its epiphytes, its last antibodies, its lingering fever-dream. They are the Keepers of the Lashing, and they have lived so long in the twilight that the boundary between their flesh and the bark has become a polite suggestion.

They are slight, these Keepers. Generations of breathing the fermented, sap-heavy air have hollowed their bones and stretched their limbs. They move like mantises through the vast, empty cathedrals of woven wood, their fingers elongated into instruments—digits of hard, keratinous growth tipped with chitinous points that click softly against the load-bearing vines. Their skin is not skin, but a smooth, tight-cased bark, mottled with the same lichenous grays and bruised chlorophyll greens as the world around them. To see one standing still against a buttress is to see a knot in the wood suddenly open its eyes. And their eyes are vast, pale, pupil-less things, evolved to drink in the penumbra. They see not by the light that filters down, but by the foxfire glow of the great flower-bells and the faint, thermal breath of the dying mega-flora; to them, the darkness is a textured tapestry of damp heat and cool shadow.

They wear nothing that is not grown. Their garments are grafted—living sleeves of parasitic lace that drink from the capillaries of their arms, providing warmth in exchange for sap. They carry the hand-tools of their ancestors—spindles carved from the hollowed root-tubes of ancient dwarf-shrubs, and grafting knives of volcanic glass set into calcified thorns. But the knives are dull now with disuse. There is nothing left to prune.

They live alone, or in pairs, separated by days of walking across the bark causeways. When they meet, they do not speak. Sound is a violation here. Instead, they press their foreheads to the great living columns, feeling the sluggish, mile-deep pulse of the weeping water, and they exchange pheromonal histories: the scent of a rotting petiole three platforms east, the taste of a fungal bloom rich with copper, the faint, acrid sweat of a topology knot slipping into the abyss. Their lineage and their laws are carried not in books, but in the fungal networks they cultivate in the damp crevices of their own collarbones—spore-memories that bloom and wither with their breath.

They are scavengers of sanity, tending to a machine they cannot fix. Their great sorrow—and their unending labor—is the fraying. A Keeper’s life is spent walking the edge of the great, cavernous voids where the warm mist hangs. They walk to the places where the banyan buttresses loop into their own shadows, where the droplets of weeping water hang suspended in mid-air like glass beads. There, they reach out with their long, bark-crusted fingers and *push*. They pinch the slipping vines, they tie new knots, they splice the exhausted roots back into flat planes of color with their bare hands, feeling the cold shock of non-Euclidean space bite into their fingertips like frost. Sometimes, a Keeper will stand for days beneath a hovering droplet, pressing their hands to the bark, waiting for the local geometry to tire of its suspension and resume its fall. They are gardeners of physics, applying tourniquets of living bark to the hemorrhages of reality.

But the illness is faster than them. It outpaces their slow, biological churning. And so, they are a people in the process of becoming ghosts.

At the edges of the Canopy Engine, where the flora grows exhausted by the logic of physical space, the Keepers are beginning to grow *elsewhere*. It starts with a hand. A Keeper will reach out to tie a slipping lashing, and their wrist will not stop at the joint. The arm extends, not stretching, but unfolding, the bark-peel revealing new fingers, new knuckles, spiraling into a recursive loop that disappears into a flat plane of pure, odorless mist. They do not scream. They simply watch, with their vast, pale eyes, as their body is absorbed into the architecture’s madness. Some have legs that step into non-Euclidean angles, their gait taking them briefly out of the visible spectrum, leaving only the scent of crushed moss and a ripple in the humid air. Some fall asleep against a petiole and wake to find their torso seamlessly braided into the load-bearing column, their heartbeats synchronizing with the slow, metabolic burn of the wood.

They are becoming the lattice. They are being woven back into the world-engine, their meristems bent, their flesh calcified, their memories composted into the thick, wet silence. They are the last generation that remembers there was ever a difference between the grown and the built. When the final Keeper unfurls entirely into the mist—leaving behind only a grafting knife and a hollow husk of seed-pod where they once slept—the Canopy Engine will finally be alone, a continent of living mahogany and impossible knots, weeping its heavy water into the void, running forever on chlorophyll and patience, in the dark.
