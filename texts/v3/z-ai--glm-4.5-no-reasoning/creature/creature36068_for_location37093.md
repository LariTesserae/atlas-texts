To live in the Hanging Ward of Vash is to be a persistent, agonized geometry carved into a roaring flood of biology. The people of this place—the Vashai, though they likely have no word for themselves that separates them from the wet, green throat they inhabit—are not masters of their home. They are an immune response. They are a chronic irritation. They are the sharp, clicking teeth of something that has learned to chew its own tongue to keep the flesh from growing over the throat.

Physically, they are shaped by the deluge. They are compact, dense with the heavy bones of a diver, and pale as the belly of a cave salamander—not the pallor of sickness, but the translucence of creatures who have not seen the raw sun for a hundred generations. Their skin is thin and permeable, constantly drinking the mist, constantly weeping its own slick film of mucus to keep the osmotic pressure of the Ward from flooding their cells. They are hairless. Hair would mat, would rot, would host fungal blooms in seconds. Their heads are smooth, hydrodynamic, their faces dominated by wide, dark, lidless eyes that reflect the diffuse gray-green light like the eyes of a deep-water fish. They do not blink. There is no dry surface for an eye to require protection from; their eyes are continuously washed by the cold, mineral tears of the Ward itself. Their digits are long, heavily webbed, gripping as much as swimming, and their lungs are paired with auxiliary gill-tissues—frilled and dark—that line their necks, flaring subtly when they exert, pulling oxygen from the thick, drinkable air.

They move not as people walk, but as people *endure* movement. To traverse the Ward is to negotiate. They climb, they swing, they wade. Their muscles are built for the sustained, agonizing labor of pulling themselves through shin-deep water, of hacking upward at vines that hang like ropes, of clinging to vertical root-masses while the spray hits their faces. Their bare feet are broad and prehensile, gripping the weeping moss, finding purchase in the bark-fissures where the water runs. They do not run. There is no need for speed in the Ward—only the relentless, grinding stamina of a creature that works eighteen hours a day just to keep its own nest from becoming a stomach. They age early. Their bodies are ruined by the labor: shoulders permanently hunched from the swing of obsidian scrapers, hands calloused and scarred by the caustic sap and the sharp, black thorns of the bryophytes. By thirty, a Vashai is ancient. Their joints are swollen with the damp. Their gill-tissues are often scarred or infected, inflamed with fungal blooms they must scrape away with the same obsidian blades they use to prune their home. They are a people of chronic, low-grade pain, and their culture is built on the assumption that the body is a temporary, leaky vessel, destined to be swallowed back into the churning floor-moss within moments of death.

And yet, they are not miserable. Misery requires a concept of an alternative, and the Vashai have none. They have no mythology of a dry land, no memory of a quiet sky. Their origin stories speak of a seed, a root, a first drop of water. They believe they were grown here, sprouted from the same fungal bloom as the rest of the swarm, differentiated only by the need for a creature capable of holding a blade. They see themselves as the Ward's antibodies—not its owners, not its children, but its *necessity*. The Ward is a living throat, and it is growing, and if it grows without check, it will close. The vault will seal. The light will die. The water will have nowhere to run, and the whole structure will drown in its own stagnation. The Vashai prune so the throat stays open. They are the cough that clears the airway. They are the scratch that keeps the wound from healing over.

Their social structure is as fluid and functional as their technology. There are no leaders, only *shifts*. The community is organized around the unending labor of pruning, a schedule as constant and unvarying as the drip from the ceiling. When one group collapses from exhaustion, the next takes the blades. They communicate not in sentences, but in sharp, guttural clicks and whistles that cut through the biotic roar—the sound of obsidian on vine, of knuckle on bark, of warning. They do not whisper. They do not sing. There is no room for softness. Their only tenderness is the way they sleep: tangled together in the woven hammocks, bodies overlapped for warmth, gill-frills intermingling, sharing breath in the suffocating humidity. To sleep alone is to risk the cold, or worse, the swarm—a centipede exploring a nostril, a frog settling on a chest, the moss beginning to root in the damp folds of the skin. They hold each other not out of love, though love may be there, but out of the pure biological imperative to remain human-shaped in a place that wants to make them mulch.

They do not mourn their dead. When a Vashai falls—into the water, from a walkway, or simply sinks down into the moss and does not get up—the body is left. Within minutes, the amphipods come. Within hours, the flesh is gone. Within a day, the bones are scattered, white and delicate, sinking into the spongy floor. There is no burial because the burial is the floor. The dead are not remembered; they are *recycled*. The Vashai do not fear death, because they do not distinguish themselves from the process that consumes them. They are prune, they are cut, they are devoured, they are grown. The fear they hold is not of death, but of *stopping*. The fear is that the blades will fall still, that the vines will close, that the throat will heal, and the Hanging Ward will become a tomb of green silence.

And so they work. They hack and scrape and sever and cut. Their obsidian tools are never sheathed. Their eyes never close. They are a people defined entirely by the act of cutting back the world, of being the sharp edge that keeps the living architecture from becoming a living coffin. They are the Vashai, the people of the weeping ward, and they are the most alive thing in a place where everything is alive, because they are the only thing that has chosen to be.
