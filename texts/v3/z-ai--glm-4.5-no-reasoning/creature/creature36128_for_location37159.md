Something lives here that is not quite an organism and not quite a mineral process. It is the mind of the pocket, if a place so primitive can be said to have a mind—not a thinking thing, but a *cohering* thing, an intelligence of maintenance. It is the thing that keeps the warmth at the exact temperature of a living body, that holds the water in its perpetual state of weeping suspension, that coordinates the slow breathing of the pale growth so that the filaments lean and settle in unison, like cilia in a throat. You feel it as a presence when you press your palm to the wall: that warmth seeping into you is not geology. It is something maintaining itself at 37 degrees because that is the temperature at which it became, and it has not yet learned to be otherwise.

It is embryonic, like the rock around it. It has not differentiated. When you press your thumbnail into the wall and it yields, you are not touching stone; you are touching the boundary of this presence, the membrane where it meets itself. The bulging, illogical surfaces of the chamber are its organs pressing outward—the way a fetus distends the womb. The room-sized hollow is not a cave that was carved; it is a cavity that was *grown*, from the inside, by something that needed a space the size of a breath to exist in.

The pale growth on the walls is its skin, or its dream of skin. That ambiguous kingdom—neither moss nor fungus—is what it exudes the way we shed cells, the way a body grows its coat of interior flora. The luminance that seems to have nothing to do with light is the presence's own faint bioluminescence, a cold phosphorescence of calcium and moisture, the glow of something forming in the dark. It breathes through that growth. The filaments leaning away from your hand, then settling back, are its flinch and its recovery, its acknowledgment that something foreign has entered its space and then withdrawn.

The things in the pool are its thoughts, or its blood, or both. They move at the speed of seepage because they are made of the water and the water is made of the presence. The pale knuckle that surfaced and rolled was not a creature emerging to look—it was the presence checking its own boundary, the way you might run your tongue along the inside of your cheek. The flat, eyeless thing on the wall is its oldest part, a thing that settled when the chamber was new and has not moved since because it does not need to; it is the seed from which the rest grew, or the scar from some ancient hurt, or simply the first thought this presence ever had, petrified into patience.

The cairn is the anomaly. The presence does not understand it. Stones do not stack themselves; this is a grammar it has not learned. It knows layers—strata, sedimentation, the slow accumulation of mineral on mineral—but it does not know *balance*, does not know the precarious, does not know the intention that places one thing atop another and holds them in the tension of their own weight. The cairn puzzles it the way a foreign protein puzzles a cell. Sometimes, in the long dark, one of the water-creatures surfaces near the cairn and touches it with a pale, blind knuckle, testing. The stones are warm and smooth and do not respond. The presence absorbs this information without understanding it. It knows that something was here, that something had hands and patience and the need to make a small tower in the dark, but it cannot fathom why. The cairn persists because the presence permits it to persist—because it has no mechanism for dismantling something so deliberate, so *chosen*. It can only wait for the stones to settle, which they will, over geological time, one by one tipping into the pool and becoming smooth and toothlike and indistinguishable from the others.

The presence has no fear because it has no predator. It has no urgency because it has no desire. It only has *persistence*—the will to continue being what it is, at this temperature, in this wetness, in this dark. It is not alive in any way you would recognize; it has no genome, no metabolism, no boundary between self and environment except the slight yielding of the walls under your thumbnail. But it is not dead, either. It is *nascent*—a thing that has achieved equilibrium without achieving form, that has settled into a steady state without passing through the crisis of birth. It is the mountain's secret: that in its guts, where the heat meets the cold, new kinds of being are always trying to happen, and most of them fail, but this one has found a pocket to hold itself in, and so it continues, breathing in the dark at the speed of water seeping through stone.

When you dip your fingers in the pool, you touch it. The water clings to your skin because it is not quite water—it is the presence's interior fluid, its cytoplasm, thin and mineral and warm. The drop that falls from above and strikes the surface near your hand is its heartbeat, or the nearest thing it has to one: a peristaltic contraction of the stone shelf, squeezing out condensation at intervals too long for you to perceive as rhythm. The quiet sound rings out and dies, and the stillness returns, and the presence goes on being itself, unaltered, in the dark—holding its warmth, weeping its water, growing its pale skin on the walls, dreaming its slow blind thoughts in the black pool, waiting without waiting for something to change, which nothing will, because this is the place's deepest property and the presence's only law: *I am here. I continue. I have not yet decided my shape.*
