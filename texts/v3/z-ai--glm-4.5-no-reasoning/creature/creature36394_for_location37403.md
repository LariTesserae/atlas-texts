To live here is to be small. To be soft. To be the only warm, sweating, bare-skinned thing in a world of armor and resin.

They are the chroniclers, the gutter-dwellers, the ones who carved out a life in the margins of a metropolis that does not know their names. They have lived in the throat of the mesa so long that they have forgotten there was ever a world where the sky was wider than a slit. They are humanoid, perhaps, in the way that a shadow is humanoid—an implication of a shape, worn down by the crush of the crevice. They are lean and hard-sinewed, their skin stained the color of the stone—ash and old blood and the yellowed pallor of the resin they sleep in. Their hands are broad and clever, the palms toughened into a leather that does not blister against the gill-shelves. Their eyes are large, pale, and rheumy, adapted to the bruised twilight, blind to the distant sun but acute in the dim, churning haze. They do not blink often. There is too much in the air—moth-wings, chitin-dust, the wet spores of the gill-forest—and blinking is a luxury that costs you your footing.

They move like rock-climbers in a vertical city, like spelunkers in a wound. The passage is narrow, and the vegetation is the architecture, and so they press themselves into the flesh of the walls, hand over hand, foot over foot, pulling themselves along the handholds their ancestors carved into the root-shelves. They move with a deliberateness that is almost reverent, almost furtive. To slip is to fall into the slow, grinding columns of the isopods, to be stripped to the bone by mandibles that do not care whether the waxen dew they graze comes from stone or from your palm. And so they cling. Their feet are bare and prehensile, gripping the resinous shelves with toes that have spread wide over generations, splayed like the gills they live among. They wear nothing that is not grown or gathered here—breastplates of shed carapace laced with sinewy vine, loincloths of moth-wing silk, tough and water-resistant in a place where water is a memory. Their tools are bone and flint, knapped from the fallen and the rare, each edge precious, each blade passed down until it is too dull to cut and then repurposed as a gouge or a scraper.

They live in hollows. Nests carved into the thickest root-shelves, just large enough to curl into, lined with shed chitin and the soft, pale fuzz that accumulates at the base of the gill-forest. They sleep in piles for warmth—their bodies run cool, cooler than the fevered hum of the crevice, and they must huddle against each other and against the yielding, sap-warm walls to maintain the heat that the dense, non-aqueous air leaches away. They do not dream, or if they do, they dream of pressure and darkness and the slow, wet rhythm of the stone weeping. They wake to the sound of the isopods grinding, the moths hatching, the great, unseen things shifting in the canopy above. It is never silent. Silence would mean the throat had stopped breathing.

They eat what the throat provides. The waxen dew, scraped from the walls with flint edges, is their sustenance—thick and resinous on the tongue, mineral-rich, tasting of ozone and old iron and something sweet and foreign, like a fruit that has forgotten how to be fruit. They eat the pale moths, catching them by hand in the dim air, pinching the blind, fluttering bodies between finger and thumb, feeling the chitin crack and the warm, sap-like hemolymph seep onto their palms. They eat the isopod-shed—the molted carapaces, softened by the humid air into something chewable, something that cracks between the teeth and dissolves into a sharp, chalky paste. They do not eat the isopods themselves. The isopods are too large, too armored, too numerous. The isopods are the city, and you do not eat the city you live in.

They have a language, though it is not spoken. The throat is too loud for speech—the churn and click and rustle drown out any voice that could be raised. And so they sign. Their hands move in the dim light, fingers flickering in patterns that are part gesture, part touch, pressing meaning into the palm of the listener, tracing words on the resin-slick skin. The language is dense and quick, a shorthand of survival: *isopods moving*, *canopy-thing above*, *dew here*, *dead moth*, *come*, *go*, *careful*, *careful*, *careful*. They have words for the taste of the stone-nectar and the smell of the ozone undertone and the particular quality of the dimness that means the slit of sky has clouded over, though no rain will ever reach them. They have a word for the sound the canopy-things make when they descend—wet, suction-cup tread, picking off the slow—and it is a word that is never spoken aloud, only signed, quickly, with a tremor in the fingers.

They do not worship, exactly. There is no room for temples, no space for altars, no闲暇 for prayer. But they have a relationship with the throat that is older than religion, older than thought. They know that the stone weeps, and that the weeping is life. They know that the gill-forest drinks, and that the drinking is breath. They know that the isopods grind, and that the grinding is time. They know that the canopy-things take, and that the taking is death, and that death is not an end but a dissolution, a feeding of the roots, a cracking of the stone, a releasing of more nectar. They have watched this cycle for so long that it has become the shape of their minds. They do not fear death in the way that the surface-dwellers fear it. They fear it in the way that a leaf fears the wind—with a practical, immediate understanding that it will carry them away, and that there is nothing to be done about it, and that the carrying is, in some deep and unspoken way, the point.

They have no history. Or rather, their history is the throat's history, and the throat's history is written in the smooth, eroded stone and the dense, suffocating mats of gill-lobes and the teeming, churning, breathless life that fills every square inch of the crevice. They know that they have always been here, or that someone like them has always been here, carving nests and sharpening bone and signing *careful* in the dim. They know that the handholds they grip were worn smooth by hands before theirs, and that the flint they knap was knapped by hands before those, and that the hollows they sleep in were hollowed by hands before those. They know that they are the latest in a long, unbroken line of gutter-dwellers, clinging to the margins of a world that does not need them and would not notice if they were gone. And this knowledge does not trouble them. It is simply the shape of their lives, the way the crevice is the shape of the mesa, the way the gill-forest is the shape of the nectar, the way the isopods are the shape of the stone.

They are content. Or if not content, then *occupied*, which is the same thing in a place where the motion is perpetual and the logic is breathless and there is no闲暇 for discontent. They are occupied with scraping and catching and carving and climbing and signing *careful* and *come* and *go*. They are occupied with living in the gutters of a metropolis of armored, clicking, grinding things that stream past them in columns and hatch above them in clouds and descend on them from the shadow with wet, suction-cup tread. They are occupied with being the only soft, bare, sweating thing in a world of resin and chitin and stone. They are occupied with being small.

And they are old. So old that the oldness has become a kind of innocence, a forgetting of the possibility of change. The world outside may die and be reborn—may have died and been reborn a thousand times while they clung to the weeping walls of the throat—but they do not know it and would not care if they did. The mesa is their world, and the throat is their mesa, and the gill-forest is their throat, and the settlement is their gill-forest, and they are their settlement—a dense, urban cluster in miniature, a vertical warren of interlocking habitation, where every resident is within arm's reach of their neighbor's hearth-smoke and every hearth-smoke is the slow, resinous breath of the stone.

They are waiting for nothing. The throat is waiting for nothing. The mesa is waiting for nothing—only the slow, ultimate erosion, the sublimation of the stone, the final, unchangeable end. And in the meantime, they live. They cling. They scrape the waxen dew from the walls and catch the blind moths from the air and carve their nests into the yielding, wet biomass of the gill-shelves and sign *careful* in the dim, bruised twilight, and the isopods grind and the moths hatch and the canopy-things shift and the stone weeps its slow, non-aqueous tear, and the fever burns on, breathless and perpetual, in the hidden, secret swallow at the top of the world.
