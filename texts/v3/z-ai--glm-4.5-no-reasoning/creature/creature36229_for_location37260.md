Before you can name what lives here, you must abandon the word "who." The question of who implies a skin, a boundary, a self contained against a world. There are selves here, but they are not who you are looking for. The true inhabitants of the Green Roar do not possess the place; they *are* the place, expressed in moving parts.

Consider, then, what lives here—not as a collection of species, but as a single metabolism made of multitudes.

**The Siphonophores**
Start with the towers. You might call them trees, but that word belongs to a world where plants are objects. Here, the towers are organs. A two-hundred-meter column of wood and bark and epiphyte is not an individual; it is a specialized tissue of the plateau itself—a photosynthetic lung, a structural bone. The fungal networks lacing through their roots are the nerves. The mycelium does not merely redistribute nutrients; it processes gradients, computes resource differentials, and issues chemical commands. When a tower falls, the fungal network registers a wound. It signals. The surrounding towers alter their defensive chemistry within hours; the understory accelerates its growth within days. This is not competition. This is a body healing. The towers are the specialists—the stoma, the bone, the muscle—of an organism so large it has confused geology with anatomy. They live, but they do so as your liver lives: essential, complex, entirely subordinate to the whole.

**The Circulation**
The animals are the medium the plateau uses to move. In a body, blood carries oxygen and cells carry out repair; here, the insects and birds and unseen movers serve that function. The billions of beetles grinding cellulose are the macrophages, breaking down the old tissue so its components can be returned to the root-mat. The ants tunneling through living stems are the capillary action, forcing fluid and nutrients through the vascular system of the green. The birds—those thousands of declarations claiming frequency and temporal niche—are the heartbeat, a systolic rush of motion and sound that forces the cycle to continue. Their deafening dawn and dusk choruses are not territory claims; they are the pulse of the superorganism, the rhythm of its breathing. The moisture that condenses on your skin is the exhalation of this respiration; the heat is its fever, its metabolic fire.

**The Ghost in the Canopy**
But there is a complication. There are shapes in the mid-canopy who do not fit the metabolism. They are not organs. They are not circulation. They are the ones who make the whooping calls—the sounds that carry information, that require a response, that vary with intent. They are the source of the stone-on-stone grinding. You hear them before you see them, and even when you see them—dark shapes moving with purpose through the branches—they resist identification. They are not merely animals; animals here do not carry stone up from the deep substrate. That act alone defies the logic of the place. To dig through meters of living mat to reach rock, to extract it, to carry it to a clearing, and to stack it—is an act of refusal. It is a declaration that *meaning exists independent of metabolism.* 

They live in the sparse places, the cathedral spaces between the towers. They are primate-adjacent, or perhaps something entirely else that found the same evolutionary niche: hands, social bonds, tool use, language. They are the ones who chip grooves into stone—structural marks, maps, counts, instructions. They are the ones who dig pits and line them with rock, who weave fiber nets that rot but leave their ghostly mineral outlines in the loam. They are the ones who maintain the paths—not the paths of least resistance, but paths that avoid the territories of certain sounds, paths that curve around the hunting grounds of things they fear, paths that assert a *way* through the chaos.

But their lives are precarious, not because of predators, but because of the nature of the place they inhabit. They are building culture inside a nervous system. The green roar is so total, its presence so overwhelming, that to be an individual inside it is to be a single thought trying to hold its shape while the mind dreams. Their stone stacks are attempts to anchor meaning in a medium that consumes meaning. The plateau digests their works. The moss covers their cairns. The fungal network consumes their fiber. The vegetation shifts and swallows their paths. 

They are being absorbed. Slowly, inevitably, the boundary between their culture and the metabolism of the plateau is dissolving. Their whooping calls are being folded into the sonic fabric; they have become another instrument in the orchestra, their language reduced to just another frequency of the roar. Their stone-stacking has become just another geological process, another way the surface is rearranged. They are cells that have begun to forget they are not the body. 

Perhaps they know this. Perhaps the stone stacks are not tools or records, but prayers—protests against the assimilation. A declaration written in the only medium the green cannot immediately digest: *I was here. I carried this. I meant something.*

**The Slow Mind**
And finally, there is the thing that encompasses all of this: the plateau itself. It lives. It thinks. Not as you think, with symbols and language and speed, but with chemical gradients and osmotic pressure and the slow contraction of root-mats. It takes days for a thought to propagate from one edge of a territory to another, carried by pheromone and electrical impulse through the mycelium. The coordination of flowering, the synchronized defense against a disease, the breathing of the root-mat beneath your feet—that is cognition. It is a vegetative mind, planetary in scale, processing information about its own state and making adjustments. It does not notice the stone-stackers any more than you notice the specific firing of a single neuron. They are part of its computation. Their tools, their paths, their protests are data points in a metabolism too vast to care about the meaning of a single groove in a single rock.

So, what lives in the Green Roar?

An intelligence the size of a continent, expressed as vegetation. A circulation system made of beetles and birds. A pulse made of sound. And, buried within that mind like a half-remembered dream, a small, handed, social creature that carries stones from the deeps and stacks them in the light, trying to speak a word that the green will never hear, fighting—generation after generation—against the quiet certainty that they are not residents of this place, but merely its latest thought, and that when the plateau finally shifts its attention, they will be folded back into the roar, leaving nothing behind but a few chips of stone, slowly sinking into the mat, waiting for the moss to come.
