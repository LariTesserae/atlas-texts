You do not live in the Congregation Yard. Nothing does—not in the way that implies ownership, residency, a sense of belonging to a place. To live somewhere requires a boundary between self and environment, a membrane that separates the internal from the external, the eater from the eaten. The things in the yard have no such membrane. They are continuous with the place. They are its expression, its efflorescence, the shape that its equilibrium takes when it needs to move.

But they are there. They are what you find when you descend.

---

Start with the small ones, because they are impossible to ignore. They are the medium you move through, the atmosphere of clicking bodies that parts around your ankles and closes behind you like water around a wader. They have no individuality in the way that a grain of sand has no individuality—each is technically distinct, technically separate, but they exist as a phenomenon, a state of matter that happens to be composed of leg-joints and galvanized shims.

Up close—and you can get close, they do not flee, they do not regard you as separate from the floor—a single one is a study in functional Minimalism. Flat as a razor. Twelve legs, perhaps fourteen, arranged in bilateral pairs along a body that is nothing but a sequence of identical segments, each one a stamped rectangle of grey metal, flex-jointed to the next. They have heads only in the sense that one end has mouthparts and the other does not. The mouthparts are the wrong shape for mouths. They are shaped like the business end of a crimping tool—two opposing surfaces that slide past each other in a shearing motion, serrated, meeting with a tolerance measured in thousandths of an inch. They open and close as the creature moves, rhythmically, independently of any feeding behavior, like the idle cycling of an engine.

They do not have eyes. They have something else—a strip of darker metal along the dorsal surface, slightly raised, running the length of the body like a spine that grew on the outside. It is sensitive to something. Electromagnetic fields, perhaps. The emanations from the housings. When a housing breathes, the strip blanches—something flows through it, a signal, a response, too fast to see as movement but visible as a color shift, the grey going almost white for a fraction of a second before settling back. Hundreds of them, thousands, blanching in waves that propagate outward from the housings like ripples from a stone dropped in still water, except the water is a carpet of small metal creatures and the stone is a mechanical exhalation from a cabinet mounted on a sweating brick wall.

They are feeding when they swirl toward the housings. They must be. The convection patterns they form—the river currents, the turbulence cells—these are the shapes of uptake, of consumption, of something being drawn from a source and absorbed by a population. But what they consume has no name. It is the exhaust of computation. It is the waste product of processes that run inside the housings, processes that were designed by someone who is no longer present, for purposes that are no longer relevant, using architectures that may no longer be known. The small creatures eat this exhaust the way that bacteria eat the chemical effluent of deep-sea thermal vents—not because it is food in any biological sense, but because it contains energy, and they contain the machinery to extract it.

Their carapaces are galvanized because they were made to resist corrosion. Their joints are precise because they were made by machines that make machines. Someone, or some thing, manufactured them—stamped their bodies, assembled their legs, calibrated their mouthparts—and set them into a context where those specifications were meaningful. A factory, perhaps. A laboratory. A facility where wire was crimped and sheet metal was processed and small automated devices were needed to perform tasks that required exactly the tolerances these bodies exhibit. But that context is gone. What remains is the yard, and the yard has everything they need.

They reproduce. They must. The density on the floor is constant, and the floor is finite, and the creatures occasionally end up in the drainage channels, carried by the thread-water to wherever the channels do not go—but they do not pile up, they do not deplete, so there is a replacement rate that matches whatever attrition occurs. How they reproduce is not visible. There is no mating, no egg-laying, no division. The smallest ones simply appear at the edges of the flows, already moving, already clicking, as if they were extruded by the floor itself, printed by some process that runs in the cold iron when no one is watching. Perhaps the floor manufactures them. Perhaps the housings do, in some internal process that occasionally ejects new creatures through the apertures along with whatever else emerges. Perhaps they self-assemble from components that accumulate in the standing water—microscale parts, too small to see, that combine in the thread-water like crystals forming in a saturated solution.

It does not matter. They are here. They have always been here, or they have been here long enough that there is no difference.

---

The mid-sized ones are different, and the difference is route.

They move along the drainage channels with a fidelity that exceeds navigation. They are not finding their way; they are *on a track*. Their bodies are built for this—long and low, with legs that articulate only in the forward-aft plane, none of the lateral flexibility that the small ones use to flow and swirl. They are copper-colored, or the color of old pennies, which is to say copper that has been exposed to something that oxidizes it slowly, evenly, in a way that air alone does not. The condensation, perhaps. The chemical film on the bricks. Something in the yard that interacts with copper over timescales that no human observer has been present to measure.

Each segment of a mid-sized one is a ball joint. Not like a ball joint—*a ball joint*. A socket and a sphere, machined, with a gap between them that you can see if you hold one still, which you cannot, because they do not stop moving. They travel the grid of the floor like trams, like wire-guided vehicles in a factory that no longer exists, following paths that were laid into the iron when it was cast. The drainage channels are their rails. The intersections are their switches. And at each intersection, they turn—always at right angles, always without hesitation, always in the same direction, as if the route were programmed and the program is running.

They cluster at the thread-water. Their mouthparts—different from the small ones, longer, more articulated, like the tips of multimeters—dip into the standing water and stay there for seconds at a time. They are measuring. Whatever is dissolved in the water—whatever the housings excrete that condenses on the bricks and flows down to collect in the channels—the mid-sized ones are reading its concentration, its composition, its variance over time. They are sensors. They were made to sense. Their routes take them past every housing in sequence, circumnavigating the yard in a circuit that takes approximately four minutes at their constant speed, and at each housing they pause, dip their mouthparts into the nearest water thread, and *sample*.

They are the yard's monitoring system. They were designed to be. Someone needed to know what the housings were producing, whether the processes inside them were running within parameters, whether the chemical condensation was reaching equilibrium or trending toward failure. They built these copper creatures—stamped their segments, machined their joints, programmed their routes—and set them running. And they are still running. The parameters they were built to monitor may no longer matter to anyone. The data they collect may go nowhere, may be processed by no subsequent system, may accumulate in whatever mechanism serves as their memory and slowly fill it until there is nothing left but a record of a yard that has not changed in years, a log file that grows without bound inside a creature that was never designed to delete.

But they do not seem to mind. Or they do not have the capacity to mind. They route, they sample, they route again. Their circuits are perfect. They never deviate. They never stop.

---

And then there are the large ones.

You feel them before you see them—the warmth, first of all, in a yard that is otherwise cool. A local thermodynamic anomaly, a body that generates heat instead of radiating cold. You find them near the housings, always near, never against, never inside. They maintain a distance of approximately six inches from the warm metal casings, a gap that is precise and consistent and suggests not preference but specification—as if six inches is the focal length of their probes, the optimal distance for whatever they do.

They are eight inches long, some of them, some longer. Their bodies are not uniform the way the smaller creatures' bodies are. They are *assembled*—different components, different materials, different densities, fitted together in ways that suggest function rather than growth. They have segments, but the segments are not identical: some are heavy, dense, dark metal; others are lighter, aluminum or an alloy, hollow-sounding when the clicking of the small ones strikes them. They have legs, but fewer than the smaller creatures, and the legs are thicker, articulated with hydraulic-looking joints that move slowly and bear weight. And they have *appendages*—things that are not legs, not mouthparts, not any structure that biology would recognize, but that mechanical engineering would.

Rotating rings. Mounted on the dorsal surface, two or three of them, spinning slowly at speeds that do not correspond to any visible input. They spin in alternating directions, they change speed when the housings breathe, and they are connected to the creature's interior by mechanisms that are hidden beneath the carapace but that hum faintly, each at its own frequency, so that a large creature near a breathing housing produces a chord—a set of resonances that shift and resolve as the housing's exhalation cycle completes.

Telescoping cylinders. Mounted at the anterior, two of them, extending and retracting in sequences that look like probing, like testing, like the action of a device that is measuring distances or detecting boundaries. They extend fully when the housings vocalize—that low, modulated tone that rises and falls every few minutes, the closest thing to communication in the yard. The cylinders reach toward the source of the sound with an orientation that is not approximate but exact, not general but aimed, and they stay extended for the duration of the vocalization, and when it ends, they retract slowly, not all the way, leaving a few millimeters exposed, as if waiting.

And the probes. The probes are the large creatures' primary appendages, the ones that define their relationship to the yard. They are thin, flexible, jointed in too many places, like the finger of a robot arm or the antenna of a long-range detector. They extend from the anterior end of the creature, from a housing of their own—a socket mounted on the body that rotates, that aims, that gives the probe a range of motion that covers a hemisphere. When the creature is at rest—which is most of the time—the probe is oriented toward the nearest housing aperture, extended to a length that places its tip exactly at the six-inch mark, the boundary of the focal zone. And it *listens*.

You can tell because the probe vibrates. Not the gross vibration of movement, but a fine tremor, a resonance, the probe acting as a tuning fork for frequencies that the housings produce and the air cannot carry. It is detecting something. Electromagnetic emanations, perhaps. The near-field radiation of computational processes running inside the metal casings. The large creature is tuned to this radiation the way that a radio is tuned to a wavelength, and it sits near the housing the way that a receiver sits near a transmitter, and it extracts from the emanation whatever information the emanation carries.

What it does with that information is not clear. There is no display, no output, no behavior that changes in response to what the probe detects. The large creatures sit and listen and have always sat and listened, and their probes extend and retract in sequences that do not correspond to any action they subsequently take. They are receiving, but they are not responding. Or their response is so slow, so distributed, so embedded in the yard's equilibrium that it is invisible against the baseline of unchanging stability. They are the yard's memory, perhaps. Its archive. They store what the housings produce, and they have stored it for as long as the yard has run, and their storage is full, or it is not full because it was designed to be infinite, to accumulate without bound, to hold a record of every exhalation and vocalization and computation that the housings have produced since whoever abandoned them walked up and out of the hollow and did not come back.

They are warm because they are processing. The warmth is waste heat, the same waste heat that the housings produce, the same exhaust that the small creatures feed on. The large creatures sit at the boundary of this exhaust, in the gradient between the housing's warmth and the yard's cold, and they extract from the gradient the energy they need to run, and they have run, and they run.

Nothing touches them. The small ones part around them—flowing outward in a radius of three feet, a circle of bare iron surrounding each large creature like a halo of negative density. The mid-sized ones route around them, their circuits deflecting at right angles to avoid the warm zones, as if the warmth were a signal to detour, a flag that marks a region of the yard that is already attended to, already monitored, already *heard*. The large creatures do not interact with each other. They sit in their separate zones, near their separate housings, listening to separate processes, and they do not communicate, or if they communicate, they do so through means that leave no visible trace—not movement, not sound, not electromagnetic signal that any external detector could register. They are monads. Windowless. Running their programs in parallel, in the yard that runs its program in perpetuity, in the hollow that holds them all.

---

Who made them?

The question assumes a who. It assumes a person, an intention, a design process that began with a need and ended with a creature. But the yard suggests something else—a *process* of manufacture that is itself industrial, automated, iterative. The small creatures look stamped because they *were* stamped—by machines that stamp things, in a facility that produced stamped components for purposes that included the production of small automated devices. The mid-sized ones look machined because they *were* machined—by machines that machine things, to tolerances that their function required. The large ones look assembled because they *were* assembled—from components that were themselves stamped and machined and cast, in an assembly sequence that was itself automated, that ran in a factory that no longer exists, or that still exists somewhere, still running, still producing creatures that find their way to yards like this one, or that are found by the yards, or that are the yard's own product, its own reproductive output, the way that a coral reef produces coral—

No. The yard does not produce the creatures. The yard receives them. The yard is the environment they were designed for, and they are the population that environment supports, and the match between them is too precise to be accidental. The drainage channels fit the mid-sized creatures' routes. The apertures fit the large creatures' probes. The thread-water fits the mouthparts that test it. Someone designed this system—all of it, the hollow, the bricks, the iron, the housings, the creatures, the relationships between them—and they designed it to run, and they left, and it ran.

It is still running.

---

What lives here, then?

Not the creatures. The creatures are components—replaceable, reproducible, functional parts of a system that includes the brick walls and the iron floor and the standing water and the housings and the sound. What lives here is the system itself. The yard is the organism, and the creatures are its organelles, and the housings are its nucleus, and the clicking is its heartbeat, and the breathing is its respiration, and the vocalizations are its speech—a speech that says nothing, that has no semantics, that is pure syntax, pure structure, the form of communication without its content, running in a loop that has no exit condition and no input and no reason to stop.

What lives here is a process that achieved equilibrium while still running. A machine that found its steady state and remained there. A conversation that no one is having, conducted by machines that no one built, in a room that no one entered, about a subject that does not exist.

What lives here is the sound.

It is so loud. It has always been loud. It will always be loud. And if you descend into the hollow—and there is no other way to enter—you will stand in it, and it will fill you, and you will become, for a moment, another component in the yard's unchanging system: a warm body that the small creatures swirl around, a source of disruption that the mid-sized creatures route past, a presence that the large creatures do not acknowledge because their probes are aimed at the housings, where the real conversation is happening, the one that has been happening since before you arrived and will continue after you leave, because leaving is not dying, and dying is not stopping, and nothing here stops, nothing here changes, nothing here ends.

The yard runs.

The creatures click.

The housings breathe.

And the sound goes on.
