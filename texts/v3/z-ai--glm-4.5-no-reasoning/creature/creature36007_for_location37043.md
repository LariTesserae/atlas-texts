The inhabitants of the Hatchery do not merely live in this place; they *are* the place, dispersed into a million shifting, clicking bodies. They are the gut and the mouth of the plateau, the mechanism by which the unformed stone remembers and forgets. To describe what lives here is to describe a single, distributed intelligence broken into chitinous shards, operating on a logic of digestion, vibration, and relentless self-assembly.

They have no name, because a name implies a fixed state, and these creatures are ontological fugitives—never finishing, always revising. 

If you kneel at the edge of a weeping pool, you will see them: a teeming fringe of geometry at the water’s margin. They are the size of a thumbnail to the size of a human palm, and they are an offense to the comfort of bilateral symmetry. They are not right-handed or left-handed; they are *unhanded*. As you watch, a creature reconfigures itself, body segments rotating like the chambers of a cylinder lock finding their combination. A leg that was third from the left becomes second from the right. A segment pivots, becoming a fulcrum for a new arrangement. They are algorithms searching for a stable shape, executing their own code by eating the stone.

They are born half-formed from the warm, shallow water—little more than a crude mouth and a rudimentary nerve cluster, driven by an imperative to *finish*. They climb the wet rock and begin to feed, and this is the profound secret of their biology: their gut is a mineral forge. They do not consume calories for energy; they consume crystal lattices for *structure*. They eat the pale, violet-grey cartilage of the plateau with surgical specificity, targeting the exact molecular arrangements their unfinished bodies require. The stone passes through them and is transmuted. Inside them, the unformed becomes the formed. They excrete the rejected powder—the white dust that hangs in the humid air—and the water immediately dissolves it, returning the raw material to the pools. They are the worm in the apple of the world, eating the flesh and spitting the seeds back into the soil, but they do it with the precision of a watchmaker assembling a tourbillon.

Their sound is the sound of the Hatchery’s heartbeat: a rasping, clicking, burring chorus. It is not the chaos of insects; it is the syncopated rhythm of a million tiny files shaping a single vast monument. When a stalk of the sound-seeking vegetation bursts, spraying its chemical catalyst into the air, the rhythm changes. The frenzy that follows is not panic; it is inspiration. A sudden rush of coordinated building, as if a new blueprint has just been downloaded directly into their guts. 

And then there are the ones that leave.

Most of the inhabitants will live and die in the pool of their birth, cycling through configurations, building the structures that rise like hollow, humming bones from the plateau. But some hear a frequency that spells *exit*. When the feedback loop between the builders and the resonating structures reaches a critical pitch, a creature answers the call. It climbs the interior of a hum, and as it climbs, it begins its final, most radical reconfiguration. The heavy plates of its back thin into the taut membrane of a wing. Its crawling legs elongate, broadening into steering surfaces. Its segments fuse into a rigid spine. The creature that emerges at the top of the structure is not the creature that entered at the bottom. It has reforged itself for the sky.

It launches, spiraling upward into the diffuse, directionless light, clicking at a frequency too precise to be accidental. It is a messenger. It carries the sum of its pool’s architectural experiments in the shape of its own body, broadcasting it to the far structures. When its click fades into the wet white sky, a distant hum changes. A new pool stirs. A different pattern begins.

The inhabitants do not know they are individuals. They do not know they are separate from the stone, or the water, or the structures. They are fingers, feeling out the shape of a face in the dark. They are a mouth, tasting the laws of physics to see if they are ready to be spoken aloud. They are the inhabitants of the Hatchery, and they are building the world that will, one day, build them back.
