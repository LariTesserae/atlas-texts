The people of Molden-Vash are not a single kind but a stratified living tissue, differentiated by depth the way a body separates its skin from its gut from its bone. They have lived in the weeping city for so long that they have become expressions of its geology—soft things made mineral by pressure, mineral things made soft by the constant attendance of water.

**The General Constitution**

To look at a Vashen is to see someone who has been marinated. Their skin is not merely pale; it is *saturated*, possessing the translucent, blue-veined quality of things that live under water or underground. Generations of un-sunning have thinned their melanin to a functional absence, leaving the blood visible beneath the skin in ways that startle lowlanders—the blush of a Vashen is a dramatic event, a sudden flooding of visible vasculature, and their anger or exertion is tracked in real time by the coloring of their throats and temples.

Their lungs are large. This is not visible, but it is the critical adaptation: the air they breathe is dense with fog, particulate scrubbed by wet but never entirely eliminated, and the spores of a hundred mosses and ferns. A lowlander gasps in Molden-Vash, feeling smothered; a Vashen breathes like a whale, slow and deep, extracting oxygen from medium that is nearly liquid. Their cough, when it comes, is a productive thing—a clearing of the membrane, not a protest. The elderly develop a permanent crackle in their breath, the sound of tissue that has accommodated spore-growth the way the city's walls have accommodated moss. It is not illness. It is just the place living in them.

Their hair is fine and straight and grows in two modes: flat, plastered to the skull by the constant wet, or, in the lower tiers where the water flows freely, matted into natural locks that accumulate mineral deposits—iron rust, calcium lime—until they become composite structures, half-keratin, half-stone. These dreads are not styled; they *occur*, and the colors they take—ochre, viridian, white-lime—are worn without modification, a record of where a person lives and how the water runs there. A person from Tier Eight can be identified by the rust-bloom in their hair. A person from the light-well residences carries a streak of algae-green.

**The Lower Vashen: Tiers Eight and Nine**

They live in the water's lap. The floor is not a boundary; it is a medium, and the people of the deep tiers move through ankle-deep flow the way others move through air. Their footwear—if they wear any—is not shaped for walking on surfaces but for wading through them: open structures of woven cord and salvaged metal that let water pass through, because any enclosed shoe fills and stays filled. Most go barefoot, and their feet have adapted in the way of feet that never meet dry ground: the skin is thick but perpetually soft, macerated to a pale pink smoothness, the toes splayed for purchase on slick stone, the grip strong enough to hold a pipe-joint while both hands are occupied.

They are the thinnest of the Vashen. The lower tiers are cold—the water leaches heat relentlessly—and the work here is maintenance: the unglamorous, endless labor of keeping the drains clear, the pumps running, the foundations from dissolving entirely into the aquifer. They move constantly, not because there is no rest but because stillness is cold. Even their sleep is restless, bodies shifting in the wet, seeking the warm spot next to a pipe or a neighbor, the way dogs circle before lying down.

Their faces are narrow. This is the effect of the dim blue-green phosphorescence that passes for light in their range—faces seen only in that glow develop a particular set: wide-pupiled eyes, still and patient, the mouths thin and closed against the damp. They do not smile less than other Vashen, but their smiles are cautious things, small movements, as if the cold has taught their muscles restraint. When they speak, their voices are low and close, the volume that carries in water but not in air, and a conversation in Tier Nine is a murmur that a surface-dweller would not even register as speech.

They are the ones who know the city's bones. When a retaining wall weeps rust, they know which pipe has failed. When the water rises suddenly in a passage, they know which pump has stalled and can navigate the flooded maintenance tunnels by feel, their hands reading the mortar seams the way a scholar reads text. They have names for the waters—*the iron vein, the sweet seep, the cold press*—that have no technical origin but describe, with a precision the engineers lack, the specific character of each flow. A Tier Eight water-worker can taste a sample from any seep in their territory and tell you where it entered the city structure above, and how long it has been falling.

They do not leave their tiers. This is not prohibition but physics: the passages narrow as they rise, the traffic is dense, and the lower Vashen are uncomfortable in the dryness of the upper levels—their skin cracks and itches, their lungs feel the air as abrasive. When they must ascend—for a structural assessment, for a favor owed, for the rare audience with a foundry-master—they wrap their feet in damp cloth and return as quickly as they can, descending back into the wet like seals returning to water.

**The Middle Vashen: Tiers Four through Six**

These are the people of the foundries, and the foundries have made them as the foundries have made everything in their range: functional, heat-tempered, dense.

They are the widest of the Vashen, thick-armed and deep-chested, their bodies built by the labor of pressing and casting and the constant resistance of machines that do not want to move but are made to move. Their hands are their distinguishing feature—enormous, calloused in specific patterns that record a lifetime of gripping particular surfaces: the rough iron of a press-lever, the smooth heat of a fresh casting, the oily texture of a bearing-housing. A foundry-worker's hands can read the vibration of a machine the way a physician reads a pulse, and they know—without thinking, without measuring—when a press is operating within tolerance and when it is beginning to strain.

Their skin is not pale like the lower Vashen but ruddy, heat-flushed, marked with the small histories of their trade: pale spots where splatter burned clean through the melanin, shiny patches where a graze healed under constant heat and formed a glaze, the permanent shadow of particulate in the creases of their knuckles that no washing removes. They smell of iron and effort, and their hair—when they grow it—is kinked and dry from the heat, a texture found nowhere else in the wet city.

They are the loudest of the Vashen, though this is a relative measure. In the foundries, where the machines roar, they have developed a speech that is not spoken but *projected—a chest-voice, a resonance that cuts through the mechanical thunder without shouting. They can make themselves heard at twenty meters in a room where a lowlander would hear nothing but the press. This voice carries into their home-lives; they are the ones who call to each other across passages, who vocalize greetings and farewells, who fill the residential tiers adjacent to the foundries with a particular warmth of sound that the lower and upper Vashen lack.

They are also the caretakers. This is the paradox of the middle Vashen: they are the most industrial and the most tender, because their work is the care of machines, and the care of machines has taught them a discipline of attention that transfers, without friction, to the care of other things. A press-operator who has spent twelve hours monitoring the temperature of a bearing-race will, upon returning to their quarters, monitor the breathing of their sleeping child with the same vigilance, the same instinct for anomaly. They are the tier where the favor-network is strongest, where the diplomacy of density is most practiced, because the foundries teach collaboration in a way that the solitary maintenance of the lower tiers and the insulated routines of the upper tiers do not.

They mourn their machines. This is not metaphor. When the old press in Tier Five broke its main-shaft in the flood-year, the operators who had worked it for decades gathered in the space beside it—there is no room for gathering in Molden-Vash, but they made room, compressing their bodies into a niche that was not designed for them—and they were quiet in a way that was not the quiet of the city but the quiet of loss. The machine was repaired. The machine ran again. But the operators noted, among themselves, that its rhythm had changed—that it was the same machine and a different machine, and that they would have to learn it again, the way you learn a person after a illness that has altered them but not killed them. They spoke to it, the first day it ran, not in supplication but in introduction: *This is your new cycle. This is where you shudder now. This is what we will watch for.*

**The Upper Vashen: Tiers Ten through Fourteen**

They live in the fog and the fog lives in them. Their quarters are the driest in the city—which is to say, they are still wet, but the water comes from condensation rather than rising flow, and they can, sometimes, achieve a state of merely damp.

They are the administrators, the coordinators, the ones whose labor is not physical but organizational—which is to say, they sit in small rooms near light-wells and manage the cascading consequences of the city's turbulence. Every allocation of coal, every assignment of living-space, every scheduling of maintenance, every resolution of the favor-network's disputes passes through them, and the work has made them a particular kind of person: still, patient, internal. They move less than any other Vashen. They sit for long hours in the grey light, writing (the paper always tacky, the ink always threatening to run) or speaking in the low, even tone that is the register of authority in a place where raised voices accomplish nothing.

They are the most heavily clothed, not for warmth but for the *performance* of status. In a city where every material is functional, the luxury of non-functional fabric—of a sleeve that is too long, of a collar that serves no thermal purpose, of a garment that is not waterproof because it does not need to be—is a declaration. Their clothes are the least stained, the least moss-grown, the most carefully maintained, because the maintenance itself is a signal: *I have the time. I have the space. I have the resources to keep this cloth dry.* In the lower tiers, such a display would be absurd; in the upper tiers, it is the grammar of hierarchy.

They are the most prone to what the Vashen call *the grey drift*—a psychological state unique to the upper levels, where the fog, the dimness, and the sourceless light combine to produce a dissociation that is not quite illness but is not quite health. The grey drift manifests as a slowing: the affected person begins to take longer to respond to speech, to move through passages at a diminishing pace, to sit in their quarters and watch the condensation form on the wall with an attention that is not curiosity but paralysis. It is treated by reassignment downward—a season in the foundries, a month in the maintenance tunnels, any immersion in the tangible and the urgent that can shock the system back into its rhythms. The upper Vashen do not speak of the grey drift often, but they all know it, and they watch each other for its onset with the same vigilance that the foundry-workers watch their machines.

They are the ones who see the sky—when the sky is visible, which is rarely. On the clear days, the days when the fog lifts and the ridge-gap shows the horizon, the upper Vashen gather at the exterior walkways and look outward, and what they see is the lowlands: green, distant, impossibly dry, a landscape that belongs to a different world than theirs. They do not speak of wanting to go there. The desire is present, but it is a desire that has learned not to speak, because the cable-railway goes down for coal and goods, not for people, and the lowlands have no use for a Vashen who cannot breathe in dry air and whose feet have never met unpaved ground. They look, and then the fog returns, and they go back to their rooms and their allocations and the grey light that is all the light they have.

**The Children**

They are everywhere, because density leaves no space for segregation by age, and they grow up in the passages and the workspaces and the living quarters without distinction. A child in Molden-Vash is a small person navigating the same wet walls, the same dim light, the same packed humanity as their parents, and they adapt to it with a speed that astonishes only outsiders.

They learn to read the city before they learn to read text. A five-year-old in Tier Six knows the sound of a press that is laboring—knows it the way a lowland child knows the sound of a car engine turning over, which is to say, not as information but as felt-meaning, a tenseness in the body that says *something is wrong*. They know which walls weep rust and which weep lime. They know the smell of a pipe about to burst—a mineral sharpness, almost electric—and they know to move away from it without being told, because the children who did not move away are not around to be told.

They play in the maintenance tunnels and the light-wells, in the spaces that adults traverse but do not occupy. Their games are spatial and physical: climbing, squeezing, balancing on wet railings, jumping from pipe to pipe across the vertical shafts where a fall would mean death or worse. They are not reckless; they are *skilled*, trained by the environment to a physical intelligence that looks like daring from outside but is simply competence from within. A Vashen child who cannot navigate a wet ledge has not survived childhood.

They do not play at being animals—there are no animals to be. They play at being machines. A group of children in a foundry passage will take roles—*I am the press, I am the furnace, I am the cable-railway*—and they will move and sound like the things they imitate, with an accuracy that is uncanny, because they have spent their entire lives adjacent to these machines and have internalized their rhythms the way other children internalize the rhythms of weather. The machine-game is how they learn the work they will do; it is also how they learn, before any formal instruction, that the machines are entities—present, responsive, demanding of care—and that their relationship with them will be reciprocal, not exploitative.

They are quiet children. Not silent—they laugh, they call to each other, they make the sounds that children make—but quiet in the way of creatures who have learned that sound carries poorly and that the space for noise is smaller than the space for silence. Their laughter is a close thing, held within the group, not projected outward. Their crying, when it comes, is muffled against a parent's shoulder or a wall or the moss that pads every surface, because crying that is not muffled becomes a public event in a place where there is no private space, and a public event requires public response, and the response is not always gentle.

**The Elderly**

In Molden-Vash, age is not a retreat from activity but a *migration* of activity from the physical to the social. The elderly cannot work the foundries; the heat and the labor are for younger bodies. They cannot wade the maintenance tunnels; the cold takes them faster than it takes the young. But they can *know*, and knowledge in a city of cascading small consequences is the most valuable currency.

They are the memory of the favor-network. When a dispute arises between two families over the allocation of a light-well, the resolution is not found in the current situation—it is found in the history of obligations between those families, and that history is held by the elderly, who remember when Family A sheltered Family B during the flood-year, and when Family B provided medicine to Family A during the coughing-season, and whether the balance is equal or whether one debt remains. Their memories are not perfect, but they are *structural*: they remember the shape of relationships even when they forget the specific events, and the shape is what matters.

They occupy the spaces that the working-age population cannot: the corners of residential quarters, the ends of passages, the niches near light-wells where they can sit and watch the dim world and be consulted. They are not retired—they are *repositioned*, shifted from the flows of labor to the nodes of decision, and their authority is not formal but gravitational, pulling disputes toward them the way a low point pulls water.

Their bodies have taken the city's imprint fully. Their joints are stiff in the patterns of their work—a former press-operator's shoulders set at the angle of the lever-pull, a former water-worker's knees bent toward the crouch. Their skin has grown moss in the places where they sit still longest: a fine green nap on the backs of their hands, on the forearms, on the napes of their necks, where the spores have found purchase in the moisture and the warmth and the stillness and have colonized without harm or objection. It is not considered strange. It is considered *elderly*, the way white hair is elderly in other places, and the moss is touched gently by children the way other children touch a grandmother's soft skin, and the elders tolerate this and do not pick the moss away, because it is a sign that they have been here, in this wet place, for a very long time, and that the place has accepted them into its growth.

**The Shared and the Collective**

Beyond the stratifications of tier and age, there is a Vashen-ness that is consistent across the city—a set of adaptations that every resident shares by virtue of being a thing that lives where Molden-Vash is.

They move through passages like water through rock: not in straight lines, but in flows, finding the gaps, the moments of open space, the brief clearing at the intersection where six corridors meet and there is room to breathe for three seconds before the next compression. They do not navigate; they *course*, and their sense of direction is not cardinal but *structural*: they know where they are in relation to the foundries, to the cable-railway, to the light-wells, and these landmarks are not spatial but functional, defined by what happens there rather than where it is. A Vashen giving directions does not say *east* or *up*; they say *toward the pressing* or *past where the moss was cleared* or *below the iron vein*.

They eat together because there is no space to eat apart. The communal meal is not a ritual in Molden-Vash; it is a *necessity*, the only way to fit the activity of feeding into quarters too small for individual cooking. The food is simple—porridge of imported grain, supplemented by the edible mosses that every tier harvests from its walls (some are bitter, some are sweet, all are nutritious, and the Vashen have a taxonomy of mosses-by-flavor that is more precise than any botanical classification), and occasionally fish, brought up by the cable-railway and distributed in portions that are never enough. The meal is eaten in silence or in the low murmur of conversation, and the silence is not uncomfortable; it is the silence of mouths occupied and space shared, and it does not need to be filled.

They sleep in the same rooms where they live and work, and the transition between activities is not marked by a change of location but by a change of *orientation*: the sitting-becomes-lying, the waking-becomes-sleeping, and the room remains the same, the walls still weeping, the moss still growing, the sound of the city still humming its constant hum. They do not differentiate rest from labor in the way that other cultures do; the boundaries are porous, and a person who is resting may rise to perform a task and return to rest without ceremony, and a person who is working may pause to close their eyes for a moment and this is not sleep but it is not quite waking, and the gradient between states is a response to a place where everything flows and nothing is dry and the distinction between one thing and another is always slightly dissolved.

They do not fear the city. This is perhaps the most important thing to understand about the Vashen, and the most difficult for a visitor to credit. They do not fear the wet, or the dim, or the density, or the constant weeping of the walls, or the moss that grows on their belongings, or the rust that consumes their infrastructure, or the water that rises in the lower tiers and falls in the upper ones and never, ever stops. They do not fear these things because they are *of* these things, in the way that a fish is of water, and their relationship with Molden-Vash is not one of inhabitant and habitat but of organ and body. The city is the condition of their existence, and they have evolved within it as entirely as the moss has evolved on the walls—not merely surviving the wet but *requiring* it, not merely enduring the density but *made by* it, shaped by three hundred years of pressure into a form that could not exist in any other place and would not choose to.

When a Vashen dies—which they do, at ages not much younger than lowlanders, despite the conditions, because the body is remarkably resilient when it is adapted to its circumstances—their body is carried to the light-well of their tier and lowered into the compost that feeds the trees. This is not burial and it is not disposal; it is *return*, a recognition that the organic matter of a person has been borrowed from the city's living system and must be returned to it, and the trees that grow in the shafts—the pale, reaching trees that are structural now, that hold up the city above them—are fed by what the Vashen were, and the wood of those trees is used to build the quarters that the Vashen live in, and the cycle is not morbid but *material*, a closed loop in a place where nothing is wasted because there is no space for waste.

And the moss grows over the place where they slept, and the water wears the stone where they walked, and the machine they tended develops a new rhythm that the next operator must learn, and the city goes on, full and wet and dim and alive, and the Vashen go on within it, adapted and adapting, dense and dense-making, a people who are not *in* the city but *of* it, who are what the city grows when the city grows people, which it does the way it grows moss and rust and ferns—because there is water here, and there is time, and there is surface, and life will find all three.
