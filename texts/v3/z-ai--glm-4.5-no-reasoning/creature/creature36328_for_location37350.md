To live here is to be a creature of the margin, engineered by desperation for a world that should not exist. They are not residents of the deep sea; they are its inmates, locked in a perpetually collapsing cell of stone and fury. In a realm where the food chain is moored not to sunlight or green growth but to the slow decay of a drowned megastructure, life has become a kind of mining operation. The living are here to eat the city, and the city, in its agonizing flexes, is slowly eating them back.

The most numerous are the Silt-Breathers, the filterers of the ruin. They move in vast, erratic clouds—shoals of eyeless, flat-bodied fish that are little more than living sieves. They possess no gills in the familiar sense, but rather vast, frilled curtains along their razor-thin edges that extract the microbial bio-films and oxidized stone-dust from the water. They are translucent, their interiors a mapping of primitive, phosphorescent organs that pulse with a dim, bruised slate-blue light—a biological theft of the ambient glow, used only to sense the pressure waves of the heaving water. They have no front or back, no left or right; they are symmetrical planes of tissue that turn as a single, panicked organism. When the currents heave into a violent jet, the Silt-Breathers do not swim; they shatter. The shoal explodes into a million diffusing fragments, each individual tumbling blindly in the hydraulic crush, relying on sheer, astronomical numbers so that a fraction might reassemble in the lee of a worn monolith. They are the chaff of this world, living and dying by the billions, their bodies dissolving into the very silt they consume if they are bruised too harshly by the moving sea.

Clinging to the vast, geometric cavities are the Grief-Shells, the armored mollusks that give this place its slow, grinding heartbeat. They are the size of surface-world barges, their bodies hidden beneath carapaces of thick, lamellar stone. To look at a Grief-Shell is to see only an extension of the architecture: their shells are perfectly mimetic, coated in the same grey-green patina as the drowned masonry, encrusted with the same eons of hydrodynamic smoothing. They are nearly invisible until they move. When they do, it is with a terrifying, glacial slowness. They navigate the vertical drops and inverted ceilings of the megastructure—preferring the overhangs where the ferocious currents cannot reach—by secreting a mineral-resin cement from a muscular, skirting foot. They are the primary lithovores. Their radulae are not fleshy ribbons, but immense, articulated planes of crystalline pyrite, geode-teeth that they press to the ancient walls and drag across the stone with a force that takes decades to build and seconds to release. The sound of their grazing is a deep, sub-audible grind that merges with the throbbing pressure of the deep. They eat the city. They scrape away the bio-film and the oxidized minerals, slowly consuming the very ground they stand on. When the architecture buckles—a sudden, catastrophic flexing of the plateau that sends shockwaves through the water—the Grief-Shells are ripped from their perches. Their cement tears free, taking layers of the masonry with it, and the massive creatures tumble end over end in the dark, their heavy shells spinning like falling coins. They cannot right themselves quickly. They drift, helpless and agonizing, scraping their radulae against nothing, waiting for the chaotic currents to slam them into another strut where, if they survive the impact, they might spend hours re-anchoring and beginning their patient, devouring march again.

There are things that live in the wounds of the structure. When a section of the plateau buckles and settles, crying out in that terrifying, resonant screech, it leaves behind fresh fractures—chips and gouges in the ancient, tech-less material that expose un-oxidized mineral surfaces. Into these wounds come the Suture-Worms. They are not worms at all, but colonies of interconnected, gelatinous polyps that seek out the fresh breaks in the stone. They secrete an acid that dissolves the raw minerals, which they absorb through their outer membrane, chemosynthesizing the exothermic reaction of the acid and the stone into raw biological energy. They are the scavengers of the violence, the first to arrive at an earthquake in the deep. They proliferate in the blinding, choking clouds of mineral silt, knitting themselves into the cracks, their bodies hardening into a chitinous, vein-like lattice as they digest the wound. For a time, they patch the architecture, their colonial bodies acting as a living, calcified grout. But they are temporary. As the Suture-Worms exhaust the fresh minerals, they die, their hardened bodies fossilizing into a brittle, organic fill that the relentless, patient mill of the sea will eventually wear away, returning the structure to its original, ruined state.

And there is the thing that moves between them all, the apex predator of this paradoxical world, though it is not a hunter of flesh. It is the Strut-Wraith. It is a creature of sheer mass and negative buoyancy, a hulking, irregular cylinder of dense, oily muscle and heavy, ossified plates. It has no eyes, no jaws, and no grace. It moves by locking onto the deep-sea currents, extending rigid, fin-like flukes to catch the violent, surging jets of water, allowing the heaving sea to propel it at terrifying speeds through the geometric canyons. It is a battering ram. Its mouth is a broad, expandable maw lined not with teeth, but with thick, keratinous ridges and an immense, muscular gizzard. The Strut-Wraith feeds on the Grief-Shells. It does not hunt them; it simply tolerates the currents until its pressure-sensitive organs detect the low, grinding frequency of a mollusk's radula against stone. Then, it angles its mass and allows the water to slam it into the grazing leviathan. The impact is catastrophic. The Wraith’s keratinous maw clamps onto the shell, and the gizzard engages—a slow, suffocating crush that takes hours, grinding the Grief-Shell’s mimetic carapace down to splinters and dust, digesting the stone-shell and the lithovore within it together. When the architecture itself buckles, the Strut-Wraiths are the first to suffer and the first to adapt. A shifting strut will crush them instantly if they are caught against a wall, their dense bodies bursting into a cloud of dark oil and bone fragments. But if they survive, they will ride the chaotic new currents to find new grazing grounds, new frequencies of stone-on-stone to collide with.

To live here is to exist in a state of constant, exhausting triage. There is no rest, only the intervals between the heaving of the water and the groaning of the city. Every creature is a geological agent, eating the architecture, patching the architecture, or being crushed by it. They are blind to the vastness of the megastructure, knowing only the inch of stone in front of them and the terrible, pressure-driven vibrations that signal the next collapse. They are not a community; they are a chemical consequence, a brief, frantic exchange of energy in the cold, dim bathyscape. They move through the drowned world like a living weather system, perpetually scattered and reformed by the violent storm, their lives measured not in years, but in the number of flexes the ancient, tech-less ruin can endure before it finally, inevitably, grinds them all to silt.
