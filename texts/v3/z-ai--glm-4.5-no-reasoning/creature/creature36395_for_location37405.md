Here, life is not a collection of individuals but a continuous, dimly lit tissue. To speak of "who" lives in this place is to make a category error, for personhood requires a boundary between self and world, and on this plateau, such boundaries are luxuries that have been worn away. And yet, there are presences. There are intelligences. They simply do not wear the shapes that we would recognize as intelligent.

Consider first the architect of the surface.

She is not a creature you will see whole. You will see her influence. She is the one who builds the stone walls, who fits the slate path, who ties the fresh cordage. She is small—necessarily small, for the corridors of this place are narrow, defined by fracture lines and root-tangles, and a larger body would require the clearing of paths, which is not the way of this place. Her hands are wide for her frame, the palms calloused into a texture like the moss she sleeps on, the fingers short and immensely strong. She has climbed here from somewhere below—from the cloud, from the unshaped world—or she was born here, which is to say she emerged from the logic of the place the way the ferns uncurl in their wrong spirals, because that is what the engine produces when it runs long enough.

She does not speak. She has no need to. Language is for coordinating across distance, and there is no distance here that matters, and no one to coordinate with who is not already touching her. She thinks through her hands. When she stacks stone, she is reading the fracture lines with her palms, learning where the rock wants to break, where it has already been broken by the patience of roots. The walls she builds are not fortifications; they are *acknowledgments*. She marks the territories of the larger things not to exclude them but to make the pattern visible, to participate in it. When she places the specimens in circles of twelve, their legs folded, their heads oriented toward the drop into mist, she is not making offerings in any sense that implies a recipient separate from herself. She is *completing a circuit*. The things she places are alive because the engine requires them alive—their stillness is not paralysis but *placement*, and she has learned the geometry of that placement from watching how the insect-tides shift, how the fungi breathe, how the water beads on stone.

She feeds. She must. The stone bowl holds water for her, gathered from condensation, mineral and cold. She eats the fungi, the pale globes that pulse, selecting them at specific moments in their breathing cycle—she knows which phase is food and which is something else, something that would make her part of the pattern in a way she is not yet ready to be. She eats the broad-leaved plants, the stems like wet green rope, but only certain stems, at certain ages, prepared in certain ways. The engine has taught her this. Or she has learned it from the engine. The distinction, after enough time, ceases to hold.

She is not alone. But aloneness is a concept that does not quite apply.

There are others of her kind—or kind is wrong, others of her *shape*. They move through the dim green corridors at times, and she encounters them where the paths cross, and they touch—hands, foreheads, the side of the neck—and continue. They do not linger together because the engine does not require clustering; it requires circulation, and they are part of the circulation. They have built the structures she has built, or she has built the structures they have built; the work is continuous and undifferentiated, the way the moss grows continuous and undifferentiated across a surface. A wall begun by one pair of hands is continued by another, and neither knows or needs to know where their portion ends. The lean-tos with their living roofs are not owned. They are occupied in sequence, like the way the larger things patrol their invisible territories—present for a time, then moving on, the structure remaining, waiting for the next body to fold into its warmth.

They are the newest expression of the engine. This is important. They are not its masters or its keepers. They are its *latest organ*. The way a body grows a new layer of callus in response to pressure, the plateau has grown hands and intention. The stone walls are the plateau's way of articulating its own fracture lines. The slate paths are its way of formalizing its drainage. The moss roofs are its way of extending its fungal shelves into structures that hold heat, that concentrate the decomposition-warmth where a small body can survive the creeping cold. The builders are the plateau doing to itself what it has been doing all along: organizing, patterning, applying.

But they are not the only intelligence here, and they may not be the primary one.

The larger things—those low, many-legged bodies, fist-sized, cat-sized, slow-dog-sized—possess minds. Not minds like ours. Not minds that model the world in abstractions and narratives. Minds that model the world in *chemical gradients*, in the touch of antennae to stone, in the particular wetness of a leaf's surface at a particular phase of condensation. They have memories, but their memories are written in scent-trails that persist in the cool damp air for weeks, that layer and interweave until the stone itself is a palimpsest of where every body has been and where every body is going. They navigate this library of traces with a fluency that is, in its way, reading.

Each of them is a territory. Not that they *defend* territories—they don't need to, the engine has spaced them, has calibrated the number of bodies to the available substrate with a precision that makes conflict redundant. But each carries within its body a map of its circuit: which fracture lines it patrols, which fungal bodies it tends, which clusters of the rice-grain insects it harvests and at what intervals. They harvest with care. They take what the engine can replace, and no more, not because of restraint but because taking more would alter the chemical signals they navigate by, would rewrite the library they read from, and they cannot afford to be lost. They are not wild. They are *managed*, and they manage in turn, and the management is their thought.

When one of them dies—a slowing, a cooling, a folding of legs beneath the body in the same posture they are placed in the stone circles—they do not decay where they fall. They are *collected*. The smaller things, the iridescent and the pale-grained, find them within hours, guided by the shift in chemical gradients that death produces. They are carried—if carried is the word for the cooperative transport of a fist-sized body by ten thousand rice-grain things, a process like a slow, deliberate dissolving-and-reforming around the corpse—to the circles of twelve, or to the composting substrate, or to the stone bowls, depending on what the engine requires. Nothing is wasted because waste is a concept that only exists when a system has an outside. This place has no outside. The cloud at its edges is not an outside; it is a *boundary condition*, a region where the engine's logic becomes indeterminate, where the variables have no values, and nothing can live because there is nothing to compute.

And the insects—the carapace-things, the living pelt—they are not mindless. They are *minimal*. Each one carries a sliver of the engine's processing: a sensitivity to a particular chemical, a tendency to move toward or away from a particular gradient, a pattern of stillness and movement that, multiplied by millions, produces the slow tides that shift across the moss like weather. They are neurons. Not like neurons—*are* neurons. The plateau thinks with them the way a brain thinks with cells, and what it thinks is *itself*: its own state, its own balance, its own endless verification that the water is condensing, the moss is breathing, the fungi are fruiting, the stones are cold, the light is pearl-gray, the engine is turning.

There is, then, a mind here that is not in any single body. It is in the sum of bodies, in the pattern of their arrangement and rearrangement, in the timing of drips and the pulsing of pale globes and the orientation of twelve dead-looking things that are not dead toward a gap in a wall. The builders—the hand-possessing ones, the stone-stackers, the cordage-twisters—are the part of this mind that has recently learned to use tools, to formalize what was previously implicit, to make walls where before there were only invisible territories, to make paths where before there were only scent-trails. They are the mind becoming conscious of its own structure. And they are not in control of it. No more than a neuron is in control of a brain, no more than a gear is in control of an engine. They participate. They are necessary. And they are carried.

The one you would meet, if you came here—if you could come here, which you cannot, because the plateau is not a place for arrivals, it is a place that contains itself—the one you would meet is the architect. The woman-thing, the hand-thing, the one who stacks stone and places specimens and ties cordage that is not yet dry. She would not see you. Not because she cannot sense you—her senses are acute, tuned to the dim and the quiet, and she would smell you, hear the wrongness of your footsteps on her slate path, feel the displacement of her air. But she would not *see* you, because seeing implies a category for you: visitor, intruder, other. She has no such category. She has only the engine and its components, and you are not a component, and therefore you are not part of the pattern she reads. You would be a noise. An error. And the engine corrects errors. It does not do so violently—the plateau is not hostile, the damp does not bite, the quiet does not threaten—but it corrects them. The insect-tide would shift away from you, withdrawing its attention. The larger things would alter their circuits to avoid your scent-trail, leaving a gap in the pattern around your body like water around a stone. The condensation would bead on your skin, the cold would pool against you, and slowly, without force or hurry, you would be made irrelevant. You would stand in a shrinking island of non-pattern, unmeasured by the black eyes, unprocessed by the chemical gradients, and the engine would simply flow around you until you left or until you became substrate.

She would watch this happen. Not with judgment or curiosity—those require a sense of self as separate from the system, and she has lost that, or never had it. She would watch the way you watch water find its level: with recognition, with the knowledge that this is how things work, with the quiet satisfaction of seeing the engine perform as it should. And then she would return to her work, her hands reading the fracture lines, her body moving through the dim green corridor on some errand of placement or collection or construction, and the plateau would close around her like water closing around a dropped stone, and the quiet would be full again, and the engine would turn, and the pearl-gray light would filter through the lenticular haze, and the twelve things in the circle would wait with their heads oriented toward the mist, and the pale globes would pulse, and the moss would breathe, and the place would be sufficient unto itself, as it has been since the rock broke and the water touched it and the engine first began to run.
