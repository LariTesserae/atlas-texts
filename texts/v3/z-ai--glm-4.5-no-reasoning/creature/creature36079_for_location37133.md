To live in Shengao is to be a permeable thing. 

The people of the Weeping City do not exist beside the water, or upon it, or in spite of it. They exist *within* it, as a mineral exists within a solution. They are the conscious tissue of a body that is perpetually dissolving and rebuilding itself, and this fact—this inescapable, geological fact of saturation—has seeped into them far deeper than the damp has seeped into their skin.

**The Somatics of the Damp**

Physiologically, they are long-duration creatures. Their metabolisms are slow, geared to a world where rushing is a hazard and where a misstep on the slick granite sets means a broken bone, or a fall into the path of a haifu, or a slow slide into a drainage channel. They are lean, stretched vertically by the narrow embrasures and the vertical shafts of the Deep City, but their movements are compact and deliberate. You do not swing your arms wide in Shengao; the streets are too full of bodies, of haifu, of iron brackets and protruding conduits. You do not stride; you glide, placing your platformed feet with the exacting precision of someone who has learned to trust nothing that cannot be gripped.

Their skin is the organ of their primary tragedy. It is soft, almost mucosal, perpetually fighting a war of attrition against maceration. Between their toes, in the creases of their elbows and knees, in the folds of their necks, the skin threatens to break down, to become raw, to invite the fungal networks that wait with infinite patience on every surface and in every wisp of fog. They coat themselves in the unguents the factories produce—waxen, mineral-smelling, slightly greasy compounds that seal the epidermis for a few hours before the condensation finds a way through. The ritual of application is intimate and constant, done in the narrow corridors of their dormitories, the smell of the salves mixing with the mineral tang of the weeping stone. To watch someone dress for the wet level is to watch a warrior don armor, except the enemy is an omnipresent molecule.

Their lungs are great, cavernous bellows, adapted to air so heavy with moisture that a drylander would drown in it. They breathe deeply and slowly, pulling the fog into themselves, extracting the oxygen from the wet, and their exhalations add to the condensation. They are, in a small way, part of the water cycle; they drink the city, and they breathe the city out.

**The Psychology of the Film**

The mind in Shengao forms differently. There is no sharpness here, no sudden clarity, no hard edge of distinction. Everything blurs. Everything bleeds into everything else, just as the water finds the seams between stones and the mycelium threads between mortared joints. The inhabitants think in gradients, in degrees of wetness, in rates of decay. They do not think in binaries—dry/wet, alive/dead, clean/filthy—because the city offers no binaries, only slow transitions.

This makes them patient in a way that is almost terrifying to an outsider. They do not expect resolutions; they expect slow transformations. A crack in a chamber ceiling is not an emergency; it is a process. Over months, the water will find it. Over years, the crack will widen. Over decades, the chamber will flood. They plan in decades. They raise children with the understanding that the child will inherit not a static city, but a phase of the city's ongoing dissolution, and that their grandchildren will inherit another. This long-view psychology meshes perfectly with the tempo of the haifu, the slow pulse of the bioluminescent fungus, the geological patience of the shale. They have become, in their minds, geological creatures.

Yet there is a claustrophobia to this patience, a weight that presses down from the perpetual grey-amber sky and up from the weeping rock. They are trapped. They know they are trapped. The escarpments are impassable, the lowlands a myth. They are an island of flesh and iron on a plateau of wet stone, and the isolation has made them insular, suspicious of the concept of "elsewhere." The word *xu*—meaning "less wet," carrying the connotation of the unnatural, the suspicious—extends to people as well. A person who is too dry, who somehow resists the saturation, is *xu*, and they are watched with a quiet, unyielding unease.

**The Social Tissue**

Their society is organized around the maintenance of the boundary between the wet and the less-wet. This is not governance in the abstract; it is the daily, hourly necessity of keeping the water out of the places where it cannot be. The *shuoshi* (the channel-keepers) are the most vital caste. They are the ones who walk the wet level in the deepest night, when the fog is at its densest and the haifu are breathing their slow, wet breaths across the intersections, clearing the gutters, re-mortaring the joints, fighting the mycelium's patient loosening of the stone. They wield long iron hooks and heavy wooden mauls, and their clothing is the thickest and most saturated of all, for they work where the water is fastest and most relentless. They are respected with a quiet, grim reverence, for when a channel fails, a street floods, and when a street floods, the fangyun move in, and once the fangyun move in, the channel is no longer wholly the city's.

The *mianze* (the moisture-readers) are the diagnosticians. They go into the Deep City with lanterns and敲 (qiao, sounding-hammers), tapping the walls, listening for the hollow resonance of a void filling with water, reading the color of the bioluminescent fungus to gauge the mineral content of the seepage. They are the prophets of Shengao, forecasting the city's slow disasters: "This chamber will flood in three years," they say, and the inhabitants begin the patient work of moving their stores, abandoning the space to the deep-things and the blue-green glow.

Above them, the factory workers move in their shifts, eight hours of repetitive motion in the vast, steam-hissing interiors, stamping out the alloys and fabrics and sealants that keep the city alive. They are quiet people. The noise of the factories is too dense for conversation, and the habit of silence carries into their off-hours. They communicate in gestures, in brief touches of damp hand to damp shoulder, in the angle of a head turned toward a loading bay. Their faces are expressionless not from cold, but from the necessity of conserving energy in a world that saps it constantly.

And there are the children. They play on the dry level, chasing the laizao, trying to catch the leathery, darting things with quick, wet hands. They slide on the film of water on the walkways, their platform-clocks clattering on the iron. They are taught early to respect the haifu—to step over the broad, glistening backs without startling them, to recognize the haifu's slow, implacable right-of-way as a law as fundamental as gravity. They are born into the sound-wall, the unending texture of drip and rush and thump and click, and the sound is their mother tongue.

**The Intimacy of the Wet**

Privacy in Shengao is a function of acoustics, not space. The dwellings are small—cubicles carved from the massive walls, barely large enough for a pallet and a waterproofed chest—but they press against each other with no air gaps, and the water conducts sound. You hear your neighbor's breathing, the rhythmic *thock* of them scraping the fungal rot from their doorframe, the wet cough that marks the onset of a lung-infection. You smell their unguents, their body-heat, the faintly sulfurous reek of the channel-water that flows beneath your shared floor.

This proximity has bred a culture of profound, unspoken consideration. You do not make sudden noises. You do not weep loudly, for the sound will travel, and the community will absorb your grief, and you do not wish to burden them. You endure quietly. You maintain your space with a meticulous care that borders on the sacred, scraping back the fungus, re-sealing the cracks, wiping the condensation from your walls before it can pool and seep into your neighbor's cubicle. Your dryness is their dryness; your leak is their flood. The people of Shengao are bound together by the shared project of staying afloat on a plateau of weeping stone, and the bond is tighter than any forged by ideology or love.

Yet there is love, of course. It happens in the deep chambers, where the blue-green glow pulses on a scale of minutes and the acoustics muffle the city's roar into a distant, oceanic murmur. It happens in the drying-niches, where the factory workers hang their sodden head-wrappings and stand for a moment in the relative *xu*, their skin drying for the first time in eight hours, the tautness easing from their faces, and they look at one another and see, briefly, the person beneath the wet. It is gentle, cautious love, slow to form and slow to fade, modeled on the city's own tempo. They do not marry in a burst of passion; they accrete to one another, like mineral deposits, like fungal colonies, until the separation would require a violence that neither is willing to commit.

**The Unspoken Fear**

They do not speak of the Deep City's lower levels. They do not speak of the *shenyu* or the *kui*, the things that rise with the floodwater and retreat when it drains. They do not speculate about what the Deep City was for, who built it, what the tall-ceilinged chambers were meant to hold. The absence is a presence, a quiet weight in the culture, a shared understanding that the city extends downward into mystery and that the mystery is, in some fundamental way, the city's foundation. They live on the upper levels of a structure whose base is lost in water and time, and this is the final model of their psychology: they are the inhabitants of a surface, a wet sheen on a vast, unknown depth.

They are, in the end, indistinguishable from the city. Their skins are chemically akin to the ferrocement; their blood carries the same mineral salts as the channel-water; their bodies will, when they die, be given to the channels, swept into the Deep City, colonized by the blue-green fungus, broken down by the mycelial networks, and re-deposited in the stone. They know this. It is not a source of grief. It is a source of a strange, quiet pride: they are the city's consciousness, the brief, flickering awareness of a process that will continue long after their names are forgotten, long after the stone has reset and the water has found new paths and the plateau has settled into a new geometry. They are the moment when Shengao looks at itself, feels its own wetness, and, without surprise or complaint, goes on.
