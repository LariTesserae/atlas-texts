The Undergrove does not have inhabitants. That word implies a separation between the dweller and the dwelt-in, a distinction between the creature and its habitat. There is no such separation here. The Undergrove is a closed loop of carbon and calcium and warmth, and whatever lives here *is* the Undergrove, just as your blood cells are you, yet are not the entirety of you.

But if you must know who breathes the heavy, metallic air, who drinks the vapor, and who generates the heat that passes through the soles of your boots, you must look at the system in its layers—from the architecture to the ghosts, from the dominant to the dissolved.

---

**The Column-Growths: The Landlords**
They are the first citizens. The forty mature columns that shoulder the ceiling are not plants; they are slow, cold-blooded engines of consumption. They do not grow upward toward a sun they have never seen; they expand outward into the gaps, filling volume the way a gas fills a container. They are heterotrophs that have learned to eat the infrared sweat of the world. They consume the ambient heat of the chamber, the geothermal warmth bleeding from the primordial stone, and the body heat of the fauna that scurries across their hides. 

When you press your palm to the fluted trunk of a column-growth, you are feeling the stomach of a predator digesting the warmth of the space. It takes eons to consume a meal, but they have had eons. They are patient enough to eat a mountain, and they are doing so. They do not think, but they *process*. The root-cables that cross the chamber—growing together at junctions, grafting into knots harder than steel—are their nervous system. Through these fused points, chemical gradients shift and slow electrical pulses travel. They share news of a rock fracture, a shelf-collapse, a strange intrusion of mammalian heat standing in their nave. They are a parliament of digestion, voting on the shape of the chamber by the slow secretion of acids.

**The Shelf-Builders: The City Planners**
The horizontal surfaces that layer the space are grown by the columns, but they are claimed by a second organism—not the columns themselves, but a symbiotic or perhaps parasitic growth that fruits from the trunk-cambium in flat, meter-wide brackets. The shelf-builders are the architects of density. Without them, the Undergrove would be a single domed room. With them, it is a high-rise, a reef, a labyrinth of ten thousand low ceilings. 

They are a deep reddish-brown, the color of clotting blood, and their undersides are smooth and cold, while their tops are furred with a micro-forest of stubby, light-catching protrusions that feed on the bioluminescent glow of the fauna above. They are territorial. Where two shelves meet, they do not touch; they leave a millimeter gap, a demilitarized zone where condensation collects and drips, forming the closest thing the Undergrove has to rain—a localized, perpetual micro-drizzle that feeds the understory below. They dictate the economy of the space. To live on a shelf is to belong to a specific layer, a specific microclimate. They are the walls that make the rooms, and they charge rent in condensation.

**The Thrummers: The Chorus and the Signal**
Clinging to the cold undersides of the shelves, packed tight like bats in a cave, are the thrummers. They are forearm-length, with folded, chitinous limbs and complex mouthparts that have long since abandoned the eating of food. The thrummers do not eat. They *resonate*. 

They are the network's voice. A thrummer presses its mouth to the shelf-membrane and vibrates at a single, inherited frequency. It is born with a note, and it will die holding that note. Together, clusters of thrummers form chords. Distributed across the chamber in a pattern that looks random but is the result of a million years of acoustic optimization, they turn the shelf-growths into giant speakers. The sound they produce is the language of the Undergrove's autonomic nervous system. It is a seismic hum, a bass-note that you feel in your sternum and your teeth. 

They are announcing the state of the whole. A shift in temperature alters the tension of the shelf, which alters the pitch of the thrummer, which is detected by the root-network. If a column-growth begins to digest a new pocket of rock, the chemical change triggers a shift in the humidity, which changes the condensation on the shelves, which changes the mass of the thrummer-clusters, which modulates the chord. The Undergrove is listening to itself think. The thrummers are its brainwaves, loud enough to rattle your skull.

**The Clickers: The Bloodstream**
Beneath the grand structures and the slow signals, the clickers move. They are finger-length, many-legged, and their shells are the color of wet obsidian. They are the most numerous large fauna in the grove. They move in darting, erratic paths across every surface—trunks, shelves, loam, your boot—with an urgency that seems frantic compared to the geological patience of the columns, but is actually the precise, efficient pace of a white blood cell.

They are the recyclers. They are the cleanup crew, the sanitation department, and the transport network. They do not eat the living tissue of the columns or shelves; they eat the dead matter, the frass, the shed skins, the microscopic decay. They carry chemical traces on their legs from shelf to shelf, transferring pheromonal instructions from the root-network to the shelf-builders. When a shelf grows too heavy and cracks, the clickers swarm the wound, eating the debris and secreting a resin that seals the break, nursing the architecture back to stability. Their sound—the *click-click-click* of their tail-segments striking the substrate—is the sound of a heartbeat. It is the baseline rhythm of metabolism. It never stops because death never stops trying to claim the space, and the clickers never stop holding it at bay.

**The Grazers: The Quiet Harvesters**
In the spongy loam at the floor, where the green chlorophyll-based understory fights for the dimmest bioluminescent glow, live the grazers. You hear them before you see them—a wet, rhythmic scraping, like a sickle being drawn through a field. They are the cattle of the Undergrove, though they resemble nothing you have seen above. They have mouthparts made of silicate, harder than the vegetation they crop, harder than the stone itself. 

They are slow, bulking creatures, their bodies low to the hummocked ground, their hides pocked with symbiotic bioluminescent patches that they cannot control, which blink on and off like sleepy eyes. They are the prey. You hear them, and you hear the things that hunt them—sudden silences in the scraping, a muffled struggle, a wet compression, and then the scraping resumes, one voice fewer. They turn the light-harvesting understory into protein, and the protein feeds the shadows that move in the deeper gaps.

**The Singers: The Ghosts in the Dark**
Above the main floor, in the interstitial spaces between the shelves, live the things you do not see. They move through the darkness, their weight causing the shelves to flex and creak—the sound of a living architecture under load. They are the apex predators and the recluses, the things that call to each other across the chamber with sounds that rise and fall, that possess syntax and grammar. 

They are not speaking to you. They are not speaking about you. You are a temporary anomaly of heat and carbon, a blip in their eternal dark. Their language is a mapping of the space: the location of a dying shelf, the timing of a thrummer-cluster's shift, the approach of a grazer. They navigate by the bioluminescence they cannot see, reading the heat of the grove with organs that feel like hollow chambers in their skulls. They sing to reestablish the boundaries of their territories, to coordinate the hunt, to mourn the fallen. Their calls are the most complex acoustic phenomena in the Undergrove, and they will remain untranslated, because to understand them, you would have to live a million years in the dark.

**The Builders: The Dissolved**
And then there are the ones who came before. The builders of the low stone walls near the eastern bedrock. They were not like the others. They were creatures of the sun, of the open air, of metal and impatience. They came down the worn staircase with chisels and ambition, and they tried to carve a piece of the Undergrove into a human shape. They built walls to keep the dark out. They built rooms to hold their belongings. They mortared stone to stand against the root-cables.

The Undergrove did not fight them. It did not need to. It simply waited, and then it absorbed them. The roots grew through their foundations. The loam filled their rooms. The shelf-growths colonized their walls. The clickers ate their fallen grain. The thrummers learned to vibrate at the resonance of their crumbling mortar. 

The builders are gone, but they are still here. Their calcium is in the columns. Their iron is in the blood of the grazers. The carbon that made their hands and their brains and their hopes was broken down by the clickers and fed to the root-network and built into the shelves and breathed out by the thrummers. They did not die; they were digested. They are circulating still, a closed loop, a permanent part of the chord.

---

When you stand in the Undergrove, you are standing inside a single organism. The columns are the bones. The shelves are the skin. The roots are the nerves. The clickers are the blood. The thrummers are the voice. The grazers are the metabolism. The singers are the mind, distant and opaque. And the builders—the people who tried to claim this place—are just a meal it ate a long time ago, still being slowly broken down, still contributing their ancient heat to the dark.
