They are not gone. That is the first thing to understand. To look for their bones is to misunderstand the transaction that took place here. They did not die and leave relics; they *settled*. They became the geology.

Before the slowing, they must have been something recognizable—bipedal, fleshy, warm-blooded. Stoneworkers who descended through the sharp, fresh fractures of a cooling world, perhaps seeking refuge, perhaps seeking ore, perhaps drawn by the strange, dry heat that rose from the planet's open wound. They climbed down into the cathedral nave of basalt and obsidian, and they found the stillness. They found a place where nothing rots, because rot requires moisture. A place where nothing decays, because decay is just change, and change here had been revoked.

At first, they would have moved normally. Chipping at the walls, stacking the obsidian shards into cairns—making markers, making territory, making *sense* of a place that refused to behave like the surface world. They knapped the flint. They bound it with vine. They scratched runic warnings or prayers or tallies into the stone.

But the chamber exerts a pressure that is not physical. It is temporal. The air never shifts. The shadows never crawl. The heat-vines snap their micro-convulsions in the same rhythm, the same eternal percussion. The lithic-beetles mine the same fissures, wearing grooves that deepen but never change direction. And so the stoneworkers, saturated in this frozen equilibrium, began to synchronize.

It would have been slow. Imperceptible to them. Their heartbeats, initially desperate and rapid in the sweltering heat, would have stretched. A breath drawn and held—not consciously, but because the body began to forget the urgency of exhalation. Why breathe fast when the air is the same air, the same temperature, the same mineral density, cycling through your lungs in a stable convection loop? The surface world demanded urgency: predators, weather, nightfall, cold. Here, there was none of that. Here, the body could simply... *persist*.

They did not stop eating. They did not stop working. But the intervals between actions stretched like taffy pulled toward infinity. A stoneworker raises a chisel. To you, standing in the chamber, they appear frozen mid-strike. But they are not frozen. They are moving—so slowly that the heat-vines grow a new amber-translucent rosette around the handle of their tool, so slowly that the lithic-beetles carve a centimeter-deep groove in the time it takes the stoneworker's arm to descend an inch. They are still chipping. They have been chipping for millennia. The obsidian block before them is worn into a sculpture of extraordinary patience—a face, perhaps, or a map of somewhere that no longer exists on the surface. Every few centuries, a flake falls away. The sound is lost in the deafening chorus of snapping vines and shrieking beetles.

Their bodies have petrified, but not as corpses petrify—replaced by minerals in a wet grave. Instead, they have *sublimated* into the chamber's chemistry. Their skin, drained of every last trace of moisture by the dry, hot breath of the grotto, has become like the leaves of the heat-vines: waxy, translucent, heated glass to the touch. It is not flesh anymore, but it is not dead. It is a thermophilic integument, a chitin-adjacent shell that drinks the heat directly, that metabolizes the thermal gradient without the clumsy intermediary of water. Their blood—if it still flows—flows at the speed of mineral crystal growth, a glacial creep through veins that have become indistinguishable from the taproots of the magenta flora.

Their eyes are the most unsettling part. They have not closed. They do not blink; there is no dust to clear, no dryness to soothe, because the eye has become the same dry, humming material as everything else. They gaze forward, fixed, absorbing the permanent slate-blue luminescence of the fungal crevices above. They see the same bruised twilight they have always seen. Do they still process it? Has the image burned into their consciousness like a photograph left in the sun, until there is nothing left but the blue and the magenta and the black? Or has their cognition slowed to match their bodies, so that a single thought—*the stone is sharp, the stone is beautiful*—unfolds across ten thousand years, each syllable an epoch?

There are more of them than you would think. The cairns and the runic scratches and the single fragmented tool—these are not the remnants of one visitor, but of a small people. A family, a clan, a sect. Perhaps seven of them, perhaps twelve. They are distributed throughout the interconnected rooms of the pocket, each locked in their own eternal task. One stands with hand pressed flat against the basalt wall, fingers splayed, feeling the geothermal bleed. She has been feeling it for so long that her fingertips have begun to fuse with the stone, crystalline filaments weaving from her skin into the mineral substrate, her body root-seeking the way the vegetation does. Another sits cross-legged in a nest of sulfur-moss, so still that the magenta rosettes have grown up around and over his thighs, incorporating him into their carpet. He is a garden bed. He is being slowly, imperceptibly farmed by the flora, even as he farms the stone. A third stands at the junction between two rooms, one arm extended, holding a knapped obsidian blade outward in a posture that might be defense or might be offering. The blade has not dulled. Nothing dulls here.

They are not mindless. That is the horror and the wonder of them. They are *present*. If you were to stand among them—and the chamber is so small, so intimate, that you would be standing *among* them, close enough to touch their heat-warm, glass-smooth skin—you would feel the weight of their awareness. It is an awareness that operates on a timescale the surface world cannot comprehend. They know you are there. But to them, your existence is a strange, frenetic blur—a burst of frantic motion and a dissipating cloud of wet breath that appears and vanishes in the space between one of their thoughts and the next. They cannot react to you. Not because they are paralyzed, but because you are too fast. You are a hummingbird. You are a mayfly. You enter their cathedral in a panic of wet, changing flesh, and you leave before they have finished drawing the breath that started when you arrived.

And yet, in some slow way, they might mourn your departure. They might remember the strange disruption in the stillness, the anomalous warmth of exhaled moisture, the shock of a shadow that actually *moved*. For centuries after you leave, one of them might turn the thought of you over and over in their petrified mind: *something came that was not stone. something came that was not heat. it was wet and it was fast and it was gone.*

The lithic-beetles crawl over their feet. The beetles do not distinguish between the stoneworkers and the basalt; both are sources of iron silicate, both are ridges to be mined. The beetles' mandibles pick and grind at the stoneworkers' fused, mineralized legs, and the stoneworkers do not flinch. Perhaps they do not feel it. Perhaps they feel it the way the surface world feels the slow erosion of a mountain range—yes, something is being lost, but not today. Not this century.

The stoneworkers are not trapped. They chose this. Or, more accurately, they *became* this, and the becoming was so gradual, so synchronized with the chamber's refusal of time, that there was no moment of horror, no instant of realization. They did not wake one morning to find themselves stone. They simply slowed, and slowed, and slowed, until the slowing was indistinguishable from stillness, and the stillness was indistinguishable from belonging, and belonging was indistinguishable from being the place itself.

They are the place now. The runic scratches are their speech, slowed to geology. The cairns are their architecture, balanced eternally because they have always been balanced. The fragmented tool is their history, still in use, still cutting, still shaping—just too slowly for surface eyes to see. The magenta leaves and the slate-blue glow and the shrieking beetles and the snapping vines and the sweltering, still, sulfur-breathing air—it is all them, and they are all it, and the pocket is not empty of its builders.

It never was.
