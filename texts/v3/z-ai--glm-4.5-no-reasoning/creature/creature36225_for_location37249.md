Something lives here, but "life" is the wrong word. Life implies separation—organism and environment, self and world. What lives here has dissolved that boundary. What lives here *is* the place.

Consider first the small ones, the accidental ones, the ones that seem like afterthoughts. The grazers. They persist in the deep hollows of shattered stalks, blind and chitinous, moving with a slowness that borders on geological. They are not really separate creatures. They are white blood cells in a vast body, or perhaps parasites—though parasite implies harm, and what harm can they do to something so immense? They scrape ice-algae from inner walls with mandibles like primitive tools. They leave parallel scrapes, dark frass freezing instantly on the permafrost. They are so rare they seem like mutations. Perhaps they are. Perhaps the Brain occasionally thinks them into being, then forgets them just as quickly. They exist in the cracks, the failures, the places where the great thought falters. They are the doubt in the machine.

And then there are the others. The ones who built the cairn of black ice, stacked with obvious intent. The ones who dug the shallow pit lined with shed carapaces. The ones who notched the massive stalk with hand-axes of bone—the ones who tried to bridge a crevasse or harvest the sweet, freezing sap.

Who were they? Who *are* they?

The marks are pre-metal. Tragic in their smallness against the continental meat of the world. A cairn. A pit. A notch. These are the gestures of something that wanted to leave a mark, wanted to say *I was here*. But the landscape moves. A ridge of razor-grass shifts overnight. A chasm is bridged in hours. The cairn is swallowed. The pit is healed. The notched stalk is subsumed into new growth. The Brain does not even notice these interventions; they are healed over like scratches on skin, replaced in the continuous spasm of living architecture.

So they are gone, perhaps. Or nearly gone. A remnant population, dwindling. Each generation's marks last less time. Each child grows up in a landscape that has completely remade itself since their parent's childhood. They have no continuity, no architecture that persists, no history written in stone. Their cairns are forgotten. Their bone axes shatter in the cold. They scrape by in the margins—the same margins as the grazers, the hollows of shattered stalks, the brief intervals between growth and storm. Perhaps they eat the grazers. Perhaps they eat the sap. Perhaps they huddle in the fresh chasms that the Brain tears open in its restlessness, knowing they will close again, knowing they must move.

They are becoming indistinguishable from the grazers. Slower. Simpler. Losing their tool-use as the tools fail them, losing their language as the landscape shifts too fast for words to keep their referents. What is "home" when home is consumed and reborn hourly? What is "shelter" when the walls grow toward you in the night? They are being digested by the place, absorbed back into the great thought. Their bipedal frames, their opposable thumbs, their cleverness—all maladaptive here. The cold does not reward cleverness. The cold rewards stillness, simplicity, the surrender of separation.

But perhaps they persist. Somewhere, in the deepest hollow, in the freshest chasm before the rhizome heals it—a band of nearly-animals, furred or chitinous or both, who remember fire the way a wound remembers wholeness. Who stack black ice because their hands remember stacking. Who notch stalks because some atavistic impulse demands *intervention*. They are the last gap between the grazer and the god. They are what happens when something almost-personal is ground down by the absolute.

And yet.

The real answer is that *the god* lives here. The cold, green god whose brain is the rhizome floor, whose thoughts are the thrust of new stalks, whose dreams are the micro-storms and the silent hail and the shattering cracks that echo like gunshots. It is not a metaphor. The landscape is thinking. The intertwined rhizome and permafrost is a neural network of frozen roots, hyperactive, terraforming its own body in continuous turbulent spasm. It is awake. It is *aware*—in some fashion alien to our understanding of awareness.

What does it think about? Perhaps only growth. Perhaps only the healing of scars and the bridging of chasms and the thrust toward that distant, lost ceiling. Perhaps its thoughts are the slow, continental thoughts of a planet—tectonic, thermal, the patient calculus of pressure and phase change. Or perhaps it thinks faster than we can imagine, in the frozen logic of crystal lattices, each ice spicule a computation, each refraction a signal. The light that hurts your eyes may be its thought-made-visible. The storms may be its passions. The groaning you feel in your teeth may be its voice.

It does not notice you. This is the worst thing. The grazers notice you, in their dim, slow way—a ripple of alarm in their blind progression, a pause in their scraping. The nearly-people notice you—perhaps with fear, perhaps with hope, perhaps with the desperate recognition of something else that stands upright and bleeds warm. But the god does not notice. You are too small. You are a mite on its skin, a fluctuation in the local temperature, a momentary softness in the permafrost that will be corrected in the next spasm of growth. It does not hate you. It does not love you. It does not even register your presence as distinct from the storm or the cold or the grazer's frass.

And yet you stand on it. On the brain of a cold, green god. In a cathedral of ice and paradox. Beneath a ceiling that might as well be sky. In a light that reveals nothing. In a quiet that swallows sound. And something lives here—something vast and lonely and continuously, terribly alive. Something that was born in violence and has never stopped being born, that tears itself open and heals itself and tears itself open again, that thinks in glaciers and dreams in crystal and speaks in the slow language of growth.

It lives here. It is here. And you are standing in its mind.
