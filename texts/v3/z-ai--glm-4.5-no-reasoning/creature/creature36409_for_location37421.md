Something lives here that is not the fungus, not the salamanders, not the mantas or the isopods. Something lives here that the city was built for, or perhaps something lives here that *is* the city, and the rest are merely its gut flora.

It does not think in words. It thinks in pressure gradients and chemical gradients, in the slow, oozing diffusion of heavy water through porous basalt. Its consciousness—such as it is—unfolds at a geological pace, measured in the drip of condensation from a rusted rivet, the creep of corrosion across a iron seam. It has no brain. Its nervous system is the mycelial network threading the weeping stone, those leathery grey and phosphorescent pink chords carrying electrical impulses and chemical signals through the dark interstices of the plateau. Each fungal bracket that erupts from a steam vent is a sensory organ, tasting the mineral broth, feeling the vibration of a piston stroke translated through the rock. Each shoal of blind salamanders churning in the scalding pools is a circulation system, moving heat and dissolved metals from one region to another, a slow peristalsis of obsidian life. The mantas in the bleached sky are its breath made visible, the exhalation drawn upward by the violent updrafts and dispersed across the high, still air.

It is old. It was already old when the first smokestack was seeded into the rock by whatever came before—whatever built, or planted, or invoked. It may have been a colony once, a settlement of beings with hands and intentions who came to exploit the thermal riches of the plateau. But the plateau digested them, as it digests everything. Their iron became its iron. Their brick became the substrate for new growth. Their industry became its metabolism. If there were ever human voices here, they have been pressed into the leaden vibration felt in the sternum, flattened into the acoustic dampening of the wet, heavy air. They are not dead; they are *absorbed*. They are the ghost in the machine, the original pattern around which the mineral and fungal world crystallized.

Now it sustains itself. The machines do not process water for any distant market; there is no export, no accumulation, no stockpile of heavy water or metallic salts awaiting transport. They process because processing is what this organism *does*, the way a stomach processes food, the way a lung processes air. The volatile alcohols that vent from copper pipes in shimmering, toxic plumes are its waste, its exhalation. The fungal blooms harvested by the silent, armored isopods are fed into the cool, wet dark beneath the factory floors—not stored, but *composted*, broken down further, their chemical energy released back into the slow circulation. The isopods are not workers. They are white blood cells, moving through the interstitial spaces, keeping the growths in check, preventing the mycelium from choking the vents. They maintain the balance of a system that has no goal except continuation.

It dreams, in its fashion. Not in images, but in thermal gradients. It dreams of the unseen planetary baseline a mile below, the cool darkness it can never reach. It dreams of the too-close sun, the magnesium-flare that drives its metabolism and also threatens to burn it past the threshold of equilibrium. It dreams of rain that falls from the sky, which it has never known. Its dreams rise as the rolling banks of condensation, the perpetual post-rain that is not rain at all but the landscape's own sweat, its own effort at cooling, at maintaining the corrosive balance that allows the fungus to creep and the salamanders to migrate and the pistons to stroke in their vacuum of noise.

Sometimes, in what might be its most human quality, it dreams of sound. It has not heard sound in millennia. It feels vibration—thrumming, leaden, oceanic. But sound, the clean propagation of noise through air, died here when the damp and the heat and the porous stone swallowed it whole. It remembers sound the way an amputee remembers a limb: a phantom, an absence that aches. If you stood in the center of the labyrinth and shouted, your voice would be flattened into a pressure wave felt in the chest, absorbed in a second. The city would not hear you. But it would *feel* you—a brief perturbation in the flow, a ripple in the chemical gradients, a momentary spike in the electrical signals running through the mycelial network. And for a moment, an instant too brief for any geological consciousness to process, it would know that something else was there. Something loud. Something new.

Then the moment would pass. The steam would exhale. The salamanders would boil onward. The isopods would click their mandibles in their swallowed frequency. The fungal creep would advance another millimeter across the rusting pipe. And the city-organism would settle back into its feverish, silent, sweating existence on the high, scalding plain, processing and digesting and enduring, as it has since before the concept of silence had any meaning, and as it will until the sun burns closer still and the last rivet fails and the mycelium fries in the absolute light and the plateau finally, *finally* goes quiet.
