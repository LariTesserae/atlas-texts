The people of Kharsaal do not live; they persist. They are the sparse, hardy fauna of a biome that defeats lushness, shaped by the wet, the warmth, and the acoustic pressure into something stripped of superfluity. They are creatures of equilibrium, having surrendered to the city what the city demanded of them, until what remains is only the durable core.

Physically, they have settled into the demands of their environment. They are not a tall people; the oppressive, sourceless humidity and the weight of the perpetual vibration compress them, encourage a posture of subtle flexion, as if they are bracing against a pressure that comes from all directions and nowhere. Their skin is remarkable. It takes on the mineral palette of the city—rust-red, grey-green, the pale yellow of lichen—a map of Kharsaal drawn in the very texture of their pores. This is not merely a coating of grime, though grime is constant. The weeping stone and the airborne rust have entered them. Their sweat mixes with the metallic condensation, creating a faint, oxidative film that seals them against the damp. They do not rot in Kharsaal; they oxidize. Their hair, when they let it grow, lies flat and dark, slicked perpetually by the atmosphere, smelling of wet iron and warm stone. Their hands are thick, the palms coated in a leathery callus that vibrates faintly in time with the pistons. 

Their ears are ruined. This is the defining mark of Kharsaal’s residents, the boundary between the visitor and the native. New arrivals shout against the roar, straining their vocal cords, their faces contorted with the effort of communication. The locals do not bother. The sound has calcified the fine bones of their inner ears, dulling the higher frequencies and flattening the world into a monophonic hum. They do not hear the machinery as noise; they feel it as topography. A shift in the frequency of a turbine’s whine is not heard, but felt as a subtle change in the vibration of the floor plates—a sensory substitution that makes them intimate with the city's health in ways no diagnostic tool could replicate. They speak in a crude, fluid sign language, broad and slow, designed to be read through the visual haze. When they must use their voices, they produce deep, resonant sounds that cut through the low-frequency wash, their throats vibrating in harmony with the presses. 

They move with the patience of the plateau. Speed is a liability in Kharsaal; the wet stone is treacherous, the condensation making every surface a potential slide into a pool of black water. Their gait is a deliberate, rolling trudge, feet placed flat to test the grip, bodies leaning into the omnipresent vibration. They are synchronized with the rhythms of the machines. When the great pistons descend, the city exhales, and the people take their step; when the pistons rise, the stone weeps harder, and they pause. It is an unconscious rhythm, a circadian cycle dictated not by the diffuse, uncertain sun, but by the three-hundred-year heartbeat of the industry.

Their minds have been weathered into the same forgiving slopes as the ancient table-mountain. Interiority is a luxury that the acoustic pressure grinds away. To think too loudly, to indulge in anxiety or elaborate planning, is to create a space inside oneself that the sound will inevitably invade and fill. They have become shallow-rooted, like the pale lichens on the rusting iron, anchoring only to the immediate moment. Their memory is institutional, not personal. A worker remembers the sequence of valves to turn, the pressure at which a specific boiler runs clean, the way a particular gear-grind precedes a jam. They do not remember their childhoods with any clarity. The past has been worn smooth by the hum; events lose their edges, rounded off into a general, undifferentiated sense of *before*. The future is an abstract concept, barely useful. There is only the next shift, the next bead of condensation tracking down the wall, the next subtle shift in the floor's tremor.

They live in the worker districts, layered above the factories like sedimentary deposits. Their homes are cells of brick and sweating stone, personal volumes carved out of the industrial mass. They do not decorate. Decoration implies a boundary between the self and the city, and no such boundary exists. Their few possessions are functional: changes of clothing stained the same mineral hues, crude lamps that burn the city's oils, tablets of compressed carbon for writing messages that will be smeared by the morning's condensation. They sleep in the acoustic shadow of massive storage tanks, finding comfort in the vibration, troubled only by silence—which never comes, but which they instinctively dread as a sign of catastrophic failure.

There is a quiet paranoia that permeates their culture, a suspicion that has the weight of geological truth: they believe the city no longer needs them. They watch the machines turn, unattended, unlubricated, operating with a self-sustaining inertia that defies the mechanics they were taught. They perform maintenance rituals—tightening bolts that are already impossibly tight, wiping condensation from sensors that no longer connect to anything, replacing gaskets on valves that have fused open by their own corrosion. They do this not because the machines require it, but because they require it. It is an act of justification. If the machines do not need them, then they are merely a crust riding the skin of a living plateau, and their existence has no anchor. They are terrified of becoming like the bats that wander in from the dark, circling in panic until exhaustion drops them to the wet stone, redundant and forgotten.

Yet, they are not entirely devoid of awe. At the edge of the plateau, where the city thins and the land falls away into the haze, they go to be alone. Here, the sound separates into its components, and they can feel, rather than hear, the whisper of water moving through the stone. They stand at the dissolution of visibility, feeling the warm updraft from the drop, and they experience a kinship with the weeping rock. They, too, are porous. They, too, are saturated with the city. They, too, are slowly being worn down by the friction of existence, their edges rounded, their surfaces settled into a patient, final shape.

When a worker grows too old to keep pace with the piston's rhythm, or when a newcomer simply stops adapting and stops noticing, they do not die dramatically. They slow down. They pause more often. They stand against the sweating walls, letting the mineral water trace paths down their own skin. Eventually, they sit, settling into a niche against the warm concrete, their bodies going as dormant as the hardy lichen. The others leave them there for a time, bringing them water and food they may or may not eat. One day, they are simply part of the architecture—another mineral-streaked shape in the haze, indistinguishable from the corroded tanks and the rusting iron, finally and fully absorbed by the slow, loud, wet metabolism of Kharsaal.
