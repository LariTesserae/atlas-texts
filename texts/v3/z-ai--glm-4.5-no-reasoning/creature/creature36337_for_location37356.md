To live here is to refuse the word *live*.

There is no metabolism as the warm world understands it—no hunger, no expulsion, no desperate multiplication against the entropy of decay. Life, where it exists at all on this plateau, is not a verb but a state of matter. It is a temporary crystallization of the water's intent. The inhabitants do not grow; they precipitate. They are the slow, inevitable arithmetic of saturation made solid.

They are the Silecids.

They exist only in the deepest, widest bowls of the plateau, where the perpetual weeping of the stone gathers into those perfect, still panes. To look for them is difficult. The annihilating light strikes the wet obsidian and blinds the eye to anything but glare. But if you lower your gaze until your vision skates the surface of the shallow pools, you will see them: clusters of impossible, frozen corals, rising from the black glass like the skeletons of thoughts that were never thought.

They are spiraling lattices of calcite and aragonite, fractal towers of translucent white. They have no cells. They possess no interiors. They are hollow, their walls the thickness of a whisper, grown over millennia by the mineral-rich water that presses against them. They are fixed, rooted in place by the physics of their own forming. They stand in the shallows, unmoving, deaf, and blind, performing their single, unending function: they strain.

The water sheeting over them carries a freight of dissolved silica—microscopic shards of the mountain itself, wept from the micro-fissures and carried in the perpetual film. The silecids are catchments for this invisible dust. The water flows through their spiraling apertures, and the silica adheres—atom to atom, lattice to lattice—extending the walls, deepening the whorls, adding another infinitesimal layer to the precipitate. They do not eat; they are built by the water. They do not digest; they merely slow the silica's journey, delaying its passage across the black glass for a thousand years before it settles elsewhere as stone.

They are the plateau's only gesture toward complexity, yet they are entirely of the plateau. They do not disrupt the equilibrium; they are expressions of it. They are as much a geological feature as the vitreous shale beneath them, a local thickening of the mineral saturation, a brief, structured pause in the water's long war.

A silecid colony is a silent city of glass. The tallest towers rise no more than a hand's breadth from the pool floor, but their complexity is infinite—each branching spiral repeating itself at smaller and smaller scales, down to the limits of light. They are architectures without architects, grown from the pure, cold logic of crystallization. They do not compete. There is no struggle for space or resource; the water brings silica to all equally, and the colonies expand only as the physics of precipitation allows. They do not reproduce. Occasionally, the eternal wash weakens a joint, and a fragment breaks away, tumbling across the smooth stone until it lodges in a shallow groove. There, if the water flows right, it becomes a seed for a new lattice, a nodule of order in the chaos of saturation. But this is not birth; it is merely a landslide on a geological timescale.

They possess no defense against the light. The annihilating glare strikes their translucent walls, and they scatter it. They fracture the white, blinding envelope into prismatic shards—brief, shimmering coronas of color that dance across the black glass when the water's surface ripples. In a world of absolute stasis, this is the only variation: the slow, shifting scatter of light through the towers, a cold rainbow that drifts and fades as the water breathes. It is not a signal. It is not communication. It is merely the physics of refraction, as impersonal as the glare that spawns it.

Yet, to stand before them is to feel a presence. They are not inert. They are complex, and in their complexity, they hint at something almost mindful. They have memories, of a sort—not of events, but of conditions. The rings and whorls of their spiraling walls record the slow, subtle shifts in the water's mineral content, the ancient fluctuations of the weeping aquifers. To read a silecid's structure is to read the plateau's deep history—a chronicle of saturation, written in calcite. They remember the mountain. They remember the water's long, slow war. They are the archive of a world that never changes, recording an equilibrium so profound that its memory is indistinguishable from silence.

They do not die. They dissolve. When the water's chemistry shifts—even fractionally—the aragonite turns to calcite, the walls thin, and the towers slowly melt back into the pool. It is a quiet, unremarkable un-becoming, the structure releasing its grip on the silica, returning it to the sheeting film. There is no decay, no rot, no scavenger to pick at the remains. They simply cease to precipitate, and the water, patient and eternal, claims them back. The plateau does not mourn them; it does not notice their absence. The sound does not change. The light does not dim. The machine of water and stone runs on, perfect, cold, and unending, and the vacant pool returns to its role as a perfect, still pane—a mirror for the annihilating sky.

These are the silecids. They are the only fauna of the plateau, and they are not alive. They are the place itself, briefly dreaming of form.
