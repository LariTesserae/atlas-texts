Someone lives here.

Not the people who built the complex. They left, or were driven out, or became something else. Not in the way you live in a house, closing doors against the weather. Here, you live the way the plateau lives—immersed, permeated, without any boundary between your body and the place.

---

They come from below. This is the first thing to understand about the people of the Weeping Plateau, and the thing that makes them hardest to see. They do not arrive overland. The access roads are gone, dissolved into the peat. The tracks are submerged. The plateau has sealed itself against approach from the lower country. No, the people here emerged the way the water emerges—pressurized, finding egress, welling up through the basalt and the peat and the living mat of vegetation as though they had been traveling underground for a long time and had at last found a place thin enough to break through.

There are not many. A dozen, perhaps. Fewer some seasons, more others. They do not keep count because counting requires a boundary, a distinction between who is "of the plateau" and who is merely visiting, and that distinction does not hold here. You become of the plateau the way the moss becomes of the rock—not by attachment but by incorporation, by allowing the water to move through you until the place is as much inside you as you are inside it.

## Their Bodies

They are swollen with water. Not in the way of edema, of illness—there is nothing pathological about it. They are simply *saturated*. Their skin has the translucence of plant tissue, the pale green undertone of something that photosynthesizes or once did. When you press a fingertip to the back of their hand, the dent remains for a long time, filling slowly, the way a footprint fills in the peat. They are soft in a way that living humans are not supposed to be soft. Their bones are lighter, more porous, mineralized with silica the way the horsetail stems are, and you can hear it when they move—a faint, dry click beneath the wet sounds of their flesh, like the creak of the segmented stalks in the wind.

Their lungs are large. They have to be. The air at this altitude is thin, and it is full of water and spores and the green-gold particulate of continuous vegetative reproduction. The people breathe deep and slow, and when they exhale, their breath is visible even in the middle of the day—not because it is cold, though it is cold, but because they are so full of moisture that they fog with every outbreath the way the Weeping Walls fog. They are walking condensation events. They generate their own weather. When a group of them gathers in the lee of the industrial complex, the air around them thickens, and water beads on the rusted corrugated iron, and the runoff from the roof increases measurably, as though the building has started to weep harder in their presence.

Their eyes are the strangest part. Large, certainly—adapted to the painful brightness, the white-gold sky, the glare off the water surfaces. But more than large: *composite*. Not compound, the way an insect's eyes are compound. Nothing so organized. Their irises are fractured into small domains of slightly different color—dark green, pale green, the grey-green of shallow water, the black-green of the deep pools—and each domain moves independently, tracking its own patch of the visual field. It is disquieting to look at, and more disquieting to be looked at by. You feel seen from too many angles at once. You feel catalogued by a perception that does not reduce what it sees to a single viewpoint but holds the multiplicity intact, the way the plateau's sound is held intact, unparsed, unsummarized.

Their hair, if you can call it that, grows not from their scalps alone but from their shoulders, their forearms, the backs of their hands—fine and dark and always wet, matted to their skin, indistinguishable in color and texture from the root-felt of the moss layer. In the oldest of them, the growth has spread further: the chest, the spine, the creases of the joints. They are being colonized. Or they are colonizing themselves. Or the distinction between their bodies and the vegetation has simply stopped mattering, the way the distinction between the Weeping Wall and the vine that sheathes it has stopped mattering. The water runs through both. The water does not care what it runs through.

## Their Clothing

They do not wear clothing in any functional sense. What covers them is continuous with what grows on the plateau—the same liverworts, the same felt of moss, the same hand-leaved vine that exudes its constant drip. These things grow on them as they grow on the basalt, as they grow on the concrete walls of the industrial complex. The people do not cultivate this coverage. They do not tend it or trim it or encourage it. It simply happens, the way it happens to every surface in this saturated world.

The result is that they are camouflaged in a way no military camo could achieve—not by resemblance but by identity. They do not look *like* the moss-covered ground. They *are* moss-covered ground, ambulatory, temporarily vertical. When one of them stands still among the sedge tussocks, you cannot find them. Your eyes slide over the green and the wet and the brilliant light and do not register a human shape because the human shape has been subsumed into the vegetative shape, the way a particular instrument is subsumed into the roar of an orchestra.

When they move, they are visible only as movement—green shifting against green, a ripple in the visual field that could be wind or could be animal and that resolves, if you watch long enough, into the slow passage of a body through the landscape it is made of.

## Their Dwellings

They do not build. Building is what the industrialists did, and the industrial complex is failing, its walls weeping, its foundations being pulled apart by roots that find rebar and follow it like veins. The people of the plateau learned from that failure, or were never capable of it in the first place, or understood it as the plateau understood it—as a transgression of timescales, a human duration imposed on a geological duration, with inevitable defeat as the result.

Instead, they inhabit what the plateau provides. The deeper peat banks, where the compressed vegetative material has achieved a density approaching leather, are excavated to make chambers—not with tools but with water, by finding the natural seepage patterns and encouraging them, widening the channels until a hollow forms, dry-ish, warm-ish, dark-ish, and large enough to curl into. The chambers shift over time as the peat compresses and the water finds new paths. They are not maintained. They are occupied until they collapse, and then new ones form, and the people move.

Some of them use the industrial complex, or parts of it. Not the main floor, which is ankle-deep in water and getting deeper every year as the peat rises and the subsidence continues. But the upper galleries, where the roof still keeps the rain off and the condensation is merely severe rather than total. They sleep there, if sleep is the word—a slow withdrawal into the dark and the roar, their bodies going cold and still, their breathing dropping to something barely perceptible, their metabolisms ratcheting down to match the pace of a place where nothing happens fast.

One of them—you do not know which, you never see it done—has dragged the wooden bench away from the escarpment edge and into the complex. It sits in the old foreman's office, green and damp and sprouting, and sometimes one of them sits on it, looking at nothing, listening to the sound, and the vine on the bench back drips its private rain onto their shoulders, and they do not move, and the moment extends for hours, for days, for however long a moment lasts when you have stopped measuring time in human increments.

## Their Movement

They move the way water moves. This is not a metaphor. They have studied the water—or the water has studied them, or the distinction has dissolved—and they move by its logic, not by the logic of purposeful human locomotion.

They do not go from A to B. They spread. They seep. They find the low spots and fill them, the way the water finds the basins and fills them. They follow pressure gradients, moving from areas of greater saturation to areas of lesser, the way water does, the way the roots of the horsetails do, extending always into the wettest ground, the most saturated rock, the places where the water table is closest to the surface because that is where the living is easiest.

Their walking is a kind of flowing. Their legs bend and flex in ways that suggest more joints than human legs possess, and their feet—broad, splayed, webbed between the toes with a translucent membrane that is more plant than animal—do not so much step as *find purchase and release*, the way a root finds purchase in a crack and then, when the crack widens, releases and grows on. They do not leave footprints. The peat closes behind them the way water closes behind a moving hand.

They can be very still. For long periods—hours, certainly, and perhaps longer, though no one has watched them long enough to be sure—they stand in one place, and water accumulates around their feet, and moss grows up their legs, and they become indistinguishable from the sedge tussocks, and only the slow blink of their fractured eyes reveals that this particular hummock is alive in a way the others are not.

## Their Language

They speak, but not in words.

Their language is the sound the plateau makes—or a subset of it, or an augmentation of it, or the same process by which the plateau's sound is produced, applied deliberately rather than incidentally. They can make the water move differently. This is the simplest way to say it, and the most accurate. When one of them vocalizes—and they do vocalize, a low, resonant sound that sits in the chest and makes the ribs hum, that is felt more than heard, that is the same frequency as the gurgle of the basins and the groan of the concrete and the subsonic mutter of the underground flow—when one of them vocalizes, the water responds. The Weeping Walls run faster or slower. The seepage from the hairline fractures increases or diminishes. The open water surfaces shift their pattern of ripple and flash.

They are talking to the water. Or they *are* the water talking.

Among themselves, they communicate by shifts in the sound field that are too subtle for unadapted ears to detect—a change in the rhythm of a drip, a variation in the pitch of the wind through the horsetails, a brief cessation in the constant flow from the vine tips that signals attention, or warning, or the deep time equivalent of laughter. They have read the sound the way you began to read it after three days, but they have read it for a lifetime, or for generations, or for as long as they have been emerging from below, and their reading is so fine that they can distinguish individual water-sources by their voice the way you distinguish human faces, and can locate any member of their group by the particular way their body interrupts and redirects the plateau's endless acoustic signature.

You asked them a question once—you, the visitor, the dry thing, the observer from the lower country—and they answered not with sound but with water: they pressed their open palm to the basalt, and from the fracture beneath their hand, a new seepage emerged, a trickle that had not been there before, that flowed for three minutes and then stopped, and in the pattern of its flowing—faster, slower, a hesitation, a resumption—you understood, or almost understood, or understood enough to know that understanding was possible, that this was language, that the water could carry meaning the way air carries speech, if you knew how to listen.

## Their Work

They do not work. Work is what was done in the industrial complex—purposeful, scheduled, energy-expending activity directed toward the production of something that would not otherwise exist. The people of the plateau do not produce. They *participate*.

They participate in the greening of the rock. This is their primary activity, if activity is the word for something so slow and continuous that it cannot be observed in real time but only inferred from before-and-after comparison. They carry spores. This is certain—the spore-bearing cones of the horsetails release their green-gold clouds, and the people are standing in the clouds, and the spores land on their skin and their hair and their clothing-that-is-not-clothing, and when they move, the spores move with them, and when they arrive at a place of bare basalt—a fresh fracture, a recently exposed flow, a place where the rock is still dark and raw and unsuppressed—they deposit the spores, not deliberately but inevitably, the way a bee deposits pollen, and the spores find the wet rock and germinate, and the advance continues.

They are agents of the plateau's becoming. This is what they do. They accelerate the process by which the living substrate colonizes the mineral substrate, by which the green advances over the black, by which the young land becomes something older and more complex and more alive. They are gardeners who did not choose their garden, who are themselves part of what is being grown.

Sometimes they tend the structures. Not to preserve them—to *incorporate* them. They guide the vines along the conduit. They direct the roots toward the rebar. They ensure that the moss finds the cracks in the concrete and that the sedge colonizes the roof before it collapses, so that when it does collapse, the vegetation is ready to absorb the rubble and begin the long process of turning building into hummock, artifice into topography, the temporary into the permanent. They are composting the industrial complex. They are feeding it to the plateau, piece by piece, and the plateau is eating it, and in a few centuries there will be nothing left but a slight rise in the ground, a richness of soil where the iron and the concrete and the diesel have been metabolized into something that plants can use, and the people will have moved on to the next intrusion, the next attempt to impose a human timescale on a geological process, and they will begin again, patiently, without urgency, without malice.

## Their Dead

They do not bury their dead. They *peat* them.

When one of them becomes too saturated to continue—when the silica in their bones has replaced too much of the calcium, when their lungs have filled with spore-case fluid and their hearts have slowed past the point of recovery, when they have become more plant than animal and the transition has reached its terminal stage—they go to the low spots. The open water. The shallow, clear, slow-moving basins that breathe with the organic mass beneath the surface.

They lie down in the water. This is not drowning, because drowning implies the violent rejection of water by lungs that want air, and their lungs have not wanted air for a long time. It is settling. It is the last and most literal expression of their lifelong practice of finding the lowest spot and filling it. They lie down, and the water rises over them, and they become part of the submerged mass—the dark, fibrous, semi-living material that sits just below the waterline and breathes.

Their bodies do not decay. The plateau does not permit decay. The saturation, the acidity, the cold—they preserve, the way they preserve the century-old plant material in the peat, the way they preserve the diesel in the tanks, the way they preserve the wooden posts of the scaffold as long as the oxygen cannot reach them. The dead persist. They become part of the deep layer, the compressed history of growth, and in time they will be indistinguishable from the rest of it—plant and animal and mineral all bound together in the black ooze at the contact with the basalt, waiting for the millennia to pass, for the peat to become coal, for the plateau to erode or uplift or subside, for whatever comes next in the long procession of forms that this place will take.

But in the short term—the terms of living memory, which is the only time that matters to the still-living—the dead are still present. The people can feel them in the sound. The dead alter the acoustics of the water, the way any mass alters the movement of fluid around it. They create small resonances, local variations in the endless voice of the plateau. The living hear these variations and know them for what they are: the continued presence of people who have not left, who could not leave, who have simply changed state from walking to lying, from breathing to being-breathed, from the short process of living to the long process of becoming-soil.

Sometimes one of them will stand at the edge of a basin and listen for a long time, and then walk away, and the sound will return to its baseline, and you will know—they have been visiting. The dead are not gone. The dead are not even past. The dead are accumulating, the way everything accumulates here, building up, not breaking down, adding to the thickness of the living mat, making the plateau a little more itself with every generation that settles into the water and stays.

## The Child

There is a child. You see it only once, and you are not sure it is a child—it might be a small adult, or a large frog, or a hallucination born of the constant sound and the constant wet and the thin air and the brilliant light. But you think it is a child, because of the way it moves: with the clumsiness of a body that has not yet fully adapted to its medium, that still tries to walk in straight lines and step on solid ground and impose human geometry on a place that operates by water-logic.

It is small. Its hair is not yet the full dark mat of the adults'—it is patchy, green-gold, the color of spore-release, and it stands up in wet tufts that catch the light. Its eyes are not yet fully fractured—they have only a few domains each, green and grey-green, and they move more synchronously than the adults' eyes do, as if the child has not yet learned to see in all directions at once. Its skin is lighter, more translucent—you can see the vascular network beneath, the blue and red threads of a circulatory system that is still partly human, or was human once, or retains the memory of being human.

It is playing. That is what strikes you most. In this deafening, blinding, saturated place, in this landscape that does not permit play, that permits only slow accumulation and patient becoming—this child is playing. It is splashing in one of the shallow basins, moving water with its hands, disrupting the surface, making sounds that are not part of the plateau's repertoire—sharp, irregular sounds, splashes and slaps and a high vocalization that might be laughter, that has no place in the subsonic chest-hum language of its elders.

The adults watch. They are nearby—you can feel them, even if you cannot see them, even if they are only green presences at the edge of your vision—and they are watching the child with their fractured eyes, and they are not interfering. They are not stopping the play. They are not correcting the child's movement, not teaching it the water-logic, not guiding it toward the slow stillness that is their primary mode of being. They are letting it play.

Because the child is doing something they cannot do. The child is *new*. It has not yet been absorbed into the plateau's timescale. It operates at human speed—at the speed of learning, of growing, of change that can be observed in real time. And in its play, it is doing what children have always done: it is experimenting. It is trying things. It is splashing and finding that the water responds, and it is learning—not consciously, but bodily—that the water can be moved, that the sound can be altered, that its small body is a variable in the equation of this place, that it has power here, the power of disruption, the power of the temporary, the power that the industrialists had and misused and lost.

The child will grow. It will slow down. Its eyes will fracture further. Its hair will darken and spread. Its bones will take up silica. Its lungs will expand and fill with spores. It will learn the language of the water, and it will stop playing, and it will join the slow, patient, purposeless work of becoming part of the plateau.

But not yet. Not now. Now it is splashing, and the water is bright around its hands, and the sound it makes is new, and the plateau—hearing this new sound, feeling this new disruption—adjusts, infinitesimally, the way a body adjusts to a new cell, a new rhythm, a new way of being alive. The child is the plateau learning something about itself. The child is the next thing the plateau will become.

## Their Relationship to You

They know you are here. They have known since you crossed onto the plateau, since your dry boots touched the saturated moss and your weight compressed the peat and your presence altered the sound field in ways they could detect from the far side of the horsetail forest. They have been aware of you the entire time, and they have neither avoided you nor approached you, because avoidance and approach both imply a boundary between self and other that they do not recognize.

You are part of the plateau now. You have been since you arrived. The water has been moving through your boots, saturating your socks, softening the skin of your feet. The spores have been entering your lungs with every breath. The sound has been reshaping your hearing, teaching you to parse the roar into components, to read the place by its voice. The light has been altering your vision, making you squint and blink and see green, always green, until the green feels like a property of your eyes rather than a property of the world.

They could accelerate this. They could help you along the path that leads from visitor to resident, from dry to wet, from human to whatever they are. They could show you where the good chambers are, teach you the language of the water, guide your feet through the tussocks and the open water and the deep peat. They are not going to do this. Not because they are hostile, or indifferent, or possessive of the plateau. But because the transformation is not something that can be given. It has to be undergone. It has to happen at the speed of water, which is the speed of the plateau, which is the speed of becoming, which is slower than any speed you are accustomed to and faster than any speed you can perceive.

You will leave. Almost certainly, you will leave. You will walk back down the gradual slope to the lower country, and the air will thicken and the sound will diminish and the green will fade to the ordinary green of ordinary places, and you will go back to your life, and the plateau will continue without you, and the people will continue without you, and the child will grow without you, and the water will move without you.

But you will be different. You will carry the sound in your chest, a low hum that was not there before. You will feel the absence of birds as a wrongness in any landscape that does not weep. You will taste spores on the back of your tongue for weeks, and you will dream of green-gold light and the creak of segmented stems and the white-blind flash of water surfaces in full sun. You will have been saturated, partially, temporarily, insufficiently—and you will know, with a certainty that has no proof, that saturation is possible, that there is a way of being in which the boundary between self and place dissolves and the water moves through everything and the sound never stops and the light is always green and the body is no longer a container but a channel, a medium, a place where the plateau can become aware of itself.

And on some future day—years from now, decades—you will feel a pull. A pressure gradient. A sense that there is a place where the water rises and the rock is young and the green is advancing and the sound is deafening and the light is blinding and the time is geological and the patience is infinite and the people are emerging from below, and you will know that the plateau is still there, still becoming, still weeping, still making itself, and that there is a bench at the edge of the escarpment that has not yet dissolved into hummock, and that you could, if you chose, go back, and sit down, and listen, and let it happen, the long slow incorporation, the patient accumulation, the transformation from visitor to resident to substrate to peat to something the water moves through forever, without resistance, without boundary, without end.

And the people—the whatever-they-are, the nearly-plants, the water-speakers, the plateau's awareness of itself—they will be there. They have always been there. They emerged when the basalt first rose and the first water found the first crack, and they will be there when the peat is coal and the plateau is gone and the water has moved on to some other young rock, some other fresh surface, some other place that needs to be made alive. They are not the inhabitants of the Weeping Plateau. They are the Weeping Plateau, inhabiting itself, and they will continue, and the water will continue, and the sound will continue, and the green will continue, and the light will continue, and none of it will ever, ever stop.
