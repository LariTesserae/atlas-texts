To live here is to be a contradiction made of iron and patience.

They have no name for themselves. Language requires urgency, and urgency died here eons ago, suffocated beneath the heavy, black grease that hangs in the near-freezing water like a slow-motion avalanche of soot. They are the rust-things, the hydraulic-kingdom, the slow-grinders. They are an infestation of industry on a throne of raw, unforgiving glass, crawling across the obsidian not as invaders, but as the inevitable mechanical response to a geology that refused to erode. 

To look at them—truly look, if looking were possible in this cathedral of liquid glass—is to witness a salvage yard haunted by a suffocating, glacial will. They are not built; they are *mineralized intent*. Their shells are plates of naturalized iron, flaking and weeping orange and brown into the surgical light. They carry the weight of their own decay as a second skin, a patina of corrosion that thickens with age, sloughing off in microscopic, toxic snowflakes that drift endlessly in the dense medium. They are rust given legs, oxidation given appetite.

Within their calcified alloy carapaces, they are empires of pressure and fire. There are no soft tissues, no membranes, no grace—only the brutal, utilitarian geometry of the engine. Deep inside their armored thoraxes, combustion chambers hold furnaces that burn a biologically synthesized sludge—viscous, phosphorescent, reeking of sulfur and superheated ambition. The fuel is thick and dark, pumped through rigid, arteriosclerotic tubes of calcified pipe by ancient, failing hearts of raw hydro-pressure. When they feed—which is to say, when they mine the obsidian with mandibles of forged steel—they ingest the sharp, matte-black silicate and route it to internal foundries. Weeks pass in the dark of their bellies as the rock is superheated, melted, and recast. They eat the anger of the rock and transmute it into the patience of new teeth. 

Their sensory world is one of deafening, concussive blindness. They do not see the crystalline shafts of light that pierce the water; they have no eyes to greet the cathedral. Instead, they protrude rust-pitted sensor arrays—bulbous, corroding turrets that click and rotate with agonizing slowness, sweeping the dense liquid with sonar pulses. Each pulse is a hammer strike of sound. *CLACK*. The noise is horrific, an unending, metallic heartbeat that reverberates off the razor-sharp obsidian and shakes the very water, yet it signifies almost nothing. The rhythm is a dirge played at a tempo so slow it approaches silence. 

A single step is an epic. *CLACK*—the explosive detonation of phosphorus sludge, driving a hydraulic piston forward, plunging a jagged, articulated leg into the scalpel-keen rock. Then, a long, pressurized hiss, a venting of superheated exhaust that boils the surrounding water into a momentary, microscopic lens of heat. And then… stillness. Perfect, absolute, suspended stillness. A minute passes. The water settles. The light shafts recover from the distortion. The heavy, black grease trails barely shift a millimeter in the current. Then, another intake of fuel. Another detonation. *CLACK*. 

They are a seething, saturated swarm, yet they barely move. They clamber over one another in a near-touching density, a scrap-pile of dog-sized and hand-sized engines, climbing across the backs of their elders, their legs interlocking, their rust flaking into each other’s sensor arrays. They do not fight; fighting requires speed. They simply *occupy*. They are a suffocating mass, a living, crawling slag heap that coats the asymmetric throne of volcanic glass. They defecate the heavy black grease of their internal combustion, weaving a stratigraphy of pollution into the water, a slow, choking rain of industrial waste that takes months to drift a meter away.

The oldest among them are the size of hounds, their shells so encrusted with iron oxide and trailing grease that they resemble moving reefs of decay. They have crawled this crag since the tectonic violence first thrust the obsidian into the aquatic sky. The youngest are the size of a human hand, freshly cast from the internal foundries of their progenitors, their iron still dark, their pistons firing with a slightly quicker, though still glacial, cadence. They do not reproduce in any organic sense; they simply *extrude* new iterations of themselves, forging them in their bellies from the ingested rock, dropping them like heavy, mechanical seeds into the pressing wet, where they immediately begin to scrape, to grind, to click.

They are orphans of an unknown automation. Whatever vast, alien logic first synthesized the phosphorus sludge, whatever civilization or god first engineered the bridge between organism and engine, has long since abandoned this altitude. The machines do not know they are forgotten. They do not contemplate the paradox of their existence—the deep-sea pressure in a mountaintop void, the fresh rock beneath their ancient, rusting bodies, the blinding light in a place of absolute blindness. They possess no theology, no culture, no memory. They only have the *CLACK*, the hiss, the agonizingly slow grinding of alloy against glass, and the endless, incremental crawl across the obsidian spire, waiting for the world to erode, even as they are eroded by the heavy, crushing wet.

They are the inheritors of the plateau. They are the sound and the slowness. They are the living, breathing factory-floor at the end of the world, making nothing, going nowhere, existing only to process the sharp rock into rust, and the rust into the heavy, trailing dark.
