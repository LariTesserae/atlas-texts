The creatures of the Drowned Works are not inhabitants; they are geology made animate. They do not live in the city the way a tenant lives in a building, making use of its spaces and discarding its waste. They *are* the waste, and the runoff, and the slow exhalation of the iron—born of saturation, sustained by pressure, existing at the strange intersection where industrial decay crosses the threshold into biology.

Consider first what they are not. They are not mammals, despite their size and their heat-seeking. They have no fur to mat and dry in the damp, no milk to offer young, no denning instinct that suggests a partition between self and shelter. They are not amphibians, though they submerge; their skin does not blister or dry in the open air, because the air has never been open, has never been dry, has never offered them any condition other than the one their bodies assume. They are not fish, though they flow; they hold the water without being held by it, absorbing its minerals through skins that seem less like surfaces than like semi-permeable membranes, the boundary between self and solution a matter of polite convention rather than biological necessity. They are not insects, though they swarm; there is no chitin, no segmentation, no jointed apparatus that speaks of leg and thorax and the mechanical logic of the arthropod.

What they are is something for which the language has no ready word—unless it is the word *precipitate*, used not as verb but as noun. They are what falls out of solution when the solution can hold no more.

Their skin is the first unreadable thing about them. Pale as the belly of a cave fish, but not the cave fish's blind pink—instead a color that hovers just at the edge of visibility, faintly opalescent, catching the amber-grey light and returning it with a wet sheen that makes each individual difficult to isolate from the next. They are translucent, not transparent. You can see the dark shapes moving inside them—not the discrete architecture of skeleton and organ, but something more distributed, more like the marbling in a stone, or the way sediment layers in a core sample. Their interiors seem to shift slowly, not with the urgency of digestion but with the gentleness of settling, as though their organs are not fixed in place but migrate over the course of a lifetime toward whatever gradient—heat, mineral, moisture—calls to them most strongly.

They have no eyes. This is not the same as being eyeless; a creature that has lost its eyes still carries the history of sight in its skull, its brain, its way of moving through space. These animals have never seen. The dimples where eyes might have been are not vestigial orbits but something else—glandular pits, perhaps, or sensory wells, always damp, always open, registering something that vision could never convey. They know where you are with an accuracy that feels like architecture, the prompt says, and this is the right word, because they do not perceive you the way a predator perceives prey or a mate perceives a partner. They *structure* you. They register your displacement of heat and wet the way a building registers the weight of a new column—intuitively, statically, as a change in the total equation of the space.

Their mouths run wide and hinge low, giving them that look of perpetual reception. It is not a face designed for biting or speaking or kissing. It is a face designed for *taking in*—not food, exactly, but whatever passes across it: water, mineral, the chemical signatures of heat and iron and the slow bacterial cultures that bloom and fade in the standing pools. Their jaws, when they open, reveal not teeth but a kind of ridged palate, like the interior of a filtration chamber, designed to let water pass through while catching whatever solid matter the current carries. They feed the way the works feed—by processing, by cycling, by being the place where the medium slows down long enough to yield its contents.

And there are so many. The prompt says thousands visible from any single point, and this is correct, but it understates the case, because *visible* depends on light, and the light here is unreliable. What you can see is only the surface expression of a population that extends downward into the submerged galleries, the flooded cellars, the cisterns and sumps and the tangled pipe-runs beneath the grating. For every animal basking on a warm pipe, there are ten more suspended in the water below, pale shapes in the dimness, barely moving, their skins slowly crystallizing a mineral rind that will eventually grow thick enough to sink them to the lowest point, where they will rest until the heat or the chemistry or some longer cycle dissolves the rind and sets them rising again, like a slow pulse through the stratified water of the drowned city.

They do not have a social structure. This is important to understand, because their density suggests collaboration, and their synchronized respiration suggests communication, and their tendency to cluster suggests community—but these are anthropomorphic readings, and they are wrong. The animals gather on warm surfaces not because they choose to be together but because they are drawn to the same gradient; the heat calls them the way a drain calls water, and they arrive and they settle and they overlap not by arrangement but by the simple physics of shared attraction. Their group breathing is not coordination but convergence—separate bodies in separate states settling, over time, toward a common rhythm, the way pendulums hung from the same beam will eventually synchronize not because they communicate but because they share the medium that carries them. They do not groom each other. They do not play. They do not mate in any observable fashion, and no young have been seen, but the population does not diminish, which means that somewhere—in the deep tanks, in the mineral-rich pools, in the slow chemical baths that the factories maintain for purposes no human remembers—new ones are forming. Precipitating. Falling out of solution. Crystallizing around some nucleus of heat or chemistry the way a pearl forms around a grain of sand, except that the grain is industrial and the pearl is alive.

They do not die in any way that death is usually understood. There are no corpses, no carrion, no bones. When an animal reaches the end of whatever process sustains it—and it is a process, not a lifespan, because lifespan implies a narrative arc and these creatures have no narrative—it does not collapse or expire. It simply slows. Its respiration extends until the intervals between breaths are longer than any observer's patience, and then longer still, and at some point it becomes impossible to distinguish the animal from the mineral crust that has been forming on its skin, and later impossible to distinguish the mineral crust from the pipe or the wall or the floor where it has settled, and the animal becomes part of the architecture in the most literal sense—its proteins denaturing into the brickwork, its minerals joining the scale on the iron, its heat dispersing into the general warmth that draws the next generation, and the next, and the next.

This is why there is no plant life. The creatures have not eaten it or outcompeted it or poisoned the soil against it. They have simply occupied the niche so completely that green growth has nowhere to begin. The heat and wet that should produce jungle instead produce *this*—this pale, quiet, mineral biology that has no use for sunlight because it runs on different energies entirely: the geothermal patience of the engines, the chemical patience of the standing water, the structural patience of the works themselves, which were built to run and have not been told to stop and will not stop, and the creatures are what running looks like when it has gone on long enough to become organic.

And the sound they make—the low, continuous, wet exhalation—this is not communication. It is not language or signal or even the involuntary vocalization of a sleeping animal. It is the sound of a process maintaining itself. It is the sound of the works breathing, filtered through a thousand medium-sized bodies that have become the works' lungs. When you stand among them and hear that sound, you are not hearing creatures; you are hearing the city itself, inhaling and exhaling on a timescale so slow that human ears can only register it as a drone, a hum, a steady wet whisper that says *I am still running, I am still running, I am still running*, and will go on saying it for as long as the engines turn and the water stands and the heat rises through the iron and the brick and the pale, quiet, countless bodies that have grown from the machine's operation the way rust grows from iron—not as disease, not as parasite, but as the inevitable expression of what the machine, left alone long enough, becomes.
