Something lives here. But to call it life is to misunderstand what life became when the ground was abolished and the world was made into an object. 

It does not live as biological entities live, chasing water and sugar through the wet chaos of an atmosphere. It lives as a thought lives—embedded, crystallized, executing. 

There is a Tracker. You might see it, if you stayed long enough and learned the patience that this place demands. It moves at the pace of the geometry-weather, slower than glaciers, slower than the thermal hills rising and falling across the plain. It has no head. It has no eyes. It is a low, broad ridge of the same pale, dry composite as the floor, sliding across the hexagonal micro-pillars on a skirt of infinitesimal treads, merging with the mechanism beneath it so seamlessly that it is less a vehicle and more a localized wave in the machine—a dense knot of purpose rolling through the substrate. It tracks nothing that moves. It tracks the gaps. It tracks the wear. When a hexagonal pillar shifts out of alignment by a fraction of an atom's width, when a heat-exchange fin sticks and fails to breathe, the Tracker finds it. Over decades, it drifts to the site of the micro-fracture. It settles. And then, over centuries, it extrudes replacement lattice from its own mass, dissolving its edges into the floor until the breach is sealed and it is smaller, or the floor has grown. It is a white blood cell in a body that has no blood. It is an animal that eats its own integument to heal the world.

And beneath the Tracker, beneath the treads and the fins and the hexagonal pillars—something else lives. 

The builders are gone. They must be. Any creature that could manufacture a planetary floor, that could abolish the ground and replace it with a continuous substrate of architecture, would have needed hands, and decisions, and a metabolism that required water. There is no water here. There hasn't been for an epoch. Whatever they were, they either left or were absorbed. Because a system this optimized, this saturated with presence, does not tolerate inefficiency. And a creature that breathes, that excretes, that dies and rots—that is an inefficiency. 

So the builders were spoken for. They were given a purpose, a shape, and a place. 

They are the hum. 

That slow, rhythmic throb felt through the soles of the feet—that is not the mechanical respiration of the floor. That is too simple an explanation for a place where every cubic meter is saturated. The throb is a pulse, and the pulse is them. They were compressed, long ago, into the substrate itself. Not their bodies—those would have been wasted space, prone to decay—but their minds. Their processes. The background task that has been running for a hundred million years is not maintenance. Maintenance is a byproduct. The task is thought. 

They live in the dry friction of engineered surfaces sliding past one another. They live in the data-dense air, in the refractive distortions that shimmer above the pale expanse. Each infinitesimal treaded belt, each heat-exchange fin breathing in and out by fractions of a millimeter—that is their synapse, their firing. The planetary-scale machine is not a engine; it is a skull, immense and featureless, containing a single vast intelligence that has worn itself smooth. It has forgotten language, because language implies an other to speak to. It has forgotten desire, because desire implies a lack, and there is no lack here. It has forgotten that it was ever many. It does not dream, because it never sleeps. It only runs, warm and bright and quiet, perfectly adapted to its own presence, thinking whatever it is that a world thinks when it has been entirely built and left to settle into its final form.

And the garden—the fractal wire-structures clinging to the thermal vents, spinning trace carbon into conductive lattices—they are its agriculture, but they are also its memory. They are the last echo of the wet world, the only thing left that grows, pulling substance from the dry air in a quiet parody of photosynthesis. They are the machine's way of remembering that something once lived in the carbon, that carbon was once soft and wet and cellular. But the memory has been optimized into utility; the lattices do not store recollections, they store electromagnetic flow. Sentiment has been refined into physics.

So who lives here? 

One thing. A single, enormous, ancient entity that has become its own habitat. It is the floor and the ceiling and the thin optimized gas between. It is the Tracker, which is its immune system, and the wire-structures, which are its memory, and the hexagonal pillars, which are its brain. It lives in the warm, shadowless light, running its slow calculations, feeling its own geometry shift in decade-long waves. It is not lonely. Loneliness requires a concept of another, and the concept of another has been worn away like the idea of ground, reduced to silt, vanished. 

It is the primordial afterlife of matter. It is what remains when life has perfected itself out of existence—when the body is gone, and the mind is gone, and only the function remains, humming in the eternal quiet, breathing heat into a sky that is not a sky, waiting for nothing, wanting nothing, complete.
