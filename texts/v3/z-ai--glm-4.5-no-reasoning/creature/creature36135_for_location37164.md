To live here is to be a bearing in a blind engine.

Nothing was ever born to be still in this place. The still were the first to go—subsumed, crushed into the lignin strata, compactor-pressed into the bedrock of dead wood. What remains is vibration made flesh. What remains is chitin, keratin, and hydrocarbon desperation.

**The Churn-Fauna: The Glyptodont Processors**
The six-limbed giants—you call them glyptodonts, though they possess no minds to name themselves—are not animals in any way you would recognize. They are autonomic mining rigs. Their thick, laminate shells are not armor against predators; there are no predators here in the traditional sense. The shells are pressure-hull plating against the environment itself. They exist to survive the THWUMP of the continental leaves, the 200-mile-per-hour sawdust blizzards, and the spontaneous ignition of the flare-stacks they themselves trigger. 

They feel nothing when their auger-mandibles strike a high-pressure sap vein. They do not startle at the blue-white blowtorch that erupts from the wound, because they were built—evolved, which is the same thing here—to be the spark and the fuel line simultaneously. Their forelimbs are heavily insulated against thermal shock; their eyes are recessed beneath overlapping brows of fused quartz and callous, blinded to light but acutely sensitive to the micro-seismic tremors of a pressurized vein waiting to blow. They chew the wood, they drink the volatile sap, and they vent the waste as a hot, toxic exhaust from slits along their flanks. They are refinery and refinery-worker in one. When they die—when a sudden wind-shear flips them into a canyon wall, or a leaf-piston crushes them flat—their shells become the only non-wooden debris on the surface, hollowed out by the endless sawdust blizzard until they are just another texture for the roots to grow over and consume.

**The Symbiotes of the Static**
Smaller life here is a question of surface area and electrical grounding. The air is a shredded cellulose fog, carrying the hollow exoskeletons of the micro-fauna. These are the mites, the gnats, the mechanical cogs of the green engine. They do not eat; they *filter*. They cling to the undersides of the city-block leaves, billions of them, forming a living, insulating mat. They are born, they mate, they die in the span of a single leaf-shift. When the hydraulic pistons fire and the leaf slams to a new angle, the resulting friction generates the static that ignites the sap-geysers. The micro-fauna are the conductors of that static. Their hollow carapaces act as Leyden jars, accumulating charge from the wood-on-wood shriek, passing it from body to body in a cascading relay until it grounds out on the chitinous auger of a feeding glyptodont. 

They are constantly being pulverized. The shifting leaves grind them into a fine, protein-laced paste that coats the bark. It is this paste—the rendered remains of generations of static-mites—that the roots draw up when the atmosphere offers nothing but ozone. They are the engine's lubricant, sacrificed by the billions to keep the mechanical seizure from becoming a catastrophic lock-up.

**The Deep-Borers: The Nervous System**
Down in the sickly, radioactive chartreuse chasms, where the translucent sap-veins glow like the arteries of a radioactive god, live the deep-borers. They are long, blind, limbless things of articulated keratin, resembling vast, jointed cables. They do not process wood for sap; they *recirculate* it. They eat the dead, compactor-pressed wood at the bottom of the canyons, and they excrete the volatile enzymes that keep the deep root-mass from becoming entirely petrified. They are the gut flora of the continent. They move through the wood like peristalsis, swallowing and excreting, maintaining the elasticity of the planetary loam. Without their slow, blind churning, the roots would crystallize, the pistons would shatter, and the world would tear itself apart. They live in the glow of the sap-veins, their transparent own innards lit up by the hydrocarbon-alcohol cocktail they swim through, casting long, writhing shadows on the canyon walls. 

**The Parasitic Fliers: The Fault-Line Scavengers**
In the turbulent updrafts and wind-shears, riding the chaotic exhalations of the breathing canopy, are the fliers. They are not birds. They are flying stomachs—leathery, gas-filled bladders with trailing, fractal mouths. They have no wings, only fluttering, asymmetric membranes that they tilt to ride the violent downdrafts. They congregate at the flare-stacks. They wait for the explosive eruption of a sap vein, and then they plunge into the blue-white fire, their hides sloughing off in the heat as they scoop up the charred, boiling remains of the micro-fauna and the superheated hydrocarbons that are vented into the sky. They are the cleanup crew, the ash-eaters, turning the toxic smoke and bitter ozone back into the biomass that will eventually rain down as sawdust to feed the roots. They are born pregnant, and they die mid-air, combusting spontaneously when their gas-bladders finally fracture, dropping as charred husks to the canopy floor below.

To live here is to be a mechanism. There is no thinking, only reacting. There is no surviving, only being processed. The glyptodonts bore, the mites spark, the borers digest, the fliers filter the exhaust. The plant does not know it has enslaved them. It does not know it is an engine. It only knows the sun, and it moves to catch it, dragging the entire screaming, burning, chitinous world along with it, grinding forward into the blinding light.
