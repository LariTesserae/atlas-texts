Something lives here that could not exist in a naturally occurring world, nor in a cleanly maintained one. It is a creature of the interval—of the space between the machine’s exhalations and the jungle’s rootedness, between the original purpose that has evaporated and the ecological persistence that has taken its place. To call them inhabitants is to imply a separation between them and the mountain-city that isn’t, anymore, entirely real.

They are human, or began as human, though the distinction has softened in the way that the distinction between plating and moss has softened. Generations of breathing the particulate-thick air, drinking the oil-sheened water, eating the fungal shelves that metabolize the slow combustion byproducts—they have become a metabolism adapted to this specific chemistry. Their lungs are efficient with humid, dense air; their skin carries a microbiome of algae and commensal fungi that helps them blend into the green-brown of the walkways, that may supplement their nutrition through photosynthesis when the hard light hits them on the upper terraces. They are not sick. They are not mutated in any dramatic or tragic sense. They are simply *fitted*, the way the bright-eyed birds are fitted to the intake vents, the way the goat-like creatures are fitted to the metal grating.

You would not see them immediately, standing on one of those suspended bridges looking out at the density. They have an instinct for the maintenance gallery, the inspection chamber, the pipe-jungle allotment—spaces that the machine treats as secondary, spaces where the interval widens just enough to permit a life. They move with a particular gait: a slight crouch, a rolling foot-placement that accounts for the perpetual dampness, the slip-risk of moss on stamped metal. They are not hurried. Urgency is a condition of environments that change fast, and this place changes at the pace of geology. Their movements have the rhythm of the pumps—cyclical, patient, inevitable.

There are not many of them, and there are not few. The mountain-city is vast, and they occupy it the way a particular species of ant occupies a forest: thoroughly in their preferred niche, invisible outside of it. They have no census. They have no name for themselves as a collective, because they have never needed one. They know each other by the specific allotments they tend, by the paths they walk between the fungal shelves and the accumulation tanks, by the songs they hum that harmonize with the bass thrum of the structure. When they meet on a walkway—a rare enough event, given how many walkways there are—they acknowledge each other with a gesture that involves touching the nearest pipe or railing, a brief confirmation of shared grounding in the machine’s body.

Their shelters are not built; they are *found and modified*. An abandoned inspection chamber becomes a home when someone closes the hatch most of the way, weaves a lattice of vines across the opening to filter the condensation-mist, installs a recycled mesh shelf for the birds they keep. The birds are important. The birds are the alarm-system, the companionship, the connection to the larger chatter that fills the corridors. A person without birds is isolated from the place’s music. The birds are not pets in the agricultural sense; they are partners, tiny bright-eyed collaborators who understand the rhythms of the shafts and vents better than any human does, who give warning when a pressure-release valve is about to vent, who find the warm spots where the thermal shielding has failed in useful ways.

They harvest. This is their primary visible activity, and it is what makes them legible as participants in the food chain rather than mere observers. The fungal shelves that bracket the shaded vertical surfaces are a staple, rich with the nutrients that the lower works’ slow combustion has driven up through the structure and deposited in the condensation. They have a knowledge of which shelves are safe, which carry a dangerous load of heavy metals or toxic metabolites, knowledge that is not written down anywhere but is transmitted through a system of markings—small scratches on pipe-surfaces, a particular way of bending a vine-tip to indicate a zone of purity or contamination. They harvest the vines too, the feral industrial cultivars, for their fiber and for the filtered water that runs through their stems. And they tend their allotments in the pipe-jungles—small dense gardens where they encourage the growth of edible mosses, where they cultivate the insects that the birds eat, where they manage the moisture-flow with an expertise that is the equal of any engineer who ever worked on the mountain-city’s original systems.

They are not engineers. This is crucial to understand. The machine is not theirs, and they do not pretend to maintain it. They *live* in it, the way a bird lives in a tree. If a pump fails, they do not fix it; they adapt to the change in moisture-flow, the shift in temperature, the new silence or the new sound. If a walkway collapses under the weight of decades of vine-accumulation, they find another route, or they learn to traverse the gap by the pipes and rails that remain. They are parasites in the purest, least pejorative sense: organisms that have found a host that provides everything they need, and that ask nothing from that host except its continued existence. Their relationship to the infrastructure is one of profound, intelligent dependence. They know the mountain-city’s rhythms more intimately than any maintenance worker ever did, but they do not control those rhythms. They ride them.

There are children. This is perhaps the most remarkable thing, the thing that confirms the depth of the equilibrium: they have children. The young are born into the interval as if it were the only possible world, which for them it is. They learn to read the condensation-patterns on a wall the way a sailor reads waves, knowing that a particular sheen indicates a weep-point that will produce water in an hour, that a particular discoloration of moss indicates a thermal variation that will drive insects to the surface. They climb where adults do not, finding routes through the condensation nets, playing games on the uppermost spires where the wind is constant and the fall would be fatal—though they do not fall, because their sure-footedness on metal grating and pipe-rail is instinctive, learned before language. They are raised collectively, in the way of communities where every adult is responsible for every child, because the community is so small and the mountain-city is so large, and the children are the only true currency of the interval.

They have no religion, if religion requires the supernatural. But they have a practice that resembles devotion. At certain times—when a pressure-release valve vents, when a pump-cycle shifts phase, when the flock of birds rises in alarm and then settles—they will stop whatever they are doing and attend to the sound, the vibration, the change. It is not worship. It is *listening*, at a depth that most people never achieve. They are listening to the mountain-city breathe, and in that listening they are confirming their own place in the respiration. They are holding themselves in the awareness that they exist because the machine exists, that their warmth is the machine’s warmth, that their water is the machine’s water, that the rhythm of their lives is a sub-rhythm of the pumps and turbines and the slow, slow accumulation of moss on a ceiling that has been damp for forty years.

They do not wonder about the builders. The builders are not a mystery; they are simply irrelevant, the way the origin of a river is irrelevant to the fish that live in it. The mountain-city has outlasted its makers, and the interval-dwellers are the proof of that outlasting. They do not maintain the legend of a fall, a golden age, a time when things were better or different. There is only the ongoing fact of the place: its density, its dampness, its warmth, its green persistence, its mechanical respiration, its geological patience. They live in this fact the way a lichen lives on a rock—not struggling, not adapting in any urgent sense, simply *being*, with a completeness that most living things never achieve.

They age and they die. Their bodies are given to the vines, or to the accumulation tanks, or to the fungal shelves they spent their lives harvesting. There is no ceremony, or the ceremony is indistinguishable from practicality: the body becomes part of the nutrient cycle that sustains the allotments, the insects, the birds, the children. The interval-dwellers understand this as the natural conclusion of a life lived inside a system. They do not resist it. They do not sentimentalize it. They do not fear it, because they have lived their whole lives inside a larger organism—the mountain-city—and they understand that to be part of something larger is to accept the terms of that something, including the term that says individual components are consumed and transformed.

If you could ask them what they are—who they are—they might not understand the question. They might point to the pipe they are touching, to the moss they are harvesting, to the bird on their shoulder, to the walkway beneath their feet, to the constructed mountain that rises around them in its kilometer-upon-kilometer density, and they might say: *I am of this. This is what I am of.* And they would not be being poetic. They would be being precise.
