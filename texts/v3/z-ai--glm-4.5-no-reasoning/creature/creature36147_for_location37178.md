To live in the Cistern is to be born into a throat that never stops swallowing. Life here does not exist; it *infests*. It is a biology of desperation and absolute saturation, a churning mass of flesh and chitin that has long since abandoned the sun, the soil, and the silence. To survive here is to be a component of the machine’s exhaust, a parasite on a dying god’s lymphatic system.

They are the Pale, the eyeless multitudes, and they are not singular creatures so much as a single, distributed hunger. 

At the apex of this living fog are the droning isopods, the thumb-sized avatars of the Cistern’s breath. They are not true flyers, but buoyant sinkers—thin, calcified shells wrapped around pockets of hydrogen-gas-producing gut flora, drifting blindly on the warm, moist updrafts that vent from the machine’s thermal stacks. They do not navigate; they permeate. Their carapaces are etched by the same nanoscale grooves as the cerametallic walls, allowing the condensing mist to bead and run along their backs, keeping them permanently slick. When they collide with one another, or with the drooping ceiling, they make the sound of a thousand wet, fingernail-sized castanets—a constant, arrhythmic clicking that forms the abrasive top layer of the Cistern’s deafening roar. They feed on the microbial slimes that bloom in the machine's micro-apertures, filtering the very mist through baleen-like mandibles as they drift. They are the Cistern’s white blood cells, wandering, consuming, dying.

Below them, clinging to the vertical world, are the Wall-Walkers—the blind, pale arthropods that treat the cerametallic alloy as both scaffold and skin. They are flattened, multi-legged slabs of translucent chitin, their internal organs visible as dark, pulsing slugs through their armored glass. They do not have eyes; they have pressure-sensory plates across their faces that read the micro-seismic shifts of the wall, feeling the machine’s sluggish pulse and the vibrations of struggling prey through the alloy. They are ambush predators, perfectly camouflaged against the dark, porous metal, waiting motionless for hours in the shallow grooves where the weeping water runs. When an eel-amphibian foolishly slides past, seeking a warm vent, the Walker strikes with a subsonic snap, dragging the thrashing prey into the micro-channels to liquefy it in secret. Their eggs are laid directly into the capillaries of the wall, injected via ovipositors into the machine’s bloodstream, so that their young hatch already swimming in the mineral-rich exhaust, nursing on the dark, tepid fluids of the earth.

And then there are the channel-eels, the blind swimmers of the shallow grid. They are the reason the floor grooves run red as often as they run clear. They look like degenerate embryos—pale, elongated, and lacking any fin save for a ridge of vibrating cilia that runs the length of their spines. They navigate the two-inch-deep aquifers of the floor by tasting the electromagnetic fields of the machine’s deep pumps. They are scavengers and prey, the foundation of the Cistern’s bloody hierarchy. They slither over one another in dense, writhing knots, fighting for the warmest spots in the shallow streams, their open, circular mouths constantly sucking in the mineral sludge that seeps from the floor’s pores. When the Walker drags a bleeding isopod into the channel, the eels swarm it instantly, a feeding frenzy of pale, boneless mouths that reduces the carcass to shell-dust in seconds. When they die, their bodies melt quickly into the very sludge they consume, returning the machine's borrowed minerals to the grid.

Everything here is an apex predator to something smaller, and everything is carrion. The ecosystem is a zero-sum engine of wet flesh. There is no waste because waste is just unfed life. The shed carapaces of the isopods are dissolved by the slightly acidic condensation within minutes, returned to the microbial slimes that coat the pipes. The excrement of the eels is the very catalyst the machine's nanoscale regrowth cycle requires to patch its eroding surfaces. The machine feeds the slimes, the slimes feed the drifters, the drifters feed the walkers, the walkers feed the eels, and the eels feed the machine. They are not living *in* the Cistern; they *are* the Cistern. They are the biological equivalent of the oxidizing rust on a forgotten bridge, an organic corrosion that happens to move, to click, to bleed.

To be one of them is to know only the pressure of the warm mist, the vibration of the tectonic hum, and the terror of the dark. It is to have no concept of distance or horizons, only the immediate, tactile reality of a wet wall, a thrashing neighbor, and the perpetual, deafening roar of the world swallowing you whole.
