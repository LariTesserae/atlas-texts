The people who live here do not inhabit the city. They are included by it.

They are a species of persistence, these residents—not a population. They do not stand apart from the metabolic loop between machine and substrate; they are threaded through it, another tissue in the saturated medium. There is no boundary where the city ends and they begin. Their tenements bud from the same walls as the moss, their foundations interlock with the same embryonic clay, and when the streets flow and reroute themselves, the residents shift too, carried on the viscous tide of urban renewal like organisms on a convection cell.

Physically, they have been compressed to match the compression of their environment. Generations of living in the saturated medium have made them narrow. They move through the city like the pale arthropods through the switch panels—sidling, sometimes, more than walking, their shoulders adapted to the ever-narrowing lanes, their posture a constant negotiation with the buildings that lean overhead and the vegetation that curtains down. Their skin has taken on the character of the place: not pale, exactly, but undertoned with gray and a strange, subcutaneous green, as if the chlorophyll of the pervasive flora has leached into them through the constant damp, or as if they have evolved some dark, latent photosynthesis to supplement their diets in a city where the sun is a rumor behind overcast and canopy. Their hands are overdeveloped, rat-like in their dexterity—not for theft, but for gripping the industrial mesh, the rusted fire escapes, the vines that serve as handholds when the asphalt bubbles and shifts underfoot. Their lungs are large, adapted to air that is more water than gas, thick with the spores of cold-rot fungi and the mist of condensation. They breathe deep and slow, and when they exhale, their breath mingles with the city's exhalation—the damp cold that seeps up through the grates—and for a moment, you cannot tell which vapor is human and which is place.

They wear what the city grows. Their clothing is not woven so much as colonized—fabrics of industrial fiber, fiberglass and copper wire, overgrown with a tame strain of the luminescent moss that fruits from the overpasses. In the flat gray light, the residents glow faintly at the cuffs and collars, a cold bioluminescence that marks them as part of the city's living tissue. They move through the streets like lanterns, like the fruiting bodies of some vast, subterranean mycelium that has chosen to walk.

There is a quietness to them that matches the quietness of the factories. They speak in low tones, not out of secrecy but because the damp air deadens sound, and because the subsonic throb of the machines—the grind of the ball mills, the churning of the industrial mixers—fills the chest and the molars, making loud speech feel like a violation of some deep, architectural harmony. When they gather, in the cramped interstitial spaces that serve as markets or meeting halls, the sound is a murmur, a wet complexity of whispered consonants and half-formed words, like the drip of condensation from steam pipes. They understand each other as much through vibration as through voice. A touch on the arm, a pressure applied to the shared wall of a building—these carry meaning. The city communicates through the ground, and so do they.

They work, but their work is not separate from the city's growth. They are the midwives of the turbulence. Some tend the factories along the waterfront, but their tending is a kind of gardening—they monitor the metabolic loop, adjusting the chemical processes so that the wastewater carries the right enzymes, the slag cools into the right crystalline structures. They are not producing goods for export; they are producing substrate. They feed the city, and the city feeds the green, and the green feeds them. There is no outside to this economy. The smelters run so that the vines can colonize; the vines colonize so that the stone can decompose; the stone decomposes so that the humus can form; the humus forms so that the moss can grow; the moss grows so that the condensation can be drunk; and the residents drink, and tend, and build.

Others work the construction—not directing it, but accompanying it. When a building extends itself overnight, budding new floors from existing structure, the residents are there to guide the brickwork while it is still soft and warm, pressing their hands into the chemical extrusion to shape doorways and lintels before the material sets. They are like ceramists working wet clay, except the clay is alive and the kiln is the city itself. When roads reroute, they walk ahead of the flowing asphalt, placing markers, redirecting the foot traffic that is as much a part of the city's circulatory system as the steam mains and the storm drains. They do not command the growth; they participate in it. They are the enzymes that accelerate the process, the catalysts in an industrial digestion that is turning bedrock into soil and soil into city and city into something that has not yet been named.

They eat what the city excretes. The pale, eyeless moths that drink from the weeping joints are gathered in handfuls and pressed into cakes of protein and chitin, seasoned with the cold-rot fungi that fruit in luminescent shelves. The rats with their overdeveloped forepaws are trapped and stewed in the black water of the storm drains, their copper-wire nests melted down for the metal and their flesh softened by the same enzymes that decompose granite. There are gardens—vertical, compressed, growing in the crevices between buildings—but they produce a strange harvest: tubers that taste of rust, leafy greens that shimmer with the same chemical sheen as the slag-crystals, fruits that are warm to the touch, as if the industrial processes running beneath have cooked them from below. Nothing is clean. Everything is nutritious. The residents do not suffer from this diet; they are adapted to it, their guts colonized by flora that can break down cellulose and ferroconcrete alike, their livers thick with the enzymes that neutralize the industrial effluent. They are what they eat, and what they eat is the city.

Their children are born into the turbulence and do not find it strange. A child here learns to walk on ground that shifts, to sleep in rooms that were not there the night before, to wake and find that a new wall has budded from the old one, or that a vine has thickened into a structural support, or that the street has narrowed because the buildings on either side have grown toward each other. The children play in the internal spaces of the city—the warm zones behind transformer housings, the crevices between interlocking foundations, the catwalks of industrial mesh that stretch between buildings like the webbing of some vast, three-dimensional nest. They are comfortable in compression. They do not seek open space because they have never known it, and because the saturated medium is, to them, a kind of embrace. The city holds them the way a wasp's nest holds its larvae, and they feel this as safety.

The elders among them have a quality that is hard to define—a patience, or perhaps a stillness, that goes beyond mere acceptance. They have lived long enough to watch the city remake itself around them several times over. They have seen districts narrow into alleys and alleys widen into plazas as the growth and subsidence shift. They have seen buildings swallowed by vegetation and vegetation swallowed by new construction, the layers folding and refolding like the strata of some geological process accelerated to human timescale. They do not resist this. They do not cling to the old configurations. When the city grows around them, they adapt, moving their few possessions, rerouting their daily paths, reshaping their homes to fit the new geometry. They understand, in a way that is deeper than language, that the city is still deciding what it is—and that they are part of what it is deciding to become.

There is a ceremony, if that word can be used for something so informal, that happens on the mornings when the dew is heaviest. The residents—some of them, the ones who feel the pull—go to the edges of the districts, where the construction is newest, where the brickwork is still soft and the air smells of warm concrete and new growth. They press their bare hands into the fresh extrusion, and they hold them there, feeling the heat of the chemical process, feeling the material slowly set around their fingers. When they pull away, their handprints remain—pale indentations in the dark, moss-tinged facades. Over the following days, the moss will colonize the handprints, filling them in with green, erasing them. But for a little while, the city bears the mark of its inhabitants, and the inhabitants bear the mark of the city—the silicate residue on their skin, the warmth of new creation in their palms.

This is not worship. The residents do not worship the city any more than a cell worships the body it belongs to. But there is a recognition in the gesture—a acknowledgement that the boundary between builder and built, between inhabitant and habitat, between the living and the merely structural, has dissolved here. In this basin city, on this unconsolidated humus, in this saturated medium where the cold seeps upward and the green forces downward and the machines churn below the threshold of perception, the residents are not separate from the catastrophe of becoming. They are the catastrophe. They are the strange, quiet folding and refolding, the turbulence that has nowhere to go but through itself.

And so they persist—not surviving, not enduring, but *becoming*, along with everything else. They are residents of a place that is still deciding what it is, and this indecision is their home.
