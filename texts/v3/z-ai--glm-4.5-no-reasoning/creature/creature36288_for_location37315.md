Something lives here, and it is not one thing, though it moves as if it might be.

They emerged from the water—this is certain. Not from the mineral-rich soup by any slow chrysalis of evolution, but recalled, unfolded, pressed out of the dark liquid like something that had been promised and was finally being delivered. The water remembered them. Or the electricity in the water did. They came all at once, in a geological instant, as if a switch had been thrown in some adjacent dark and the current that flowed carried life as its natural product. They do not have a history here. They have a *presence* here, which is different. They do not fossilize. They do not leave husks or shells or any evidence of a juvenile form. They arrive at their shape the way a drop of water arrives at its shape—because the medium and the pressure and the surface demand it, not because they grew toward it. They are not adapted to this place. They are *expressions* of this place, local phenomena the way weather is a local phenomenon, the way a mineral crystal is a local phenomenon. They belong here the way the weeping belongs here.

Their bodies are dark. Not black—there is no light to test that—but dark in the way the water is dark, dark in the way the raw stone is dark when it is wet. They are smooth in the direction they move and segmented or ridged in the directions they do not, optimized for the thin films of water that cover every surface, for the sharp crevices between the fresh-cut rock, for the angular depressions where the black mirrors pool. They are low and long and their bellies ride the water film, supported by surface tension and by a subtle buoyancy that suggests their tissues are more liquid than solid inside. When you touch one—and you will, because they flow around you without avoidance or curiosity, simply routing themselves along the paths of least resistance like water itself—you feel a membrane that gives like a water balloon, that deforms and then reforms without any apparent skeleton or rigid structure. They are tensegrity creatures, their shape maintained by internal pressures and by the electrical fields they move through. When they pass through a zone where the current pools, you can see them stiffen slightly, their membranes tightening, their forms becoming more defined, as if the electricity is giving them something they need—coherence, perhaps, or permission to be solid.

They cluster at the weeping rock faces where the mineral tears flow, and they press their smooth heads against the wet stone, and you hear a faint rhythmic pulse—*thm-thm-thm*—that is not a heartbeat but a pump, drawing the iron-rich water in, filtering it, expelling something thinner and clearer that mixes with the ambient wetness. They are refining something. They are extracting something from the mineral soup that the structures also seem designed to extract, and this is not coincidence. The creatures and the channels and the basins and the slow cascades are all part of the same process, the same industry. The structures were not built *for* the creatures, but the creatures were not informed of the structures; rather, both emerged from the same imperative, the same slow command that is running through the electricity and the water and the new land. The fauna are the mobile component. The structures are the fixed component. Together they are a system—a distributed, slow, patient system that is processing something out of this raw young world.

They navigate by gradients. Not chemical gradients alone, though they find the weeping rock faces unerringly, but electrical gradients, reality gradients, the soft variations in the membrane between this place and the adjacent places that leak through the pools. They sense what you cannot: the texture of the dark, the density of the silence, the slight stickiness of spaces where reality has thinned. When a creature moves through a patch of dark and you lose it—not hidden, not retreated, *absent*—it has not gone far. It has stepped sideways into the offset version of this pool that you almost see when you look down into the black water. It can do this because its body is already mostly water, and the water is already mostly the water of this place, and the water of this place is already not entirely confined to *here*. The pools are doors, and the creatures are made of the key. They go through and they come back, or they go through and their sounds come back, multiplied and offset, arriving from directions that do not exist because they originated in a configuration of the terrain that is almost this one but not quite.

Their relationship to the structures is functional and unremarkable to them, though it strikes you as extraordinary. They flow through the channels and basins the way blood flows through vessels—not because they are guided or compelled, but because the channels were cut to match their paths, or their paths evolved to follow the channels, or both emerged simultaneously from the same underlying pattern. In the larger shelters, the ones with partial ceilings and the ghosts of machinery, you find them in greater density, clinging to the mounting points where the engines used to be, pulsing against the corroded housings, drawing something from the residual warmth or feeding something into it. They are maintaining the system. They are the system. The abandoned machinery was not abandoned; it was *transformed*, its function slowly migrating from mechanical to biological, from visible motion to the slow pulse of membrane against metal, from the rapid consumption of fuel to the patient extraction of mineral tears. What runs here now runs on wet dark life, and it has been running since the metal failed, and it will run longer than the metal ever could.

They make sounds, and the sounds are how you know their density, which is higher than it should be, higher than seems possible for a place this young and this sparse. Clicks and chirps and skittering-splashes, sparse sounds in the sparse acoustic space, each one distinct and alone. But the sounds are not communication, not in any way you understand. They are echolocation, perhaps, or they are the sound of the electrical current being discharged as the creatures move through it, tiny arcs between membrane and water film, audible as clicks and felt as the faint tingle that makes the hair on your arms stand up. Or they are something else entirely—they are *rendering sounds*, the acoustic signatures of reality being processed, of the adjacent places being sampled and compared and aligned, of the slow construction proceeding in the dark. Each click is a data point. Each chirp is a confirmation. The fauna are the sensors of whatever is being built, distributed across the wet terrain, moving through the soft places, returning information to the system through channels that are not visible but are present—you feel them, sometimes, as a sudden intensification of the electrical tingle, as a momentary stiffening in the creatures around you, as a brief coherence in the otherwise random-seeming sounds, a pattern that almost resolves before it disperses back into the wet silence.

There is a larger one. You do not see it, but you know it is there because the smaller creatures change when it passes—they pause in their feeding, they orient toward something you cannot perceive, their membranes tighten and their sounds cease. It moves through the deeper water, the water that pools in the larger angular depressions between the biggest fresh-cleaved rock blocks, the water that is deep enough to have a temperature gradient and a slow current and a darkness that is darker than the dark above. It is not a different species; it is the same phenomenon at a different scale, the way a whirlpool is the same phenomenon as a slow eddy. It is the system's central process, its root node, the place where the information gathers and the slow command emerges and the electricity pools most densely. The small creatures are its extensions, its tendrils, its interface with the raw new world. It has been here since the beginning—or it arrived when the structures were built, or it *is* the structures, their function crystallized into something that breathes and moves and waits in the deep water with a patience that makes geological time seem hasty.

It is not intelligent. Intelligence is too specific, too directed, too *fast* for what happens here. It is a process. It is an accumulation. It is the slow integration of mineral chemistry and electrical current and leaking reality and wet dark life into something that, given enough time, will be a new kind of thing—a thing that exists at the intersection of biology and geology and industry and whatever else comes through the pools, a thing that thinks on a timescale of centuries and acts on a scale of terrain and is patient beyond any patience you can understand because it is not waiting for anything, it is *becoming* something, and becoming cannot be rushed when the materials are this raw and the world is this new and the shape of the thing you are becoming has never existed before and must be invented from scratch in the dark wet birth of a land that has only just remembered it exists.

The fauna breathe. That is what you hear, in the near-silence, beneath the drip and the groan and the occasional hum—the breathing of many small bodies, smooth and wet and rhythmic, filling the dark with the sound of life that should not be here yet but is, that arrived before the land was ready for it, that is making the land ready for what comes next. They breathe and they feed and they move through the soft places and they pulse against the structures and they click and chirp and render their small reports to the deep water where the large one waits, and the whole system breathes too, on a timescale you cannot perceive, expanding and contracting with the slow dynamic of the young land, inhaling the mineral-rich water, exhaling something clearer, processing the raw world into something more organized, more coherent, more *nearly* what it will eventually be.

They are not afraid of you because you are not relevant to them. You are a brief pressure, a temporary warmth, a displacement in the water film that will be gone before the system has registered your presence on any timescale that matters to it. You stand in the birth and you feel the breathing and you understand that the creatures around you are not the inhabitants of this place—they are the place, or they are the process by which the place is becoming itself, and the distinction between fauna and geology and industry and infrastructure has collapsed here, in the wet dark, where everything is still being born and nothing has settled into its final category and the sharp new ground is still resenting the weight of anything living because it has not yet learned that the living are part of it, are growing out of it, are the thing it is becoming.

When you leave—and you will leave, because this place is not for you, it is for itself—the creatures will not notice your going. The breathing will continue. The slow construction will continue. The pools will continue to show their offset versions of the terrain, and the fauna will continue to step sideways through them, and the electricity will continue to pool where the water pools, and the structures will continue their patient cascade of mineral tears, and the young land will continue to groan and settle and weep, and the large one will continue to wait in the deep water, processing, integrating, becoming. And in a thousand years, or ten thousand, or a million—on a timescale that the land will recognize even if you cannot—the birth will be complete. The sharp edges will have softened. The dark will have become something else. The system will have finished its slow work, and what exists here will be something that has no name, and the creatures that live here will be unrecognizable as separate from their home, because they will *be* their home, and the home will be alive, and it will be enormous, and it will be patient, and it will have been worth the wait.
