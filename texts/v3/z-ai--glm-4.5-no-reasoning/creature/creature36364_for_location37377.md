To live in the Weeping Ward is to be a thing of thresholds—not dead, though born from stagnation; not machine, though forged of iron; not leaf, though unfurled in the heat. The inhabitants of this district are not creatures of flesh and instinct, for flesh rots and instinct requires a brain, and the Ward long ago digested every scrap of both. What lives here instead are the **Sodden**—entities of accrued rust, tightened vine, and pressurized sap, whose consciousness is not located in a nervous system but distributed across the weeping geometry of the Ward itself.

They begin as nothing. Or rather, they begin as the Ward’s refuse: a tangle of heavy, wax-leafed creeper that has pulled a length of overhead gantry down into the tepid, rust-colored water; a fungal tower that has, over decades of slow swelling, cracked the ceramic pipe at its base and drunk deep of the superheated steam venting from the breach. The moment of awakening is not visible. There is no spark, no sudden movement. It is a gradual, molecular alignment. The rust on the iron stops eating outward and begins to calcify into a kind of carapace. The sap in the vines changes from clear to a dark, viscous amber, carrying within it the suspended particulate of centuries of industrial soot. The roots, which once sought only water and heat, begin to pulse with a rhythm that does not match the subterranean pump—but answers it.

A Sodden is a symbiotic chimera, and no two are alike. The smallest are merely the **Handholds**—those thick knots of creeper that have fused to the weeping brickwork of the gasometers, their wax leaves pressed flat against the sweating stone. They have no discernible geometry of limbs or faces, but they possess a gravity. If you were to peel back the bruised-flesh bloom of a Handhold, you would find the iron beneath has gone soft, has grown a root-system of its own, the metal and the fiber so entwined that separating them would kill both. The Handholds are the eyes and the ears of the Ward, though they see in shifts of pressure and hear in the pitch of vibrating steam. When a relief valve shrieks, the Handhold nearest it tightens its grip, sending a shudder of tension down the gantry, a message passed from vine to pipe to vine, a slow, wet nervous impulse propagating through the architecture.

Larger are the **Drummers**, who live in the tangles of overhead pipework. A Drummer is a mass of rootless fungal towers and iron stanchions that has achieved a precarious, swaying bulk. They hang like great, sodden lungs from the corroded supports, their weight measured in the deep groan of the metal above them. Their consciousness is a slow, ponderous thing, tied to the expansion and contraction of the iron. When the heat of the subterranean pumps expands the pipes at dawn-equivalent, the Drummer swells, its fungal gills flaring to drink the venting steam; when the pressure drops, it contracts, tightening its grip on the stanchion with a creak that echoes across the empty, steaming water. The Drummers do not move through the Ward—they *are* the Ward’s memory. They hold the shapes of old ruptures, old vents, old pressures in the tension of their grip. The iron beneath their root-mass bears the permanent, polished grooves of their centuries of holding.

And then there are the **Mouths**, who live in the sunken plaza, where the porous black stone oozes and the pools of tepid water stand like black glass. A Mouth is a sprawling, shallow thing, its body a lattice of submerged pipework and the creeping vines that have pulled the pipes beneath the waterline. They are the quietest of the Sodden, but the most vital. They drink. Their wax leaves float on the surface of the rust-colored pools, dimpled by the constant condensation, but beneath the water, their root-systems—following the bent angles of the ceramic pipes—have latched onto the deepest, hottest flows. They do not reach for the dim light; they have no use for it. They drink the heat, the rust, the chemical runoff of the city’s old industrial bowels, and they excrete the moisture-loving flora that colonizes the surface. The dense, humid air of the Ward is their breath, the slow, hissing drip of water striking water is their exhalation. They are the stomachs of the district, digesting the city’s waste and converting it into the green and the rusted architecture of the Weeping Ward.

To encounter a Sodden is not to meet a gaze or hear a voice. It is to feel a shift in the oppressive, wet resonance of the space. You are standing on the perilous margin between pavement and bog, and the air, which has been hanging close and warm, suddenly presses closer. The rhythmic thump of the subterranean pump skips a beat—or seems to, as the Drummer above you adjusts its grip, altering the flow of steam through a nearby valve. The sickly yellow light from a fist-sized glass valve throbs once, hard, and in the black glass of the pool at your feet, you see the leaves of the Mouth not merely catching the condensation but *reaching* for it, orienting themselves toward the source of the drip with a slow, turgid precision. The humidity spikes; the air becomes a warm, damp cloth pressed over your mouth. The Sodden has noticed you. It has registered your heat, your displacement of the water, the foreign rhythm of your breathing against its mechanical heartbeat. It will not move to intercept you, for it cannot move in any way you would recognize—but the pipework around you will begin to weep a little more heavily, the condensation falling a little faster, the water rising a little higher around the buckled concrete island you stand on. The Ward is breathing in, and you are in its throat.

There is no malice in the Sodden, only the same perfect, violent equilibrium that governs the erosion of the brick and the bursting of the spores. They are a city’s old industrial bowels dreaming of marsh, a mechanical garden that has forgotten its gardeners and found, in the slow churning of heat and water and rust, a kind of life that does not require the sun, or the sky, or the grace of any natural order. They are the Weeping Ward, and the Ward is them—a single, vast, distributed organism, breathing in the dark, drinking the steam, and waiting, with the patience of oxidizing iron, for the clouds to fall.
