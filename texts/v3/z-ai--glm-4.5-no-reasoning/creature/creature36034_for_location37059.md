In the deep channels between the drifting root-masses, where the pale light fails and the water turns to black glass, the *Kuu-var* make their slow passages. They are the ones who breathe the iron and the green, whose gills flutter like dark, bruised petals along the necks that weave between the dangling root-hairs. They are long—long enough that their passing takes half a morning, their bodies looped in loose, patient sine curves through the channels. Their scales are the color of the lake bottom: a slate so dark it holds the blue, and they are furred, near the head, with the same algae that coats the clicking things. They do not hunt. They do not flee. They eat the slow detritus of the root-cities, the silt of rotting leaves, the pale spores that drift down from the funnels above. When they surface—just the crown of the head, the eye like a wet, black stone reflecting nothing—they make the low boom. It is a sound from the chest, from a cavity that resonates with the depth and the cold, and it is the sound the city measures itself against. The Kuu-var know the deeper rooms. They go where the water is cold enough to crack bone, and they stay, sometimes, for seasons. When they return, their scales are scraped clean of algae in long, pale lines, as though they have been brushing against something smooth and vast in the dark below.

The *Tikkik* are the sound’s high register. They cling to the undersides of the root-platforms, their bodies flat and broad, their shells knobbled and furred with a green that makes them, when they are still, indistinguishable from the root-warts they live among. Their claws—six, eight, sometimes ten, arranged in bilateral clusters that do not match from side to side—hang down into the water, and it is these that make the clicking. They click when they feed, tearing at the root-bark with the orange-bright claws; they click when they mate, drumming on their own shells; they click, most of all, when they listen. And they are always listening. The Tikkik have no eyes that anyone has found, but they have, where eyes might be, depressions in their shells, like the bowls of small spoons, lined with a membrane that quivers. They hear the Kuu-var. They hear each other. They hear, perhaps, what moves in the deeper water, what makes the channels widen and narrow, what pushes the root-masses on their micro-currents. When the clicking synchronizes—when the Tikkik on one float-mass and the Tikkik on another, separated by a channel of dark water, fall into the same rhythm, the same pattern of threes and sevens—it is because something has passed below, and they are mapping it, calling its shape to each other across the dark. They are the city’s warning system, and its music, and its insomnia. They never sleep. The clicking never stops.

And then there are the *Gren-thal*, the boomers, the displacement. You do not see them. You see the water round and swell, a hump that moves without breaking the surface, a rolling that sends the leaf-walls swaying and the Tikkik clicking in sudden, sharp, asynchronous bursts. The Gren-thal are deep. They are large. They are, perhaps, the oldest things in the lake—not the oldest living things, but the oldest *present* things, the ones that were here when the caldera was new and the water was first cupped in its stone. They are the depth’s inhabitants. They come up into the channels at intervals that the surface-dwellers cannot predict, though the oldest among them say the intervals are lengthening, that the Gren-thal are spending more time in the cold rooms below, that something is changing in the deep. Their bodies, seen in the moments they break the surface—a ridge of dark, rough hide, a venting of air that is not breath but some other exchange—seem more geological than biological. They are the size of the root-masses. They are the size of the channels. They move the water the way the moon moves the sea: slowly, gravitationally, without malice. They are what the people above do not name, because to name is to define, and the Gren-thal are the thing that defines this place—the weight at the bottom, the sound in the dark, the reason the water goes down further than anyone has found and the clicking continues, even there.

But the people. The people who move through this at the pace of their setting.

They call themselves- no. They *are* the ones who walk the root-streets, who tend the shelters that press against their foreheads, who cut the living cord and feel it tighten under their hands. They are slow because their city moves under them, because a path that was straight last year curves now, because the channel is narrower and the leaf-wall has grown thicker and the Tikkik are clicking in a new pattern that means the deep water is restless. They adapt by growing. Their fingers are long and supple and calloused in the specific patterns of the cord they weave. Their lungs are deep and efficient and never quite full of the wet air. Their eyes are dark—irises so dark the pupil is lost—and they see, it is said, further into the green than others can, further into the mist, to where the shapes resolve not as solid things but as densities, as thicker patches in the wet. They do not look at the horizon. There is no horizon. There is only the caldera rim, the grey sky, the green architecture, the dark water.

They are not many. A few hundred, perhaps, across the root-masses that drift and cluster in the lake’s center, where the water is deepest and the Gren-thal pass most often. They do not count themselves. To count is to fix, and they are not fixed. They are born in the shelters of bent living cane, born to the sound of clicking and the sway of the leaf-roof and the steady drip of water from the gutters. They are born with the cord already growing around them—the cord that will lash their shelter to the platform, that will tie their boat of hollowed stalk to the channel-edge, that will, in time, be their shroud. The cord is their lineage. They know their mothers by the cord she tied, by the knot she used, by the way it has grown and tightened over the seasons. They know their children by the cord they have not yet cut, the one that still runs green and bitter with sap.

They wear the leaf-fiber and the resin. They carry tools of stone and bone and the hardened spine. They do not work metal because there is no ore, no forge, no dry place to keep a fire. They cook, when they cook, over smoldering pits of packed root-matter that burn slow and wet and produce more white smoke than heat. They eat the Tikkik, when they can catch them—pried from the root-undersides with hooks of bone, their orange claws cracked and the meat inside pale and sweet and tasting of the iron water. They eat the leaves, the young ones, before they unfurl, when they are still tight spirals of green fiber wet with resin. They eat the Kuu-var, rarely, when one is old or sick and has come to the surface to die, its long body looping slow and final through the channel, and even then they take only what the water leaves, and they give the rest back.

They do not worship the Gren-thal. Worship is a word for a relationship with something distant, something above. The Gren-thal are not distant; they are below. They are the foundation, the substrate, the thing on which the root-masses float, the thing whose movement is the movement of the city itself. When a Gren-thal passes and the channel swells and the shelters sway, the people do not pray. They stand. They adjust. They wait for the water to settle and then they go on. This is not reverence. This is accommodation. This is the same patience with which they cut the doorway that has grown too low, the same patience with which they watch a channel narrow over years and know that their walking route will change, that the city is reshaping itself, that they must reshape with it or be pressed, slowly, into the dark water.

But there is something in them that is not patience. There is something that watches.

The oldest among them—those whose cord-knots are so old the fiber has woody, whose shelters have settled into the root-mass so deeply that they are more cave than structure, whose eyes have seen, they say, the open water from the center of the lake, before the leaves grew so thick—these oldest ones know the patterns. They know that the Tikkik click in threes and sevens and that the threes mean *feeding* and the sevens mean *passing* and that there is another pattern, rarer, that comes in elevens, and that when the elevens come the Gren-thal change their course, and the channels widen, and for a season the city drifts. They know that the leaves funnel the rain in spirals sometimes and that this means the mist is thick in a way it was not thick before, and that when the mist is thick in that way the Kuu-var do not surface, and the water in the channels grows colder, and something—not the Gren-thal, something else, something further down—rises, just far enough to make the deepest roots shiver, and then sinks again. They know the geometry is wrong. They have walked the root-streets for longer than memory and they know that the channels should grid and the platforms should tessellate and they do not, that the angles are off in ways that are not the angles of growth but the angles of *design*, of a design that was made before the roots, before the water, before the caldera wore down to its soft shoulders.

And they know—this is the thing they do not say, the thing they tend instead of speak, the thing they cut into the living cane of their shelters in patterns that are not decoration and not writing but something between, something that grows as the cane grows and must be cut again each season—the knowing that the mist has attention. That the lake is observed. That the cold that seeps into the marrow is not the cold of elevation and wet but the cold of being *seen*, of being held in a gaze that is patient and old and does not blink and does not judge and does not intervene but simply watches, as a surgeon watches a wound, as a gardener watches a sprout, as something that is very large and very still and very deep watches something that is small and wet and loud and alive.

This is what lives here: the Kuu-var in the channels, the Tikkik under the roots, the Gren-thal in the deep. The people on the floating streets, with their leaf-fiber clothes and their bone hooks and their living cord and their slow, slow adaptation to a city that grows around them. And the attention in the mist, the observer in the deep, the thing that is not quite a presence and not quite an absence but a condition, a quality of the place, as fundamental as the cold, as the wet, as the sound that never stops.

And they are all—every one, from the smallest Tikkik clicking its orange claw to the oldest woman cutting her doorway again, to the Gren-thal rolling in the deep and the Kuu-var breathing the iron water—they are all in conversation. A conversation that is mostly listening. Listening to the water, to the roots, to the patterns that are almost right and the geometry that is almost familiar and the clicking that is almost speech. Listening to the lake, which is almost a sky, which is almost a mind, which holds them all in its cup of stone and water and green, and listens back.
