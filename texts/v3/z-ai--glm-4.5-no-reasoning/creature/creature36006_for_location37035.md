To live in the Chorus Vault is to be born into a roar, to be shaped by a vibration so total that stillness becomes an abstract concept, a myth from a softer world. Whoever—whatever—dwells here has been refined by the extremes into something no surface-dweller would recognize as kin. They are not inhabitants of the vault; they are *expressions* of it, biological chords struck by the aeolian organ and left to sustain.

They are the Hearers, though that name is a misnomer born of an outsider's logic, for they do not hear as we do. To possess a tympanic membrane in a place where sound is a concussive force would be to possess a wound. Instead, their relationship with the vault's apocalyptic acoustics is architectural. They are built to be resonated.

Their forms are tall and lamentably thin, stretched toward the light shafts like the pale vegetation they cultivate. Their skeletal structure is not rigid but flexed—a lattice of dense, interlocking cartilage and hollow, mineralized struts that resemble the internal structure of a reed. When the subsonic thrum strikes them, they do not resist; they oscillate. Their bodies are tuned. A Hearer moving through the crossing sunbeams is a living frequency, their stride paced to the harmonic of the wind, their very posture an equation of acoustic pressure. They lean, perpetually, into the dominant chord of the vault, and their silhouette is always shifting—a smudge of dark against the amber brilliance, bending as the wind gusts and recedes.

Their skin is the vault's skin. It is a thick, calloused hide, pale as the limestone they carve, pocked and boreholed like the ceiling above. These pores are not for breath or sweat; they are sounding holes. The shrieking white noise of the vault flows through them, channeled across networks of fluid-filled sinuses beneath the skin that act as acoustic dampeners. They feel the sound as a full-body pressure, a topography of tension and release. They navigate the deafening roar by reading the interference patterns where the noise cancels out—walking the rare, silent knots of acoustic stillness the way a sailor walks the calm eye of a storm.

To survive the heat, they have surrendered the wetness of mammalian life. They are creatures of condensation. Their metabolism is a slow, patient alchemy of mineral vapor and the gelatinous sap of the acoustic plants. They drink the vault's greenery, drawing the heavy, rubbery fronds across their mouths—slit-like, lipless, positioned low on their faces—to siphon the thick, bearing-fluid harvested by the chitinous fauna. This fluid coats their insides, a lubricant against the crushing vibration, allowing their organs to slip and shift against one another without bruising. They do not sweat; they weep a fine, chalky dust, excreting the eroded stone they inhale, their bodies acting as living filters for the cyclone of mineral powder.

Their eyes are their most human feature, and yet, perhaps, the most alien. They are large, dark, and heavily lidded, adapted not to the dark, but to the searing, blinding chaos of the crossing light beams. Their pupils are pinpricks, overwhelmed by the amber-white brilliance. They do not see detail; they see luminance. They navigate the vertical city by the geometry of the light, reading the shifting, cross-hatched beams as a scaffolding. To look at a Hearer is to see a face composed entirely of shadow and glare, a heavy brow casting a cliff of blackness over two points of seared, reflective light.

They are builders, but their architecture is surrender. They do not fight the green riot or the micro-erosion; they collaborate with it. They chip and stack the pale limestone without mortar, fitting blocks into the natural talons of rock, but they build merely to give the vines a spine. They construct the suspended bridges of woven rock-fiber knowing the rubbery tendrils will swallow them within a generation, transforming the walkway into a living artery. Their urban logic is not a conquest of nature, but a trellis for it. They build to be overgrown. To them, a finished building is dead; a building strangled by dark, bruised-purple succulents, pulsing with the metabolic pace of the wind, is alive.

They are sparse, almost painfully so. In this vast, hollow gut of earth, they number perhaps in the dozens. They are solitary by constitution, for there is no need for society when the vault itself speaks continuously. The mountain's song is their conversation, their history, their god. They sit for hours on the high ledges, bathed in the impossible light, their bodies humming with the subsonic frequencies, their pores drinking the shrieking white noise. They are not pondering; they are *attuning*.

Occasionally, they will gather at the acoustic pressure-nodes—those rare pockets where the intersecting beams of light heat the air just so, and the opposing sound waves cancel into a profound, physical silence. In these temporary voids, they communicate. Without the wind to resonate their bodies, they must press their foreheads together, transferring vibrations through bone-conduction. It is a fleeting, intimate collision, a sharing of frequencies before the wind shifts and the roar crashes back in, driving them apart to their separate ledges.

They do not fear the emptiness below. The plunging, bowl-like abyss of pale chalk is simply the quietest place in the vault, the deep bass note of the organ. If a Hearer falls, they do not scream—they cannot, for their throats are built to carry the wind's drone, not to project their own. They simply descend, oscillating all the way down, becoming a fading harmonic in the dark.

To be a Hearer is to be a parasite of the sublime. They are small, fragile things wrapped in the anatomy of a catastrophe, drawing breath from a storm, drinking the blood of a vibration, sheltering in the ribs of a dead song. They have carved out a niche in the unlivable by becoming the medium of the unlivable—by letting the deafening roar play them, until there is no telling where the wind ends and their bodies begin.
