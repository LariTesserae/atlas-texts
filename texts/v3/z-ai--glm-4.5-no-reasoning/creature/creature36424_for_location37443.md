To live in the Permeate is to be an expression of its central paradox: to be lush without drinking, to be kinetic without sound, to be singular while being utterly subsumed. The inhabitants do not reside *upon* this world; they are its interlocking tissues, its continuous breath. Here, the boundaries between geography, flora, fauna, and architecture do not blur—they *dissolve*. To be alive in the Permeate is to be a temporary geometry in a permanent accretion.

### The Crawlers: The Circulation of the Quiet

The crawlers are the pulse of the mats. Low, flattened, and relentless, they move in grazing loops that feel more like tides than migrations. Their bodies are engineered by the world around them: armor of mineral-chitin, segmented and interlocking, cladding them in the same chemistry as the Substrate below and the columns above. When they move, they do not bend so much as *articulate*, their segments sliding over one another with a faint, dry crackle—like the settling of a salt crust, like knuckles popping in a silent room.

They do not eat in any way that implies consumption. They graze by abrasion and absorption, their mouth-parts scraping away the gelatinous upper layers of the mats and metabolizing the chemical-laden tissue with an efficiency that borders on the impossible. There is no waste. No excretion. No droppings. The hydrogen, the oxygen, the complex mineral compounds—all are stripped and reorganized. What they take in becomes the crystalline lattice of their own growing shells; what they shed becomes the dense, compressed layers of peat-like matter beneath them. They are walking digestive furnaces, refining the mat's chemistry into armor.

A crawler does not die in the way flesh dies. There is no rot, no softening, no decay. There is only a slowing, a cessation of grazing, a final settling onto the mat. And then, the *subsuming*. Over days, the boundary between the crawler's shell and the mat below dissolves. The chitin softens, fuses, and crystallizes into the Substrate. The crawler becomes topography. Its internal structures—its dense, dry organs, its leg-sockets, its mandible hinges—flatten into geological features, micro-undulations in the endless swales. To look closely at an older mat is to see the ghosts of a billion crawlers, their subsumed geometries stacked and compressed like the pages of a closed book. They are the world's method of packing its own history into the ground.

### The Climbers: The Vertical Occupation

Where the crawlers are the circulation, the climbers are the musculature of the column-stands. They move through the dense verticality with a quiet, unsettling precision. Their bodies are longer, more deeply articulated, built for the interstitial spaces between the chitinous trunks. Their gripping appendages—hard, ridged, locking seamlessly onto the growth lines of the columns—allow them to move with a soundlessness that makes them feel like thoughts rather than animals. You do not see a climber move; you simply notice, a moment later, that it is somewhere else.

They are the apex of this enclosed system, though "predator" feels too chaotic a word for what they do. When they descend to the mats, they hunt the crawlers not with pursuit, but with interruption. They drop, they seize, they dismantle. Their feeding is a systematic disassembly, breaking the crawlers' armor along its natural seams to access the dense, dry metabolism inside. Even this violence is quiet—a subtle cracking, a precise taking. When they are done, what remains of the crawler is left to the mat, accelerating the subsuming process. The climbers take only the energy; the structure returns to the city.

But mostly, they stay above. They inhabit the amber lung of the canopy, where the air is thick with the mineral exhalation and the light is a filtered, steady gold. They rest on the receptors, their long limbs splayed across the translucent structures, absorbing the warmth and the chemical breath. They are patient. They can remain motionless for so long that the builder colonies might begin to secrete the preliminary layers of structure around their resting bodies, only to have the climbers shift, silently, and leave behind a perfect, hollow mold in the urban tissue—a trace of their stillness.

### The Floaters: The Breath of the World

The floaters are the detail that makes the Permeate feel intentional. They are the sky's only inhabitants, drifting in the thermal differentials between the canopy and the open air. They are vast, ancient bladder-organisms, membranes filled with the rising gases of the Substrate. Their skins are translucent, almost imperceptible, carrying a faint, opalescent sheen from the mineral compounds that permeate them.

They do not navigate; they *are* the navigation. They rise on the warm exhalations of the Substrate and sink when the thermal pulses quiet. In doing so, they enact a respiration for the entire world. At altitude, they inhale the raw, volatile compounds rising from the crystalline lattice. They hold these compounds within their vast, quiet interiors, where slow, membrane-level reactions strip away what they need and transform the rest. When they sink, they exhale—releasing a processed chemical breath at the canopy level that the receptors of the columns open to receive. The floaters are the world's alveoli. They connect the deep geology to the high biology, making a closed circuit of chemical breath.

The oldest among them are leviathans, several meters across, their membranes thickened with layers of accumulated mineral condensation, their surfaces textured with the faint traces of climber rests and the settling of mat-dust. When the air is still, they hover—neutrally buoyant, immense, and motionless. It is then that the Permeate is quietest. The silence is not empty; it is the sound of the floaters holding their breath, of the world waiting for the next exhalation.

### The Builders: The Slow Urbanity

The builders are the strangest inhabitants, for they are the ones who make the city without knowing they make a city. They are colonial organisms—something like a fungus that secretes stone, something like a coral that builds in salt, something like a termite that chemically reads the shape of a space and fills it. They have no eyes. They have no hands. They have only the chemical sensitivity to the Substrate, to the crawlers' paths, to the columns' exhalations, and the capacity to secrete a dense, mineral-chitin paste that hardens into architecture.

They do not design the streets; they *formalize* them. A crawler grazing loop, worn deep enough into the mat, leaves a chemical signature—a depression of compressed, subsumed tissue that the builders' sensing tendrils can read. They begin to secrete along the edges of the path, building low walls that channel the crawlers, that narrow the loop, that make the path more efficient. The crawlers, channeled, wear the path deeper. The builders, sensing the deepening, build the walls higher. Over unmarked time, a corridor emerges. It was not planned. It was a conversation between the grazer and the secretor, a feedback loop of movement and structure.

The chambers are the same. A builder colony finds a sheltered node—the intersection of column-bases, the hollow of a subsumed crawler, the lee of an undulation. They secrete a dome, a wall, a hollow. They fill it with themselves, with their dense, wet, slowly hardening tissue. When the colony buds, a new generation extends the chamber, links it to the nearest adjacent hollow, builds a passage. Over generations, the chambers accrete into stacks. The stacks link into complexes. The complexes bridge between columns, growing upward, seeking the warmth and the chemical breath. The result is a city of organic density—a medieval intricacy of arcades, alleys, stairs, and vaults, all secreted without tool or mind, all occupied by the builders and the crawlers and the climbers and the mats and the subsumed dead.

The builders are always building. Their secretion is a constant, soft, wet sound—like very slow pouring, like the settling of mud. It is the closest thing to industry the Permeate possesses, and it is entirely silent.

### The Collective: The Body of the Permeate

To be an inhabitant of the Permeate is to be a cell in a body that is still writing itself. There is no individualism here, no solitude, no separation. The crawler's path is the city's street. The climber's rest is the city's mold. The floater's breath is the column's sustenance. The builder's secretion is the world's accretion. The mat's growth is the Substrate's extension. And the Substrate—warm, dense, endlessly forming—is the bone of it all, the crystalline skeleton of a world that has not yet decided what it is, but is already fully alive.

The inhabitants do not fear the quiet. They do not seek the night. They do not thirst. They move through the amber light, in the dry warmth, with the packed patience of processes that have nowhere else to be. They are the world becoming itself—slowly, silently, and with a completeness that makes the distinction between resident and residence an illusion. In the Permeate, to live is to be grown; to die is to be built; and to be built is to be the ground on which the next living thing will rise.
