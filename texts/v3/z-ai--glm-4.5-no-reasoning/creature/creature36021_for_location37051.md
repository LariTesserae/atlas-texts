To live on the Water-Bearing Stone is to live as an extension of the plateau itself. The people—who go unnamed in any external tongue, who refer to themselves only in the plural, as the continuous action of inhabiting—have been digested by the landscape right alongside the mountains. They are not a population that settled upon the rock; they are a condition of the rock, an organic secretion no different from the pale lichens or the oil-black moss. 

Generations of subtracting stone have subtracted the sharp edges from the people as well. They are a quiet, slow-moving kind, their bodies having adapted to the cold, the damp, and the narrow geometry of the trenches. They are stocky and broad-handed, their shoulders rounded from a lifetime of navigating passages where the walls are always within arm's reach. Their skin has taken on the visual temperature of the place—a pallor like wet slate, suffused with a faint, subcutaneous flush of iron-oxide red where the blood runs close, a consequence of the altitude and the unrelenting chill. Their lungs are large and deep, built to pull thin, saturated air from the fog, and their eyes are pale, the irises washed out to the color of the morning mist, perfectly calibrated for the deep amber glow of the afternoon sun and the impenetrable shadow of the alcoves. In the dark of the trenches, they do not rely on sight; they navigate by the acoustic memory of the stone, reading the shift in the ambient drip and gurgle to know exactly where they stand in the labyrinth.

Their hands are their primary tools, and they are remarkable—thick-palmed, heavy-fingered, creased with deep lines that hold a permanent dampness. The fingertips are broad and flat, calloused not from striking, but from pressing. They press the moss into the cracks. They press their palms to the weeping walls to read the water's pulse. They sweep the wet steps with the flat of their hand. They have no need of hammers or chisels; when a new alcove must be carved, they use harder nodules of quartz found within the sandstone, and when the stone grows stubborn, they simply wait. They will score the rock, pack the score with saturated moss, and let the freeze and thaw of the plateau's nights do the breaking. Their patience is not a virtue; it is a metabolic necessity. They operate on geological time.

They are swaddled in heavy, waterproofed hides—the only thing they possess that is not born of the stone or the moss. The hides come from the rare, pale salamanders that grow to surprising sizes in the deepest, wettest cisterns, or from the fat, eyeless crabs that scavenge the dark. The pelts are thick and leathery, cured in the mineral-heavy upwelling water and smoked over the rare, smoldering fires they build from dried, strap-leaved plants. The hides are draped in overlapping layers, sealed with the same mud and moss that binds the slate roofs, rendering the wearer into a shapeless, dripping monolith. When they move through the fog, they are indistinguishable from the wet stone outcrops. They move like water themselves—slow, finding the path of least resistance, never rushing, never striking out against the cold. 

Their culture is one of maintenance and listening. They have no writing, no recorded history, because the stone is their archive. Every scoop, every worn step, every deep trench is a record of a generation's labor, and they read it daily with their hands. Their spiritual life is entirely acoustic. The unceasing sound of water is their liturgy. They believe that the aquifer is the blood of the world, and that the weeping stone is its skin. They listen to the hiss of condensation, the gurgle of the cisterns, the drip from the eaves. A change in the timbre of the dripping is a message; a sudden silence in a usually vocal weep-hole is an omen that demands immediate tending. 

Their society is organized not by hierarchy, but by function and depth. The elders—the ones whose hands are the most flattened, whose hearing can detect a blocked seep three chambers away—live in the deepest, most stable pits, where the temperature is unchanging and the water runs clearest. The young, who still generate their own heat, tend the highest, most exposed channels, sweeping the freeze-thaw debris from the slate roofs and ensuring the moss-joints are packed tight before the fog rolls in. There is no ownership of space. An alcove is occupied when it is needed and vacated when it is not. They leave no trace of their passage other than a slight smoothing of the stone, a polishing that takes centuries to measure.

They eat sparsely, running on the minimal fuel the ecosystem provides. The bulk of their sustenance comes from the moss itself. They harvest the oil-black moss from the older, abandoned sections of the city, cultivating it in the drinking cisterns where it grows thick and dense with the upwelling water. It is a bitter, heavy food, tasting of iron and loam and ancient dust, but it is profoundly sustaining. They eat it raw, rolling it into dense, wet pellets that they keep in their cheek pouches, letting it dissolve slowly as they work. They supplement this with the pale salamanders and the hard-shelled land crabs, cracking the shells against the stone and eating the meat raw and cold. They do not cook their food. Fire is a disruptive force, an intrusion of dryness into a world that depends on saturation. They use it only for curing hides, and even then, they avert their eyes, uneasy around the quick, consuming dryness of the flame.

To be one of them is to accept a profound diminishment of the self. They do not speak loudly. They do not sing. Their voices have atrophied into a soft, wet clicking and a low, sub-vocal hum that resonates through the stone, used to call out to others in the dense, shadowed trenches. They do not look to the horizon; the horizontal sprawl of the plateau is a given, an unchanging fact of existence, as infinite and unremarkable as the sky. They look only inward, down into the stone, tracking the water, tending the moss, maintaining the equilibrium. 

They are the immune system of the carved city. Without their constant, quiet sweeping, the weep-holes would clog, the water would pool, the freeze would shatter the stone, and the trenches would collapse. Without their pressing of the moss, the joints would fail, the slate roofs would slip, and the dark chambers would flood. They are bound to the place in a symbiosis so total that they have forgotten there is a separation. When a worker grows too old to tend the channels, they simply retreat to the deepest alcoves, pulling their waterproofed hides tight, and allow the cold to take them. The others do not mourn; they simply redirect the water flow to wash the alcove smooth, and in a few generations, the stone has digested the remains entirely, leaving only a slight, wet polish on the living rock. 

They are the plateau's final articulation. Just as the mountains were ground down to an immutable flatness, and the rock was ground down to a weeping sponge, the people have been ground down to a quiet, damp persistence. They are the hands of the Water-Bearing Stone, reaching up to clear its own pores, listening to its slow, unending breath, waiting out the centuries in the cool, dripping dark.
