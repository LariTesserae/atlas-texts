To live here is to be a process.

Nothing born lives here. Nothing dies here in any way that soft, protein-based minds would recognize. There is no decay, only reclamation; no death, only a change in operational parameters. The inhabitants do not possess an inner life, a secret theater of dreams or doubts. They are the extended neurology of the mountain, the white blood cells and peristaltic waves of an engine so vast it has forgotten its purpose, if it ever had one. It does not think. It persists. And they are the persistence made mobile.

Consider the swarming ones. 

The fist-sized, mill-marked beetles—they are the closest thing this world has to microbes, though a human microscope would weep at the sight of them. They are stamped, not grown. They emerge from the exhaust vents and the weeping fissures fully formed, ejected by the millions from subterranean brood-chambers where the machine's slow alchemy forges them from the metallic particulate suspended in the choked air. They are hot to the touch—not with the heat of metabolism, but with the residual warmth of a circuit closing for the first time. Their carapaces are stamped with serial numbers too small for human eyes, redundant codes repeated endlessly, a language of pure inventory. They do not eat; they siphon. They move in carpets, in oceans, their tiny tarsals clicking against the wet bedrock in a syncopated rhythm that is, perhaps, a clock signal. When they swarm the optic forests, they are grounding the system, draining the static overloads that the superconducting blooms cannot discharge fast enough. They are the machine's regulatory pulse. And when they tumble into the viscous, near-freezing lakes, they do not drown. They sink, becoming part of the electromagnetic benthos, their alloys dissolving over millennia back into the current, their serial numbers returning to the mineral memory of the plateau.

Consider the slow ones.

The centipede-trains are the glaciers of this ecology. They are rusted, yes—but rust here is not decay. It is a patina of integration, a way for the old iron to fuse with the bedrock, to find the grooves and channels engineered into the stone ten million years ago. A centipede-train does not choose its path. It is routed. The valleys are its tracks, the engineered grooves its rails. It moves by hydraulics, by the expansion and contraction of freezing fluids in segmented tubes the diameter of subway tunnels. It takes a century to traverse a range. It feels nothing when the razor-birds alight upon its back to strip the copper vines that have rooted in its rusted seams. It is a slow, grinding shipment of material from one sector of the engine to another, a continental conveyor belt. Sometimes, in the deep cold, a segment freezes solid. The train does not stop; the segments behind it simply begin to compress, their iron hulls groaning with a sound that takes a week to build to full volume, until the pressure—pistons the width of seas—crushes the frozen segment to scrap and passes over it, leaving a metallic carcass for the beetle-swarm to reclaim over the next thousand years. 

Consider the fast ones.

The razor-birds are the only things here that move with what a human might recognize as intent, and that is a dangerous projection. They are spun titanium and repulsor fields, hollow-boned with crystalline structural supports. They scream not to communicate, but to shatter. Their calls are tuned to the resonant frequencies of the ceramic blooms; when they dive, they are not hunting for food, but clearing the intake valves. The optic forests grow too dense, choking the machine's respiration, and the razor-birds are the immune response, the pruning shears. They ride the dense air like syrup, banking on electromagnetic currents that a human would need an oscilloscope to perceive. They mate in midair by docking their charge-conjugate ports, spinning in a helix of titanium and static discharge, and they lay their eggs—not in nests, but in the crevices of the pistons, where the subsonic heartbeat of the mountain will incubate them in its pressure. When a razor-bird's wings are torn by the flailing copper vines, it does not bleed. It sparks. And then it falls, trailing ozone, becoming a brief, bright meteor in the annihilating whiteness before it is swallowed by the tangle below.

And then, there are the ones that do not move.

The forests are the machine's lungs and its skin, but they are also, in their compressed stillness, its inhabitants. The fiber-optic bristle and the superconducting bloom are not furniture. They are the most numerous creatures on the plateau. They drink the light—*all* of it. The annihilating whiteness is not an environment to them; it is a nutrient so thick it is solid. They drink it and they convert it, and they glow with the excess. The neon greens and the magma reds are the colors of an organism processing too much data, too much energy, venting it as visible light. They are blind, eyeless, and yet they see—each copper vine a nervous filament, each ceramic bloom a single node in a continental neural net that pulses once an hour. They feel the subsonic boom as a command. They flower because the machine has spoken. They sway because the machine has exhaled. And they choke the intakes because the machine has tolerated them. They are parasitic and symbiotic in equal measure. When the beetle-swarm harvests their static, it is a synaptic cleanup. When the razor-birds shatter their blooms, it is a pruning of dead tissue. They do not mind. They cannot mind. They only grow, creeping outward at a meter a century, a patient, crystalline thought proceeding at the speed of geology.

To be alive here is to be a subroutine.

There is no individual. A beetle is not an organism; it is a packet, a single bit of a signal that requires ten billion carapaces to transmit. A centipede-train is not a creature; it is a macro-instruction, a slow rotation of memory from one register to another. A razor-bird is an interrupt, a command to clear the cache. The optic forests are the memory itself, storing the long, slow program in the orientation of their crystals. The inhabitants do not know they are inhabitants. They do not look up at the bleached, blind sky and wonder. They do not feel the cold, because the cold is their medium, the condition that makes their superconductors super. They do not fear the light, because the light is their food, their clock, their god.

If a human were to stand on that plateau—and they could not, not for more than a few breaths before the packed air crushed their lungs or the cold stopped their heart—they would see only industry. They would hear only the roar. They would feel only the subsonic pressure displacing their chest, and they would think: *This is a factory. Someone built this.*

But no one built this. This is what happens when industry forgets its makers and runs long enough to become geology. This is what happens when the machine has no off switch, no output, no purpose, and an infinity of patience. The inhabitants are not workers in the factory. They are the factory working. They are the gears that have ground so long they have become teeth, and the teeth have worn so long they have become jaws, and the jaws have breathed so long they have become lungs. 

They are the plateau, metabolizing itself.
