The Drumming Hollow does not hold inhabitants so much as it grows them.

---

**The One Who Stayed**

She came to the hollow before it was the Drumming Hollow—though the shaft was there, and the wind, and the sound must have already been present, already folding itself into the stone's memory. Perhaps she found it by accident, sheltering from weather. Perhaps she was sent, or left, or was looking for a place whose shape matched something broken inside her. It does not matter. She stayed.

She was not large. Not remarkable. A woman of an age that was already old for her time, when staying still was a kind of power and leaving was what the young did. Her people—she must have had people once—left signs of their passing in the valleys below: cairns, burials, the usual artifacts of community. But here there is only one sleeping platform, one fire ring, one set of grooves at eye level.

She learned to build in the hollow's breath. The fire ring is placed where the smoke would be drawn up through the shaft—whoever built this understood the hollow's breath. She understood it. Lived inside it. Let the resonance rearrange her until she fit.

What did she eat? The trough would have held more water then; she could have drunk from it. The mosses and liverworts—some sphagnum is edible, if barely. Perhaps she brought stores with her, caches in the entrance cleft. Perhaps she trapped the springtails, gathered the shelf fungus, ate things that would disgust a well-fed person. She did not need much. The hollow asks nothing of the body but endurance, and she had endurance: the long patience of someone who has stopped expecting change.

At night, when the sound became a tide she could not crest, she pressed her palms flat to the trembling walls. You can imagine this: the boom entering through her hands, her chest, the bones of her forearms, and her body learning to conduct it rather than resist. The grooves she cut—vertical, diagonal, clustered—may have been a count of days or drumbeats or something else entirely. But their placement at eye level, where she would see them upon waking, suggests they were the first thing she needed to know each morning: *I am still here. The sound is still here. We are still here together.*

She did not leave. She became part of the equilibrium. When she died—it would have been quiet, quieter than anything in the hollow had been for decades—the moss began to grow over the sleeping platform, and the soot sank into the stone, and the hollow absorbed her the way it absorbs everything: slowly, without urgency, incorporating her minerals and her carbon and whatever small perturbation her life had been.

She is still there. Not as a ghost—nothing so dramatic. As a chemistry. As a thinning of the moss in one spot where calcium from her bones leached into the soil and altered its pH. As a faint warmth in the stone where her body lay long enough to leave its heat. The hollow does not remember her, or if it does, it remembers her only as another slight perturbation in its long equilibrium. But perturbations do not vanish. They dissipate, spread, become part of the system. She is in the sound now, the way a grain of sand changes the shape of a dune.

**The Bat**

There is one bat.

It is not a colony. Colonies cannot tolerate the drumming; the social vocalizations interfere with the resonance, the young cannot develop, the mothers cannot hear their pups' calls. But a single bat—*Myotis*, perhaps, or something without a name yet, isolated enough to have speciated inward—clings to the shaft wall near the opening, its body compressed tight, its ears folded down against its skull.

It hunts outside at dusk. Flies the ridgeline where the wind is cold and the moths are small, navigating by calls that the hollow has probably damaged over the years. Its hearing is not what it was. But hearing is not the sense it relies on here; in the hollow, it navigates by the resonance itself, by the way the sound moves differently through stone than through air, by the subtle variations in the drum's frequency that map the space like a topology of pressure. The hollow has given it a new kind of echolocation: passive, continuous, ambient. The hollow drums, and the bat reads the reflections.

It returns each morning as to a bed it cannot leave. Not because it loves the sound—the sound is still violence, still a pressure that would drive any sensible creature away—but because it has adapted to the violence, and now the silence outside feels like the more dangerous condition. The bat has been shaped by the repelling. It is what remains when everything else has fled: a creature whose very tolerance has become a kind of dependence.

You might call it trapped. You might call it in love. The distinction may not matter.

**The Mosses**

They are not individuals. They are a parliament, a tissue, a skin.

*Sphagnum* holds the water. *Andreaea* grips the stone. The unnamed forms—the ones that may have no common name because no one has come here often enough or stayed long enough to name them—grow in dark cushions and trailing veils, filling gaps, sealing cracks, making the hollow's interior into something almost soft, almost upholstered. They have been here longer than the woman. Longer than the bat. Longer than the ferns. They were here when the shaft first opened and the wind first found its voice in the stone, and they will be here when the shaft collapses and the drumming stops.

The resonance stirs their spores. This is the key to their profusion: each pulse of sound shakes the capsules, lifts the spores into the vibrating air, distributes them more widely than stillness would permit. The drumming is not their enemy. It is their dispersal mechanism, their wind-without-wind, their way of finding every available surface and claiming it. They do not merely tolerate the hollow; they exploit it. They are its most successful residents because they have turned the condition that drives others away into the condition that spreads them further.

In the dimness, they are almost black. Their green is so deep it has forgotten it is green. But touch them—press a finger into a cushion—and they are wet, alive, warm with the slow metabolic heat of things that never stop growing, never stop spreading, never stop filling the space they are given with more of themselves.

They have grown over the sleeping platform. Over the fire ring. Over the antler and the chert. They will grow over anything that stays still long enough. They are the hollow's true inhabitant: not a creature but a condition, a living pelt, the way that life manifests when it has given up on moving and decided instead to simply *be*, everywhere at once, in the cold and the dark and the sound that never stops.

**The Flatworms**

In the thin water of the trough—no deeper than a finger, never drying entirely—pale flatworms glide. They are nearly transparent. You could watch one for minutes and lose it against the stone floor beneath the water, its margins blending with the mineral film, its eyespots—if they can be called eyes—two dark smudges that see nothing as you understand seeing.

They feel the world as vibration. Each pulse of the drum reaches them through the water, attenuated but still present, still shaping the medium they move through. They have learned to navigate by it: toward the vibrations that indicate food (the bacterial film, the organic detritus that washes down from the moss above), away from the vibrations that indicate danger (though what danger exists in the hollow for a flatworm? Nothing hunts them. Nothing competes with them. The trough is their entire universe, and it is a universe of almost perfect safety.)

They reproduce by fission. When a flatworm grows long enough, it splits, and each half regenerates, and the trough holds two where it held one. Given enough time—and time is the only thing the hollow offers in abundance—the trough could fill with them, layer on layer of translucent bodies, each one carrying some fragment of the original's memory in its chemistry. But it never fills. The population stays stable. Something limits them: food, space, the occasional dessication when the trough shrinks in a dry season, the pressure of the resonance itself, which may tear the most fragile among them apart.

They are the hollow's simplest residents. They ask nothing, contribute nothing, change nothing. They glide and split and die and are replaced by others that glide and split and die in the same trough, in the same finger of water, in the same pulse that has been shaking the stone since before they evolved and will continue shaking it after they are gone.

**The Ferns**

They should not be here. Ferns need light—not much, but some—and the hollow offers almost none. Yet in the central area, where the shaft opens above and the faintest illumination descends on overcast days, a stand of *Asplenium*—or something related, something that may have diverged from its sun-loving cousins long ago—grows from a pocket of accumulated soil.

Their fronds are stiff. Leathery. Dark-veined. They do not unfurl in the delicate spirals of forest ferns; they emerge already hardened, already braced for the acoustic assault, and they vibrate visibly with each pulse of the drum. This is not damage. This is adaptation. The trembling stirs their spores the way it stirs the moss's; it shakes the sori on the undersides of the fronds, releases them into the air column, sends them up toward the shaft and out into the world above. The drumming is their reproduction, their wind, their way of sending children into distances they cannot walk.

They are decades old. The humus at their bases has accumulated an inch at a time, each year's leaf litter adding a millimeter to the soil that sustains them. They grow slowly—so slowly that to a human eye they seem frozen, static, the same stand of ferns this year as last. But they are growing. They are alive. They have found the one spot in the hollow where light occasionally reaches, and they have filled it, and they will continue filling it until the shaft closes or the soil gives out or something else outcompetes them for that narrow niche.

Around their bases, the leaf litter is soft. If you knelt there—if you pressed your hand into the humus—you would feel warmth: the residual heat of slow decomposition, the warmth of things dying and becoming other things, the warmth of life so patient that even its rot produces energy.

**The Springtails**

They leap between moss cushions. Tiny—barely visible as individuals, visible only as movement, as a scattering of white flecks that appear and vanish in the dimness. Their populations are small but stable, governed by the same forces that govern everything here: the damp, the cold, the resonance that is too much for larger creatures but merely a fact of existence at the millimeter scale.

They eat the detritus that falls from the moss above: dead cells, spore cases, the occasional fungal thread. They are eaten, in turn, by the spiders—though "eaten" may be generous, since the spiders' webs are so sparse and so frequently damaged by the resonance that catches must be rare. Mostly the springtails die of cold, of age, of the bad luck of landing in the water trough where the flatworms will find them. Mostly they live long enough to reproduce, and their numbers hold steady, and the hollow contains them without noticing them, the way a body contains mites in its eyelashes: present, persistent, below the threshold of attention.

---

**The Spiders**

Not many. A few *Meta* or their troglodytic kin, pale and long-legged, spinning webs in the upper crevices where the resonance is weakest. The webs must be continually repaired—each strong gust sends a shudder through the stone that tears the silk, and the spiders emerge to re-spin, re-attach, re-build the architecture of their hunting. It is a losing proposition, energetically: the cost of constant repair must nearly equal the caloric return of the occasional springtail or wayward midge. But the spiders persist, because spiders have no choice, because the web is what they do, because the alternative to building in the hollow is building outside where the wind is colder and the rain is wet and the predators are larger.

They are the hollow's most Sisyphean residents. They work and their work is destroyed and they work again. They have done this for generations—long enough that their bodies have grown paler, their legs longer, their metabolism slower, each adaptation a concession to the difficulty of their chosen home. They do not thrive. They endure. And in the hollow, endurance is the only form of success.

**The Hollow Itself**

There is one more inhabitant, if inhabitant is the word for a place that contains itself.

The Drumming Hollow lives. Not metaphorically—not in the way that all ecosystems can be called alive—but actually, functionally, as a single organism of stone and air and water and sound. The shaft is its mouth. The cavity is its body. The resonance is its heartbeat, or its voice, or its breath—depending on what you believe it is trying to do.

It was born when the limestone fractured and the shaft opened and the wind found a new path through the rock. It has been growing since then—not larger (the hollow has reached its equilibrium, its most stable self) but deeper, more complex, more filled with the interdependencies of its small residents. It maintains itself: the shaft clears itself of debris (each strong gust blowing out whatever falls in), the water finds its paths (carving grooves that channel future water), the moss regulates the humidity (absorbing excess, releasing it in dry seasons). It responds to stimuli: storms make it louder, droughts make it quieter, the passage of a body through its space briefly alters the resonance the way a stone in a river alters the current.

Does it have intentions? Not as you understand intention. But it has behaviors: it admits some creatures and repels others, it absorbs visitors and then releases them, it creates the conditions in which specific forms of life can persist and no others. It has been doing this for longer than the woman lived, longer than the bat has hunted, longer than the moss has covered its walls—though not longer than the stone, which is the hollow's skeleton and substrate and the material of its being.

When you stand inside it and feel the pulse in your sternum, you are not standing in a cave. You are standing inside a living acoustic organ, a resonator that has been humming since before your species had language to describe humming, a place that is also a process, also a creature, also a kind of music that plays itself without beginning or end.

---

**What Lives There**

What lives in the Drumming Hollow is *persistence*.

Not the persistence of the stubborn, who fight against what would expel them. Not the persistence of the adapted, who change to fit their environment. But a deeper persistence: the persistence of things that have been so long in a condition that the condition has become them, that they can no more leave it than a bone can leave the body that grew it.

The woman who stayed until she died and became calcium in the moss. The bat that cannot sleep in silence. The mosses that use the resonance to spread. The ferns that tremble and release their spores on the pulse. The flatworms that feel the world as vibration. The spiders that build and rebuild and build again. The springtails that leap between cushions and never know any other world.

And the hollow itself, which persists most of all—which goes on resonating, patient and ancient and largely empty, filled only with its own enormous sound and the small, tenacious life that has learned to live inside it.

Which is, when you think about it, a kind of music.
