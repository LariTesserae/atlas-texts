What lives here is not a creature. It is a condition. It is the rust-colored tide that breathes where the mountain’s blood runs close to the skin.

To call them individuals is to misunderstand the geometry of consumption. They are a skin—a living, slow-grinding pavement that adheres to the heat. They do not possess will in any way we would recognize; they possess *appetite*, which is older than consciousness and far more patient. They are flat, wet, overlapping things. In shape, they are the memory of a lung that never inhaled, a leaf that never unfurled, a fluke that never swam. They are the color of oxidized iron, of old blood, of terracotta left in a kiln too long—the inevitable color of things that eat stone and breathe steam.

Their topsides are slick and dark, riding the weeping condensation. They do not drink; they *are* the drinking. Water moves through them the way it moves through a sponge, drawn by capillary action and a thirst that has no mouth. Their undersides are the hidden factory: a soft, secreting chemistry that does not exist in any terrestrial pharmacopeia. It is an acid that loves silicates, an enzyme that speaks the language of magma. Where they touch the rock, they do not scrape or bore. They *dissolve*. They turn the newborn, glassy edges of the mountain into a mineral paste, which they inhale through the whole flat expanse of their bodies. They are stomachs without interiors, drawing the world into themselves by simply lying upon it.

They reproduce by failing to hold themselves together. A single flat body feeds until it is taut with stolen mountain, until the mineral paste makes it heavy, and then it thins. It stretches. The center gives way, and without ceremony, without the violence of birth, it becomes two. They are adjacent. They press together, wet edge to wet edge, and resume feeding. There is no parent. There is no child. There is only the original surface, now doubled, now overlapping, climbing on top of its former self to reach the untouched rock above.

Because they must reach. They layer—three deep, four deep, a stratigraphy of hunger. The ones at the bottom are the slowest, pressed against the rock they are actively eroding, breathing in the dark, processing the hardest stone. The ones at the top ride upon the backs of their neighbors, drinking the purest condensation, their undersides resting on the slick topsides of the colony. It is an architecture of proximity. They cannot exist alone; they require the heat of the rock and the wetness of the compressed air, but they also require each other. They are a scaffold. They are a living shingle.

And they are patient. Their movement is the movement of tectonic plates on a small, wet scale. When they shift—seeking a fresher edge of silicate, climbing over a neighbor that has grown too thick—the sound is the sound of a wet tongue dislodging from the roof of a mouth. It is organic friction, a slow sloughing. They do not vocalize. They do not click or scrape. They have no hard parts. They are entirely soft, entirely wet, entirely yielding, and yet they are erasing a mountain. They are making the sharp edges soft. They are turning geology into biology, one microscopic slide of rust-colored flesh at a time.

But it is what they *do* to the space that makes them terrible and holy.

They do not merely consume rock; they consume the *certainty* of the rock. As they dissolve the crystalline lattice of the young volcanic stone, something leaks out of them, or out of the stone, or out of the space between them and the stone. It is a mineral memory, a structural tension that the mountain held within its atoms, and when the fauna eat it, they cannot digest it fully. It off-gases. It enters the thick, wet, suffocating air.

This exhalation of structural truth is what bends the chamber. They are eating the mountain’s dimensions. They are processing the very logic of near and far, of before and after. The rust-colored tide on the walls is not just consuming silicate; it is consuming *spatiality*. The glitches in time—the parent budding after the child, the wall moving out of sequence—are the fauna’s waste. They are belching fragments of broken geometry into the trapped humidity.

And so, what lives here is a single, vast, distributed organism that is slowly digesting reality. It does not know what it is doing. It has no malice, no grand design. It is simply hungry, and the mountain is here, and the heat is here, and the water is here, and so it feeds. It presses its wet, lung-leaves against the sharp young stone and it smooths it into paste, and the byproduct of its slow, silent meal is a localized collapse of the rules that hold the world together.

What lives here is the end of a shape. It is the rust that eats the edge off a blade, expanded to cosmic scale, compressed to the size of a room, wet, quiet, and waiting for the mountain to finish becoming something else.
