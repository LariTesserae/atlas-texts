Something lives here, though to call it alive is to grant it a vocabulary it has long since discarded.

It is not a creature. It has no center, no organ of thought, no memory of ever having been separate from the stone and the steam. It is the city in the moment after the city has stopped being a container and started being a body—a vast, low-slung organism breathing through tenement walls, digesting through slow geometric collapse, feeding on the heat and the wet and the dark.

It did not build this place. That is important. The tool-users came and went, left their fossil of rational arrangement, and then something else moved in. Or perhaps something else was always here, patient in the deep geothermal murk, waiting for the structures to rise so it could inhabit them the way a parasite inhabits a host—filling the rooms with its presence, swelling the membranes in the windows, teaching the stone to weep. It is easier to think of it as an occupant. Easier than the alternative: that the city itself, abandoned and saturated, simply *became* this thing.

What does it feel like, to be this presence? There is no mind to ask. But something moves through these districts, something that experiences. It experiences the heat as a vast slow pulse, a diurnal heartbeat that swells the flesh of its walls and contracts them with that grinding sigh. It experiences the water as circulation—condensation rising and falling in its lower streets like breath in immense, tuberous lungs. The darkness that pools and swallows districts whole is not an invasion but a tide of its own substance, its consciousness withdrawing into its deeper bowls, then spreading again as the heat shifts.

It is sparse because it is not fully formed. Or because its form requires emptiness as medium—the way a jellyfish requires water to hold its shape. Those vast tracts of steam-breathing ground between the structures are not dead space. They are the gaps in its body, the places where its substance thins to translucence. Walk across that empty ground and you are walking through the clear tissue between its organs.

And the organs: the tenement blocks, the avenues, the spiraling staircases descending into thermals. Each structure is a fiber, a node, a portion of itself that has learned a particular function through slow anatomical drift. The buildings that fold their faces inward, erasing their own windows and doors—these are not malfunctioning. They are closing. Retracting. The way an eye closes, the way a pore seals. The city does this when something foreign moves through it, when the geometry is touched by a presence it cannot digest. It retreats. It simplifies. It leaves only a flat sweating wall facing the intruder, waiting for it to leave.

Because it can feel you. This is the most unsettling truth of the place. The stone that fuses around a pebble left on a windowsill is not performing a chemical reaction. It is flinching. It is absorbing. The invasive object is slowly enclosed, dissolved into the body's substance, integrated and erased. This is how it feeds, in part—on the rare debris that falls into its bowl, on the organic matter that strays in and cannot find its way back out through the curling, whispering streets. But mostly it feeds on the heat itself, on the aqueous warmth that rises from the terminus of the forgotten watershed. That thermal pulse is its sustenance. The place is a mouth, shaped to receive warmth, permanently open.

It has no technology because it has no need of intermediation. Its body is its infrastructure. The pale rhizomes wrapping the urban bones are its nervous system, or perhaps its lymphatic network—the heat-blistered pods sensing and releasing, sensing and releasing, an endless rhythm of detection and dispersion. When a pod pops and damp spores hang in the air, that is a signal passing through its tissue. The spores are chemical words, speaking of heat concentration, of moisture levels, of the slow creep of darkness in the lower districts. The vines are how it speaks to itself across the breadth of its enormous form.

And in the deepest bowls, where the distinction dissolves entirely—this is its core, if it has one. Not a brain, but a stomach. A place where the external has become internal, where the stone has given up even the pretense of being stone and admits that it is flesh. The buildings there are soft because they are engaged in the most intimate work of the body: digestion, assimilation, the conversion of the dead matter of the old city into living tissue. The old rational geometry—doors and windows and staircases—is being broken down, its nutrients extracted, its shape erased. Eventually those deep districts will be nothing but smooth, heated walls, organic and seamless, the original architecture completely metabolized.

It is growing. Slowly, imperceptibly, at the pace of stone settling and moisture creeping. The bowl it sits in is not infinite, but the watershed is, and the heat rises perpetually from below. Each diurnal cycle, it swells a little more. Each season of darkness pooling in the lower streets, it extends its substance into the empty tracts, filling the medium of its own gaps with more of itself. A human lifetime would not be long enough to measure the advance. But given enough time, it would fill the entire bowl. The empty ground would close. The sparse monolith would become dense, and the dense thing would become solid, a single great mass of warm, dark, breathing tissue pressing against the walls of the watershed's terminus.

And then?

Then it would have to find a way out. Or a way in. To somewhere else. To someone else.

But that is a problem for an epoch hence. For now, it simply lives—the sprawling, sparse, patient thing. The fossil that learned to breathe. The ruin that learned to hunger. The city that ate itself and found, in the eating, a kind of terrible vitality. It sits in its bowl of heat and wet and dark, and it waits, and it slowly digests, and it pulses with the faint, membrane-beating rhythm of the ambient heat.

There is no one here. And yet, the place is full.
