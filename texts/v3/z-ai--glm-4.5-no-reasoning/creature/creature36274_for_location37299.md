To live here is to be a fixture. In a place that has reached the end of time, the concept of a "who" dissolves into the "what." There are no inhabitants here, only extensions. The creatures do not reside in this architecture; they are its final ornamentation, its conscious residue. 

To understand who lives here, you must first understand that the boundary between organism and environment has calcified into non-existence. They are the bioluminescence of the architecture itself.

Consider the Sessile Ones, the cnidarian-colonies that crust the massive buttress roots. They are not jellyfish nor anemones, though they share their ancient cnidarian heritage. They are滤-feeders who have not retracted a single polyp in eons. Their tissues have grown into the bark of the wood, their gastrovascular cavities sharing space with the xylem and phloem. They do not hunt; they simply open, and the thick, organic consommé of the water drifts through them. They glow—faint, rhythmic pulses of cold blue and green—because their chemistries demand it. It is the exhaust of their existence, a slow burning of the dissolved past. They are the stars of this lightless cosmos, constellations mapped onto the wooden deep, pulsing in time with the subsonic thrum that resonates through the very walls they are fused to. They have no eyes to see their own light. They glow because they are still slightly alive, and life, even at its terminus, leaks energy.

Then there are the Torpid Anchors, the massive creatures clinging to the undersides of the great horizontal shelves. To call them animals is to rely on a taxonomy that no longer applies. They are slabs of tissue, flattened by the immense hydrostatic pressure, their bodies spread across the lignin ceilings like living shrouds. Their metabolic rates have slowed to a geological crawl. A heartbeat takes an hour. A breath—a drawing of the oxygen-rich fluid through simplified gills—takes a day. They are mostly gut and gonad, but even those have atrophied to the bare minimum required for cellular maintenance. Their flesh is dense, saturated with the water of the place, and they cling not with claws or suckers, but through a gradual osmotic fusion with the substrate. They are the dark spots in the void, barely visible even when their faint bioluminescent freckles flare. They exist as the memory of predators, now reduced to the ultimate passive state. They hang, and they hold, and they wait for a future that will not arrive, their waiting indistinguishable from death.

And in the open water, in the vast negative space between the columns, hang the Lateral-Line Fish. They are the most tragic and the most beautiful of the inhabitants, for they still possess the *shape* of wanderers. They have the fusiform bodies of swimmers, the remnants of fins, but the fins have become vestigial vanes, used only for minor stabilizing adjustments in the still water. They do not swim because there is no current to ride, no prey to chase, no predator to flee. Every chamber is identical to every other. The gradients of survival have flattened to zero. 

Their eyes have vanished entirely, smoothed over by skin. Instead, their heads are dominated by hypertrophied lateral line organs—ridges and canals that trace across their skulls and down their flanks like the grooves of a record. They do not see; they listen. They feel the permanent, resonant chord of the architecture. They hang in the water, suspended by their slight buoyancy, and they vibrate with the world. They are the instrument's audience, feeling the deep, subsonic throb of the plant-matter under tension in their bones, reading the mid-range harmonic that hums through the water. They school not by sight or chemical cue, but by the shared frequency of their resonance. They are a congregation of the deaf and blind, holding onto each other through the vibrations of a world that never changes. They glow the faintest of all—a sickly, dim phosphorescence along their bellies, a ghost of countershading for a world where light never falls.

And finally, there are the Chimeras—the ones that break the already broken rules. They are the hybrids of animal and vegetable, the final proof that this place is collapsing into a single super-organism. There are things with the bodies of fish but the root-hairs of plants, anchored to the wood, drawing nutrients from the sap-flow while their gills still filter the water. There are worm-like creatures whose chitin shells have sprouted leaves—stunted, useless leaves that photosynthesize nothing in the eternal dark, but exist because the genes for them were never turned off. There are colonies of polyps that have developed notochords, becoming vertebrate in their structure while remaining plant-like in their immobility. 

These Chimeras are the most profound revelation of the place: they are the path not taken, or perhaps the path that was taken and completed. They are the evidence of horizontal gene transfer across eons, of life bleeding into life until the tree of life became a net, and the net became a knot. They cannot be classified because classification requires distinction, and here, distinction is extinct. They are the flesh of the architecture becoming aware of itself, or the architecture becoming the flesh of its inhabitants.

These are the inhabitants of the place where time ends. They do not think. They do not move. They do not evolve. They exist in a state of perfect, frozen equilibrium, their forms dictated by the necessity of being still in a still world, of hearing a sound that never changes, of glowing in a darkness that never lifts. They are the final citizens of a completed ecosystem, a terrarium that has sealed itself and run out of surprises. They are the living proof that life does not need a future to persist; it only needs a present, even if that present is eternal and unchanging and dark.
