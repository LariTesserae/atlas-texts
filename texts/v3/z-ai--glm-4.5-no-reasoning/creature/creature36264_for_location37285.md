To live here is to have never arrived.

The inhabitants do not possess a history of migration, construction, or awakening. They did not build the ferric city; they were precipitated by it. Just as the water exudes from the saturated air and the stone weeps without eroding, the residents are an emergent property of the plateau’s equilibrium, condensing out of the oppressive heat and the deafening, stationary roar. They are the biome's internal organs, the only moving parts in a machine that has solved itself.

They are called, if language can stretch to hold them, the Sudaries.

To look upon a Sudary is to see the architecture wearing flesh. They stand as tall as the doorways they pass through—seven, perhaps eight feet tall—and they move with the inescapable, frictionless grace of objects caught in a thick, invisible current. Their bodies are not symmetrical in the way of terrestrial life; they are elongated, attenuated, echoing the stacked verticality of the city itself. Their skin is the exact matte black of the cooled magma-stone, but it is porous, continually slick, shedding sheets of the ambient water just as the walls do. They do not sweat; they transpire, acting as additional veins in the city’s circulatory system of moisture. To touch one would be to feel the dense, gravitational pull of the stone, the yielding resistance of water, and the deep, sub-audible thrum of that infinite chord vibrating in the muscle beneath.

They have no faces. Their heads are smooth, steeply angled ovals, sloping backward like the wind-carved apex of a basalt tooth. Where one expects eyes, there are only shallow depressitions that run with continuous rivulets, weeping into the grooves of their necks. They do not see the city; they feel it. The deafening, unceasing Sound—the roar of water, heat, and stone—is their sensory baseline. Where a human would be driven mad by the vibration in the teeth and the pressure in the chest, the Sudaries navigate by its micro-fluctuations. They hear the geometry. A hallway that takes four steps too many to cross broadcasts a specific harmonic distortion; a Sudary walks into it without hesitation, adjusting their internal meter to the room's non-Euclidean wear. They feel the spiral of water on the floor in the deeper wards and understand the shifted gravity as a language, stepping diagonally to accommodate a center of mass that points toward the building's dark heart.

Their hands are singular instruments of synthesis. They possess four long, segmented digits, but no opposable thumb—no grasp. Their hands are flat, pressing, smoothing tools. They move through the labyrinthine terraces and bridges with their arms slightly raised, their fingertips trailing against the weeping walls and the glossy leaves of the colossal angiosperms. When a Sudary's hand passes over a root grafted into the urban geometry, the sub-harmonic of the root intensifies for a moment, a pulse of deep resonance that travels down the stem and vibrates the petals. They are tuning the city. When they walk the open corridors, their hands catch the water that runs across the ceilings before it can fall, dragging it along their arms, drinking it through the pores of their skin, and exhaling it as a warm, visible vapor from the angled slits that serve as their noses. They do not consume to survive; they survive to regulate the flow. They are the valves and the gutters, ensuring the water never pools, never stagnates, never breaks the perfect recursion.

They wear no clothes. Covering would disrupt the transpiration, insulate them from the acoustic grid of the city, and catch in the thick, sapping gravity. Instead, their flesh is mapped by the flora. Over centuries of pressing through the dense, wet leaves, the spores and microscopic shoots of the banyan-pipe-organs have colonized the crevices of their hides. Smooth, dark-green nodes dot their shoulders and the ridges of their spines—not as parasites, but as symbiotic antennae. When the canopy above drops a hand-sized petal, it falls through the still, heavy air in a slow, inevitable arc. A Sudary will step beneath it, allowing the petal to strike their shoulder, where it instantly dissolves into the running water of their skin. They are the decomposition that the plateau denies; they are the stomachs that digest the falling blooms.

There is no society among them, for there is no scarcity, no conflict, and no narrative. They do not speak, for the Sound has already said everything that can be said, forever. They do not mate, for they do not reproduce; when the geometry of a deep ward shifts, when an alcove retreats just slightly further than the wall's thickness should permit, the friction of broken physics generates a heat that condenses in the saturated air. Over the span of a century, a new Sudary will form, weeping itself out of the stone and the mist, stepping forward into the corridors with the full knowledge of the harmonic chord already vibrating in its bones. 

They do not acknowledge each other in the plazas. They do not gather. Yet they are never truly alone, for they move in choreographies as intricate as the water's spiral. If a Sudary pauses on a bridge arching over a chasm of white mist, leaning outward at an angle that should invite a fall, another will eventually pass behind it, reaching out a flat, four-fingered hand to press against the small of the first one's back. It is not a rescue—the equilibrium of the air would catch them both—but a calibration. A moment of shared resonance, two nodes of flesh and stone touching to confirm that the Sound still holds, the water still weeps, and the great, frozen machine endures.

To exist on the plateau is to be a function of its weirdness. The Sudaries do not wonder at the sky that never clears or the heat that does not rise. They do not fear the warped gravity or the rooms that are too large. They push through the dense, sap-like air, their hearts beating in sync with the acoustic pressure, their flesh an extension of the ferric stone. They are the inhabitants of a place that does not breathe, and so they, too, merely hold.
