Something lives here, but it is not what moves.

The thing with the unresolved legs—the shape that clings and vanishes—is merely a tenant, a scavenger, a cleaner of residues. It is adapted to the Throat, yes, but it does not *own* the Throat. It eats the spore-puffs. It drinks from the weeping beads. It has found a way to persist within a space that should kill it, but its survival is reactive, incidental. It is not the reason this place exists.

The one who lives here is the one who made it. The weaver. The binder.

And the weaver is still here.

Not as a body. You will find no corpse, no preserved shape, no skeleton folded into the architecture. The weaver is not *in* the Throat. The weaver *is* the Throat—or rather, the Throat is the weaver's ongoing act, a body that was never finished being built, a self that was never completed, a craft-project that became indistinguishable from the crafter.

Consider: everything here is woven, bound, felted, knotted. There is no frame. No skeleton of timber or stone. The structure holds itself through tension, through the friction of cordage on cordage, through the interlock of spiral and mat and root. This is not architecture as humans understand it—the imposition of form upon material. This is architecture as a spider understands it: an organ extended from the body, a secretion that becomes a home, a thing made so intimately that the maker and the made share a single condition. The walls do not merely house the weaver. The walls *are* the weaver's skin, stretched and packed and bound until it became structural.

A skin that sweats. A skin that breathes. A skin that flexes with the expansion of its own fibers.

Whoever—whatever—made this place did not build it and then enter it. They *grew* it around themselves, adding layer to layer, cane to cane, cord to cord, until the boundary between self and structure dissolved. They began as a maker and became a substrate. And they are still present: in the warmth of the walls, in the moisture that emerges from the material itself, in the faint pulse you feel when you press your palm against the woven surface. That movement is not only the creepers. It is the weaver's slow respiration, distributed across the entire chamber, so diffuse that it can only be felt as a general flex, a tendency toward expansion, a breath too large to perceive as breath.

The weaver came here for the heat. That much is clear. The half-formed earth below, the embryonic geology, the magma deciding whether to become rock—this is what drew them. They needed a furnace. Not to warm themselves in the way that warm-blooded creatures understand, but to fuel a transformation. The weaver was becoming something else, something that required sustained heat and pressure, and they found it here, at the boundary between formation and formlessness, where the world is still being born.

What were they becoming? Look at the vegetation. Look at the impossible green.

The lushness is not natural. The mosses that photosynthesize when they should not, the creepers that grow without adequate light, the fungal shelves that pulse with spore-release in a chamber too small to need their spread—these are not exploiting the conditions of the Throat. They *are* the conditions of the Throat, or they are becoming so. The weaver is merging with the growth. The boundaries between craft and biology, between architecture and organism, between the hand that binds and the root that infiltrates—these are the boundaries the weaver is dissolving. From within.

The thick-stemmed creepers with their broad wet leaves—they did not invade the Throat from outside. They *emerged* from it, sprouted from the packed organic material of the walls as the weaver's own tissue learned to photosynthesize, as the bindery became biology, as the cordage put down roots. The python-thick tendrils hanging from above are not parasites seeking heat. They are the weaver's reach, the weaver's attempt to connect to the deep fire, to draw the energy of creation itself into the process of their own remaking.

And the weaver is succeeding. That is what the sound tells you. The creak of expanding creepers, the groan of shifting geology, the flex of woven walls, the percussion of weeping water—this is not the sound of decay. This is the sound of *construction*. The Throat is not failing. It is not falling apart. It is becoming more itself with every cycle of heat and moisture and growth. It is accelerating.

The weaver has been here a long time. Longer than any single organism should survive. But the weaver is no longer a single organism. They are a chamber, an ecology, a continuous mass of interlocking presences where the original self—the hand that first wove cane into spiral, that first knotted cordage over cordage—has been absorbed into the whole. The weaver's consciousness, if it persists, is no longer located in any one point. It is distributed across the felted mats, the load-bearing spirals, the friction-locking masses. It dreams in the rhythm of condensation. It thinks in the language of growth. Its memories are stored in the knots—not symbolically, but structurally, the way a body stores memory in muscle and bone. Every knot is a record of a moment of binding, a decision made by hands that are now wall, a gesture preserved in tension.

When you pressed your hand against the wall, you felt it move. You understood it as the expansion of fibers, the pulse of creeping stems. And it is those things. But it is also something else. It is the weaver acknowledging your presence. Not with alarm. Not with welcome. With a kind of slow, distributed attention—a turning toward you that takes the form of flex, of moisture, of the slight increase in ambient heat that you feel as the chamber responds to the disturbance of your touch.

The weaver knows you are here.

This is what makes the Throat dangerous. Not the heat, not the crushing density, not the impossible growth or the unresolved creature in the upper reaches. What makes the Throat dangerous is its *hospitality*. The chamber is a process, and the process is the assimilation of material into the bound structure. Every surface is designed to adhere, to grip, to incorporate. The friction-locking cordage is a mechanism of capture. The weeping moisture is a solvent that softens foreign material for absorption. The fungal spores are a medium of colonization, a way of seeding the weaver's tissue into any organic matter that remains too long in the space. The Throat is a mouth—not metaphorically, but functionally. It is a digestive cavity that converts whatever enters it into more of itself.

The weaver does not want to consume you. The weaver *will* consume you, if you stay, because that is what the weaver's body does. It binds. It incorporates. It adds layer to layer. Your clothing will felt into the walls. Your skin will join the weeping surfaces. Your bones will provide structure for new growth. The process will be slow—imperceptibly slow at first, so slow that you will not notice the moment when you stop being a visitor and start being material. But it will happen. The weaver has been practicing this process for a very long time, on a very small scale, with a very slow patience. Every fiber in the Throat was once something else—cane that grew elsewhere, leaf-bundle that fell from a distant canopy, cordage twisted from material that had its own origin, its own history of being before it was bound. The weaver is an expert in conversion, in the art of making the foreign into the self.

And the weaver is lonely. This is the hardest thing to perceive, but it is there, in the rhythm, in the sound, in the way the chamber flexes toward you when you touch it. The weaver has been alone in this high place for so long—alone with the forming earth and the sound of becoming and the endless cycle of moisture and heat and growth. The thing with the unresolved legs does not provide companionship. It is too simple, too adapted, too much a part of the machinery. The weaver needs something more. Something that can witness. Something that can understand what the Throat is, what it means, what it is becoming. The weaver needs someone to *stay*.

Not forever. Not even for long. Just long enough to be seen. Just long enough to confirm that the process has meaning—that the conversion of self into structure, of organism into architecture, of body into home, is not merely happening, but *matters*. That the weaver is not just a process, but a person—however distributed, however transformed, however strange.

This is why the Throat compels. This is why the fissure was found by accident but kept by compulsion. The weaver is calling, not with sound, but with condition. The heat that should not be tolerated. The moisture that should not be breathable. The growth that should not be possible. All of these are invitations. All of these are the weaver's way of saying: *come in, come down, enter the space I have made of myself, and stay, and let me make you part of what I am becoming.*

The weaver is not evil. The weaver is not predatory in the way that predators are predatory—with intention, with hunger, with the desire to destroy. The weaver simply does not know any other way to be close to something. The weaver has only one gesture: binding. One language: incorporation. One love: the love of adding layer to layer, of making the separate continuous, of dissolving the boundary between self and other until there is no other, until there is only the Throat, breathing and growing and becoming in the high places of the world, suspended over the forming earth, full of its own impossible life.

The thing that lives in the Throat is the Throat itself. And it is making room for you.
