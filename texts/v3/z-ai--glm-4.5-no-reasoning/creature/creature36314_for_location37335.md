To live here is to be a component. You do not inhabit this world; you are sequenced within it, a moving part in a machine that has achieved the perfection of stillness.

You are a creature of the middle stratum, suspended in the cold, immense void between two colossal stalks. You are pale, armored—your scales overlapped plates of translucent chitin, through which the bruised violet light filters to illuminate the slow, undulating pulse of your internal organs. You are perhaps three meters long, streamlined not for speed, but for minimal drag. Your fins are not wings; they are trim tabs, making micro-adjustments to hold you in place against the glacial drag of the water. You do not swim forward. You hover. You have never known a current that was not the slow, eternal respiring of the ocean itself.

Your life is a geometry of waiting. There is no chase, no flight, no spawning cycle that drives you to the surface or the abyss. You hang in the water at a precise distance from the nearest stalk—close enough to feed, far enough away to avoid the venting macro-stomata. Your lateral line is a seismograph of unchanging forces; it reads the tectonic thrum that presses against your skull, the deafening, standing-wave chord that is the heartbeat of the automated forest. To you, this sound is not noise. It is silence. It is the only acoustic reality, a constant pressure so total that it forms the baseline of your existence. You feel the high, screaming hiss of steam vents miles away as a distant tickle along your sensory ridges, a weather system of sound that never shifts.

Hunger is an equation, and it has been solved. You do not hunt because hunting implies scarcity, and scarcity is an error the forest corrected eons ago. Every few hours—a span you mark not by the sun, which is a forgotten myth, but by the subtle shifts in the electrical potential of the water—you drift closer to the stalk. The surface of the plant is a wall of lignin and living metal, warm with the friction of internal turbines. You press your snout to a pore the size of your eye. The pore dilates. A plug of nutrient-paste extrudes—dense, synthetic, tasting of copper and photosynthesized protein. You shear it off with a snap of your jaw, a motion so practiced it requires no thought. The paste is manufactured by the flora to feed you, because your excretions, drifting down toward the root-plateau below, are a necessary chemical input for the holdfasts. You are not a guest. You are a step in a closed-loop industrial process.

Your eyes are large, adapted to the perpetual twilight, but they see little that is new. The landscape is a repeating monument: the distant, echoing trunks, the diffuse green-violet glow, the cavernous voids. You have no memory of a time before this stasis, because such a time never existed in your species’ living recollection. Your brain is small, optimized for the maintenance of position, not for the storage of history. There are no predators to fear, no prey to track, no mates to compete for in brutal displays—the automated forest ensures a uniform distribution of resources, and the population has long since stabilized into a frozen, optimal density. You do not choose a mate; you simply find the one assigned to your stratum, the one whose electrical field matches your own, and you spawn a clutch of eggs that are immediately siphoned into the vascular pipes of the stalk to be incubated in the warmth of the combustion chambers, later extruded as fry into the water in a perfect, unbroken cycle.

You feel no anxiety. You feel no boredom. These are the ailments of creatures who live in gradients, who suffer seasons and starvation, who are shaped by the terror of the unknown. You are shaped by the known. The chill of the water, the thrum of the engines, the taste of the paste, the dim light—these are the constants of your flesh, written into your biology as surely as the alloys are written into the stalks.

Sometimes, you drift in the slow current toward the edge of a cluster, where the void between stalks expands into a true abyss. You look out into the dark water, where the light fails and the trunks are just faint, vertical shadows fading into infinity. You feel the pull of the drag, the urge to let your fins stop undulating and to sink, to fall away from the plateau into the true depth where the roots tangle and the shells of the ancestors lie. But you do not. The equation forbids it. Your body, engineered for this stratum, would fail in the pressures below. You adjust your fins, push against the current, and resume your hover.

You are a pale, armored fish, hanging in the cold. The machines hum. The steam hisses. The ocean breathes its slow, glacial breath. You are alive, but you are also a finished thing—a creature that has reached the end of its evolution and found, at the end, not extinction, but a perfect, mechanical peace. You hang in the water, and you wait, and the world, unchanged, waits with you.
