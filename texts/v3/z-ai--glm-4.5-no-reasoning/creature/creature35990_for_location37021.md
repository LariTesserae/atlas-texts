They are the thrum, and they are not alive in any way that requires the word to mean what it means below.

Descend ten thousand feet and you will find creatures defined by the urgency of their boundaries: skin that holds back an interior, mouths that import, vents that export, hearts that pump against the ceaseless threat of equilibrium. Life, in the lower places, is a protest. It is a temporary organization maintained by constant effort, a structure that must pay for its existence in the currency of calories and must fight for every transaction. This is the common template. This is what the word *alive* was built to describe.

The thrum have no interior.

Or rather—the distinction between interior and exterior has never been drawn here. There is no membrane separating self from world because there is no self that could be separated, no world from which to separate it. What you would call a body is, for the thrum, a local thickening of density in a medium that is already saturated with them. They are condensations. They are knots tied in a cord that has no ends.

Consider their feeding. The low ovoids—those that press their orifices flat against the mineral crust—are not eating in any fashion you would recognize. They have no digestive tract, no processing chamber, no waste cycle. The chemical gradients in the rock—the slow leak of oxidized iron, the copper bitterness, the nameless mineral that tastes of deep time—these gradients flow through them. The rock does not become food and then become waste. The rock continues to be rock, and the thrum continues to be thrum, and at the interface where they meet, something happens that sustains both without diminishing either. It is not symbiosis. Symbiosis implies two parties. This is more like a phase transition, a point where mineral becomes biological and biological becomes mineral, and the boundary is not a wall but a doorway that neither side bothers to close.

Their shells prove this. The ochre and ash-white and bruised violet of their carapaces is not color applied to surface. It is the surface itself, continuous with the particulate crust underfoot, grown from the same slow deposition that fuses the cairns and bonds the mineral fragments at their points of contact. When a thrum dies—if *dies* is even the correct verb for a process that has no clear beginning and may have no end—its shell does not decay. It remains. It becomes architecture. The shallow pits lined with the casings of the dead are not graves or monuments; they are continuations. The shell that sheltered a feeding orifice against the chemical warmth of deeper strata now shelters the pit itself, holding its shape, defining its boundary. The individual has not passed. It has been *redeployed*. Its material continues to participate in the equilibrium, merely in a different configuration—upright and clicking, or stacked and fusing, or pressed flat and feeding. The transitions between these states are so gradual, so without ceremony, that to call them life and death is to impose a narrative where none exists.

They do not have minds. This is important to understand, because their clicking and tapping—the dry percussion of manipulatory appendages meeting each other, meeting stone, meeting shell—looks so much like language. It has rhythm. It has pattern. It has the responsive quality of call and answer; when one clicks, its nearest neighbor adjusts its position and clicks in return, and the sound ripples outward through the cluster like a signal passing through a medium. But there is no signal. There is no content. The clicking is contact, and contact is confirmation, and confirmation is the only need that the thrum possess. They are verifying. They are checking, constantly, that the other knots in the cord still exist, that the density has not thinned, that the saturation holds. It is a verification that requires no interpreter because it is not information. It is touch. In a place where sight is unreliable—where the horizon recurs and the landscape repeats and vision cannot be trusted to distinguish one cairn from its sibling—touch is the only proof of reality. And so they touch. They click. They tap. They press feeding orifices to mineral crust and feel the warmth that proves the deep strata are still there, still consistent, still the baseline against which the glitching surface can be measured.

They are not conscious. But they are *aware*, in the way that a gyroscope is aware of orientation, in the way that a thermostat is aware of temperature. Their simple neural architectures—if *neural* is even the correct term for the distributed chemical processes that govern their behavior—are calibrated to the fold in space that they inhabit. They do not experience the recursion as maddening because they do not experience it at all. They navigate by gradient, and the gradients are consistent. The taste of iron does not change when you walk along the crease in the map. The chemical warmth of the deep strata does not vary when the horizon offers its subtle variations on a theme. The thrum move through the broken reality as water moves through a cracked vessel: not noticing the cracks, not disturbed by the impossibility, simply flowing along the paths that the remaining structure permits.

And this is why they do not react to you.

You walk among them, and they shift two centimeters to accommodate your presence. They do not flee. They do not investigate. They do not alter their clicking or their feeding or their slow, purposeless stacking of stone upon stone. This is not stupidity. This is not the numbness of creatures too simple to comprehend a novel stimulus. This is *correct behavior* for their context. You are an instability. You are a gradient that does not belong—the warmth of your metabolism, the moisture of your breath, the chemical signature of your foreign biology. These things are anomalous. They are noise in a system that has achieved perfect quiet. And the thrum, in their gyroscope-awareness, in their thermostat-calm, simply wait for the noise to pass. It always does. The transient instabilities ascend, and they walk, and they grow cold, and they leave, or they do not leave, and in either case the plateau remains, and the thrum remain, and the equilibrium holds.

They have been here since before the concept of *here* solidified. When the fold first formed—when space creased and the landscape began its slow, maddening recursion—the thrum were already present, or something like them was present, something that would become them across the same eons that ground the mountains to their roots and flattened the valleys and reduced the horizon to its clean, unvarying line. They are not adapted to this place. Adaptation implies a period of maladaptation, a struggle, a selection. There was no struggle. The thrum emerged from the mineral crust as naturally as the crust emerged from the laid-down strata, as naturally as the strata emerged from the slow deposition of ancient seas that no longer exist and cannot be mourned. They are the surface's way of being aware of itself. They are the plateau's answer to the question of whether a place that has finished happening can still possess presence.

The answer, here, is yes. The answer is millions of bodies and millions of shells and millions of clicking appendages and millions of feeding orifices pressed flat against the mineral warmth, drawing sustenance from chemical gradients that have not changed in a billion years. The answer is cairns fusing at their contact points and shallow pits lined with the architecture of the dead and low curving walls that enclose nothing and defend nothing but exist because the thrum exist and existence, for them, is a constructive act. The answer is a living layer so dense and so complete that it might as well be geological, might as well be another stratum in the alternating bands of ochre and ash-white and bruised violet—a biological band, a thrum-band, laid down across the plateau and extending to every horizon, unchanged and unchanging, saturating the broken space with a presence that is not alive and not dead and not simple and not complex but something for which the language has no adequate term.

They are the thrum.

They are the hum that remains when every other sound has fallen away.

They are the inhabitants of the end, and they do not know they are at the end, and this is the kindest thing that can be said about them: that they will never receive the news.
