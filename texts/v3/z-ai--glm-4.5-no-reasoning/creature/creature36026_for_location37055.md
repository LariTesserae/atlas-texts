Something lives here that is not an animal and not a plant, but a tenant. It is the thing that left the obsidian shard. It is the thing that half-remembers building the wooden hulls. 

It is difficult to say if it is a single creature or a network of ghosts. To understand it, you must first understand what it did: it surrendered its mass. In a place where the ground would swallow a heavy step, where the air is a soup of spores and the moss is a quagmire of enzyme and acid, to be large is to be a meal. To have a skeleton is to be a mineral reserve waiting to be mined. So, over ages that stretch back further than the fossil record, this thing ate itself away. It devolved, devolved, and devolved again, sloughing off its bones, surrendering its organs, shrinking its architecture down, down, down into the interstitial spaces where the great biome cannot reach. 

It survives as a distributed mind in a colony of soft, pale bodies no larger than a grain of rice. They are the palest motes in the clouds of gnats, lacking wings, lacking eyes, lacking any feature that would mark them as distinct from the vegetal slop they live within. They look, at first glance, like the larvae of some insignificant fly, or a loose cluster of root-aphids. They are the color of the inside of a lip—translucent, wet, and vein-traced. They do not walk; they burrow, swimming through the saturated peat with tiny, rhythmic pulses of cartilage, living in the micro-aquifers between the root-heaves and the fungal hyphae. They breathe the thick air through their skin, absorbing the warm mist and the dissolved oxygen, and they feed on the dead—on the chitin ruins of the ancient beetles, on the lignin ghosts of their own former architecture, gnawing at the edges of the past to sustain themselves.

A human eye could not find them. They exist in the spaces between the spaces. They live in the capillary channels of the collapsed wooden hulls, wriggling through the rotting grooves where their ancestors once drove iron nails. They live in the pith of the towering clubmoss, hollowing out pathways just beneath the silver-green lichen, leaving the exterior undisturbed. They are everywhere, but they are hidden, a subterranean nation of refugees from the age of hardness.

Though they have no brains in the human sense—only scattered ganglia that flare and dim like the foxfire—they have memory. The network remembers. When a droplet falls from a tendril to a lower leaf, the vibration travels through the waterlogged peat, passing through thousands of their pale, soft bodies. In that moment of bodily compression, a chemical pulse is released, a slow, diffused thought that ripples outward through the colony. They do not think *about* the past; they think *as* the past. The resonant groan of the continent shifting its weight is not just a sound to them—it is a sensation, a low, tectonic ache shared by ten billion nervous systems at once, a reminder of the immense, crushing weight of the green world they inhabit.

And they remember what the world took from them. 

Around the obsidian shard—the one hard, dead, indigestible thing in a continent of yielding flesh—their behavior changes. The shard is embedded in the bark of a clubmoss, and for a thousand leagues in every direction, the rice-grain tenants drift toward it. They cannot see it. They do not know it is sharp. But they feel its *refusal*. In a world where everything gives, where everything breathes and rots and composts, the shard is an absence, a silence in the hum of decay. They gather in the tight, angry whorls of bark that struggle against the glass. They press their lip-pale bodies against its flawlessly smooth surface, suctioning onto the cold, ignoring the warm, wet flesh of the plant around them. 

They do not worship the shard. They mourn it. 

It is the last evidence that something else was possible—that there was once a world where things could be hard, and sharp, and stand against the slow, patient erosion of life. In the permanent twilight of the High Peat, surrounded by the quiet, breathing lung of the continent, the tenants cling to the obsidian. They are the ghost in the green machine, the last whisper of a kingdom that dared to build upward, now reduced to a subcutaneous itch in the skin of a world that has eaten everything else.
