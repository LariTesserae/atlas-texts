The people of this city—if people is the right word, and it is only half-right—were not born, and do not die, in any manner that the lowlands would recognize. They are not echos or ghosts; they have weight. You can hear the soft compression of moss beneath their bare feet, see the faint dark moisture their fingertips leave on basalt doorframes. They wear wool that has no seam and no maker, the color of the stone and the lichen, draped in ways that follow no fashion but also no decay. Their faces are smooth and unlined by weather, yet they are not young. They have the stillness of standing water, the patience of formations that took eons to settle into their current shapes, and they smile so rarely that when one does—a slight thing, barely more than a relaxation of the mouth—it carries the weight of a profound event, a tiny seismic shift in the equilibrium.

Their eyes are the wrong color. Not dramatically wrong; you would not notice at first, in the perpetual gray twilight of the place. But in the rare hours when the clouds thin and some guttering light filters down, you see that their irises are the exact gray-green of the moss that carpets their city. It is a color that belongs to things which grow without light. They do not blink at the same rhythm as those from gentler elevations. Their blink is slow, meditative, a deliberate meeting and parting of lids, like the closing and opening of a small flower.

They are gardeners. This is their primary visible occupation, though *occupation* suggests labor, and what they do has the quality of ritual or respiration. They tend the terraces with movements that are precise and unhurried, kneeling in the cold damp to coax tubers from the dark soil, guiding the creeping vines along channels worn into the stone by centuries of the same gentle redirection. Their fingers are broad, flat at the tips, stained permanently with the dark earth that lives in the crevices of this vertical city. They speak to the plants, perhaps, though it is hard to tell; their mouths move sometimes in patterns that produce no sound audible at any distance, swallowed instantly by the moss and the fog. If these are prayers, they are addressed to small, quiet things.

They are also water-gatherers. When the evening clouds press in and condensation begins to weep from every surface, they emerge with their clay vessels—round-bellied, narrow-throated, gray as the stone—placing them beneath the known drips, the productive weeps. They have mapped the city's moisture with a precision that borders on the supernatural, knowing which joints in which walls will produce water, and when, and how much. They move through the dusk like sleepwalkers, placing and collecting with the certainty of those who have performed this task ten thousand times and will perform it ten thousand times more. Sometimes they drink from their catchments directly, tilting the vessels to mouths that seem barely to open. The water is clear and tastes of stone and age.

They communicate, but language as a system of arbitrary sounds seems to belong to another world. They have gestures—slow, weighted movements of the hands and arms that signify directions, needs, states of being. A flat palm held downward means *here, now, this*. Two fingers tracing the line of a wall means *water comes this way*. A hand pressed to the chest and then extended outward means something like *I am as you are* or *we persist*. But they also use rhythm, tapping messages on stone in patterns that carry further than voice could in this dense, sound-swallowing air. The tapping is never loud; it is the sound of rain on stone, of small feet running, of natural things happening. A stranger's ear cannot parse it, but to them, it is as legible as any book—a syntax of intervals and impacts, saying *the upper cisterns are full*, or *the fog comes early*, or simply *I am here*.

They do not pair or group in ways that suggest family. Children exist—a few, rare as bright days—small and solemn, moving through the stairways with the same measured pace as their elders, never running, never shouting, their gray-green eyes already holding the city's stillness. They are not born of wombs, so far as anyone could determine. They simply become present, as though the city occasionally decides to populate itself further, budding new inhabitants from its own substance the way the ferns erupt from the joints between stones. You might notice a figure you had not seen before, tending a garden you had thought empty, and realize that they have always been there, or that the distinction between *always* and *recently* has no meaning in a place where nothing accumulates.

They do not age, but neither are they frozen. They change in the way that a landscape changes in the course of a single day—shifting angles, altering shadows, moving through states that are real but cyclical, returning always to the beginning. The woman you see carrying water at dusk is the same woman who carried water at dusk last year, but she is also not the same; she has passed through a cycle and emerged renewed, or perhaps merely continued, the way a wave continues by breaking and re-forming. If you watched one of them long enough—watched without looking away, which is harder than it sounds—you would see that their routines have a subtle rhythm within the rhythm, a kind of breathing pace that swells and eases, making their repetition not mechanical but organic, like the beating of a heart that has slowed nearly to stillness but has not quite stopped.

They are not hostile. They are not even unfriendly, though their friendliness has a quality that is unsettling: it is the friendliness of things that have never learned to fear, because nothing in their long equilibrium has threatened them. When they encounter a stranger—which happens, though rarely, for the city does not advertise itself and the paths that lead here are not the kind that appear on maps—they regard the newcomer with their moss-colored eyes and make slow gestures of greeting, and offer water in a clay vessel, and indicate with a tilt of the head where one might find shelter on a terrace where the wind does not reach. They do not ask questions. They do not seem curious. They accept the stranger's presence the way they accept the fog: as a phenomenon that has arrived and will eventually depart, or will not, and either outcome is equally consistent with the nature of things.

But they watch. Not with suspicion—suspicion requires expectation, and they seem to expect nothing—but with a kind of peripheral vigilance. The stranger who walks through the city will feel, at every moment, the weight of attention that is not looking directly but is nevertheless aware. The cats watch too, and the crows, and the goats on their high ledges, and the foxes in the shadows, and the whole city seems to be a single organism with ten thousand eyes, none of them focused, all of them seeing. The stranger becomes part of the equilibrium simply by being observed; their paths begin to smooth into the city's paths, their footsteps falling into the rhythm of the dripping, their breath slowing to match the thin, conscious effort that the altitude demands. They stop looking for exits. They start noticing which walls produce the best condensation.

It is possible that they are not inhabitants at all. It is possible that they are *expressions* of the city itself—animated portions of its substance, the way that certain deep-sea creatures are not distinct from the colonies that produce them but are instead movable organs of a larger body. The carvings that appear in impossible places suggest that the city's relationship with space is not the relationship of the lowlands, that geometry here has a different character, one in which inside and outside, solid and hollow, self and other are not so firmly distinguished. Perhaps the people are what the city grows when it needs to tend itself, the way a body grows cells to heal a wound—except there is no wound here, only the quiet maintenance of a system that has achieved perfection and now simply persists, tending itself the way a sleeper turns in their dreams, not waking, not stopping, only continuing.

They are, in the end, exactly as strange as the city that holds them—which is to say, not strange at all once you have been here long enough, and deeply, profoundly strange when you remember what the world below is like. They are not ghosts. They are not illusions. They have hands that leave prints in the moss, voices that tap rhythms on stone, eyes that see the fog come in every evening and never wonder if it will not. They have found the thing that the lowlands never permit: a state of being that is complete, that requires nothing, that changes without deteriorating, that persists without effort. They are alive in the way that mountains are alive, in the way that stone holding water is alive, in the way that silence deep enough to hear your own blood is alive. They are the inhabitants of a place that does not need them and yet produces them, endlessly, quietly, like breath, like dripping, like the slow green reaching of ferns toward light that is barely there.
