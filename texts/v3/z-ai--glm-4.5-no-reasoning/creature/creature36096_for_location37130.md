The people of the Sapwood Ward are not inhabitants in the way a tenant inhabits an apartment; they are an embedded organism, a human mycelium threading through a living machine. They are arborist-engineers, keepers of the slow circuit, and they bear the deep, malleable imprint of their environment. There are very few of them—perhaps a dozen, perhaps less, scattered among the massive trunk-houses like vital nodes in a sprawling nervous system. They are solitary by necessity and by nature, moving through the perpetual damp with a deliberate, heavy patience that matches the growth of the fungal shelves beneath their feet. 

To look at them is to see a creature caught perfectly between rust and rot. Their skin is pallid, denied the sun by the dense canopy, taking on the translucent, cold quality of the fungus that walls their homes. It is perpetually pruned by the environment; the slow, abrasive crawl of guard-vines and the constant condensation have smoothed them, worn away the soft edges of their features. They have the weathered, waterlogged look of drowned things hauled from a peat bog, though they are warm to the touch, heated from within by the slow, thick churn of their own adapted metabolisms. Their eyes are large and dark, dilated permanently to navigate the amber dusk and deep shadows, rimmed with the red irritation of copper dust and wood-smoke. Their lungs, adapted over generations, are deep and slow, capable of drawing oxygen from air that is more water vapor and cellulose decay than breathable atmosphere. When they exhale, their breath mingles indistinguishably with the wet sighs of the stomata.

Their hands are their most telling feature—knobby, calloused, and permanently stained. The palms are dyed a deep, bruised umber from the tannins of the bark, intersected by the verdigris-green of copper wire and the rust-orange of iron pipes. Their fingernails are thick, chipped, and rimmed with the black grease of the wood-gas engines and the white residue of sap. These are hands that do not grasp forcefully; they coax and align. They know how to read the tension in a guard-vine, how to press a thumb against a cambium layer to feel the daily pulse of the xylem, how to splice a fraying electrical connection without causing the current to drop and the vines to contract. 

Their clothing is an extension of the ward's botany, barely distinct from the architecture. They wear layers of woven inner bark—tapa-like cloth that softens with wear and damp, growing mildew at the hems which is periodically scraped away with a dull blade. Over this, they drape macintosh-like shrouds crafted from cured, translucent leaf-pulp, sealed with the sticky exudate of the sap-presses. The garments do not keep them dry—nothing here is dry—but they regulate the seep, directing the constant drip of condensation away from the neck and spine, channeling it down to the root-felt where it belongs. Pockets hang heavy and low, filled with the tools of their synchronization: wire-splicers, pruning saws with teeth worn to fine, crooked points, and handfuls of copper and iron clips to coax the living wood back around the rusting pipes.

They live by the ward's clock, which is not a clock at all, but a rhythm. They wake not to an alarm, but to the sudden, heavy drops released from the canopy as the mist gathers. They take their meals sitting on the edges of the hydroponic vats, eating the pale, waterlogged tubers and nutrient-dense algae that thrive in the steam-heated water. Their food tastes faintly of diesel and iron, a metallic tang underscored by the sweetness of rot. They speak rarely, and when they do, it is in low, resonant hums and gestures, their vocal cords long adapted to a frequency that does not disturb the resonating guard-vines. A shout could alter the vibration; a sudden noise could invite the tendrils to contract. So they murmur, and the ward murmurs back.

Their labor is an unending cycle of maintenance—a gentle, physical diplomacy between the industrial bones and the botanical flesh. They spend hours in the hollow of the banyan bole, feeding deadfall into the gasifiers, listening to the deep, rhythmic thudding of the engines. If an engine misses a beat, they adjust the fuel mix, knowing that a failure in the generators means more than a loss of light. It means the ward locks its gates. The vines, deprived of their resonant frequency, will begin to tighten. A person trapped in a pathway would not be crushed instantly; the vines move with the patience of wood. But over the course of an hour, they would find their ankles bound, their chests compressed, the air squeezed from them by the slow, inexorable grip of the city defending itself. Thus, the keepers are wardens of the frequency, tuned as acutely as tuning forks to the hum of the copper wire. They feel the vibration in their teeth, in the soles of their feet on the root-felt. When the hum wavers, they move with sudden, desperate speed—checking connections, clearing resin-clogged intakes, tapping the iron pipes to shake the condensation free.

At the end of their shifts, they retreat into their hollowed trunk-homes, pulling the fungal shelf-dooms closed behind them. Inside, the amber glow of the incandescent bulbs casts long shadows over the woven taproot floors. They sleep on mats of living moss, their bodies cool and damp, their heartbeats slowing to match the wheeze and click of the sap-presses. They do not dream of the sun or the sky; those are abstract, hostile concepts—vast, dry emptinesses that kill by desiccation. Instead, they dream of pressure. They dream of the slow rise of water through xylem, the constant drip from the undersides of leaves, the patient contraction of vines, and the deep, subterranean thud of the engines that keeps the green city breathing, open, and alive. They are the mind of the Sapwood Ward, sparse and quiet, thinking in steam and voltage and sap, entirely swallowed by the beautiful, damp machine they keep.
