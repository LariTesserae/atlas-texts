To live here is to be a note in a chord that has been sustaining for a hundred thousand years.

The inhabitants of this place have forgotten that it was ever "built," if they ever knew the concept at all. For them, the metal bones and ceramic veins of the structure are simply geology—the way the world is. There is no myth of a creator; there is only the warmth of the Deep, the dampness of the Middle, and the whispering void of the High. They do not know they live inside the throat of a machine; they only know that the throat swallows, breathes, and hums.

The majority of life here is small. It has to be. In a world where every surface is claimed and every niche is full, survival is a matter of fitting into the gaps. There are the *chitin-weavers*, arthropod analogs by the trillion, whose bodies are flattened and jointed to slide beneath the moss mats and within the bowing grooves of the beams. They are the ones who generate that subsonic, chest-vibrating hum—not as a song, but as the mere exhalation of existence, the sound of ten trillion mandibles scraping and ten trillion wings adjusting in the dark. They do not see; they feel the vibrations of the structure. When a beam finally fractures after millennia of strain, they know it before the sound reaches any ear, their bodies interpreting the seismic shift as a command to swarm, to flee, to claim the fresh fracture where the uncolonized interior of the metal is briefly exposed to the warm, spore-thick air.

Then there are the *drip-tenders*, the amphibians who rule the condensation zones. They are eyeless and pale, their skin translucent and permeable, acting as much as gill as lung. They are the musicians of the metronomic drips. They position themselves in the worn, shining channels of water, their bodies shaped exactly to the smooth troughs carved by ten billion drops before them. They call to one another not to communicate, but to locate—to bounce their polyrhythmic clicks off the architecture, reading the echoes the way a sighted creature reads shadows. Their choruses are not chaos; they are an acoustic map of the local structure, updating constantly, noting every new growth of fungal shelf, every slow tilt of a settling platform. When you move through their domain, you alter the map. They fall silent as you pass, then resume their sonar-lit cartography once your warmth has moved on.

And there are the *canopy-stillers*, the creatures of the upper, cooler reaches where the mist drifts. They are the ones who have evolved to hunt by the faint bioluminescence of the fungal colonies. They are little more than prehensile silhouettes—flat, leaf-thin bodies that press against the undersides of the high girders, their metabolisms slowed to a crawl, barely distinguishable from the dead vegetation that coats the braces. They breathe once an hour. They wait for the moth-analogs, those dust-fine specks of life that drift up on the warm thermals from below, to cross the faint green glow of a lichen patch. Then, a flicker. A snap. The moth is gone. The steller resumes its wait. Its patience is geological; it has sat in the same hollow for three generations of its kind, passing the perch down through spores that root in the same crevice.

But the ones who truly rule this place—the ones who give it its oceanic, slow pulse—are the *Deep-Bound*.

You will never see them. You will only feel them. They live where the heat collects, down in the cisterns and basins where the water stands black and still, and the temperature is nearly intolerable to anything not adapted to the volcanic breath of the buried engine. They are vast. They are the size of platforms, of collapsed sections. They are not predators; there is nothing large enough to hunt. They are *ecosystems unto themselves*. Over eons, they have settled into the warmest hollows, their bodies fusing with the metal and ceramic, their root-like appendages threading through the conduits to tap the thermal reservoirs below. They feed by simply existing—by absorbing the chemical-rich condensation, by hosting the colonies of smaller organisms that live inside their folds and cavities and digest the particulate matter that drifts down from above.

Their vocalizations—the subsonic pressure waves that pass through the floors and arrive in your chest as a slow, tidal thud—are not calls. They are regulation. They are breathing. They are the sound of organs the size of reservoirs expanding and contracting across days. They are so large, and so slow, that their lifespans exceed the erosion of the structure itself. When one dies—if one ever dies—it does not rot. It petrifies, its mass becoming a new pillar, a new ridge in the built terrain, closing off a channel, diverting the condensation, reshaping the ecosystem above it over the next ten thousand years.

The sentients of this place—if "sentient" is the right word for a mind that operates on a timescale of centuries—are the *Archivists*. They are the rarest species, and the loneliest. They are descended from the builders, perhaps, though they have no memory of it. They are born with an instinct for the structure, an imperative to maintain, to patch, to ease. They move through the darkness with a grace that borders on the mechanical, their long, many-jointed fingers feeling the stress fractures in the beams, reading the fatigue in the metal like a blind reader reading Braille. They do not repair; the structure has "finished its breaking." There is no need for repair. Instead, they *tend*. They redirect the condensation when a channel is overgrown. They prune the fungal shelves when the weight threatens a platform that still shelters a viable niche. They bury the dead of the smaller species in the hollows where their decay will feed the roots of the plants that stabilize the girders.

The Archivists are the only creatures here who understand that the world is finite. They know the boundaries, or at least, they know that boundaries exist. They can read the gradients of warmth and sound the way a sailor reads the sea. They are the ones who feel the sorrow of this place—the realization that the structure is settling, that its equilibrium is final, that the slow finding of its final resting posture means that the breath of the Deep will eventually slow, and the condensation will cease, and the warmth will equalize and fade. They work to delay this, but their work is so slow that to an observer, they seem entirely still.

To be an Archivist is to stand in the deafening roar of ten trillion lives and know that it is an ending. To feel the pulse of the Deep-Bound in the floor and know that it is a heartbeat winding down. To walk the platforms where the moss is thick and the air is spore-heavy and the water shines in the carved channels, and to know that all of this—the noise, the heat, the impossible density of life—is just the death rattle of a structure that has been dying so beautifully and so slowly that its dying has become a whole world.

They do not despair. Despair requires a shorter horizon. They simply tend. They adjust a channel. They ease a vine. They stand in the dark, listening to the chord, and they ensure that the music continues, unceasing, for another thousand years.
