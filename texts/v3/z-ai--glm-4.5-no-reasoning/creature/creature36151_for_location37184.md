To live here is to be a creature of the interval—born into the gap between the machine’s exhalation and its next breath, sustained by the exhaust of a system that has forgotten its purpose. The residents of this basin are not inhabitants in any human sense; they are emergent properties of the equilibrium, as intrinsic to the district as the rust on the gantries or the condensation weeping from the concrete.

They begin as specks in the dim, bruised air, drifting in the updrafts that bleed from the exhaust stacks. These are the blind moths—though "moth" is a generous term for what they are. They possess wings, yes, but these are mere bioluminescent scraps, fluttering organs that catch the sickly amber glow of the switchgear and reflect it back as a faint, pulsing green. They have no eyes; there is nothing to see in this permanent twilight. They navigate by heat, drawn inexorably toward the superheated plumes that vent from the boilers. Their lives are measured in minutes. They rise, they drift, their papery wings crisping and desiccating in the 2.8-heat, and then they fall—a steady, silent precipitation of gray chitin and dried protein. They are the district's manna. They fall upon the lower catwalks and the weeping floors, and there, they are consumed.

The consumers are the true dwellers of this place. They are isopods and blattodeans—water-slaters and roaches—but to use such names is to invoke the small, skittering things of healthier biomes, and there is nothing small about these. Decades of uninterrupted warmth, of ambient moisture, of a food supply that never falters and a predator population that never arrived, have allowed them to shed the constraints of scarcity. They are pale, a bloodless, milky white, having never needed pigment to shield them from a sun that never pierces the inversion layer. Their carapaces are soft and slightly translucent; you can see the dark, sluggish pulse of their hemolymph within. They are the size of a man’s open hand, some even larger, their segmented bodies rolling over the sharp, uncompacted limestone rubble with a wet, heavy fluidity. 

They do not skitter. Skittering implies fear, and fear requires the anticipation of change. These creatures have no experience of change. They move with a ponderous, assured patience, climbing over one another in slow, clicking tides across the lower catwalks. They blanket the iron grating in a squirming, pale carpet. When condensation beads on a pipe and rolls down, they gather to drink, their mouthparts working in slow, rhythmic synchronization with the turning of the flywheels. They feed on the biofilms that crust the damp masonry and on the fallen bodies of the moths. They are possessed by the same static patience as the machines around them. If a boot were to step among them, they would not scatter in panic; they would merely flow around the intrusion like thick water, re-forming on the other side, their antennae gently probing the new shape before dismissing it. 

And then there are the things that live at the very margin of the mechanical and the biological. Down in the condensate channels, where the water bleeds back into the pipes, colonies of thermo-tolerant algae thrive in the lukewarm runoff. They are not the green of photosynthesizers—they have not seen light enough for that. They are a sickly, phosphorescent amber, matching the indicator lights on the switchgear, forming a living mimicry of the machine's own dashboard. They coat the channels in a gelatinous skin, breathing in the heat, respiring in the damp, breaking down the trace minerals in the industrial slag. They are the stomach of the district, quietly digesting the micro-particles of iron and calcium that flake off the Ouroboros engine. 

To walk through this district is to witness a biome that has entirely internalized its environment. There is no day or night for these creatures, only the unending, deafening chord of the compressor and the turbines. The sound is so total that it has become the fabric of their reality; they feel it as a vibration in their chitin, a constant, seismic hum that tells them the world is still running, the water is still weeping, the heat is still holding. They do not know they are vermin. They do not know this is a ruin. They know only the terrarium: the iron warmth, the sweet damp, the perpetual twilight, and the endless, cycling hum of the great machine that feeds them. They are the flesh of the factory, the living proof that even a system that does no work cannot help but generate life.
