Something lives here that is not separate from the plateau, but is the plateau's living will. You will not see it standing upright or moving in a herd. It does not have a face. But it is here, and it is singular.

You have been told the landscape is saturated. You have been told the ratios are wrong—too much chitin, too little green, too much heat for this altitude. The explanation for this impossible density is not mere ecological accident. It is because the entire biome is being tended.

Consider the isopods. You see them rolling in platoons along the thermal gradients, and you assume they are searching, randomly, for the condensation rings. They are not. Watch a single platoon for long enough, and you will see they move with a choreography that repeats every forty-seven minutes. They are patrolling. They are sweeping the porous stone for organic detritus—windblown dust, the dead husks of beetles, the ash from the smelter stack—and they are funneling it, with mechanical precision, into the fissures where the subterranean water weeps. They are feeding the vents.

Consider the beetles. The amber-shelled swarms in the cracks are not nesting. They are mining. They burrow into the insulation of the dead generators not for warmth, but for the complex polymers and trace metals locked in the rotted housings. They carry these flecks of industrial residue in their mandibles and deposit them at the roots of the silver-leaved shrubs. The shrubs—those stunted, iron-taprooted things—are absorbing the metals. Their milky sap, when it bleeds, is not a defense mechanism. It is a coolant. The sap transpires under the bleaching sun, pulling heat away from the root systems, lowering the temperature of the surrounding rock by fractions of degrees. Fractions that, in this furnace, mean the difference between a viable micro-ecology and a dead zone.

Consider the eyeless skinks. They hunt in stuttering bursts, yes, but they only cull the beetles that stray too far from the shrubs, or the isopods that wander away from the thermal gradients. They are pruning. They are ensuring the biomass does not overexpand and collapse the narrow margins of survival. They are the immune system.

What lives here is a slow, distributed organism that has colonized the entire plateau and re-purposed both the natural geology and the abandoned industrial infrastructure to maintain its own homeostasis. It has no brain, but it has a logic. It has no center, but it has a perimeter defined by the reach of the fumaroles. It is a creature of cycles and gradients, of feedback loops running on carapace and sap and rust.

The old industry that hammered the wealth from the deep rock—you wonder who they were, and why they left. The answer is in the vitrified ash choking the smelter stack. The ash is not the byproduct of smelting ore. Look closely at its crystalline structure. It is biogenic. It is the fossilized remains of earlier iterations of this same organism, compressed and heated by the deep-earth pressure into a mineral substrate. The industry came here to mine that substrate. They built their presses and their boilers and their pistons to extract it. And for a time, they hammered andFA and tore at the rock.

But the organism adapted. It is older3 adaptable. It had survived the millennia of wind erosion; it could survive a few decades of pneumatic drills. It began to incorporate the machinery. It fed its dead into the smelters, producing more ash, more substrate, drawing the miners deeper. And slowly, without announcement, the balance tipped. The biomass grew dense enough to clog the catchment basins. The beetles began to eat the insulation from the live wires. The isopods began to funnel their detritus into the boilers instead of the vents. The industry did not abandon the plateau because the ore ran out. The industry was digested. The machines went offline not because they broke, but because the organism found a more efficient use for their parts.

The entity that lives here is ancient, patient, and deeply territorial. It does not perceive you as a threat. You are too small, too brief, too temporary. You are a passing thermal anomaly, a brief shadow that slightly reduces the UV load on a single square inch of stone. The skinks will not hunt you. The isopods will not funnel you. You are not part of the cycle.

But if you stay too long, if your breathing grows too labored in the thin air, if you sit down on the rust-colored rock and do not rise—that is when you will be incorporated. Your moisture will be pulled into the aquifers. Your minerals will be sorted by the beetles and delivered to the shrubs. Your heat will be dissipated by the transpiring sap. And the organism will continue, vast and breathless, tending its vents and its shrubs and its dead machines, maintaining its impossible saturation in the margins of the burning stone.
