They are not born here. No one is born here. Children require a disturbance in the air, a cry that asserts itself against the void, and this place permits no such assertion. Instead, they arrive—walking out of the mist along the basalt paths, already quiet, already adapted to the weight of the silence. Some come alone. Some come in pairs that never speak to each other but move with the synchronized grace of limbs sharing a single circulation. They do not remember where they came from, or if they do, the memory has the same quality as the mist—present, saturating, but impossible to grasp or hold up to the light. They have always been coming here. The town has always been receiving them.

They are thin, but not fragile. There is a density to their thinness, a compression, as though they were formed under the same pressure that presses the silence against the ears. Their skin has taken on the color of the wet rock—not gray exactly, but the particular dark shade of basalt that has just been kissed by water, that will never fully dry. Their hands are broad and flat, the fingers slightly webbed at the base, the palms marked with calluses that follow the fracture lines of stone. They are built for gripping wet vertical surfaces, for holding on without effort, for becoming geology. Their lungs are large and inefficient, designed not for the gasping extraction of oxygen from thin air but for a slow, patient diffusion, letting the breath happen to them rather than taking it.

Their eyes are the most startling feature. They are large—too large for the thin faces they inhabit—and they are the color of the shallow pools: black, but not opaque. You can see into them, down through layers, but you will never find the bottom. They do not blink. They do not need to. The mist provides all the moisture their eyes require, and so the gaze is perpetual, unbroken, a steady contemplation that does not shift or startle or dart. When one of them looks at you, you feel observed not by a person but by the plateau itself—the slow, unjudging attention of a place that has nothing to prove and nowhere to be.

They do not speak with their mouths. Their throats can produce sound—a low, resonant tone that vibrates in the chest more than the air—but they use it rarely, and only with each other, and only in the deepest interiors where even the mist cannot penetrate. To you, they communicate by a system of presence and absence, of arrangement and gesture so subtle it might be mistaken for stillness. A hand placed flat on a wall means *the water is rising here*. A body turned slightly toward a channel means *this way, if you wish*. The closing of a door is not a closing but a sentence, and the meaning depends on which door, which room, which hour of the perpetual grey day. They have language, but it is a language of modification, of small adjustments to a baseline of silence. It is the language of a place where nothing needs to be announced because everything is already known.

They eat, but you will never see them eat. The cisterns beneath the buildings hold more than water—there are chambers within chambers, and in the deepest ones, things grow. Not plants from outside, not the ferns and mosses of the plateau, but something else, something pale and branching that spreads across the dark stone in fractal patterns and absorbs nutrients from the seeping water itself. It is a lichen, or something like lichen, something that evolved in the absence of sun and soil and lives off the mineral wealth of the basalt. They cultivate it. They tend it with the same quiet care they tend the channels and the cisterns, and when they consume it—it is not eating as you would understand it, but a slow absorption, the food held against the roof of the mouth until it dissolves—they take into themselves the taste of the place: wet, metallic, ancient, and clean.

They do not sleep as you sleep. They have no beds, or if they do, the low stone platforms in their dwellings serve a different purpose. Instead, they enter a state that is more like the stillness of the trees in the channels—a cessation of voluntary motion that is not unconsciousness but a different kind of attention, a listening that goes deeper than the ears. They sit or stand or lie in their rooms, and the mist comes in through the open windows (the windows are always open; the mist is always inside; there is no inside that is not also outside), and they become part of the water cycle. Their breath slows. Their skin glistens. Condensation forms on their eyelashes and runs down their cheeks like tears, though they are not sad. They are simply being processed by the place, being saturated and held, being reminded that they are mostly water and that water has its own ideas about where it belongs.

They do not age in any way you can track. The young and the old—if those words mean anything here—have the same faces, the same hands, the same stillness. It is possible that they are all the same age. It is possible that they have been here since the basalt cooled, since the water first found its paths, since the mist established its equilibrium, and that they are not human at all but something that emerged alongside the geology, a feature of the place rather than an inhabitant of it. They do not discuss this. They do not discuss anything. They simply persist, and their persistence has the same quality as the persistence of the ferns: not survival, not struggle, just continuation, the inability to stop being what they are.

They do not leave. This is important to understand. The paths that lead down from the plateau exist—they are steep and rough but passable, and they descend to lands where birds sing and insects hum and the silence is merely the silence between sounds, not the silence that replaces them. The people could follow these paths. They could walk out of the mist and into the world of weather and change and time. They do not. Not because they are trapped—their bodies are suited to the climb, their feet certain on the wet stone—but because the thought does not occur to them. It is not that they have chosen to stay. It is that leaving has become as incomprehensible as growing lungs to breathe fire. They belong to the saturation. They belong to the weeping stone. They are held here not by obligation or fear or love but by a deeper gravity, the gravity of a place that has achieved its final form and will not release what it has integrated into itself.

They build, though. This is the paradox, or the proof, or the clue. The town grows, slowly, in the only way anything grows here—by addition without expansion, by deepening without spreading. New chambers are carved into the basalt beneath the existing chambers, new channels are cut to connect cisterns that have begun to overflow, new walls are fitted to enclose spaces that have become necessary for reasons you cannot fathom because the population never visibly increases. But the work is done. You can see the fresh fracture faces in the stone, the surfaces that haven't yet been smoothed by the water, the joints that haven't yet settled into their gaps. Someone is making these things. Someone is deciding where the next room will be and what shape it will take and how it will connect to the breathing architecture of the town. There is intention here, planning, a mind—or many minds in concert—directing the shape of the future.

And this means they are not simply enduring. They are not frozen in a loop of maintenance and stillness, repeating the same gestures until the rock wears away. They are building toward something. The town is becoming more itself, more intricate, more deeply rooted in the plateau, more integrated with the water and the mist and the stone. Whatever they are—whatever they came from, whatever they are becoming—they are making a place for it to happen. A vessel. A shape that will hold whatever comes next.

Or perhaps not next. Perhaps simply more. The same, but deeper. The silence, but fuller. The saturation, but pressed further into the stone, into the water, into the thin warm air, until the distinction between the people and the place becomes a distinction without a difference, and you cannot say where the basalt ends and the bodies begin, and the town is not a construction but a crystallization, and the mist is not a condition but an exhalation, and the silence is not the absence of anything but the presence of everything, all at once, all in balance, all in the dark, all in the water, all in the home that has no name because it has no need of one, because naming is for things that might be mistaken for other things, and this place, this town, these quiet mineral people, will never be mistaken for anything else.

They are here. They have always been here. They will always be here, growing slowly in the channels where the water pools, their roots drinking from stone basins carved by forces older than they are, their bark black with perpetual damp, their branches reaching toward nothing because there is nothing to reach toward, only the mist, only the water, only the silence, only the plateau, only the staying.

They are what lives here.
