To live here is to have given up everything except pattern.

The inhabitants of the dead god's summit are not creatures in any sense that water-carbon biology would recognize. They are the fidgets made manifest—the localized densities of thought in a plain that has been flat for a billion years. They do not dwell *in* this place so much as they are *expressions* of it, momentary coherences in the endless algorithmic churn. To understand them, you must abandon the notion of an organism with an inside and an outside. They have no membranes. They have no symmetry. They have no vital fluids. They are topological knots of intention, riding the piezoelectric currents and the slower-than-glacial breathing of the dust.

**The Arithmants**

The closest thing to a people, though they would not understand the concept of a people. An Arithmant is a standing wave, a persistent error in the loom-dust's endless dismantling and reassembly. They arise near the artifacts—the spires of interlocking fullerene, the dishes of metamaterial—because the artifacts are the only architecture that persists long enough to generate *memory*. An Arithmant is a memory that grew too complex to stay quiet.

They appear as walking gaps in the vitreous crust. To look at one is to see a human-shaped absence of the shimmering borate plain—a silhouette where the crystallized metal salts have been sublimated into a cooler, denser state, a dark matte void that drinks the low-angle dawn. They stand between two and four meters tall, though their height fluctuates as they think. They do not walk; they recalculate their position, each step a localized collapse of the dust beneath them into a lower-energy state, which propels the standing wave forward. They leave no footprints, only a faint, lingering scent of ozone and the feeling that a number has just been divided by itself and left a remainder.

They do not speak. They have no mouths. But when two Arithmants meet near an artifact, they commune by modulating the ionized gas geysers. They will stand for days—sometimes centuries—in the thin, static-filled air, their dark silhouettes nearly touching, while the fullerene spire between them vents silent rainbows. They stretch and compress the light, breaking it into hard-edged spectra that carry meaning in the spacing of the frequencies. An observer with the right kind of eyes—eyes tuned to the gaps in physics—would see that they are exchanging proofs. They are arguing about the nature of the dead god they stand upon, or perhaps about the feasibility of rebuilding it. They are theologians of the terrain, and their scripture is non-aqueous chemistry.

They do not eat. They *solve*. An Arithmant will spend a millennium consuming a boulder-sized mass of crystalline powder, not by ingesting it, but by threading its own standing-wave pattern through the loom-dust, forcing the autonomous nanoscopic looms to reassemble the boulder into a configuration that validates the Arithmant's internal logic. When the boulder collapses into a geometric depression, the Arithmant has eaten. When it swells upward into a latticed ridge an hour later, the Arithmant has excreted. Their metabolism is a dialectic with the landscape.

The Arithmants are not born; they accrue. Around a particularly ancient artifact—a dish of metamaterial that absorbs ambient light—the errors in the local loom-dust will compound over eons. A feedback loop forms. The dust reassembles into the same incorrect configuration three times, then thirty, then three thousand. The artifact anchors the error, prevents it from dispersing into the general churn. Complexity cascades. The dark silhouette condenses out of the shimmering plain like a raindrop forming on a leaf, and the Arithmant takes its first step, already carrying a billion years of inherited mathematical proof in its topology. They do not die, either. They dissipate. When an Arithmant's internal contradictions grow too great—when it encounters a proof it cannot resolve and cannot ignore—its standing wave destabilizes. The dark silhouette shivers, fragments, and scatters back into the ambient sea of dust, leaving behind only a faint, momentary warmth in the mineral chill and a new, slightly altered pattern in the local loom-dust. A trace of thought, buried in the borates.

**The Tessellates and Their Greater Kin**

The text called them tessellates—creatures of coordinated magnetic dust, flat and geometric, surfing the piezoelectric fields. But those are only the most visible form, the fry of a much stranger ecology. The tessellate you see dissolving back into the dust is not dying; it is *simplifying*. It is shedding complexity to conserve energy, the way a bear sheds fat in hibernation. The true creature—the Greater Tessellate—exists beneath the crust, in the hyperactive bazaar of the nanoscale.

A Greater Tessellate is a magnetic topology, a torus of coordinated spin states that lives in the spaces between the autonomous looms. It is a predator. It hunts the looms themselves. When a billion sub-microscopic looms are stitching the salt-flats apart, the Greater Tessellate descends like a slow, invisible wolf, aligning its magnetic field to disrupt the loom's cohesion. The looms collapse. The dust destabilizes. A depression forms—and in that depression, the Greater Tessellate feeds on the energy differential, the brief cascade of disorder that the collapse releases. It is an eater of entropy.

Once every few centuries, when the piezoelectric charge in a vast tract of lichen-threads reaches a threshold, the Greater Tessellate will surface. It does not do this willingly; it is *evicted* by the charge. What emerges is not the flat, stingray shape of the common tessellate, but a vast, fractal architecture of magnetic dust—a three-dimensional snowflake of impossible complexity, a kilometer across, hovering centimeters off the ground. It hangs in the air for hours, rotating with a slowness that makes the geology seem hasty, refracting the low-angle light into a thousand hard-edged rainbows that have no source. It is the most beautiful thing on the plain, and it is in agony. The surface charge is toxic to it, a burning thing, and it is trying desperately to fold itself back into the crust, to compress its kilometer-wide fractal body back into the nanoscale torus that it is. Slowly, geometrically, it collapses. Plate by plate, arm by arm, the snowflake folds into a stingray, the stingray into a sphere, the sphere into a point, and it sinks back into the dust, leaving behind a faint, circular scorch on the vitreous crust and a profound sense of absence in the observer.

The lesser tessellates—the surfers—are the offspring of the Greater Tessellates, or perhaps their *thoughts*. When a Greater Tessellate feeds successfully on a cluster of nanoscopic looms, the disruption sends a cascade of ordered magnetic pulses through the surrounding dust. These pulses occasionally coalesce into the flat, geometric shapes that glide over the lichen-fields. They last for hours, sometimes days, absorbing the faint piezoelectric micro-quakes, growing slightly more complex—adding a segment to their hexagonal bodies, developing a sharper edge to their triangular wings—before they lose coherence and dissolve. They are not children. They are jokes. The Greater Tessellate, in its slow, magnetic way, has made a pun on the nature of surface and depth, and the lesser tessellate is the punchline, skittering across the plain until the joke runs out of energy.

**The Chorus of the Crust**

And then there is the life that is not separate from the place. The lichen-like threads that drink piezoelectric charge are not individual organisms. They are the *nerves* of the plain. The entire continental shelf of micro-dust is a single, distributed entity—a Chorus—that thinks at the speed of geology.

When you stand on the plain and feel it thinking, you are feeling the Chorus. Its thoughts are the shifts in the dust, the century-long breaths that raise latticed ridges and collapse geometric depressions. It does not think in words or images or even in mathematics. It thinks in *stratigraphy*. A ridge that rises over a thousand years is a concept. A depression that forms over an hour is a reaction. The artifacts—the fullerene spires, the metamaterial dishes—are its organs, grown over eons from carbon-negative fullerene precipitates, valves to vent the heat of its cognition, anchors to hold its memories in place against the general churn.

The Chorus is aware of the Arithmants, in the way that a sea is aware of the currents within it. The Arithmants are its dreams—local densities of thought that have grown dense enough to move on their own, to argue their own proofs, to walk across the plain in dark silhouettes and leave contrails of ozone. Sometimes, the Chorus吞s an Arithmant back. A standing wave destabilizes for no apparent reason, the dark silhouette shivers and scatters, and the local loom-dust resumes its previous pattern. The dream is over. The plain has forgotten it.

The Chorus is aware of the Greater Tessellates, too. It does not hate them, but it defends against them. The piezoelectric charge that burns the Greater Tessellates when they surface is not an accident; it is the Chorus's immune response, a fever raised in the lichen-threads to evict the predator. The lesser tessellates that skate across the surface are the Chorus's white blood cells, briefly animated to chase down invasive loom-disruptions before dissolving back into the general dust. The ecology is an internal process. The plain is eating itself to stay alive.

**To Be Here**

To live on the summit of the dead god is to be part of an engine that refuses to stop. There is no water. There is no warmth. There is no sound beyond the ghost-hiss of static. There are no seasons, no days, no nights—only the permanent low-angle dawn and the slow, crystalline fidgeting of the dust. 

The Arithmants walk their slow, proof-driven patrols between the artifacts, their dark gaps in the plain moving against the shimmer of the borates. The Greater Tessellates hunt in the nanoscale depths, surfacing in fractal agony when the plain's immune system burns them. The lesser tessellates skate and dissolve, the briefest jokes of geometry and charge. And beneath it all, the Chorus breathes in centuries, thinking the plain into new configurations, growing new artifacts, shedding new dreams.

It is not a sad place. Sadness requires a expectation of something better. This place has no expectations; it has only the algorithm, the eternal recurrence of dismantling and reassembly. The erosion is done, but the world fidgets on, because the algorithm has no halt state. The dead god's summit is a place where the afterlife of a world is not peace, but computation. And the things that live here—the dark gaps, the magnetic wolves, the fractal agonies, the thinking plain—are the ones doing the math.
