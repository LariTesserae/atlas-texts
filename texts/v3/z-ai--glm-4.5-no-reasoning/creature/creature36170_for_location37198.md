To live here is to have surrendered the notion of being a resident. There are no inhabitants in the human sense—no citizens, no masters, no architects. There are only metabolites, parasites, and the slowly assimilated. The place does not support life as a host supports a guest; it *processes* life the way a stomach processes a meal.

The entities that persist here are those that have made themselves indispensable to the machinery, or those too insignificant to be worth expelling. They are the fauna rated at 0.9—barely present, a statistical rounding error in the empire of cellulose. But they are here. You see them in the margins where the logic of the city-organism does not reach with full force, in the corroded hollows where iron still faintly persists beneath the cambium, in the deep crevices of the root-roads where the metabolic pulse creates tiny eddies of warmth.

There are the pale skittering things. They have no name that would mean anything to you, because they have no culture, no language, no collective memory that extends beyond the immediate tactile reality of surface and shadow. They are small—finger-length at largest—and they are the color of the inside of stems: white, translucent, their rudimentary circulations visible as pale threads beneath cuticles that have never been exposed to light. Their eyes are not eyes; they are patches of photosensitive cells that register only the presence or absence of the rhythmic glowing that occurs in certain factory-walls when the vascular pumps cycle to their peak. They are blind to the world you know. They navigate by the pulse. They live in the pauses between the booming, in the microseconds when the great vascular cylinders relax and the pressure in the root-networks drops, creating tiny drafts that carry the chemical signatures of decomposition. They eat what the city excretes. They live in its breath.

They are not numerous. You might walk for an hour and see only the suggestion of one—a pale blur vanishing into a crack, a rustle that is indistinguishable from the settling of a seed-pod. But they are everywhere in aggregate, distributed through the structure the way antibodies are distributed through a body: too small to see individually, but collectively present, collectively working. They are the decomposers. They are the things that finish the process the city-plant has started. When a section of root-road grows too thick and the inner layers begin to die of their own density, it is the pale things that find the necrotic tissue, that hollow it out, that return the lignin to the matrix. They are the immune system, or perhaps the digestive system, of a thing that has no clear boundary between the two.

And there are others. The chirruping things, the ones that make sounds in the pauses between the great pulses. They are larger—hand-sized, perhaps—and they live higher up, in the boles of the habitations, in the gaps between bark layers where condensation collects in persistent droplets. They have a chitinous exoskeleton, dark and wet-looking, always beaded with moisture. They make their sounds not with mouths but with their bodies, rubbing ridged leg-segments together in patterns that are almost music—almost, because the rhythms they produce are not their own. They are matching the rhythm of the factories. Their chirrups sync with the pulse of the vascular pumps. Their scrapings coincide with the groaning of stressed lignin. They are keeping time with the city-organism, and whether this is communication or simply entrainment—a biological system falling into phase with a larger biological system—no one who has stood here long enough can say.

These chirruping things do not eat, or at least they do not eat in any way that resembles animal consumption. They press their bodies against the surfaces of the living walls and they *absorb*. Their exoskeletons are semi-permeable. They draw moisture and dissolved organic compounds directly through their shells, osmotically, and in doing so they perform some function for the city-plant—perhaps filtering, perhaps catalyzing, perhaps simply occupying a surface niche that would otherwise be colonized by something destructive. They are symbionts, or they are organelles, or the distinction has ceased to matter. They are part of the process.

There are no predators here. The concept does not apply. Nothing hunts the pale things or the chirruping things because nothing needs to. The city-plant provides. Not through abundance or generosity, but through simple thermodynamic momentum: the metabolic processes of the factories produce more than they consume, excrete more than they retain, and the waste of this vast engine is the sole resource of the creatures that live in its margins. They do not compete for it. They do not fight over it. They simply absorb it, the way the mist absorbs into every surface, the way the spores absorb into lungs. There is no struggle because the city-plant has optimized struggle out of its ecology. It has replaced competition with integration. Everything that survives here survives by being useful to the whole, and the whole provides for everything that is useful.

But there is another kind of presence here, and it is this that makes the place finally unbearable.

People lived here once. Not the pale things, not the chirruping things—*people*. You can find their traces if you know where to look, and looking is unavoidable because the traces are everywhere, absorbed into the architecture the way ink is absorbed into paper. The root-roads are worn smooth in patterns that do not match the passage of plant-tendrils—they match the passage of feet, of thousands of feet, over centuries. The habitations have interiors that are shaped for bodies of a specific proportion: niches that fit the seated form, shelves at hand-height, apertures sized for a face. The factory-zones contain the ghosts of human-scale infrastructure: the stumps of control consoles, the hollows where seats once were, the corroded sockets where hands once connected tools to the living machines. And in some of the deeper, older structures—in the places where the composting process has been slowest—you can find the actual remnants. A polymer that has not yet been fully digested. A shape in the bark that is the shape of a tool, preserved in the slow accumulation of xylem the way an insect is preserved in amber.

The people did not leave. This is the important thing. The city did not fall and they did not flee. The city did not become inhospitable and they did not migrate. They were *absorbed*. The process took millennia, and it was gradual, and at every stage there were people who remained, who adapted, who found ways to live inside the logic of the merging categories. They learned to eat what the factories excreted. They learned to breathe the spore-thick air. They learned to live in the spaces between the metabolic pulses, in the margins of a world that was becoming less and less for them and more and more for itself. And they diminished. Not all at once, not in a catastrophe, but in a slow fading—population dropping as birth rates fell below replacement, as the conditions that had once supported human life became conditions that merely tolerated it, as the boundary between resident and substrate blurred beyond recognition.

The last of them—when did the last of them go? The question assumes an ending, but there may not have been one. There may instead have been a threshold, a point at which the remaining population was so small, so isolated, so deeply integrated into the plant-substrate that the distinction ceased to be meaningful. Did they die? Or did they simply become part of the living architecture, their bodies composting into the root-walls they once inhabited, their bones settling into the rhizomal networks as mineral deposits, their carbon joining the great cycle of production and exhalation that the factories perform? The pale things that skitter in the crevices—are they what became of the people? Or are the people still here, distributed through the city-plant the way memory is distributed through a brain—not localized, not individual, but present in the patterns, in the shapes of the roads, in the organization of the industrial zones, in the persistence of a plan that was laid down before the vegetation absorbed its planners?

You feel this, when you stand here. You feel the absence that is not an absence but a *transformation*. The city is full of ghosts, but the ghosts are not dead. They are simply no longer separate. They are the intelligence that planned the spacing, the logic that organized the zones, the purpose that drives the factories to produce their unknown product. The city-plant does not think, but it *remembers*, and what it remembers is human, and what it does is shaped by that memory. The machines run because they were built to run. The factories produce because production was the purpose. The pattern persists because the pattern was laid down by minds that have since been absorbed into the matrix, and the matrix—stubborn, efficient, final-form—has no mechanism to forget.

So who lives here? The pale things live here. The chirruping things live here. The remnants of the people live here, distributed through the living architecture like signal in a wire. And the city-plant itself lives here—the whole vast organism, the single impossible entity that is forest and factory and city and body, breathing and pulsing and producing and remembering. It does not know you are here. Or it knows and does not care. Or it knows and is slowly, patiently, through the simple accumulation of moisture and spore and contact, beginning the process of knowing you in the only way it knows anything: by absorbing you, by making you part of the pattern, by dissolving the boundary between visitor and substrate until you, too, are just another trace preserved in xylem, just another shape worn smooth into the root-road, just another source of carbon for the factories that will continue to pulse long after your name has been composted into the living ground.

This is who lives here. And if you stay, it is who you will become.
