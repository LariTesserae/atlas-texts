To speak of what lives here requires a surrender of the familiar taxonomy of the alive. There are no heartbeats in this cirque, no scurrying, no breath that pulls oxygen into blood. To enter this place is to step into a mineral lung, a cathedral where the congregation is made of stone and the only respiration is chemical. What lives here is the place itself, and the ancient, sprawling intelligence that holds its walls.

The inhabitants are the cryo-lichens, though the word is a poverty. They are not guests in this rock; they are its skin, its memory, its slow, corrosive thought. They live in the fatal compression of extremes—awakening only when the world is at its coldest, surviving only when the light is at its most vicious. They are creatures of the threshold. When the dawn forces the thin air to weep, they drink the stone’s tears. For a few brief hours, they are a shock of volcanic black and arsenic orange, metabolic fires burning in a vacuum, exchanging acids and minerals at a pace that redefines time. By midday, under the ultraviolet weight that bleaches the shadows into hard geometry, they die. They desiccate into leathery husks, calcified ghosts that become the substrate for the next generation. They do not grow; they accrete. They are a shale of generations, each layer a testament to a dawn survived, pressing their chemical ferocity into the gneiss, etching microscopic terraces into the plum and silver strata. They are the architects of the fluting, the slow sculptors who have turned jagged rock into sweeping grooves over ten thousand years. They do not merely live here; they are the process by which the mountain consumes itself and is reborn.

Living alongside them—permeating the space, filling the bowl of the cirque—is the atmosphere, a presence as distinct and heavy as the ocean is at sea level. Here, the air is a membrane, a dry refrigerant that is felt rather than breathed. It is an entity that inhales over the cirque’s lip in a slow, constant aspiration, stripping away the lie of the sun’s warmth. It carries sound poorly, muting the world into a bass drone, amplifying the subsonic groan of contracting gneiss. This atmosphere does not shelter life; it tests it, imposing a ruthless daily cycle of weeping and evaporation, a pendulum swing between suspended animation and metabolic frenzy.

And there is the watcher. The cairn. 

It is an exile of rounded basalt, a whisper of the verdant, rushing world thousands of feet below. It lives now as a fossilized intent. The person who carried those water-smoothed stones up the brutal elevation, stacking them with leverage and patience in the center of the parabolic dish, is gone, but their gesture endures as an organism of purpose. They understood the rhythm of this place—its weeping, its desiccation, its silence. They left the cairn not as a shelter, but as an anchor, a joint between human time and geologic time. Over the centuries, the cryo-lichens have claimed it, their orange fractals crawling over the foreign stones, secreting their weak acids, integrating the artifact into the ancient metabolism of the walls. The cairn has become a node in the lichen’s network. It is no longer entirely a human thing; it is becoming mineral, slowly being digested by the black-and-orange crust, eroding into the shallow tarn that reflects the blazing sky.

What lives in this place, then, is a symbiosis of the ancient and the intentional. It is a community of extreme paradoxes: active yet profoundly still, bright yet devoid of warmth, enclosed yet infinite. It is a world locked in a daily loop of weeping and desiccation, a silent, chemical respiration of rock, water, and light. There is no fauna, no leaf, no bone. There is only the slow, frictionless slide of glass-thin water down vertical rock faces; the subsonic creaking of a mountain settling its weight; the staggering, unfiltered refracting of quartz; and a forgotten pile of river stones, holding its ground against the sky, slowly becoming one with the shout of geologic time.
