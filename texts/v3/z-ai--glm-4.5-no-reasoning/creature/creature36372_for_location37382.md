They are the weepers, the stone-eaters, the quiet architects of this cold wound in the earth.

They do not walk; they haul. To move through their city is a labor of shoulders and breath, threading the crevices where buildings interlock, pressing through archways carved for bodies shaped by pressure and time. They are built for the narrow squeeze—compact, dense of bone, with ribcages like barrels and limbs thick with the slow, cold muscle of a life spent pulling oneself across wet stone. Their hands are broad, the palms leathery and calloused from a lifetime of gripping tufa, their fingers short and wide, webbed to the first knuckle to spread the constant water, to feel the vibration of the weeping rock.

Their skin is the pale, translucent white of things that have never known the sun—not the white of albinism, but the gray-white of wet limestone, mottled with the electric green of the moss spores that live in the creases of their knuckles, the folds of their necks, the insides of their elbows. They do not merely carry the moss; they are colonized by it. The same vivid, freezing green that packs the crevices of their city grows in the hollows of their bodies. It is a symbiosis. The moss draws moisture from their skin, and they draw warmth from the biological engine of the moss, a faint vegetal heat that staves off the structural cold settling into their marrow. When they are still—when they press themselves into a crevice to sleep or wait—they become indistinguishable from the architecture. Green and gray and wet. Only the slow blink of an eye, dark and flat as the standing water, marks them as alive.

And their eyes are strange. Not blind, but *sighted differently*. They do not see by the diffuse, pearlescent light that filters through the mist; that light is merely a color to them, a pale silver wash. They see by the water. Their eyes are tuned to the micro-impacts of droplets on stone, to the displacement of the black water table, to the subtle differences in surface tension where a film of wetness thickens on a living body versus a dead wall. In the saturated air, water is everywhere, and it is their medium of perception. They can feel, through the pores of their skin and the sensitive webbing of their hands, the displacement of a pale amphibian hauling itself up a wall three dwellings away. They can hear, in the wet slap of a fern frond folding, the weight of the condensation that brought it down.

They speak in the language of the place. Not with breath—they conserve breath, for the air is heavy and cold and wet to pull into the lungs—but with touch and pressure. They press their broad hands to the wet stone and *hum*, a low, resonant thrum that travels through the saturated rock, through the standing water, through the interlocking walls. It is a sound felt in the bones, not heard in the ear. A weeper calling across the city sends a vibration through the tufa, and the recipient feels it in their own ribs, a cold pulse of meaning. They have words for the kinds of wetness: the slow exudation of fresh fracture, the slick film of ancient moss, the black stillness of the water table, the mineral tang of shattered chalk. They have words for the sounds water makes: the tear down a vertical face, the click of a crustacean carapace swallowed by damp, the silent suck of a submerged wake in the deep cellars. They do not have a word for *dry*.

They are ancient, but they are also young—like their landscape, caught in the raw moment of exposure. Their city was built before metal, before hardened bone, and they are the inheritors of that logic of pressure and biological secretion. They did not carve the stone; they *grew* it. Their ancestors pressed the soft, erodible rock with their hands, packing the tufa blocks, mortaring them with the living mud of their own secretions—the same thick, gelatinous substance that brackets their doorways in pale fungal shelves. The city is not built upon the basin; it is *of* the basin, grown from the patience of soft hands working soft stone in the cold dark. Every dwelling bears the thumbprints of its maker, pressed into the tufa like fossils, worn smooth by the constant drip of water but still present, still recognizable to the touch of a weeper's hand.

Now, in the freshly exposed wound, they are renegotiating with the world above. The roots that plunge through their roofs—they know them. They have watched them descend, inch by patient inch, over the generations. They do not cut the roots; they incorporate them, straddling them like the legs of buried giants, building new gutters to guide the water the roots bring, new cisterns to catch the weeping. The explosive ecology is not an invasion but an intensification. They farm the pale amphibians, coaxing them from the walls with offerings of moss spore, harvesting their soft, suction-cup mouths for the gelatin that seals their dwellings. They cultivate the fungal shelves, spreading the spores with their fingers, eating the pale tiers that grow thick enough to harvest. They hunt the translucent crustaceans, flicking them from the standing water with quick, webbed hands, the click of their shells a brief disturbance in the quiet before they are swallowed, raw and cold, in the dark.

But they do not go into the deep, flooded cellars. Those are the domain of the larger thing, the unseen presence felt in the displacement of water. The weepers know it is there. They feel its slow, patient wake moving through the submerged foundations, the subtle pull and suck of water around their feet when they pass near the flooded thresholds. They leave offerings at the edges of the black water—mounds of crustacean shells, amphibian eggs, thick coils of the electric moss—and the offerings disappear in the night, pulled beneath with a silent displacement that sends a shudder through the stone. They call it the *deep architect*, and they believe it is older than the city, older than the basin, a thing that was here when the limestone was still forming at the bottom of a shallow sea. It does not prey on them, and they do not hunt it. It is part of the held breath, the patient, freezing embrace.

They are few, now. The exposure of the escarpment has brought the cold, the light, the explosive colonization of the ferns and the roots, and the city is eroding faster than they can repair it. Water dissolves the mortar of their secretions; roots pry apart the walls they have shared for generations. They work constantly, hauling themselves through the crevices, pressing new mud into the cracks, guiding the weeping water away from thresholds with their bare hands. But the pace of cold and wet is relentless, and they are slow, and they are tired, and the green engine of the moss on their skin is not enough to stave off the cold that settles, always, into the marrow.

When one of them dies, they do not mourn. They carry the body—haul it, shoulder to shoulder, through the narrow crevices—to one of the deeper cisterns that still holds water, and they lower it in. The body sinks slowly into the black water, and the displacement is felt throughout the city, a final pulse of pressure through the saturated stone. The moss continues to grow on the skin, the fungal shelves sprout from the creases of the neck, and in time the body becomes part of the architecture, a new joint in the masonry, a new surface for the water to weep from. They do not distinguish between the living and the dead, between the body and the stone, between the city and the self. They are all of the basin, all of the wound, all of the held breath.

And when the escarpment closes—as they believe it will, as it must, for the earth is patient and the fracture is young—they will be sealed again in the dark, in the wet, in the structural cold. The ferns will wither without the pearlescent light. The roots will slow their descent. The explosive ecology will settle into the slow, silent motion of growth and erosion at the pace of cold and wet. And they will continue, as they have always continued, hauling themselves through the crevices of their interlocking city, pressing their broad hands to the weeping stone, humming to one another in the language of water and pressure and bone. Waiting for the next exposure. Waiting for the next breath.
