The people of Verashalt are not settlers; they are an epithelium.

They grew here in response to the wound. They are the living film that forms over something raw and wet and still organizing itself, and they perform the function that all epithelial tissue performs: they protect, they absorb, they hold the margins together with their bodies. They do not think of themselves this way. They think of themselves as workers, as engineers, as people who came here for a wage and stayed because the wage was good and the canyon was deep and the world outside had somehow become less substantial than the world within it. But watch them. Watch how they move along the catwalks in the pre-dawn fog, their shoulders hunched against the damp, their hands out to touch the railing, the wall, the pipe—always touching, always reading the city through their skin. They are feeling for the shift. They are checking whether the distance between this tier and the next is the same as it was yesterday. They do this constantly, and they do not know they do it.

**THE ENGINEERS** are the ones who understand, or who pretend to.

They are easy to recognize. They carry measuring tapes that are never retracted, because there is always something to measure. They carry notebooks that are swollen with damp and dense with numbers, and the numbers do not agree with each other. They have a look—something behind the eyes that is not quite anxiety, not quite resignation, but some alloy of the two. They have seen the canyon change. They have documented the change. They have written reports about the change, and the reports have been filed, and the filing has changed nothing, because how do you file a report on the geometry of space? How do you note, in the margin of a form, that the distance between catwalks seven and eight has developed a slight but measurable curve?

The chief engineer is a woman named Pell. She has been here nineteen years, which is longer than anyone else in her position has survived. She is not old—forty-three, forty-four—but her hair has gone the same rust-orange as the lichen that grows on the oldest pipes, and her posture has adapted to the canyon: she leans, always, slightly inward, toward the rock face, as if she is listening to it, or as if she does not trust the void behind her. Her office is on tier eighty-two, which is in the middle of the inhabited band, and her office is always full of models—physical models of the canyon, built from wire and clay and scrap metal, that she updates by hand each week. The models do not match. She builds a new one each month, and each month the old one is wrong in ways she cannot explain by reference to settling or erosion. She keeps the old models anyway. They line the walls of her office like a timeline of something going subtly but persistently wrong.

Pell does not sleep well. None of them do, but Pell's insomnia has a specific quality: she lies awake counting the beats of the foundry below, and she counts them because if she stops, the rhythm might change, and if the rhythm changes, something else will change, something in the rock, something in the space between the tiers, something that she will not be able to fix with concrete and cable. This is not rational. She knows this. She counts anyway.

Her crew is loyal in the way that people are loyal to someone who is holding something together that should not be able to hold. There is Merek, who specializes in foundations, which means he specializes in failure—he knows every way that a building bolted into soft stone can fail, and he checks for all of them constantly, tapping walls with a rubber mallet, listening for hollowness, for the wrong resonance. There is Senn, who maps the tears, the seeps and weeps, and who can predict where a new one will appear by reading the mineral stains on the cliff face like a physician reads symptoms. There is Vesh and Orin, twins, who work the high tiers where the erosion is fastest, who spend their days on scaffolding that sways in the canyon's drafts, adding new anchor bolts, new braces, new stitches of iron that will hold until the rock decides to be something else. They all have the same look behind their eyes. They all touch the walls when they walk.

**THE MINERS** go lower than anyone else.

They work the seams at the base of the city, where the rock is softest and the cordite—the mineral, the compound, whatever it is—is densest. They are a separate people, almost. They have their own canteen, their own dormitories, their own slang, their own silences. The miners do not talk about what they see in the deep tunnels, and what they see is this: the rock changes when you look at it.

Not quickly. Not in any way you could film or measure. But the miners work in the stone for ten hours at a stretch, and they develop a relationship with it that is more intimate than marriage, and they will tell you—quietly, privately, after the second drink in the third-shift bar—that the stone has moods. That some days it yields the ore easily, and other days it resists, as if it does not want to be touched. That the strata shift when no one is watching. That the shapes in the rock—the imprints, the negative spaces, the compressed architecture—are different each time you look, not in a way you can point to, but in the way a face changes as you get to know it, revealing expressions you didn't see at first.

The miners have a ritual that the management does not know about. Before each shift, they touch the wall. All of them, every one, pressing their bare palm to the rock for a moment before they descend. They do not discuss this. It is not organized. It simply happens, the way the fern grows toward the lights—because something in them has learned that the rock is not dead, that it is not inert, that it is participating in whatever is happening here, and that it is better to acknowledge this before you enter it with a drill.

There is a miner named Thade who has been here longer than anyone—twenty-six years, which should not be possible, which is longer than the city has been operational in its current configuration. Thade is not old in the way that Pell is not old, but his skin has gone the same grey-green as the moss that grows on the steam pipes, and his eyes have adapted to the dark in a way that goes beyond physiology: he can see in the lower tunnels where the lamps have failed, where even the bioluminescent moss cannot grow, where the only light comes from the ore itself, which glows faintly, which is one of the properties of cordite that the records do not explain. Thade can see in that light. He can also see—so he says, so he will not say in front of the foremen—the things that move in the rock when the drills stop. He describes them as currents. As slow rivers of something flowing through the stone, visible only when you have learned to look at the rock not as a solid but as a medium, a medium through which things move.

No one believes Thade. But no one disputes him either, because Thade is still alive, which is more than can be said for miners who dismiss what the rock shows them.

**THE WORKERS** in the foundries are the loudest and the most exhausted.

They are the human component of the industrial process, and the process does not care about them. The foundries run continuously—three shifts, eight hours each, the machinery never stopping, the ore carts arriving on their rails and departing on their rails and the smokestacks breathing their brownish smoke into the fog. The workers tend the furnaces, monitor the presses, clear the slag. They are strong in the specific way that foundry workers are strong—thick forearms, broad shoulders, a kind of structural integrity that comes from lifting heavy things in awkward positions for years. But they are also soft in ways they should not be. The damp gets into them. The constant cool temperature—twelve, fourteen degrees—seems to lower their body heat over time, until a foundry worker who has been here more than five years will feel warm to the touch in a way that is not quite fever but is not quite right either.

They live in the dormitories on the middle tiers—long barracks-style rooms with bunks for twelve, twenty, forty, depending on the tier. The bunks are metal frames with thin mattresses, and the mattresses are damp, because everything here is damp, because the condensation comes in through the walls and the windows and the ventilation shafts, and the fern tries to grow on the mattresses if they are not moved often enough. The workers develop habits: they flip their mattresses each morning, they shake out their blankets, they check the corners of their bunks for the pale mushrooms that fruit in any warm, wet place and which, if ingested, produce dreams that are not dreams but something else, something the medical officer calls *disassociative episodes* and which the workers call *going deep*.

Some of them go deep on purpose. They brew tea from the mushrooms—carefully, in small doses, using recipes passed from shift to shift—and they lie in their bunks and they listen. To what, they cannot say. The sound of the stone. The sound of the canyon forming. The sound that comes from the floor in the deepest part of the night. They say that if you listen while deep, you can understand the sound. You cannot remember what it said when you come back, but you remember that it made sense, that it was saying something specific, that it was speaking in a language that you almost know.

The foundry workers die younger than they should. Not from accidents, though there are accidents—falling into the slag, crushed by the presses, the ordinary catastrophes of industrial life. They die from something else. A kind of settling. As if their bodies, like the buildings, like the rock itself, are subject to forces that compress and distort them over time. Their spines curve. Their joints calcify in strange patterns. Their blood develops trace minerals that should not be there—cordite, in concentrations that have no business in a human body, that the medical officer cannot explain by reference to inhalation or ingestion or any known pathway of exposure. The cordite is in them. It is integrating itself into their tissue. The foundry workers are becoming, very slowly, something that is not quite human anymore, or which is human in the way that the city is a city—defined by its function, its process, its ongoing adaptation to conditions that were not in the original design.

**THE WOMEN AND MEN** of the maintenance crews fight the fern.

This is their war, and it is unwinnable, and they know this, and they fight anyway. They are the infantry of Verashalt, the ones who go into the abandoned tiers with scythes and torches and tanks of herbicide that the fern has learned to metabolize, that it actually seems to enjoy, its fronds greening visibly within hours of application. They cut and burn and scrape, and they clear a walkway, and they seal a doorway, and by the time they have returned to the start of their circuit, the fern has grown back. Not the same fern. New fern. Fern that was not there yesterday, that sprouted from spores that were not visible, that emerged from the mortar between the bricks as if the bricks themselves were generating it.

The maintenance crews are the ones who see the old tiers most clearly, and the old tiers are where the city is losing its battle with the vegetation. The abandoned tenements on the lower-middle tiers are not empty. They are full. Full of fern and moss and the pale mushrooms and the vine that taps the conduit and glows, and full of something else—a humidity, a warmth, a sense of occupancy that has nothing to do with people. The maintenance crews will not enter the old tiers alone. They go in pairs, or in groups of four, and they do not speak while they work, because speaking feels wrong in those green cavities, feels like interrupting something, like walking into a room where a conversation has just paused.

There is a woman on the maintenance crew named Brin who has been fighting the fern for eleven years, and who has developed, as a survival mechanism, a kind of respect for it. She does not cut it carelessly anymore. She cuts it precisely, selecting which fronds to remove and which to leave, and she has found—she does not tell her supervisor this—that the fern responds to precision. That if you cut it thoughtfully, in patterns that acknowledge its growth patterns, it grows back more slowly. That if you work with it rather than against it, you can reach a kind of truce, temporary and local and unstable, but real. Brin talks to the fern while she cuts it. Quietly, under her breath, words that are not quite encouragement and not quite apology. She does not know if the fern can hear her. She does not know if hearing is what plants do. But she has been here long enough to have stopped believing that she knows what anything in this canyon can or cannot do.

**THE CHILDREN** of Verashalt are different from the adults in a way that is difficult to articulate.

They were born here, most of them, or they arrived young enough that they do not remember any other place. This matters, because Verashalt is not like any other place, and the children have adapted to it in ways that the adults—who came from outside, who remember sunrise, who remember dry air and stable geometry—cannot. The children do not notice the wrongness. Or they notice it and do not consider it wrong. To them, the shifting distances between tiers are simply how distances work. The sounds in the stone are simply how stone sounds. The fern that grows toward the lights is simply how fern grows.

The children play on the catwalks in ways that terrify their parents. They lean over the railings. They run along the edges of the tiers. They climb the scaffolding that the engineers are always extending, scrambling up to places where the iron is new and the bolts are not yet tight, where the canyon wall is still soft and crumbles under their hands. They do not fall. This is the thing that the parents cannot understand: the children do not fall. They have a balance that is not normal, a spatial awareness that seems to account for the geometry of the canyon as if they can feel it, as if they are reading it through their feet, through their palms, through some sense that the adults have lost or never developed.

The children also play with the dwellers.

This is not supposed to happen. The dwellers—the lemur-like primates in the lower canyon—are shy, nocturnal, unnerving. The adults avoid them. The children seek them out. They leave food at the edges of the inhabited zone: scraps from the canteen, pieces of fruit, sometimes a piece of cordite ore that a miner parent has brought home and that the child has taken from the shelf. The dwellers come for the food. They come at dusk, when the fog is beginning to form and the sodium lamps are flickering on, and they sit at the edge of the tier and eat what the children have left, and the children sit across from them and watch.

The children are not afraid of the dwellers' eyes, that purple reflection that isn't quite right. They are not afraid of the dwellers' silence, the way the creatures watch without blinking, without moving, without making any sound at all. The children—some of them, the ones who have been here longest, whose bodies have had the most time to adapt—make sounds back. Not words. Not the cadence of human speech. Something else. Something that is closer to the sounds the dwellers make when they are alone, when they are performing their rituals with stones and pipes and wrenches in the fog below.

The parents discourage this. The parents pull their children inside at dusk and close the shutters and check the locks. But the children find ways. Children always find ways. And at night, in the dormitories, some of the children hum. They hum a single clear tone that is almost beautiful, that is almost music, that almost makes sense. They hum it in their sleep. They hum it with their eyes closed. They hum it in unison, all of them, the ones who have played with the dwellers and the ones who haven't, as if the sound is something they share, something that has been passed to them or drawn out of them by the place where they live.

The adults hear the humming. The adults lie in their damp bunks and listen to their children humming the sound that comes from the canyon floor, and they do not understand, and they are afraid, and they love their children, and they do not leave, because where would they go? This is the place where their children were born. This is the place where their children belong.

**THE OLD ONES** are the people who have been here longest.

Not Thade, who is twenty-six years into the canyon and counting. Not Pell, who is nineteen. The *old ones*—the workers' term, spoken quietly, with a respect that borders on unease—are the people who were here before the city. Before the mines. Before the engineers came with their measuring tapes and their plans and their bolts and their iron. There were people here when the canyon was first forming. There were people here when the rock was still deciding what it would be.

The old ones live on the highest tiers. Not the new tiers, the ones the engineers are building at the rim as the canyon erodes outward. The old tiers. The ones that were here first, before Verashalt was Verashalt, before there was a name or a purpose or a wage. These tiers are not made of brick and iron. They are made of the canyon itself—shelves of rock, natural ledges that were here before the city, that were occupied before the city, that will be here after the city if the city ever stops being built.

No one knows how many old ones there are. They do not interact with the city below. They do not come to the canteens or the dormitories or the foundries. They are visible, sometimes, from the upper catwalks—figures moving on the high ledges, smaller than the workers, thinner, with a density to their stillness that makes them look like they are part of the rock. They wear clothing that is not clothing: it is the moss, the lichen, the fibrous bark of some plant that grows only on the highest tiers, woven into shapes that cover without constraining. Their skin is the color of wet stone.

The old ones do not speak any language that the workers recognize. They do not seem to speak at all, to the workers' observation, but they communicate—they must, because they coordinate, because they move together, because they have been seen performing actions that require cooperation: moving rocks, redirecting the flow of tears, tending to the vegetation in ways that seem deliberate. They garden. This is the word that the workers use, when they talk about it, when they allow themselves to talk about it. The old ones garden the canyon. They shape the growth of the fern. They guide the water. They tend the strange purple lichen that appears only where the rock is newest.

And sometimes, in the early afternoon, when the sun grazes the upper tiers for that single hour, the old ones stand at the edge of their ledges and look down at the city below. They look at the smokestacks and the cable-cars and the foundries and the dormitories and the fern and the fog and the workers moving along the catwalks like cells moving through a body. They look at all of it, and their expressions are unreadable—not hostile, not welcoming, not sad, not curious. Something else. Something that the workers cannot name because the workers have never needed a word for it before. It is the expression of something that is watching a process that it is also part of, that it has always been part of, that will continue whether it watches or not.

The old ones are not the canyon. But they are not separate from the canyon either. They are something in between—a presence that emerged with the canyon, that is native to it in a way that the workers and the engineers and the miners can never be, that understands the canyon not as a problem to be solved but as a condition to be inhabited. They do not fight the fern. They do not measure the geometry. They do not document the anomalies or file reports or shore up foundations. They simply live here, in the way that the fern lives here and the dwellers live here and the sound lives here: as expressions of the place itself, as parts of what it is becoming.

**THE FOREMAN** is a man named Crel, and Crel is the one who keeps the city running.

He is not an engineer. He is not a miner. He is not management, though management thinks he is. Crel is something older than any of these categories: he is the person who makes sure the work happens, who resolves the disputes, who knows whose shift is whose and which tier is failing and where the water is flowing today and who is sick and who is missing and who has been taken by the settling—the term the workers use for the slow degeneration that is not quite disease and not quite aging and not quite anything that has a name.

Crel has been here twelve years. He is fifty-one. He looks sixty. His hands are huge and rough and always moving—tapping on railings, adjusting pipes, wiping condensation from surfaces that do not need to be wiped, as if he cannot stop maintaining, as if the city will fall apart if he stops touching it for even a moment. He knows everyone. He knows their names, their shifts, their bunk assignments, their injuries, their habits. He knows who is going deep on the mushroom tea and who is fighting the fern and whose child has been playing with the dwellers after dark. He does not approve, but he does not stop them either, because he has learned—slowly, against every instinct he has—that this place cannot be managed. It can only be inhabited.

Crel keeps a ledger. The ledger is a record of everything: ore output, water flow, worker rotations, structural failures, deaths, births, anomalies. He writes in it every night, after his shift, sitting in his small office on tier fifty, the foundry noise coming up through the floor, the fog pressing against the window, the fern already beginning to grow over the frame. He writes carefully, in a hand that was once neat and is now cramped and urgent, and he writes things that he should not write, things that would get him fired if the management read them, things like: *tier 23 distance to tier 24 measured 11.4 feet at 0600, 11.7 feet at 1400, 11.2 feet at 2200. cannot explain variation. rock feels different today. softer. listening.* And: *found another body in the cistern. not recognizable. not any animal i know. taking no action.* And: *the sound last night was louder. or closer. or both. the children were humming. the children were humming the same sound.*

Crel does not sleep. He catnaps, twenty minutes at a time, between entries in the ledger, between rounds of the tier, between the small crises that constitute his working life. He catnaps and he dreams, and his dreams are not dreams but something else—walks through the canyon when the fog is total, when the city is invisible and only the rock is real, when he can see the currents that Thade talks about, the slow rivers of something flowing through the stone, and in the dreams he is walking with the old ones, and the old ones are showing him something, a pattern, a shape, a way of building that does not fight the canyon but follows it, that does not resist the change but anticipates it, that does not bolt and stitch and shore but grows, the way the fern grows, the way the city would grow if the city were not afraid.

He wakes from these dreams and does not remember them. But his hands remember. Sometimes, when he is working—when he is directing the reinforcement of a failing wall, when he is routing a new pipe through a space that was not there yesterday—he will pause, and his hands will move in a pattern that is not the pattern the engineers specified, and the wall will hold, and the pipe will fit, and the space will stabilize, and Crel will not know why he did what he did, only that it worked.

**THE ABSENT** are the people who have left, or who have been taken.

There is a category of person in Verashalt that the workers do not discuss openly: the ones who went into the canyon and did not come back. Not the ones who fell—falls are common, falls are documented, falls are a known risk of vertical living and are accounted for in the wage. The absent are the ones who walked into the fog and simply stopped existing. The ones who went down to the lower tiers and were not found at shift change. The ones who were seen on a catwalk at dusk and not seen again, not ever, not even as a body in the cistern or a stain on the rock.

The absent are not dead. This is the belief that the workers hold, without evidence, against all evidence. They are not dead because there are no bodies. They are not dead because sometimes, in the fog, you can hear them. Not voices—never words—but footsteps on the catwalks, the specific sound of boots on iron grating, moving in a direction that no living person is moving. Or you see them at the edge of your vision, on a tier below or above, turning a corner that is not there, walking into a part of the canyon that does not exist on any map.

The absent are the ones who have been incorporated. This is the theory that the miners hold, that Thade has articulated, that no one wants to believe but everyone feels: the canyon is growing, and the city is growing, and the process of growth requires material, requires substance, requires something to become part of what is being built. The absent have not died. They have been *integrated*. They are in the rock now. They are in the fern. They are in the sound that comes from the canyon floor at the deepest part of the night. They have been absorbed into the process, and they are still here, and they are still participating, and they are not coming back.

The workers do not talk about the absent. But they count each other. They count each other constantly, at shift changes, at meal times, at the end of the day when the fog is thick and the sodium lamps are halos and the fern is rustling without wind and the sound is rising from below. They count each other because they need to know who is still here, who is still separate, who has not yet been called into the process that is producing this place, this city, this canyon, this reality that is still being born.

---

**THEY ALL HEAR IT.**

The sound. The one that comes from the canyon floor in the deepest part of the night, that is like singing or the memory of singing or the shape that singing would make if it were pressed into stone. They all hear it, and they do not talk about it, and they go on with their work—measuring and mining and maintaining and fighting and counting and humming—and the canyon goes on with its work—forming and shifting and weeping and growing and incorporating—and the city goes on with its work—bolting and stitching and shoring and abandoning and building—and the process continues, the slow becoming, the patient emergence, and the people of Verashalt are part of it, whether they know it or not, whether they want to be or not.

They are what lives here. They are the human part of the epithelium that is closing over the wound, that is making something new out of something raw. They are the part that thinks and measures and worries and counts and fights and loves and hums in its sleep. They are the part that will, if the process completes, become what the old ones already are: native, integrated, part of the place rather than separate from it, expressions of the canyon rather than inhabitants of it.

But the process has not completed. The canyon is still forming. The city is still being built. The people are still becoming. And birth is loud and wet and strange, and the thing being born is not what it will be, and the people who are being born with it do not know what they will be either, and they are afraid, and they are faithful, and they are here, and they are not leaving, because this is their home now, this raw damp loud green crowded empty mechanical alive half-real half-dreaming place that is still being born, and they are being born with it, and they will be what it becomes.
