To live here is to be co-extensive with stone. The people of this embryonic city do not merely inhabit the excavation; they are trapped in the same geologic equation, slowly carving their own voids in the packed density of the earth. They are the negative space made flesh, the living agents of the "slow, manual removal of the unnecessary."

They are a people defined by the boundary where raw rock meets the chisel. You see it in their bodies before you see their faces. They move with a hunched, asymmetrical gait, spines curved not from age but from a lifetime of pressing upward and inward against low ceilings of slate and packed clay. Their shoulders are elevated, perpetual shrugged against the damp chill, and their hands—are monstrous. They are hands that have been repurposed into organic wedges. The palms are calloused into dense, yellowed pads, thick as leather, insensitive to the coarse bite of unfinished stone. Their fingers are broad, flattened at the tips from gripping mattock handles and driving augers, the nails rimmed perpetually with the grey-white dust of compressed ash and mudstone. When they grip, it is with the blind, crushing force of things that must hold on to rock or fall.

Their clothing is a second skin of necessity, roughspun and dense, worn not for style but to act as a barrier against the weeping walls. Yet it is always damp. The cloth hangs heavy, clinging to the blades of the back and the pits of the arms, saturated with that steady, riverine humidity. The dye—if it ever existed—has long since washed away into a uniform palette of mineral-stained earth: the pale ochre of dried mudstone, the iron-rust blotches where they have leaned against oxidized fissures, and a generalized, pervasive grey that matches the bedrock. At their waists, they wear heavy belts laden not with metal—there is no refined industry here—but with the crude, heavy implements of their existence: stone-headed hammers with wrapped rawhide grips, wicker baskets woven tight enough to hold loose tailings, and chisels of sharpened flint or dense, basaltic glass that chip and dull with every strike, requiring constant, tedious re-knapping.

There are no faces of leisure here. In the diffuse, tallow-amber light, their features are hollowed out, shadows pooling in the deep sockets of their eyes and the trenches of their cheekbones. Their eyes are adapted to the gloom, large and pale, stripped of the need for bright sun; they do not look far, for there is no far to see—only the next few feet of stone, the next chisel mark, the next fault line. They are quiet people. Speech is a luxury of breath, and breath is a luxury in the thick, wet air that smells of struck stone and clay dust. When they communicate, it is through a terse, percussive language of knocks and gestures, a tap of the hammer handle against the wall to signal a crack, a flat hand waved to call for the bailers. They are becoming part of the city's hum, just another layer in the aggregate dynamic, their voices subsumed by the *tak-tak-tak* and the basso *crack*.

They work in a rhythm dictated not by the sun—there is no sun—but by the exhaustion of the body and the resistance of the rock. The labor is organized with a grim, municipal logic. There are those who seek the fault lines, running calloused fingers over the bedrock, reading the subtle textures of the stone to find where it will yield. There are those who work the fire-quench cycle, tending the shallow, blackened craters where brush is burned against the stone, their faces briefly illuminated by the orange glow before they dash cold water from the pooled hollows, flinching as the rock snaps and shrieks. And there are the haulers, the silent, endless column of them, dragging wicker baskets of loose tailings along the carved roads, bent double under the weight of the displaced mountain, their feet finding the grooves of the stone picks in the floor as if following tramlines.

For them, there is no concept of "finished." They do not build a home and then dwell in it; the dwelling is the act of chipping away the not-home. The doorway they rough out today is merely the promise of a threshold; tomorrow, they will widen it, and the next day, they will deepen the chamber beyond, sleeping in the half-carved hollows they have won, their bodies pressed against the fresh, scored walls while the water weeps onto their roughspun blankets. They are a people mid-birth, holding the chisel at the very moment the mountain becomes a city, frozen in the breathless pause between geology and hearth, enduring the immense, packed density of the world above them by patiently, stubbornly refusing it.
