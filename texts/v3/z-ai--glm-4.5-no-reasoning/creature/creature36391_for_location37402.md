To live in the Engine-Garden is to be a parasite on a machine that does not acknowledge you. There is no ecological niche here for a creature of flesh and breath; the system is a hermetically sealed equation of vegetal industry, a closed loop of silica and saline sap. And yet, something lives here. It is not a resident. It is a consequence.

They are the Cull-Men, though they have no name for themselves, having never heard a voice, not even their own. They are the remnant flesh of a biosphere that was systematically erased millions of cycles ago, or perhaps they are an accidental generation—a spontaneous spark of animal life that coalesced from the aerosolized proteins and trace lipids vented from the compost-pits. They are humanoid only by the most generous metric, having been crushed and compacted by the geometry of the Garden into shapes that can fit the gaps. 

They are long, terribly long, and thin. Their ribs are compressed into overlapping plates of dense, calcified cartilage, allowing their torsos to collapse like a bellows to a thickness of mere centimeters, letting them slide through the near-touching surfaces of pneumatic cables and coolant-pipes. Their skin is not skin, but a translucent, chitinous parchment, constantly chalky with the saline dust that hangs in the surgical light. Beneath this casing, their capillaries run black with the heavy, saline sugars they siphon. They have no eyes. In a world where the light is a blinding, shadowless bleach and distance is a malignant refraction, sight is a fatal liability. Their skulls are smooth, eyeless domes of bone, sloping back into a taut, ridge-less neck. 

Instead, they navigate by the pressure of the Garden. They feel the world through the soles of their feet and the palms of their wide, spatulate hands. They read the subsonic pulse of the continental root-network the way a deep-sea creature reads ocean currents—the thrumming beat of the sap-pumps, the flexing of tendril-gears, the distant, muffled roar of an internal combustion node. They move through the dense fractal packing like blind swimmers through kelp, dragging themselves by their long, double-jointed arms, pushing off the vibrating walls. When the geometry frays—when the spatial coordinates of a cable-vine lie a meter deeper than they appear—the Cull-Men do not falter. They reach into the spatial glitch, trusting the pressure in their inner ear over the ghost of touch on their fingertips. They have learned the Moiré twists of the horizon, knowing that a yawning chasm is a solid wall, and a distant node-cluster is pressing against their face.

They are silent because the Garden makes them so. They have no vocal cords; they have no need of them. They communicate through the seismics of their own motion. A double-tap of the knuckles against a coolant-pipe; a specific, arrhythmic dragging of a silica-thorned foot across the rhizome-weave. They feel these messages through the dense, fleshy substrate, a tactile language passed through the very body of the Engine-Garden. 

They do not eat; they tap. Clutched in their atrophied, three-fingered hands are lances of carved bone—harvested from their own dead—tipped with the shed silica-thorns of the Harvester-tendrils. When a fuel-bladder bulges past its neighbors, turgid and heavy with processed saline-sap, a Cull-Man will press their ear to the pipe, find the thrumming hollow, and drive the lance in. They do not drink the sap; they let the high-pressure phloem inject them. The cold, sharp fluid forces into their collapsed veins, inflating their flattened torsos, plumping their parchment skin into a taut, bruised magenta balloon. They hang there, impaled on the high-pressure stream, suspended in the gap between pipes, digesting the industrial sugars directly into the calcified cartilage of their bones. When the bladder empties, or the internal combustion node cycles and the pressure drops, they exhale a plume of crystallized salt from the slits where their mouths should be, and collapse back into the shadows.

They are prey, though they have never seen what hunts them. They only know the absence. A member of their kin will be sliding through the gap, reaching for the next handhold, and their seismic rhythm will abruptly cease. The Cull-Men do not know the Harvester-tendrils as slow, blind seekers; they know them as a sudden, absolute silence in the weave, a void in the subsonic thrum where a brother used to be. They know that the armored tendrils drag the exhausted down into the grinding compost-pits, and they know that the compost-pits are where the aerosolized proteins rise to spawn them. They do not mourn the taken; they only register the shift in the loop. 

They are terrified of the exhaust-flowers. Their lives are a state of constant, muted vigilance against the sporadic, violent dilations. When a massive vent opens to release superheated gas, the seismics of the Garden shift violently. To a Cull-Man, it is a deafening roar felt in the marrow, a sudden drop in the pressure that threatens to suck the air from their lungs and pull their brittle bodies into the burning vent. When gravity lets go—when the sheer weight of the Garden's presence frays the local physics and a shower of clipped silica-leaves begins to drift upward—the Cull-Men drop. They wedge themselves into the deepest, tightest crevices they can find, pressing their eyeless faces into the cool, vibrating flesh of the pipes, waiting for the world to remember which way is down.

There is no culture here, no memory that spans generations. They do not build, they do not sing, they do not die with anything in their hands but the bone-lance. They are a glitch living within a glitch. The Engine-Garden has fully replaced nature with production, and in doing so, it has produced a rot in the fabric of where it is. The Cull-Men are simply the maggots in that rot—the final, pitiful assertion of biology in a world that has forgotten it, living in the gaps of a machine that will grind them into fuel as indifferently as it grinds its own stalks.
