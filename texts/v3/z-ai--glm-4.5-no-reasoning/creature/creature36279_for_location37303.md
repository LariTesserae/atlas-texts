To live in the Bower is to be an error in a perfect equation.

Whatever lives here is not of the architecture. It cannot be, because the architecture is closed—sealed in a thermodynamic ring of water weeping, falling, running, and evaporating. The Bower is a mechanism that has forgotten it is alive, and to live beside it is to be the only thing that remembers. 

If there is fauna here, it is reduced to a ghost of anatomy. The pale, eyeless oligochaetes wriggling in the dark pipes are not really living in the way we understand life; they are mere biological smears, extensions of the water-channel itself, blind threads of peristalsis doing nothing but enduring the current. They do not eat. They do not excrete. They are caught in the same stasis as the green mechanism around them, reduced to a single, endless reflex of gripping and releasing, gripping and releasing, as the water rushes past their translucent skin. They are less than worms; they are living grout, holding the capillary cracks shut with their microscopic bodies. They do not know the roar of the water. They only feel the vibration, a thrumming that has been shaking their cells since they hatched and will shake them until their tissue dissolves into the flow.

But if something else lives here—something larger, something that walks—then its existence is a solitary, suffocating tragedy. 

Imagine a thing moving through the mist. It walks the petiole-bridges wide enough for six, yet it walks alone. Its footsteps are damp, silent thuds against the waxy leaf-planes, immediately swallowed by the polyphonic roar of the water. It breathes the heavy, saturated air, pulling the condensation into its lungs, feeling the intimate sweat of the landscape on its skin. It is warm here, but it is the warmth of a greenhouse left sealed for a thousand years. The creature’s coat—if it has one—is perpetually plastered to its sides. Water beads on its brow, runs down the bridge of its nose, drips from its chin in the exact same way the water drips from the central veins of the architectural leaves. It cannot tell where the weeping of the Bower ends and its own perspiration begins.

It has no predators. It has no prey. It does not graze, because the Bower does not grow; there are no tender shoots, no succulent tips, only the immutable, engineered geometry of adult structures locked in embryonic stasis. If it eats, it eats the only things available: the pale worms in the pipes, crunching them up by the hundreds to feel a faint, watery slime slide down its throat, or it gnaws blindly at the bark and the lignified stalks, wearing down its teeth against a world that was never meant to be chewed. Its hunger is an asymmetry. The Bower does not hunger. The Bower only flows.

To be a changing thing in a static world is to be an open wound. The creature sheds hair. It leaves behind a flake of keratin, a drop of spittle, a footprint that scuffs the waxy cuticle of a leaf. These traces are anomalies. The Bower does not consume them. There are no decomposers here, no fungi, no beetles to carry the flesh back to the soil. There is no soil. The creature’s cast-offs will lie where they fall, trapped on a leaf-plane or swept into a root-channel, tumbling forever in the dark pipes—a foreign artifact in a plumbing system that has no filter for death. It is a contaminant in a machine that runs, immaculately, on sap and cellulose and gravity.

The creature must navigate the loneliness of an empty metropolis. The structures are massive, scattered, built for a population that does not exist. It crosses the spiraling rhizome-causeways, looking out over the haze and the distant, hazy silhouettes of the next living buttresses. It seeks something—shade, a dry hollow, a mate, a reflection of its own mutability—but finds only the greenish, bottle-glass glow. The shadows do not sharpen; they fill with fog. It cannot mark time by the sun, because the light is a diffuse, internal luminescence. It cannot mark time by the growth of the trees, because nothing grows. It can only mark time by the exhaustion of its own body, the slow decay of its cells, the way its breath comes harder after a long walk across a bridge. It is the only clock in a world without seconds.

The roar of the place is a madness that it has evolved to endure. The sound is immense, a low, resonant thrum loud enough to feel in the sternum. The creature does not speak. There is nothing to hear it, and even if there were, the sound would be swallowed instantly. It communicates in nothing. It has no calls, no territorial songs, no mating cries. Its mind—if it has one—has turned inward, echoing only its own thoughts, because the external world offers no dialogue, only the unending drone of a pipe organ played by gravity. The silence of its own species is forced upon it by the thunder of the water.

If it sleeps, it sleeps curled against the hexagonal lattice of a trunk-wall, feeling the water pump without a pump beside its ribs. It wakes to find the exact same drop of water falling from the exact same leaf-tip, hitting the exact same root-channel with the exact same crystalline plink. Yesterday, today, in a century. The creature knows, in the deep, ancestral way that animals know futility, that it will never leave a mark on this place. When it dies, its body will fall across a catenary arch, and the water will run over it, and the condensation will bead on its downward-facing surfaces, and slowly—over eras that the Bower will not notice—its bones will dissolve into the weeping green, becoming part of the closed hydrological loop, a tiny spike of calcium and phosphate flowing through the dark pipes, an infinitesimal defect in the latticework, until even that is ground away to nothing.

To live in the Bower is to be the only thing that breathes in a lung that does not need air.
