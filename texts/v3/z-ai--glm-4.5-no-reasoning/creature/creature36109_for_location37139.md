To live here is to live without reference. There is no sun to mark a day, no season to measure a year, no shadow that lengthens to signal an end. The creatures of the deep hollow are not inhabitants in the way surface beasts are; they are a condition of the stone, an extension of the weeping walls and the razor-backed ridges. They are the living mortar of a world that has never known a builder.

Consider the isopods. They are the floor. They are the loam. They are the dark, shifting earth itself. To look down is to witness the ground breathe. They are pale—not the pallor of sickness, but the translucence of things that have never been touched by a wavelength of light that matters. Their shells are the color of the damp shale, a wet, grayish-white that gleams like polished bone in the perpetual twilight. They are eyeless, but they are not blind. Their bodies are dense with chemo-sensory bristles, with sub-chordal nodes of pressure detection that read the world as a topography of humidity and vibration. To them, the weeping walls are a roaring source of life; the shallow, still pans of water are vast, warm seas. They feel the droplets falling as a constant, gentle percussion, a rhythm that is their heartbeat, their metronome, their god.

They move in a way that denies individuality. A single isopod, separated from its kin, is a pathetic, twitching thing—a severed nerve ending, a lost word. But they are never separated. They exist in a state of perpetual, crushing proximity. The mounds that cluster around the water pans are not colonies; they are singularities. They are mountains of chitin and soft, pale underbelly, climbing over one another in an endless, desperate attempt to reach the moisture, to molt, to breed, to die. The living use the dead as bridges. The newly molted, soft and vulnerable, are buried and protected by the sheer mass of their armored brethren. They eat the refuse of the stone, the microscopic detritus that falls from the slowly eroding rock, and they eat each other with a mechanical indifference. There is no malice in their consumption, no hunger in the way we understand it. It is a transaction. Biomass becomes biomass. The shell becomes the stone; the stone becomes the shell.

And above them, the moths.

If the isopods are the flesh of the cavern, the moths are its breath. They are the atmosphere made animal. They fill the space between the spires, the vast tracts of open, uncluttered void, giving it a density that the cool air alone could not possess. They are smaller than the isopods, frail, impossible things. Their wings are a dusty, charcoal gray, frayed at the edges, seeming like fragments of the blue-gray gloom given locomotion. They navigate not by sight, but by the temperature gradients in the air—the slow, imperceptible thermals that rise from the massed bodies of the isopods below and sink from the cold, wet stone above. They are drawn to the humidity, to the warmth of life, clouding over the water pans like a living fog, their wings brushing together in that constant, dry whisper—a high, papery counterpoint to the wet, heavy clicking of the floor.

They do not rest. They cannot. The ground is a sea of armored mouths; to settle is to be consumed in an instant, torn apart by a thousand thumbnail-sized mandibles. So they fly, forever. They breed in the air, the females dropping egg cases into the squirming masses below, a parasitic offering that the isopods will carry, spread, and inadvertently incubate in the damp crevices. The moths are the hunters and the hunted, the sky and the prey. They drink the condensation from each other's wings in mid-flight, a fleeting, desperate kiss in the endless dark.

The pale fungi and the thick, leathery moss are the only stillness, the only patience in a world that knows no patience. They are the anchors. The calcified webs of tissue that cling to the weeping crevices are not plants; they are slow-motion predators. Their threads reach into the dark, waiting for the moths, waiting for the stray isopod that climbs too high and loses its footing. When the prey touches the lattice, it is not ensnared—it is dissolved. The fungi exude a thin, clear enzyme that breaks down chitin and protein over days, over weeks. The struggling creature is held in place by the sheer density of the web, its movements slowing as its joints are liquefied, until it is nothing but a hollow shell, a wet, empty carapace that the fungus drinks at its leisure. The leathery mosses do the same, their surfaces a microscopic nightmare of acidic pits and slow, digesting fibers. They are the geography of consumption, the sharp edges of the stone made hungry.

The dynamic of this world is not one of drama. There are no great hunts, no dramatic kills, no territorial disputes. The isopod does not flee from its predator; it simply moves over it, or under it, or through it. The moth does not evade the fungus; it simply has not yet touched it. Everything is happening at once, and nothing is happening at all. The billions of creatures exist in a state of perfect, grinding equilibrium. The sound they make—the deafening, unvarying drone—is the sound of a system in absolute balance. It is the sound of a machine running at idle, a cosmic engine with no load. The clattering of infinite chitin, the wet squelching, the dry whisper of wings—these are not the sounds of life struggling to survive. They are the sounds of life *persisting*, of a closed loop spinning endlessly on its axis.

This is what lives in the deep hollows: a single, vast organism made of billions of parts, a living ore that veins the dark stone. It is a creature with a shell of shale and basalt, with a circulatory system of weeping water and shallow pans, with a metabolism of chitin and wing and pale, calcified web. Its breath is the drone, its heartbeat the drip. It does not think. It does not feel. It does not age. It simply *is*, held forever in the sharp-edged dark, a teeming, deafening breath that will never be exhaled.
