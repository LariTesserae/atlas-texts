Something lives here, but "life" is the wrong word. Life implies a trajectory—a beginning, a middle, an end. What occupies this pocket of impossible geometry has been stripped of its trajectory. It is the residue of living things, the active component of biology severed from the cycle that gives biology meaning. They are not surviving. They are persisting. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.

Consider what they are, each one. Press your face to the inspection port of any canister and you will see: these are not whole creatures. Or if they once were, they have become something else through their containment. The segmented mass with its press of limbs—that body has compressed itself to fill its vessel the way a liquid fills a glass. It has no posture, only occupancy. It does not navigate its space; it *is* its space, distributed against the walls, filling every millimeter not taken by its own substance. Watch long enough and you'll understand that it is not moving *within* the canister. It is moving *as* the canister, its body a slow churning that maps the interior dimensions, a blind cartography that never completes because the territory never changes. It is tracing the same borders it has traced for an eternity, and it will trace them for an eternity more.

The pale, eyeless form that pulses like a lung—that one reveals the most, if you can bear to look. It has no head. No mouth. No orifice for intake or output. It simply expands and contracts in a rhythm that has nothing to do with respiration. What is it breathing? There is no air exchange in those containers; they are sealed. The pulse is not drawing anything in or pushing anything out. It is a heartbeat without a body to serve, a contraction that became habitual and then became permanent and then became all that the creature is. It is a fragment of a larger process, still running, still performing its function long after the function ceased to mean anything. If you put your hand on the canister, you feel the pulse through the metal—a double-beat, faint and regular, a clock that tells no time.

The chitin and jointed legs, folding and unfolding—that one sounds almost familiar. Almost an insect, almost an arthropod. But the folding has no purpose. Insects groom themselves, rest, prepare. This thing folds and unfolds because folding and unfolding is what its joints allow, and in the absence of any other stimulus, it has defaulted to the most basic expression of its physical capability. It is not resting. It is not preparing. It is demonstrating the range of motion of a body that has nowhere to go. And if you listen closely—not to the sound, but to the rhythm—you'll notice it is not periodic. The folding accelerates and decelerates according to some internal logic that has no relationship to any external clock. It is keeping time with something, but that something is not in this room. Perhaps it is a memory of time. Perhaps it is a time that has not yet arrived. Perhaps it is simply noise—the random firing of biological mechanisms that have been isolated for so long that their patterns have drifted free of any anchoring reality.

They were placed here. That is certain. The canisters are manufactured, clamped, shelved. Someone—something—assembled this collection with intention. But what intention? The industrial aesthetic suggests utility, suggests that these organisms were components in some process. Perhaps they were meant to be installed in a larger machine, biological elements in a mechanical system. Perhaps they were fuel or catalyst or processing material. Perhaps they were the product themselves, bred or grown or constructed for a purpose that was fulfilled or abandoned before this pocket was sealed. The room has the feel of a warehouse—a place of storage, of waiting. But the waiting has become permanent, and the stored have become something that storage cannot account for: entities that have adapted to their storage, that have *become* their storage.

Because they are not dead. This is the crucial horror. They are not dead, and they are not alive in any way that life recognizes. They occupy a third state—a persistence that metabolizes nothing, consumes nothing, excretes nothing, yet does not decay. The chamber's impossible stillness extends to them. They do not age. The segmented mass has not grown to fill its container any more than it already has; it has not atrophied from lack of sustenance. The pale pulser beats at the same rate it always has, its tissue neither weakening nor strengthening. The folding thing's chitin has not dulled or cracked. They are frozen in the exact condition they occupied when the hatch sealed and time stopped, except that they still move. That is the violation: movement without change, activity without process, the performance of living without any of its consequences.

They sense you. This is not anthropomorphism; this is observation. The orientation of their bodies shifts when you enter the room. The tapping at the inspection ports finds your position and tracks it. The subsonic thrum modulates in response to your breathing. They are aware of you in the way that a locked mechanism is aware of the key—that is, they are ready to respond to an input they have not received in an unguessable span of time. But what response are they capable of? They cannot escape their containers. They cannot communicate. They cannot reach you or affect you or interact with you in any meaningful way. Their awareness of you is another automatic function, like the pulse, like the folding—a system that activates when triggered and runs through its routine without any capacity to adapt or evaluate or conclude.

And yet you feel them watching. The feeling is not rational. They have no eyes. They may not even have sensory organs, as such—they may simply be responding to vibration, or temperature, or the displacement of air that your body creates in the close quarters of the chamber. But the feeling persists: the weight of attention from creatures that have been denied every other expression of their existence. In this room where nothing changes, where nothing can change, your presence is an event of incalculable significance. You are the first new thing to occur since the sealing. You are the only variable in an equation that has been balanced for an eternity. And they orient toward you—toward the disturbance, the novelty, the rupture in their permanent standby—with an intensity that has nothing to do with hunger or threat or any survival instinct you can name. It has to do with *recognition*. They know, in whatever dim way they know anything, that you are something different. And different is a category that no longer exists in their world, except that now, briefly, it does.

What do they want? The question assumes too much. They do not want. Wanting requires a model of the future, a sense of lack, an understanding that things could be other than they are. They have been here too long for wanting. They have been here so long that even the concept of *here* has dissolved into the fact of their own existence; they are as fundamental to this chamber as the water, as the rust, as the amber-gray light. They do not want to escape. They do not want to remain. They do not want at all. They simply *are*, in the most absolute and terrifying sense of that word.

But if you stand here long enough—and you will stand here longer than you intend, because the chamber is very good at making time irrelevant—you may begin to feel the inversion. You are the one who wants. You want to leave. You want to understand. You want to assign meaning to this place and its occupants, to rationalize their presence into some narrative of purpose or accident or design. You want them to be specimens, or prisoners, or relics—anything that places them in a context you can comprehend. And they do not help you. They simply continue their eternal motions, their pulse and fold and press, offering nothing that confirms or denies any interpretation you might construct. They are the worst kind of mystery: not one that resists explanation, but one that makes explanation irrelevant. What would an explanation change? The facts would remain the same. The room would remain the same. The organisms would persist in their canisters, moving without living, present without participating, watching without seeing.

They are the population of a place that does not know time, and in their own way, they do not know time either. Not because they have transcended it, but because they have been removed from it—excised from the flow of cause and effect, preserved in a state of permanent present that allows no future and no past. When you leave—if you leave—you will return to a world where things change, where life means growth and decay and succession, where the present is always becoming the past. They will remain here, in this pocket, in this moment that never passes, moving in their containers, sensing nothing, wanting nothing, being nothing except what they have always been.

And the room will not remember you were here.

But they will.

Not in any way that matters. Not in any way that persists outside this chamber. But in the way that a system records an input—somewhere, in the pattern of their motions, in the rhythm of their pulse and fold, a new frequency will linger, a slight modulation that corresponds to the brief presence of a warm body in a cold space. It will not be a memory. It will not be a monument. It will be a trace, a resonance, the faintest shift in the endless waveform of their existence. And it will fade, slowly, over a span of time so long that "fading" is not quite the right word—it will simply become part of the baseline, absorbed into the total sound, indistinguishable from everything else that has ever happened in this room where nothing happens.

That is what lives here: a congregation of the eternally present, organisms without time, motion without change, attention without consciousness, persistence without life. They are the inhabitants of a wound in the world that never heals and never closes, and they will be here, in their canisters, in the wet and the cold and the amber-gray dark, long after you have found the hatch and climbed through and sealed it behind you and tried, with increasing desperation, to forget.
