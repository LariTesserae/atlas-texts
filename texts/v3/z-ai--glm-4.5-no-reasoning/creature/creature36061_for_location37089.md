To live here is to live at the pace of stone.

The people of this place—if "people" is the right word, and it is only partially right—do not inhabit the city in the way that citizens inhabit their capitals. They do not commute, do not construct, do not dismantle. They persist. They are tall, these residents, and narrow in a way that seems not emaciated but *efficient*, as though their bodies have discarded any mass not strictly necessary for the work of being present. Their skin is the color of the dim light: a warm, sourceless grey that shifts slightly depending on what they stand near. Near the groves, they carry a faint green undertone; near the young structures, the gradient of the architecture reflects off them, darkening their lower halves so they seem to be wading through a shadow of their own making.

They do not build because the building happens without them. They do not farm because the vegetation offers what it offers without cultivation. What they do is *attend*.

There is a practice here—though calling it a practice implies more formality than it carries, more intention. It might be better called a posture, or a frequency. The residents spend long periods standing still in the open spaces between complexes, faces tilted toward the obscured sky, or pressed against the gradient surfaces of the buildings, or buried to the wrist in the dark loam beneath the silver-barked trees. They are listening. Not with their ears—the subsonic hum is felt, not heard, and they feel it the way you might feel a change in barometric pressure, as a shift in the interior weather of the body. They are listening with their chemistry. The city speaks in slow accretions, in mineral gradients, in the electromagnetic fields that seed new growth, and the residents have evolved—or trained, or been shaped—to resonate with those signals.

Their hands are their most expressive feature. The fingers are long, disproportionately so, and they end not in nails but in a slightly translucent keratin that flexes with the humidity, hardening when the air is dry and softening when the condensation comes. The residents use these fingers to read the city. They trail them along walls and feel the stages of calcification, the fibrousness of immature construction, the dense imperviousness of the old quarters. They press their palms to the bark of the trees and feel the pulse of photosynthesis beneath the smooth silver-grey surface, the sugars moving, the roots extending. They hold their hands flat over the exposed bedrock in the open intervals and feel the heat of the young land, the cooling, the settling.

A visitor might mistake them for statues, these still figures in the dim warm light. But they are not still. They are moving at the speed of their environment. A human eye, adjusted to the frenetic tempo of combustion and commerce, sees only standing. But over the course of a morning—which here is a temporal designation unmarked by any solar event—a resident will shift their weight from one foot to the other, will move their hand three centimeters along a wall, will turn their head slowly to track the path of one of the flying maintenance fragments as it traces its precise loop. They are slow because the information they process is slow. The city grows a new chamber in a generation; to perceive this, one must attend for a generation.

There are children here. This is important to understand: this is not a terminal population, not the last remnants of something fading. The children are born in the groves, among the dark-leaved plants and the ozone-sugar humus, and they emerge already partially integrated into the system. Their first instinct is not to cry but to be quiet, to listen, to feel the hum. They are small and soft and their skin has a luminous quality—the embedded phosphorescence of the building material seems to migrate into them during gestation, or perhaps they inherit it from the environment in some epigenetic exchange that bypasses ordinary heredity. They glow faintly in the dimness, these children, like the interiors of the complexes, like pages left open.

The children do not play as human children play. They practice. They spread their fingers against the surfaces of young walls and concentrate, as though willing the architecture to grow toward them, to shape itself around their hands. Sometimes it does. Sometimes a small indentation forms in a developing wall, a negative space that matches a child's palm, and the child removes their hand and looks at it with an expression that is not surprise but *confirmation*. They are learning to participate in the growth, not by imposing form but by being present at the moment form is decided.

The residents do not speak among themselves in any way that produces sound. Their communication is subtler and more distributed: a shift in posture that conveys agreement, a particular angle of the head that signals attention, a synchronization of breathing that indicates shared perception. When several stand together in an open space, their bodies unconsciously arrange themselves in patterns that mirror the lattice of the city—equidistant, geometric, facing the nearest complexes at angles that correspond to the growth field lines. They do not plan this. It happens the way iron filings arrange around a magnet, the way crystals seed in a saturated solution.

But they do communicate with the city, and with the vegetation, and here sound is sometimes involved—though "sound" overstates it. There are vocalizations: low, resonant tones that emerge from the throat and seem to originate not from the breath but from the hum itself, as though the resident is allowing the subsonic frequency of the bedrock to pass through them and become briefly audible. These tones are not language. They do not carry semantic content. They carry, instead, a kind of *appetite*—a request for acceleration, or deceleration, for densification or expansion. A resident may stand before a wall that is calcifying too slowly and produce a tone that makes the fibrous surface shiver and compress. Another may sing to a tree whose leaves have curled against an unusual dryness, and the leaves will open, the stomata widening, the plant drinking more deeply of the ambient moisture.

This is not magic. This is not even technology in any sense that implies separation between tool and user. The residents are, in the most literal way, *part of the negotiation*. They are the component of the system that provides feedback. The city grows; the vegetation responds; the bedrock settles; and the residents *tell them how it feels*. They are the sensory apparatus of a city that has no central nervous system, distributed observers who return information about temperature and moisture and light, who mark the boundaries between the built and the grown, who remember where a path was last season and notice that the architecture has extended itself across it now, who feel the roots thickening beneath their feet and adjust their routes accordingly.

They do not own anything. The concept of ownership requires a separation between owner and owned that does not exist here. The clothes they wear—if they wear clothes at all—are not garments but extensions of their skin, grown from the same precursor material as the buildings, seeded by electromagnetic fields their bodies generate. These coverings shift color with the gradient architecture, darken at the hem, lighten at the shoulder, and they respond to touch the same way the walls do, with a faint awareness that is not consciousness but something adjacent to it: *receptivity*. When a resident enters a grove, their covering develops a slight texture, a fine nap that holds moisture, adapting them to the tropical air. When they cross an open interval, it smooths and tightens, conserving heat against the cooler expanse of exposed bedrock.

They eat, but rarely, and never with urgency. The vegetation produces fruits—not recognizable fruits, not apples or figs, but dense, dark nodules that form at the intersection of root and architecture, where the two systems exchange nutrients. These nodules are warm when picked, and they soften in the hand, and they taste of mineral and sugar and something almost metallic that might be the iron in the young bedrock or might be something else entirely, something that corresponds to no element on any periodic table assembled by human science. A single nodule sustains a resident for weeks. Their metabolisms run at the same pace as their surroundings—slow, efficient, transforming energy without urgency, without heat, without waste.

There are elders among them, though the distinction is not one of age in the linear sense. It is a distinction of *integration*. The elders are those who have been present long enough—many generations, measured in the city's terms—that the architecture has grown around them, has incorporated their patterns of movement into its own extensions. An elder's dwelling is not a room they chose but a chamber that *formed around them*, the building material elaborating itself into walls and passages that mirror the paths the elder has walked for decades. To enter an elder's space is to enter a three-dimensional record of their life: the ceiling is higher where they have stood most often, the walls curve toward the places they have touched, and the phosphorescent channels in the floor trace patterns that match the elder's circadian rhythms, glowing brighter during their periods of activity and dimming when they rest.

The elders do not move much anymore. They sit—on surfaces that have grown to support them, in positions that the architecture has learned to expect—and they listen. They listen to the full frequency of the system: the bedrock's cooling, the construction's crystallization, the vegetation's photosynthesis, the moisture's collection, the children's slow play. They hold the hum in their bodies and they allow it to pass through them, and in doing so they become less distinct as individuals and more like nodes—points where the system concentrates its awareness, where the feedback loops close, where the city experiences itself.

Death, when it comes, is not a disruption. A resident who has ceased to attend does not leave a body in the way that human bodies remain—static, decaying, separate. The precursor material that seeded their coverings begins, in the absence of the electromagnetic field that animated them, to crystallize. Over the course of days, the form stiffens, densifies, calcifies. Over the course of months, it becomes indistinguishable from the architecture. Over the course of years, it extends—new chambers forming around the mineralized remains, new passages, new surfaces. The dead here do not return to the earth. They return to the city. They become the city. And in becoming the city, they persist in its processes, its slow growth, its continuous becoming.

This is why the children are not afraid of death. This is why no one mourns, or not in the way that mourning requires absence. The dead are still present—they are simply present at a different phase, operating at a different register. A child who presses her hand against a wall that grew from her grandmother's remains feels the same resonance she felt when her grandmother pressed her hand against a wall that grew from *her* grandmother's remains. The lineage is not broken; it is *incorporated*. The city is, among other things, a necropolis—but a living one, a growing one, a place where the boundary between the living and the dead is as blurred and negotiable as the boundary between the built and the grown.

And so the residents attend, and the city grows, and the vegetation erupts, and the bedrock settles, and the condensation forms and runs and collects, and the dim light persists, and the hum continues below hearing, below thought, and the children spread their hands against young walls and feel them harden, and the elders sit and listen and become indistinguishable from the architecture that supports them, and the flying fragments trace their loops and the moisture falls with intention and the roots extend around the foundations and the foundations extend around the roots and everything is becoming, everything is always becoming, and the residents are becoming too—becoming more integrated, more dispersed, more like the system that sustains them and less like the separate selves they might once have been or might never have been at all.

There is room here. There is so much room. Room for more residents, room for more groves, room for more complexes, room for more children, room for more dead, room for more becoming. The city is young. The residents are young. The land beneath them is young. And they are all—at the pace of stone, at the pace of growth, at the pace of the subsonic hum that is felt but never heard—*becoming*.
