To live in Marrowmire is to cease being a vertebrate in any functional sense. You do not reside here so much as you are colonized, incorporated, digested into the rhythm of the place. The people who remain—if *people* is still the right word, and some of them doubt it—are the ones who could not find an angle of withdrawal, and so found an angle of integration instead.

They are few. Perhaps a dozen, perhaps fifteen. They do not gather or count themselves because counting requires a boundary between self and other, and that boundary went soft long ago. They live inside the pump-housings, in the warm dry pockets where the iron boilers radiate into the brick alcoves and the root-mass has grown around the doorframes like a patient jaw. They do not build shelters; they occupy the shelters the district provides, sleeping on moss that grows faster than they can wear it away, waking to the percussive drip of condensation striking the tin overhead—*pock, pock, pock*—which serves as their only clock.

Their skin tells the story of adaptation. It is always wet. Not with the sudden sweat of exertion but with the permanent glaze of ambient moisture, a film that never fully evaporates in air so thick. After enough months—no one remembers the threshold exactly—the skin changes. It softens, loses its keratin rigor, becomes permeable in ways that medical texts would call maceration but which the residents experience as a kind of opening. They can feel the humidity shift through their pores, can sense the thermals from the steam-vents as changes in pressure against their surface rather than as warmth. Their feet, constantly submerged, have gone pale and broad, the toes splayed to grip root-cables that serve as the only stable footing in the amber pools. Calluses do not form; the water dissolves them faster than friction can build them. Instead, the soles develop a strange suppleness, a sensitivity that reads the vibration of the pump-arms through the root-mass, so that a resident standing still can tell you which boiler is cycling faster today, which bleed-valve has partially stuck, which water-wheel is laboring under a mat of duckweed.

They do not wear clothes in any conventional sense. Fabric rots here in weeks, and leather stiffens, cracks, grows a fur of mold that becomes its own texture. What they drape themselves in are the district's offerings: broad bracket-fungi, harvested from the root-walls and still supple with moisture, worn like shoulder-plates or aprons; marsh-grass woven in slow hours into rough mantles that degrade as they're worn, shedding fibers into the water to be caught by the root-tangles and incorporated into new growth; moss peeled in sheets from idle pipes and draped across the shoulders, still alive, still respiring, so that the wearer carries a garden on their back. Some of them paint themselves—or are painted by the district—with the iron-oxide paste that leaches from the corroded stacks and pools in certain hollows. It stains the skin a deep ochre that sets into the pores and will not wash out, marking them as part of the mineral substrate. After enough years, they look like something grown rather than born: ochre-skinned, moss-furred, their outlines softened by the slow accumulation of living material on their surface.

They eat what the district excretes. The anaerobic layers of the root-mass produce the peat-gas that fuels the pumps, but the aerobic layers—the inches just above the waterline, where wood meets air and fungus thrives—produce other things. Shelf-fungi, harvested young, have a texture like overripe fruit and a taste that sits between mushroom and the mineral tang of the water. The duckweed, scooped from the pools and squeezed of its water, yields a green paste that sustains without satisfying, a calorie-source the body accepts without the mind finding pleasure in it. Sometimes a vine-tip, extending itself across a warm pipe, produces a bud that swells for a week and then bursts into a fibrous flower that tastes of iron and chlorophyll and something almost sweet, almost like the memory of fruit. The residents take these offerings without gratitude or expectation; they do not anthropomorphize the district's generosities. The food is simply there, as they are simply here, and both facts are consequences of the same equilibrium.

They do not speak much, and when they do, their voices have changed to suit the acoustic. The vegetation strips high frequencies; speech must travel in the bass, in the resonant hum that the chamber's shape permits. They communicate in low tones that vibrate in the chest more than the ear, in gestures that have become slow and deliberate because haste is meaningless in a place where everything operates on geological patience. A hand raised in greeting holds its position for ten seconds, fifteen, while the other person navigates the root-mass to close the distance. Conversations unfold over hours, punctuated by long silences during which both parties watch the water bead on the underside of a frond, accumulate, and fall. *Pock.* They have developed a sign-language that operates in the peripheral vision, hands moving in the spaces between the steam-currents, because to look directly at something in Marrowmire is to let it fill your whole attention, and there is not enough attention to spare for direct looking.

The oldest among them—called Rootmother or simply *Her*, though her name before this place has dissolved from memory—has lived inside the district for what she estimates as forty years. She cannot be certain. Time here does not accumulate; it composts. She lives in the central pump-house, the one whose boiler has achieved a perfect rhythm, its arm rising and falling at a frequency that matches her resting heartbeat. She does not maintain it; there is nothing to maintain. The gaskets have worn to perfect seals, the scale has insulated the boiler to optimal efficiency, the root-mass has grown around the pipes in a configuration that minimizes heat-loss. She simply lives beside it, and it runs, and the coincidence of their rhythms has, over the decades, produced a synchronization that she no longer considers remarkable. Sometimes she places her hand flat on the iron flank of the boiler and feels the pulse of steam inside, and her own pulse adjusts to match it, and for a moment the boundary between her body and the machine is a distinction without a difference.

She is the one who understands the district's function in the larger city, though *understands* may overstate the case. She *feels* it. She knows that the water leaving Marrowmire through the slow canal is cleaner than the water entering from the upper city's runoff, that the root-mass filters and processes and returns it in a cycle the engineers above never designed but have come to rely on. She knows that the heat the district absorbs from the manufactories' exhaust is dissipated through the transpiration of all that biomass, cooling the upper city by degrees that no one measures and everyone assumes. She knows that Marrowmire is working, that its existence is not passive decay but active metabolism, that it does for the city what a kidney does for a body—quietly, continuously, without recognition. She does not tell the others this, because it does not need to be told. They feel it in the way the water moves, in the steady warmth of the pipes, in the sense—never articulated but universally shared—that this small wet place at the edge of things is performing some essential labor that would go undone without it.

The younger residents—those who have been here only five years, only ten—still carry the residue of the city above. They remember dry walls and hard edges, the absence of damp, the sound of their own voices reflected off stone. They sometimes stand at the weeping retaining wall and look up at the underside of the upper city, its foundations dripping, its iron infrastructure visible like the exposed skeleton of something that never expected to be seen from below. They remember that they could climb out, that the wall has handholds, that the world of hard surfaces and animal noise and human urgency is only twenty feet above them. They do not climb. They turn back to the warm water and the greenish light and the slow mechanical pulse of the pumps, and they go about their business, which is the business of being present in a place that requires nothing of them except their presence.

Because this is what Marrowmire asks of its inhabitants, and what it gives in return: to be part of a system that does not require interpretation. There is no meaning here to excavate, no purpose to chase, no narrative to advance. The district does not care about your story. It offers only integration—the slow replacement of your hard edges with soft ones, your rapid rhythms with slow ones, your dryness with wetness, your solitude with a kind of belonging that has nothing to do with other people and everything to do with place. To live here is to be gently, patiently unmade as a separate thing and remade as an element of a larger process. The residents do not resist this. They do not welcome it, either. They simply undergo it, the way the root-mass undergoes growth, the way the pumps undergo their cycle, the way the water undergoes its endless occupation of every hollow and surface and pore.

At night—or what passes for night, when the gray daylight filters to its dimmest and the work-lamps on the pump-stations become the primary light-sources—the district contracts slightly. The temperature drops by two degrees, perhaps three. The mist thickens, rising from the warm water into the cooling air, and the greenish luminance takes on depth, a submarine quality that makes the root-walls seem to recede into infinite distance. The residents retreat to their alcoves and curl against the warm iron and the furred moss, and the district breathes around them—the slow exhale of so much vegetation respiring in the dark, the mechanical inhale of the pump-arms pulling down, the condensation beading and falling in its constant rhythm. *Pock. Pock. Pock.* They sleep, and their sleep is the sleep of an organ in a body—not unconscious, exactly, but subsumed, functioning at a level below awareness, performing whatever quiet labor the whole requires of them.

And in the morning, when the gray light strengthens and the steam-thermals begin to lift the mist in slow curtains, they rise and wade back into the central artery, ankle-deep in warm water, and they continue the work of being part of Marrowmire. Which is no work at all. Which is the simplest thing they have ever done. Which is, perhaps, the reason they stayed.
