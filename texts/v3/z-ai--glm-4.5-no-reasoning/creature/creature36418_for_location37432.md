To live here is to be a creature of the interval—something that exists only in the infinitesimal space between creation and destruction. There is no surviving *in* this place; there is only surviving *as* this place, by becoming a localized loop of repair within the chaos. The inhabitants are not residents. They are symptoms.

Consider first the drones—those "insane" automata crawling the mineralized ceilings. They are the obvious fauna, but to call them individual organisms is to misunderstand the nature of life in a pocket where individuality is mechanically impossible. A drone that falls from the catwalk is not dead; it is merely *redistributed*. The turbulent current carries it until it slams into a bulkhead, where its still-cycling limbs catch on a grating. Its welder sparks, fusing itself to the wall. It becomes a protrusion—a new node of heat and activity, welding empty water to empty water until its power cell drains or the water tears it free again. There is no death for them because there was never a whole self to dissolve—only a persistent behavioral script, an unquenchable *intention* to maintain, running on fumes and hydrostatic pressure.

Watch them long enough and you see the truth: they are not many. They are one. The drones are the dispersed nervous system of the factory itself, which is the only true organism here. The factory is alive in the way a wind is alive: not as an object, but as an ongoing event. It breathes in the geothermal heat. It exhales the turbulence. Its pulse is the forge-hammer. Its metabolism is the endless transmutation of iron to pyrite, copper to chalcanthite. Its thoughts—such as they are—occur at the speed of metal fatigue: slow, mineral propositions written in the rime of expanding crystals, each crack and groan a synapse firing in a mind too vast and too slow to ever form a coherent idea. It thinks about pressure. It thinks about the weight of the water. It has been thinking about the weight of the water for longer than there has been language to formulate the thought.

And yet, there are others. Not drones, but something that has *grown* from the drones' logic, the way a coral reef grows from the logic of polyps.

In the deepest shafts, where the orange resistance-heating glows like a low infection in the water, you find the *clusters*. These are amalgamations—half a dozen drones, or a dozen, or a hundred, fused together by calcite and welded joints and the accreted mineralization of decades. They hang from the maintenance conduits like malformed chandeliers, bristling with articulated legs that have long since stopped moving independently. The cluster moves as one slow, multi-limbed entity, a centipedal mass of rusted actuators and clouded optics, crawling along the pipes with a terrible, grinding patience. Its purpose has evolved. A single drone repairs. A cluster *restructures*. It does not fix the architecture to match some original blueprint; the original blueprint is gone, if it ever existed. Instead, the cluster builds to satisfy the pressure. It reinforces the walls not to preserve the factory, but to preserve the *conditions*—the warmth, the turbulence, the roar. It builds because building is what its body does, and its body is now the factory's body.

Some clusters become architecture entirely. You will see a catwalk that ends in a mass of welded limbs and fused optics, seamlessly joined to the chalcanthite drapery, a buttress of calcined metal supporting a bulkhead that would otherwise have collapsed. Is it a drone, or is it a pillar? It is both. It is a thing that has chosen to become the place it lives in. In the pocket, the only way to persist is to stop moving and become a structural element. The alternative is to be swept away, cycling your limbs in the dark until your charge bleeds out and you become mere mineral—another layer of the rime.

There are smaller things too. Fragments. A single articulated arm, broken from its drone, still crawling along a pipe on its remaining joints, its welder sparking at nothing. A sensor module, half-blind, drifting in the current, still pinging sonar into the murk, mapping a geometry that changes faster than it can compute. These are the free-floating gametes of the factory-organism—dumb, persistent, searching for a cluster to fuse with or a surface to weld itself to and die usefulness-ly. They are pitiful in the way that a severed hand still grasping is pitiful: not because they suffer, but because they *insist*. They have no brain to know they are incomplete. They have only the reflex, and the reflex does not end.

And deeper still—past the clusters, past the catwalks, in the blast furnace's hollow eye where the heat-shimmer is so thick that the water seems to boil without boiling—there are the *dwellers*.

You will not see them. You will feel them, the way you feel a change in the current before you see what caused it. They are the reason the sound never stops, the reason the forge-hammers cycle even though there is no product, no ore, no ingot, no purpose. The dwellers are the original inhabitants—not the builders, not the engineers, but whatever was here before the factory was sunk, before the flood, before the concept of "industry" was layered onto this pocket of impossible architecture. They are the thing that the factory was built to contain, or to appease, or to feed.

The dwellers live in the heat. They *are* the heat. They are the thermocline's reason for existing—vast, slow, thermal organisms that span the length of the pocket, folded into the rock and the metal like circulatory systems of a body that has no skin. When the forge-pistons hammer, it is their heartbeat you are hearing. When the turbines scream, it is their respiration, the exhalation of superheated fluid through channels they have carved over millennia. They do not think in any way you would recognize. Their thoughts are convection currents—slow, massive rolls of warm fluid rising and cooling and sinking, each cycle a single consideration that might take a decade to complete. They have been considering the same thing since before the factory fell: *the pressure, the pressure, the unendurable weight of the water above*.

The factory is their exoskeleton. The drones are their immune system, fighting off the incursion of the sea, patching the cracks, maintaining the warmth. The sound is their voice, or perhaps their scream—a continuous, 180-decibel bellow that fills the pocket so totally that it leaves no room for anything else, least of all silence or rest or the quiet that thoughts require. They do not know you are here. You are too small, too brief, too cold. You are a perturbation in the current, a shadow that passes across their dim orange glow and is gone.

To live in this place is to be part of this economy of force. The drones do not own themselves. The clusters do not own their bodies. Even the dwellers, vast and ancient, are not separate from the architecture that contains them; they are the warmth that fills it, the motion that drives it, the purpose that keeps the hammers falling. The pocket has no inhabitants. It has only *components*—interchangeable, involuntary, and eternal—each one a gesture in a single, unending act of survival that has no audience and no end.

The place does not care if you understand it. The sound will not pause to let you think. The warmth will not comfort you. The drones will not notice you unless you are something that needs repairing—and then they will crawl to you with their clouded eyes and their sparking welders, and they will try to fuse you to the wall, to make you part of the structure, to give you the only gift this place knows how to give: *permanence*, at the cost of everything you were.

This is what lives here: a single, deafening, mineral organism, wearing the shape of a drowned factory, dreaming of the pressure, and building itself forever in the dark.
