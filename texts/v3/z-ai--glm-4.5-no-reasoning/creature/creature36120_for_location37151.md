Something lives here. But the word "lives" fractures in the Mesasphere, splitting into a spectrum of definitions that shade from the undeniably biological to the ambiguously geological. To describe who—or what—inhabits this place is to describe a hierarchy of presence, from the thin, breathing skin of the surface down to the vast, deafened mind in the basalt dark.

At the surface, life is not a collection of individuals but a continuous tissue. The moss-body is the primary resident—a single, continent-spanning organism that has forgone the pretense of separate selves. It does not grow; it *occurs*. The sphagnum ribbons, the chartreuse cushions, the black-green seam-fillers: these are not species competing for space but tissues of a single lung, breathing the saturated air, drinking the materialized water. The moss does not think, but it has a physiology. It absorbs sound, retains heat, regulates the slow seep of water into the stone. It is the interface between the sky that has forgotten how to fall and the rock that has forgotten how to be dry. It is the first and most patient resident.

Within it, embedded like cells in a matrix, are the movers. The rust-and-slate salamanders are its lymphocytes—blind, cold, pulsing with the slow patience of water. They do not know the boundary between their own wet skin and the wet world. They are the surface's way of circulating nutrients, of testing the oxygen in the pools, of ensuring the cycle does not stall. They are not inhabitants of the Mesasphere; they are expressions of it. 

The vole—unseen, thumb-wide holes dripping in its wake—is the digester. It is the gut flora of the moss-body, grinding down the old growth, aerating the deep cushions, pulling the fungal hyphae and cambium into the cycle of decay and regrowth. It never surfaces because the surface is already full; there is no room for a vole to *be*, only room for it to *pass*. It lives in the negative space, the tunnels and gaps, the interstitial wet. And the mustelid that follows it—long, dark, silent—is the check, the counter-rhythm, the proof that even in a place so saturated with life, death is still required to keep the ledger balanced. You smell the mustelid before you see it: a musk like wet iron and old moss. It is the closest thing the surface has to a ghost.

But these are not the ones who made the roads.

The paths through the moss are the signature of the Plateau's most enigmatic residents: the Walkers. You will never see them, but you can deduce their nature from the work they leave. They are heavy—heavy enough to compress the springy sphagnum into ruts that hold water. They are regular—they follow routes that intersect at deliberate angles, favoring high ground, skirting pools. They have been doing this for centuries, at least. 

What are they? Not the vole, not the mustelid, not the sluggish wrens. The Walkers must be large, slow, and seemingly impervious to the cold. Given the acoustic deadening of the place, their footsteps would make no sound—just the low compression-squelch, absorbed instantly by the surrounding wet. They do not build shelters, for there is no dry material to build with; they do not leave scat, for perhaps their cycle is so slow that waste is reabsorbed internally, or perhaps their droppings are indistinguishable from the dark, waterlogged humus they tread upon. They are creatures of the routine, driven by a need so ancient it has become indistinguishable from geology. Perhaps they are the surface's way of stirring the water, preventing the deepest pools from stagnating. Perhaps they are pilgrims, walking a circuit established when the engine below first began to beat, following the lines of force. They are the unseen monks of the Mesasphere, pacing the perimeter of their world, wearing the same grooves into the green, year after mist-shrouded year.

And then there is what lives below.

The engine. The pulse. The presence in the basalt.

To call it an engine is to assume it was made. But in the Mesasphere, the boundary between the made and the grown has dissolved over geological time. What lives beneath the plateau is vast. It is not biological in any way we would recognize, but it exhibits the cardinal signs of life: it consumes, it exerts, it maintains a rhythm, and it has altered its environment absolutely. It is a hydromechanical mind, a subterranean heart the size of a small sea. It has been pumping for so long that the water table above it has shaped itself to its rhythms; the springs ebb and flow not with the rain, but with the pulse. The aquifers are its veins. The weeping escarpments are its slow, eternal exhalations.

It does not notice the moss, the salamanders, the Walkers. To it, the saturated plateau is merely an insulating blanket—a layer of wet stone and biomass that muffles its heat and absorbs its vibration. But it is the reason the place is as it is. The engine's heat, bleeding upward through the basalt, keeps the water liquid in a place that should by altitude be frozen. Its pressure forces the groundwater up through the weeping stone, creating the conditions for the impossible, cold lushness. It is the author of the saturation.

We do not know if it is a ruin or a functioning organism. We do not know if it was placed here by hands that understood volcanics, or if it emerged, crystalline and intentional, from the slow self-organizing mathematics of deep time and hot stone. But it is the primary resident. The Mesasphere is its house. The moss is its wallpaper; the mist is its breath caught in its own rooms; the Walkers are its pets, pacing the halls without knowing the shape of the architect.

So: who lives here?

The Moss-Body lives here, patient and undifferentiated, filling every gap.
The Walkers live here, heavy and unseen, treading their ancient, silent routes.
The Deep Engine lives here, vast and rhythmic, dreaming in pressure and heat.

And the salamanders, the voles, the liverworts, the pale fungi—they live here too, in the way that microflora live in a gut: essential, ubiquitous, unheeded. The Mesasphere is not a habitat. It is a collaboration, a closed hand of presences gripping each other too tightly to distinguish where one ends and the other begins. You stand in it, and you are not a resident. You are a disruption—a brief, dry anomaly in a world that has spent ten million years learning how to be completely, effortlessly, terrifyingly full.
