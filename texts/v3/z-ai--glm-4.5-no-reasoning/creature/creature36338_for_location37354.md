The people who live here are not inhabitants; they are a tissue. They call themselves something, surely—some guttural syllable passed mother to daughter in the damp brick galleries—but the name would sound like a cough, like the tick of a dripped condensation bead, like the thump of a piston. They are the human expression of the mesa's logic: compressed, vertical, slow, and fused.

They are physically slight. Not starved, but reduced, as if the immense atmospheric pressure and the perpetual close air have compacted them over generations. Their bones are dense, carried low to the ground; they move with a practiced, deliberate caution that makes them seem older than they are, even the children. There is no quickness here. Quickness requires dry air, sharp light, a horizon. These people have the leaden twilight in their eyes—a pale, diluted gray-blue that seems to wash out the pupil, making their gaze appear unfocused, as if they are always looking at something just behind the visible world. Their skin carries the tone of the stone: a blue-gray pallor beneath, mottled with rust spots where the iron-heavy water has stained them over a lifetime of damp.

They are wrapped in layers. Not for warmth alone, though the chill is bone-deep, but to create a personal microclimate against the wet. Their clothing is a complex stratigraphy: a base of rough, undyed nettle cloth (the fibers long and slightly oily, repelling the condensation), then a middle layer of quilted wadding salvaged and re-salvaged until it is more patch than original fabric, and finally an outer shell of oiled canvas or, among the older families, a strange leather tanned from something indistinct—perhaps the pale salamanders, perhaps something else. The outer shell is always damp to the touch. They accept this. Dryness is a foreign concept, a desert memory. When they press their backs against the sweating brick, they do not flinch.

Their hands are remarkable. The hands tell the whole story. The fingers are long, thick-jointed, prehensile in a way that modern hands are not. They have the grip of climbers, of people who must hold onto wet iron gantries and moss-slicked stone lips in the fog. But more: the fingertips are broad, flattened, almost spatulate, and the nails are thick, yellowed, and slightly concave—shaped, unconsciously, to fit into the mortise gaps between bricks, to pry, to feel for the subtle warmth of a steam pipe behind a wall. Their hands are sensory instruments as much as tools. An old grandmother can press her palm flat against a tenement wall and tell you the depth of the root intrusion behind the plaster, the temperature of the condensation pipe within, and whether the brick was laid in the First Expansion or the Third. The skin of their palms is calloused into a strange, ridged topography, like the tread of an old boot, like bark, like the liverwort they walk over without looking down.

There are no crowds. The density of the place is structural, not demographic. The honeycomb is full, yes, but it is a fullness of proximity, not multitudes. Families occupy single rooms—six, eight, ten people in a space the size of a large closet, stacked in rope hammocks that sway imperceptibly with the deep, subsonic pulse of the engines. But they are quiet in their proximity. They have developed a body language of minimum intrusion: a slight turn of the shoulder to pass, a downturned gaze, a stillness that is almost geological when observed from outside. They do not argue loudly; the echoes make that impossible, and so they have unlearned shouting. Their disputes are settled in long, damp silences, in the withholding of shared heat, in the subtle re-angling of a hammock rope. Their affection is equally minimal and equally deep: the sharing of a warm pipe-seam to lean against, the placement of a chipped enamel cup of hot water (never quite boiling—the altitude and the damp make boiling a theoretical state) near someone's hammock without word.

They eat what the system excretes. There are no farms; the terraces are too built-over, too vertical, too shaded. Instead, there are the fungal gardens. Deep in the interior of the honeycomb, where the brick walls sweat most profusely and the air is warmest from the pipe-chases, they cultivate dense shelves of mushroom and bracket fungus. The substrate is everything: rotted wood fiber, the stripped husks of the liverwort, the ash from the slow-burning coal braziers they use for heat, and—critically—the warm, oily condensate that drips from the iron pipes. This water, touched by the machine, carries minerals and strange, iridescent compounds that the fungi drink and transmute. The mushrooms are large, pale, and rubbery, with a faint sulfuric aftertaste that the people no longer notice. They are the staple. Alongside them: small, blind fish caught from the drip-catchers (sleek, eyeless things that swim in the dark, water-filled cisterns on the lower terraces), and the occasional, precious egg from the feral cats—which are not pets, but rivals, thin and cunning, who watch from the gantries with the same pale, diluted eyes.

They work, because the system requires it, and because they are of the system. The work is maintenance. The great engines turn, but they require the steady, ritual attention of hands: the tightening of a bolt that has loosened from a century of vibration, the packing of a valve gland with tallow and oakum, the clearing of a condensation drain choked with root and moss. The workers—the Maintainers—are a hereditary caste, though they do not use that word. They simply are. A child born to a Maintainer learns the vibration of the wall before they learn to speak; they know the specific, subtle tremor that means the left exhaust valve on Engine Seven is weeping steam faster than the catch-basin can handle. The Maintainers carry heavy iron keys on loops of greasy cord around their necks; the keys are ancient, their teeth worn to nubs, but they still turn the locks because the locks are also ancient, and both have worn together, like roots and pipes.

There are others. The Drip-Readers, mostly women, who track the condensate. They know which seams are running sweet and which are running oily; they can taste the difference, and they chart the slow migration of the warm water through the city's veins. The Moss-Pullers, children and the elderly, who keep the pedestrian ways clear of the aggressive emerald siege—though they know it is a holding action, and that in a generation, a neglected alley will be knee-deep in wet green. The Rooftop-Walkers, a loose fraternity of the young and bold, who traverse the upper eaves and the gantries, checking the guy-wires and the lightning rods, moving in the fog with a casual surety that would terrify their ground-bound elders, if the elders had the capacity for terror left.

And there are the ones who go still. It happens. A person—young or old, it does not discriminate—will simply stop moving. They will find a warm corner where a pipe bleeds steam into a brick alcove, and they will sit, and they will not rise. Their eyes will focus on the wet wall in front of them, and their breathing will slow to match the great engines, and their skin will cool to$—$no, warm to$—$the temperature of the stone; and they will not speak, and they will not eat, though food will be left near them. They are not dead. Their eyes move, tracking something unseen. Their hands will occasionally make a slow, deliberate gesture—pressing the air, tracing5 palpating an invisible surface—as if they are reading the wall from the inside, as if they have finally understood the city as a body and are tracing its buried nerves. The others do not disturb them. They are moved, gently, to the designated alcoves—the Still-Rooms—and tended, and fed if they will eat, and simply allowed to be. Some sit for a season. Some sit for years. Occasionally, one will rise, blink, and return to their hammock with no memory of the elapsed time. More often, they simply... settle. They become part of the architecture. Their clothing fuses with the brick damp, the moss colonizes their shoulders, the roots find their legs, and they are there, a permanent fixture, like the pipes, like the stone.

They do not fear this. They do not fear the place. Fear requires the possibility of an alternative, and they have no conception of one. The mesa is all. The city is the mesa. The engines are the city's heart, the pipes its veins, the fog its breath, the moss its skin. And they—the people—are its consciousness, its slow, dispersed, multi-bodied awareness. They know this the way you know your own heartbeat: not as a thought, but as a rhythm, a subsonic pulse that they share, that moves through all of them at once when the great engine turns over and the deep vibration thrums through ten million tons of stone and brick and iron and root and water, and for a moment, in the leaden twilight, every hand on every railing pauses, and every gray-blue eye looks up, and the city breathes, and they are breathed.
