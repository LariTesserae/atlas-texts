To live here is to have ceased becoming. The inhabitants of this saturated cwm are not creatures who occupy a place; they are continuations of it, expressions of the cold, weeping stone as inevitable as the water that runs from it in uncountable micro-trickles. They have lived so long in equilibrium that the boundary between organism and environment has dissolved. They are the stillness.

Consider first the ancient amphibians, the giant salamanders thick as a man's thigh, who lie along the weeping gutters of dark schist. They are the oldest living things in the basin, though they have not aged in any way that matters. They do not hunt; they do not flee. They simply reside, their pale, translucent flesh barely distinguishable from the leathery liverworts that plate the rock beneath them. They have absorbed the cold so completely that they are cold itself. Their old-gold pinprick eyes have not blinked in memory, for there is nothing to look at that was not there a century ago and will not be there a century hence. They do not see light; they see the absence of it, the profound submarine darkness that is their constant medium. Their gills pulse in slow, metrical time with the dripping walls—inhalation and exhalation perfectly matched to the condensation and evaporation of the cwm. They are breathing the place itself, cycling the cold damp, and their pulse is the pulse of the granite: slow, interminable, indifferent.

In the black pools of spongy, water-logged earth, the sightless fish swim. They are no longer than a finger, dark as the tannic water, and they exist only as a collective. A single fish is an impossibility here; the basin knows them only as a seething, shivering cloud of ten thousand scales that turns in unison, parting and reforming without decision. They navigate by pressure, by the displacement of the gelid fluid they share with the leeches and the pale roots of the reeds. They have never seen sun, and their bodies have long since traded eyes for lateral lines so sensitive they register the fall of a single droplet from the weeping cliffs as a tremor across their collective spine. They are a single organism distributed through the dark water, a thought the basin is thinking, repeated in miniature. They have no predators that move faster than they do, and so they have forgotten how to dart or startle. They drift in their dense clouds, sated, waiting without anticipation.

Upon the stone, the snails move, and their movement is an act of geological patience. Their shells are thin and opaline—lacking the calcification of sunlit creatures, they have grown soft, adapted to a world where calcium is leached and light does not demand hard armor. They are grazers of the bio-film, the thin living varnish of algae and bacteria that coats the wet rock. They are everywhere, and their grazing is the sound of the place: a scraping like a fingernail drawn slowly across silk, a sound so quiet and pervasive it becomes indistinguishable from the dripping. They are the basin's immune system, keeping the moss and liverwort from suffocating entirely under its own success, scraping the old growth away so the new can remain exactly as it was. They move in paths of clean stone, leaving trails of slightly darker wetness behind them, and they will follow these paths until the stone erases them, which will take longer than any of them have to live.

And the leeches—pale as blisters, hanging in clusters from the undersides of pallid fern fronds. They are parasites without urgency, waiting for something that may never come. They have evolved out of time. In a place where no predator moves faster than its prey, where the chase has been negotiated out of existence, the leeches have abandoned even the pretense of pursuit. They wait because waiting is the only verb that functions here. They cling to the ferns, soft and obscene, their bodies turgid with the cold fluid of the basin, and they wait for warmth, for pressure, for the rare moment when something brushes their frond and they can latch on and drink and return to waiting. They are not hungry; hunger implies a future in which it might be sated. They are simply empty, and they will remain empty until the basin fills them, because here, all things are filled.

The moss and the ferns and the reeds are also inhabitants, though they have pressed so far into the climax state that they have become architecture. The club moss is so dense it reads as velvet, and it is alive, but it has not grown in centuries. It has reached its final form and stopped, entering a state of frozen maturity. The pallid ferns unfurled in the dark when the climate settled and have remained in that posture ever since, locked in the act of opening, suspended between gesture and completion. Their roots are thick, pale, and exposed, weaving over and under one another in a knotwork that holds the whole basin in a rigid, interlocked embrace. They are the skeleton of the place, the tension that keeps the cwm from collapsing inward. They do not compete; they have already competed, and the competition ended in a draw so total it became a symbiosis. They share the water, the thin nutrients, the cold. They breathe out the scent of cold loam, tannin, and wet flint, and the scent settles back into the saturated air and is absorbed back into their leaves. They are breathing their own exhaust, living in a closed loop, and the loop is so tight, so perfectly balanced, that nothing escapes and nothing enters. They are the green that has forgotten how to turn.

Even the stone is inhabited. The bio-film—a living slime of bacteria and algae thinner than a thought—coats every surface. It is the first layer of the velvet, the ground from which all the moss and liverwort and fern grows. It is the most active thing in the basin, and its activity is invisible. It photosynthesizes on the borrowed, diffracted light that filters through the ragged slit above, and it does so at a rate so slow it is indistinguishable from stillness. It is the basin's dream of the sun, the only thing here that remembers light, and it remembers it only as a chemical necessity, a faint pull toward the grey crack overhead. It is the food of the snails, the bed of the salamanders, the film that makes the stone slick. It is everywhere, and it is the reason the water does not run clean but runs black, stained with the cold life of the place.

And there is something else that lives here, something that is not organism nor film nor root. It is the hum. The low, atonal drone that sounds at the very threshold of audibility, woven from the combined micro-sounds of a million small, cold bodies, the invisible dripping, the creak of root pressure, the wet breath of the stone. This hum is the voice of the equilibrium, the sound a system makes when it has reached perfect balance and ceased to change. It is not alive in any biological sense, but it is the presence that contains all the others, the sum of their slow metabolisms and their patient waiting. It is the sound of time accumulating, pressurized and still, layered like the silt. It is the sound of a place that has forgotten how to change, and in forgetting, has achieved a kind of life—a cold, breathless, interminable life that will outlast everything that moves above it in the world of wind and frost and sun.

To live here is to be part of the hum. The salamanders pulse to it. The fish turn in it. The snails scrape in time with it. The leeches hang from it. The roots creak with it. The water drips in it. And the stone, the vast, slumbering beast whose ribs interlock overhead, breathes it in slow, infinite cycles, going nowhere, waiting for nothing, existing in a perpetual, breathless equilibrium that has no need of anything as transient as meaning or purpose or end.
