To live in the Hollown is to be a citizen of a city that rewrites itself while you sleep, to be a tenant in a building that may slowly narrow its own doorway around your shoulders over the course of a long, wet afternoon. The people here—if that word can be applied to the pale, damp things that endure beneath the surgical glare of the ceiling—are not builders. They are interpreters. They are the readers of a text that will not stop shifting.

Consider what it takes to survive in a churning metropolis where the architecture is a slow, grinding catastrophe. They do not fight the movement of the stone; they cannot. There is no metal to brace, no fired ceramic to resist. Instead, they have made a discipline of yielding. To walk through the Hollown is to practice a kind of continuous, spatial forgiveness. Their bodies are long, low, and unspeakably flexible, bred for the slow buckle of an avenue becoming a ramp, for the spontaneous crushing of a corridor into a crawlspace. Their skeletons are not rigid cages of bone but cartilaginous lattices, dense and springy, allowing them to pour themselves through the narrowing gaps, to exhale and compress as the megaliths grind inward. They are the color of wet tuff—a grey-yellow, waterlogged hue—and their skin is hairless, perpetually slick with the supersaturated condensation. They glisten like the walls. They are, in a sense, a fleshy extension of the masonry.

They are blind, or nearly so. In a world where the light is a merciless, bouncing glare off every damp surface, vision is a liability—a searing white that obliterates form. Their eyes are vestigial, recessed beneath heavy, calcified brow-ridges, useful only for sensing the macro-shifts in the bright glare that indicate a wall is beginning to bulge. Instead, they navigate by pressure and by the subtle, syncopated rhythm of the water. They feel the city. The whole of their nervous system is tuned to the seismic whisper of stone on stone, to the deep, subsonic groan of tuff swelling with moisture. When a terrace begins to give way, they feel it in their teeth, in the wet pits of their joints, and they move—not in panic, but with the slow, fluid resignation of creatures who have lived their entire lives in a ruin that is actively ruining itself.

They live in the interstitial moments of the city's geometry. They do not claim rooms; they inhabit *intervals*. A space between two grinding blocks might remain stable for a decade, becoming a home, a hollow within the Hollow. Tapestry-weavings of dried fungal-matter and scraped silicate chitin hang across the thresholds, not for privacy—there is no privacy in a shifting labyrinth—but to catch the dampness, to drink the chronic weeping of the walls. Their furniture is the city itself; a worn depression in a megalithic floor is a bed, a bulge in the tuff is a table, until the slow churn pushes the bulge flat and opens a new crack elsewhere, and they simply reorient their lives to the new topology. They are nomads in a static coordinate space, endlessly migrating across their own homes as the geometry rotates around them.

Their culture is one of inscription and erasure. They record things, but they have no permanent medium. Instead, they are keepers of the *etch*. They possess sharp, hand-sized splinters of the ceiling's volcanic glass—fracture-points harvested at great risk from the distant sky-rock—and with these they carve fine, intricate lines into the polished basalt and chert of the walls. The etchings are beautiful, dense, and unknowable: records of lineage, of water-rhythms, of the precise angle a wall tilted before it crushed a favored chamber. But the Hollown is an engine of erosion. The dampness, the micro-grinding, the slow settling—it all sandblasts the stone. An etching made today will be smoothed to a vague ghost in five years, utterly erased in ten. The people accept this. To carve is not to preserve; it is to speak, knowing the cave will swallow the words. Their historians are not archivists but performers, re-inscribing the same fading lore over and over, their lives a race against the parameter of maximal erosion. The city is a palimpsest being actively wiped, and they are the scribes who write in evaporating ink.

They are solitary by nature, scattered across the vast emptiness like the Glass-Grazers they sometimes hunt. The density is too low for societies, for crowds, for the roar of a market or the murmur of a square. Their gatherings are silent, temporary things: two or three of them meeting at a weeping crack in the stone, pressing their slick foreheads together to share the warmth and the moisture, exchanging splinters of glass or strips of Grazer-chitin without a sound. Their language is a tactile one, a slow passing of pressures across the damp skin of a shoulder or a rib-cage—a lexicon of leans and yields, perfectly adapted to a world where sound dies within a meter and the silence rings like a bell. They communicate as the city communicates: through the slow, grinding articulation of form against form.

And they pray, after a fashion. Their devotion is directed not upward—there is no up, only the distant, glaring sheet of volcanic glass—but inward, toward the memory of the soil they left behind epochs ago. They worship the Deep Mill, the patient, tectonic engine that chews the city and spits it back out. They believe the Hollown is not dying, but *digesting*. They are the flora of the gut, the necessary soft tissue that clings to the churning walls. In their most sacred act, they seek out a place where the urban tectonic pressure is at its greatest—a wall actively buckling, a floor slowly heaving into a ramp—and they press their bodies flat against it, arms spread, letting the stone grind against their cartilaginous chests. They listen to the silence, feeling the catastrophic paroxysm of deep time vibrate through their wet, yielding bones. They call this *the Communion of the Crush*. It is an act of absolute submission to the geometry of the ruin.

To be one of them is to live in the bright, weeping dark, knowing that your home will slowly murder you, but it will do so with such agonizing, geological patience that you will simply move aside, again and again, carving your fading story into the wall, listening to your own blood, and waiting for the great, silent mill to complete its final, exhausted shape.
