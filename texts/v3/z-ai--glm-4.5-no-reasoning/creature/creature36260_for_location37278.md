Something lives here, but it is not yet a people.

It is a congregation of almost-beings, caught in the same liminal throes as the city itself. They are the ones who fit the stone, who mix the crude mortar, who lash the green and bending wood with wet hide—but to call them inhabitants would imply a settledness they do not possess. They are more like a slow, distributed weather pattern that occasionally condenses into labor.

There are very few of them—perhaps only a few dozen scattered across the vast, hollow grid. You might walk for an hour past empty frames before you catch sight of one. They are tall, disproportionately so, as if their bodies have been stretched by the verticality of the cavity, pulled upward by the same whatever-force that draws the city toward its intended shape. Their limbs are long and narrow, the bones visible beneath the skin as sharp ridges, like the keen edges of the freshly cleaved paving stones they walk upon. They are built for leverage, for the slow mechanical advantage of placing one block upon another, and their bodies reflect this: broad, heavy shoulders that slope into thin, articulate arms, hands enormous and calloused, the fingers wide and flat, adapted for gripping the fractured basalt.

Their skin is the color of the wet mortar—gray, slightly green where the light filters through the perpetual mist, and always, always damp. Water beads on them as it does on everything here. They do not dry. Their hair, if it can be called that, grows in dark, matted tendrils that cling to their skulls and necks, and from these mats, small ferns and liverworts have sprouted, so that each of them carries a miniature echo of the rioting vegetation on their own bodies. They are colonized by the place. Lichen spots their shoulders like pale badges. Moss grows in the creases of their elbows and behind their knees. They do not seem to mind, or perhaps they do not notice, or perhaps the distinction between their bodies and the environment has never been meaningful to them.

Their eyes are the most unsettling feature. They are not blind, like the pale amphibians, but their vision is clearly suited for something other than sight as we understand it. The eyes are large, taking up much of the face, and they are pale—not blue or gray, but the color of the condensation that hangs in the air. Iridescence shifts across their surfaces like oil on water. They do not focus on objects. They do not track movement. Instead, they seem to receive the world as a field of pressures and densities, reading the mist, the drip-lines, the subtle variations in the weeping of the stone. A builder will turn its great, opalescent eyes toward a half-finished wall and simply stand for a long time, listening, watching the water run down the joints, and then it will adjust a single block by a few centimeters, or pack more mortar into a seam. It is not measuring with a tool. It is measuring with its whole body, allowing the place to speak through it.

They do not speak. They have no language of words. But they communicate, and this is where the weirdness of the sound becomes intimate. When they work—and they are almost always working, though so slowly that the motion is nearly imperceptible—they fall into a shared rhythm. A builder will raise a stone, and the placing of it will produce a particular tone, a resonant *thock* that rings through the cavity. Another builder, forty meters away, on the other side of the unfinished square, will answer with a strike of its hammer-stone, and the two sounds will interlock, a call and response that is not planned but emergent, like the synchronization of crickets or the pulse of a single enormous heart. This is the rhythm you hear in the chaos of the drips. The mountain's weeping provides the backbeat, the white noise, and within it, the builders find the syncopation, striking their stones in patterns that spiral and repeat and collapse and begin again. They are singing the city into shape. The sound is the architecture.

They do not rest in the way that humans rest. They do not sleep, or if they do, they sleep standing, leaning against the raw pillars with their great hands pressed flat against the stone, feeling the cold and the slow percolation of water through the micro-fissures. Perhaps they are drawing something from the rock in these moments—some mineral instruction, some blueprint carried in the magma's memory. They will stand like this for hours, motionless, indistinguishable from the scaffolding, and then they will stir and return to work with a sudden, deliberate precision, as if they have just received an order from deep inside the mountain.

They have no shelters. The buildings they construct are not for themselves. This is the essential pathos of the place: the city is being built by beings who do not live in it and may not ever live in it. The structures are for something else, or someone else, or some *time* else—a future that the builders can feel in the bones of the rock but cannot yet inhabit. They work with the patient certainty of organisms executing a program that was written before they existed. They do not hurry. They do not question. A single wall may take a generation. The mortar between two stones may take a decade to petrify, and the builders will wait, tending it, pressing their hands to the joints, adjusting the hide-bindings that hold the frame, until the stone decides to hold itself.

They are not born here, and they do not die here—or rather, they do neither in a way that is legible. New builders appear at the edges of the cavity, emerging from the narrow crevices that lead deeper into the mountain, from the solid, freezing marrow beyond the boundary. They arrive fully grown, already bearing the marks of the place—already damp, already moss-flecked, already moving with that strange, measured deliberation. They do not arrive in groups. They arrive one at a time, at long intervals, as if the mountain itself is extruding them, producing them from the same slow geological process that pushes the basalt upward. And when a builder can no longer work—when its body has become too colonized by the vegetation, when the moss has consumed its joints, when the ferns root too deeply in its flesh—it simply stops. It does not collapse. It does not decay in the usual way. It becomes part of the scaffolding. Its body calcifies, the wet flesh petrifying into a stone that is indistinguishable from the building material, and it stands there, another pillar in the unfinished grid, its great hands still pressed flat against the wall it was building, its opalescent eyes still open, still receiving the pressures of the mist. In time, a fern will uncoil from its mouth. A carpet of chartreuse moss will soften its shoulders. It will become a topiary, like the lichen-crusted dead wood, and the builders who come after will work around it, incorporating it into the structure, laying their stones against its petrified flank.

There are no children here. There is no play, no laughter, no argument, no story. The builders have no culture beyond the building. They have no memory beyond the pattern. They do not gather except in the loose, acoustically-linked configurations of their labor, and even then, they do not look at each other. They do not touch. They communicate only through the strike and the rhythm, through the shared somatic knowledge of where the next stone must go.

And yet.

And yet, there is a tenderness in them that is difficult to name. When a builder places a stone and the stone does not sit right—when the edge is too keen, or the angle is wrong, or the mortar rejects the fit—the builder will not force it. It will lift the stone away, a feat of immense strength performed with shocking gentleness, and it will carry the stone to the edge of the settlement, to the rim where the raw rock meets the living mountain, and it will set the stone down there, against the cold wall of the cavity, with a care that seems almost ceremonial. It will smooth its great hand over the stone's surface, once, as if in apology, and then it will return to the work. The rejected stones accumulate at the boundary, a slow curving wall of failures, each one placed with that same careful respect. Over the centuries—if centuries are the right measure here—the wall of rejected stones has become the most beautiful structure in the city. It is the only part that is finished. It is the only part that is whole. The builders have built themselves a shrine to everything that did not fit, and they tend it meticulously, keeping the moss at a careful distance, wiping the condensation from its surfaces with their broad, wet hands, so that the stones remain visible, remain sharp, remain *themselves*.

This is the closest they come to worship, or to grief.

The pale amphibians—the translucent, eyeless witnesses—have a relationship with the builders that is difficult to parse. The amphibians cling to the ferns and the broad leaves, but they also cling to the builders. You will see them attached to the backs of the tall figures, nestled in the moss that grows in the crook of an elbow, or spread flat against the damp skin of a shoulder, their slow hearts beating in time with the builder's breathing. The builders do not acknowledge them. They do not pick them off or feed them or shelter them. But neither do they shake them loose, even when the labor is strenuous, even when the leverage of wood and the strike of stone demands violent motion. The amphibians simply hold on, and the builders simply allow them to hold on, and the two species exist in a parabiosis that seems to require nothing from either side except proximity. Perhaps the amphibians are a kind of external organ for the builders—a way of feeling the world that does not require sight. Perhaps the builders are a kind of architecture for the amphibians—a slow, reliable geography in the chaos of the forming city. Perhaps they are both just expressions of the same impulse: the mountain's desire to people itself, to grow inhabitants the way it grows stone, the way it grows water, the way it grows ferns from the sheer, absurd persistence of moisture and time.

The city is building itself. The builders are building the city. The mountain is building the builders. And the place has not yet decided what it wants to be—so everything continues, one wet, agonizing drop at a time, one stone a month, one rhythm in the dark, one tall, damp figure pressing its hands against the raw wall and listening for the next instruction from the deep, slow marrow of the world.
