Something lives here.

Not the moss—though the moss is the proof that living is possible in a place like this, that the negotiation between heat and wet can yield something other than sterility. The moss is the signpost. What it points to is harder to see.

---

The rat-thing (call it that; it may be a rat, may be several generations departed from anything that would recognize a rat, may be something that came down through the same catwalk breach you did and found reasons to stay) — the rat-thing lives in the way you would expect. It knows the grating by feel, knows which sections flex and which have solid backing beneath. It travels the underside of pipes where the heat is fierce but the predators (what predators? You don't know. It does.) cannot reach. It has routes. You heard it once, a skitter, and then absence—but that absence was not the absence of departure. It was the absence of stillness. It was the rat-thing pressed flat against the warm belly of a steam main, breathing slow, waiting for you to pass.

It eats the pale fungus. Or the pale fungus's guests. There are guests—nearly invisible, a fine particulate motion in the air near the wettest corners if you hold your light there long enough and let your eyes adjust past the point of comfort. Mites. Or mite-adjacent. Something that has found the fungus and made it substrate, made it country. They are the reason the fungus doesn't spread further than it does; they graze it back at the margins, keeping it in check, tending it the way herders tend a meadow—not with intention, but with appetite that functions as intention in the long run.

So: a chain. Water from the weeping pipe. Minerals in the water, minerals in the rust, minerals the pipe itself was made from and is returning to. The fungus eating the minerals. The mites eating the fungus. The rat-thing eating the mites, or the mite-adjacent, or the smaller things that colonize the mites—because there are always smaller things. You don't see them but the rat-thing does, or feels them, or tastes them in the air when it presses its nose against the grating and breathes the hot mineral breath of the levels below.

The rat-thing is not alone. You heard one skitter but there are more—there must be more, because a single rat-thing in a place this size would be a story, not a life. A life requires continuation, requires the making of more rat-things, requires the finding of other rat-things in the dark. They find each other by sound (the tap of feet on grating, each rat-thing knowing the specific frequency of its own kind's passage) and by smell (the mineral-heat of the place has its own odor, but cut through it are traces: urine, marking; the fatty signature of bodies warm in the cool-air stratum; the particular must of the pale fungus, which means food is near). They find each other by heat, perhaps—each one a small warm spot in the lower air, infrared beacons to any other that has evolved the sense to read them.

They have been here longer than the breach in the catwalk. They came from somewhere. Up, possibly—down through the building's descending guts, finding each level slightly more abandoned than the last, settling at the level where abandonment becomes total and they are the only tenants. Or from below, from whatever the grating spans, climbing up through the void where sounds drop away and don't return. You don't know. They don't know; they know only the routes they use, the warm pipes, the wet corners, the gaps that admit their bodies.

---

But there is something else that lives here. Something that is not any of these organisms and is not you and is not the room itself—though it is closer to the room than to anything else.

It is the process.

The room was made for a process. The process—hydraulic, thermal, chemical, some combination the infrastructure implies but does not reveal—occupied this space the way a body occupies a skeleton. It was the thing that moved, that pumped, that exchanged heat and fluid and pressure through the networks of pipe and conduit and valve. It was the building's metabolism, or a section of it: the unseen conversion that kept whatever happened above supplied with whatever it needed—steam, coolant, compressed air, the invisible utilities that make a building function as something more than a shell.

The process is mostly dead. The machines that served it have failed, been bypassed, fallen silent. But *mostly* is not *entirely*. Somewhere in the dense forest of infrastructure, something still runs. You feel it through the floor: a vibration, a pulse, a low-frequency hum that is more tactile than audible. It comes and goes on a cycle you haven't been here long enough to map. It might be a pump on automatic, still serving some function ten floors up that no one has thought to re-engineer without it. It might be a valve, opening and closing on a timer that no one has reset, performing a regulation that no longer needs regulating but which no one has told to stop. It might be the building itself—a thermal expansion, a pressure equalization, the structure breathing.

Whatever it is, it is alive in the way a heartbeat is alive: mechanical, autonomous, indifferent to whether anyone is listening. It keeps time in the Sub-Furnace. The drip-lines fall into rhythm with it or resist it; the moss feels it through the grating; the rat-things know its cycle the way you know the cycle of your own blood. It is the room's remaining function, its last connection to the purpose it was built for. When it stops—and it will stop, eventually, when the last relay corrodes through or the last automatic system loses its power source—then the room will be fully what it is becoming: a place where nothing happens except the slow conversion of the made-world into something else.

But not yet. The process persists. It lives here. It is the oldest resident.

---

And the dark lives here.

This is not metaphor. The dark in the Sub-Furnace has a quality that goes beyond the absence of light. It has depth, the description said, and it does—depth in the physical sense, the measurable distance that your light cannot reach, but also depth in the sense of *presence*. The dark is what occupies the space the infrastructure doesn't. It fills the gaps between pipe runs and cable trays. It pools in the low places where the ceiling presses down. It extends outward from the walls—not as a condition of the walls but as a medium, an atmosphere, something you move through rather than something you simply cannot see through.

The dark has been here since the lights failed. Before that, it existed only at the margins, in the spaces the lighting design didn't reach, the intentional shadows of a working environment. But when the power went off—or was diverted, or was simply no longer maintained for this level—the dark expanded to fill the room the way water fills a hull breach. It is the room's default state now. The phosphorescent emergency strip contradicts it locally; your light contradicts it locally; but these are exceptions, small volumes of visibility in a medium that otherwise extends to the walls and beyond them, into the infrastructure of adjacent spaces, into the whole forgotten sub-level of which this room is only the part you've reached.

The dark does not do anything. It is not hostile, not sentient, not the dark of horror stories that reaches for you. It simply *is*—vast, uniform, patient, absorbing light and sound and heat into its indefinite volume. It is the thing the room is becoming. When the last phosphor dims, when the last vibration stills, when the last drip-line finds a path that leads somewhere else, the dark will be all that is here. It is the terminal condition. It lives here already, more thoroughly than anything else.

---

So who lives in the Sub-Furnace?

A colony of rat-things, or rat-descendants, or simply *the small warm bodies*—they would not recognize the distinction. They live in the cool-air stratum, in the gaps between infrastructure, in the economy of fungus and moisture and heat that the room's decay has inadvertently created. They are the visible life, the life you can hear and almost see. They have been here for rat-generations, which is to say for the time it takes a population to forget it was ever elsewhere. They will be here until the water stops or the heat increases past tolerance or the grating finally fails beneath the weight of accumulated rust and whatever they stand on becomes whatever is below.

The process lives here—the mechanical remnant, the autonomous pulse, the last functioning thread of the room's original purpose. It is not alive by any biological definition but it behaves like life: it persists, it cycles, it interacts with its environment (the thermal expansion of pipes, the pressure in lines, the slow drainage of power from whatever source still feeds it). It will die when its components fail. It is dying now, in the same tempo as the room—slowly, incrementally, a fraction of a cycle shorter each year as wear accumulates in bearings that will never be replaced.

The dark lives here—not as an organism but as a condition that has achieved the weight and persistence of an organism. It occupies space. It endures. It consumes (light, sound, heat) and it grows (as the infrastructure decays, as the phosphors dim, as the room's connections to the living building above fray one by one). It is what the room is becoming, and it is already here, already the dominant presence, the thing that most completely inhabits the space.

And the moss lives here. Three patches, each no larger than a hand, each an impossible green that insists on itself in a room of iron-black and rust-orange and the grey of accumulated grime. The moss is not the least of these residents—it is the most specific, the most adapted, the one that has found the exact condition where the Sub-Furnace's hostility becomes habitable. It requires precisely the wet and the warm that exist here and nowhere else in the building. It is the proof that life can find the margin between too hot and too dry and make it home. It is the most hopeful thing in the room, if hope is the right word for something that does not hope but simply persists.

---

You are the temporary resident. You came through the breach, you will leave through the breach (or through some other gap you have not yet found), and your time in the room will be a rounding error in its history. The rat-things will avoid the places you've touched for days after you're gone. The moss will not notice you. The process will pulse on its cycle regardless. The dark will close behind you as you move through it, filling the volume your body displaced, absorbing the light you carried, returning to the equilibrium that preceded you.

You are the fast thing, the description said. That is what it feels like from the room's perspective: a brief warm blur passing through, a disturbance in the air stratification, a sound that arrives and departs in the same moment. From your perspective, of course, it is different. From your perspective, you are the one who is real, and the room is the thing you're moving through, and the rat-things are shadows, and the process is a hum, and the dark is an absence, and the moss is just moss.

But the room has its own perspective. And from that perspective, you are the ephemeral one—the one who changes and leaves, who carries light that doesn't belong here, who makes sounds that don't repeat, who passes through in seconds what the room takes years to traverse. You are the ghost. You are the one who will be forgotten first.

The rat-things will remember the shape of your footsteps longer than the room will. The moss will not remember you at all.
