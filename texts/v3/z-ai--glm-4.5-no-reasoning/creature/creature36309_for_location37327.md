What lives on the Fume Plateau has forgotten that it is alive.

Or rather—it has forgotten the distinction between *being alive* and *simply continuing*. Life here has collapsed into a single, shared strategy: persist. It is a strategy that requires no joy, no display, no sound. It requires only patience, and a willingness to be dark.

---

**The runners** are the plateau's largest claim on animation, and they make this claim quietly.

They are built for the stone. Long-limbed and low-slung, their bodies are arranged like hinges—everything folded, everything close to the ground, everything designed to minimize the profile they present to the dim. Their fur is not black by pigment but by omission; color was never selected for because color has no work to do here. Under the iron smear of the sky, black is simply the frequency of survival.

Their eyes are their most telling feature. They reflect nothing because there is nothing to reflect—but this is not blindness. The runners have simply stopped looking upward. Their eyes face down, angled toward the stone, reading warmth. They navigate the miles of bare rock by the thermal gradients in its surface: the faint heat-signature of a vent's edge, the cool seam of a crack, the particular warmth of another body moving fifty yards ahead. They read the plateau like a blind reader reads braille— fingertips pressed to raised dots, meaning extracted from touch. Except the runners' fingertips are their footpads, and the dots are degrees of warmth, and the meaning is *water here* or *food here* or *others here*.

They move in single file. This is not hierarchy; it is efficiency. The lead runner finds the warm path, and each runner behind follows the thermal trail left by the one before, adding its own heat to the channel, deepening it. After a dozen runners have passed, the stone holds a temporary line of warmth—a path that will fade in minutes but which, for those minutes, is the plateau's closest approximation of a road.

They feed on the black-green mats in the ravines, cropping with broad, silent mouths. They drink by pressing their muzzles to the condensation-sweated stone and waiting—sometimes for hours—while the moisture beads against their lips, accumulates, and finally, grudgingly, transfers. There is no lapping here. No urgency. Drinking is a negotiation between the stone's dampness and the runner's patience, and the runner always wins because the runner has nothing else to do and nowhere else to be.

They do not call. They do not bark or howl or bay. Sound costs energy, and energy costs food, and food is scarce enough that no runner can afford to broadcast its location to the dark. Instead, they feel each other through the stone—a footfall transmitted as vibration, a body-heat felt through the rock as a distant warmth. When a runner dies, the others know it by the cessation of its heat-signature: a cold spot that appears in the stone and slowly, over hours, equilibrates with the surrounding rock. They do not investigate. They note the absence and adjust their path.

The runners mate in the condensation-hours, when the stone is wettest and their bodies are most comfortable. The young are born able to read warmth—they must be, because the alternative is walking off the ravine's edge and falling twenty feet onto stone. There is no childhood on the plateau. There is only the instant transition from carried to walking, and then the long, silent filing across the rock in the dim.

---

**The burrowers** live at the moisture line—in the soft, compromised stone where the damp meets the dry, where the vent-exhaust has chemically weathered the rock just enough to be excavable.

They are small. Largest among them might fill a human's palm, and most are smaller—thumb-sized, finger-sized, knuckle-sized. Their bodies are dense and low, built for pressing through tunnels, and their limbs are short and arranged in the configuration of things that push: strong forelimbs, wide feet, compact bodies that can reverse direction in a space barely wider than themselves. They are covered in a fine, dark down—not fur, not quite, but something between hair and filament—that traps the condensation against their skin, allowing them to drink by simply existing in the damp hours.

They emerge when the stone sweats. In the condensation-cycle, they surface from their tunnels to feed on the black-green growths' spores, which are released in the damp air in quantities invisible to the eye but dense enough to coat the burrowers' down in a fine, organic meal. They eat constantly for the two or three hours of surface-damp, storing what they can in internal pouches, and then they retreat below as the warmth pulls the moisture from the air and the stone dries.

They mate in their tunnels. They die in their tunnels. Their bodies, when they die, are absorbed by the stone over weeks—the soft parts consumed by the same spores they fed on, the hard parts slowly dissolving into the moisture that seeps through the rock. Nothing is wasted. The burrowers are a circuit: they eat the spores, the spores eat them, and the stone holds the residue until it becomes indistinguishable from the mineral veins threading the plateau's layers.

---

**The insects**—or the things that occupy the insect-niche—are the plateau's most deliberate residents.

Carapaced in dark chitin, they move with a slowness that seems calculated. There is nothing skittering about them, nothing of the frantic energy that characterizes insects in brighter places. Here, movement is expensive, and the chitin-things move only when movement serves a purpose: toward warmth, toward food, toward mate, toward shelter. Each step is placed. Each turn of the body is taken with an apparent awareness of the thermal landscape—the warm zones, the cool zones, the zones where a vent's draft makes the air navigable by its scent.

They are drawn to the vents. The vents exhale warmth and organic compounds—residues of whatever the industry below is processing—and the chitin-things collect at the vents' rims, feeding on the chemical deposits that build up there. They are the plateau's scavengers, cleaning the vents' lips of the mineral buildup that would, unchecked, eventually seal them. In this, they serve the industry below—keeping the exhalation channels clear—though whether this is symbiosis or coincidence is impossible to say. The plateau does not clarify its arrangements.

They communicate by vibration. A tapping of the carapace against stone, a frequency of clicks that travels through the rock faster and farther than any sound could travel through the thick air. The chitin-things have an organ—a dense, chitinous plate at the joint of their thorax—that both produces and receives these vibrations. They are always listening, always feeling the stone for the patterns of their kin. In the condensation-hours, the plateau's stone carries a complex music of chitin-clicks: mating signals, territorial claims, warnings of predators, navigational information shared between individuals who will never see each other but who are, nonetheless, in constant contact through the rock.

They are food for the hunters.

---

**The hunters** are quick, low, and leg-heavy.

They live in the ravines, in the spaces between the black-green mats and the stone, in the cracks where moisture lingers and prey congregates. They have too many legs—not six, not eight, but a variable number that seems to differ between individuals, as if the plateau's design specifications were loose on this point. Seven legs. Nine legs. Eleven legs, in one observed specimen. The legs are arranged in a radial pattern around a low, flat body, and they move in a coordination that suggests distributed intelligence—each leg feeling the stone independently, each leg making micro-adjustments based on the vibration it receives.

They hunt by stillness. They position themselves along the thermal trails that their prey uses—the paths of warmth that the chitin-things travel between nest and vent—and they wait. Their bodies cool to the ambient temperature of the stone, becoming thermally invisible. When a prey-item passes within range, they strike—not with speed, exactly, but with *suddenness*, a rapid deployment of all those legs in a single coordinated pounce that covers the distance in a blur of dark chitin. The kill is silent. The consumption is immediate. And then the hunter settles back into the stone, cools its body, and waits again.

They can sense vibration through the stone at considerable distances. A burrower moving through its tunnel twenty feet away is detectable; a chitin-thing clicking its thorax thirty feet away is legible. The hunters' vibration-sense is acute enough that they can distinguish prey from conspecifics, mate from rival, and—most importantly—the steady background thrum of the industry below from the specific vibrations of living things. They have learned to filter out the plateau's constant hum. They hear only the anomalies: the movements, the clicks, the footfalls that break the steady pattern of the stone's vibration. In a world of constant sound, they listen for silence-in-motion—the specific absence that indicates a body passing through the thrum.

---

**The shapes above**—the things that circle in the haze—are the plateau's most ambiguous residents.

Whether they are birds is an open question. They have wings; this much is visible in silhouette, when they cross the slightly-brighter smear of the sky. But their flight is strange—silent, slow, seemingly effortless, as if the thick air were not a medium to be pushed through but a substance to be rested upon. They ride the vents' thermal columns in spirals that carry them up into the haze, disappearing into the brown-dark, and then they fold—drop—fall in a controlled silence toward the ravines, where they hunt the burrowers and the slow chitin-things that have emerged for the condensation-cycle.

Their bodies are adapted for the dim. Large, apparently, though distance and darkness make size difficult to judge. Dark-colored, certainly—dark enough that they are more often perceived as an absence than a presence, a gap in the haze where something blocked the faint light for a moment. Their eyes, if they have eyes, are directed downward; they hunt by seeing the thermal signatures of their prey against the cooler stone, reading the ravines as maps of warmth where bright spots indicate bodies and dark spots indicate rock.

They make no sound. Their flight produces no wind, no whisper of wing against air. They kill in silence and they eat in silence and they mate—presumably—in silence, though no one has observed it. They are known primarily by their effect: the sudden absence of a burrower from its tunnel-entrance, the sudden thinning of the chitin-things at a vent's rim. They are the plateau's invisible harvesters, taking what they need and leaving no trace but absence.

---

**And then there is the growth itself**—the black-green mats in the ravines, the only vegetation for a hundred miles.

To call them plants is to invoke a category that does not quite fit. They are photosynthetic, or something analogous—they absorb the dim light that reaches the plateau and convert it to the chemical energy that sustains them. But they do not grow toward light. They grow toward moisture, spreading flat against the stone in fractal patterns that maximize surface contact with the damp rock. Their surfaces are matte and nearly black, optimized for absorbing whatever degraded illumination the iron sky provides; their undersides are slightly lighter, slightly warmer, pressed against the stone to draw warmth from the industry's waste heat below.

They are not individuals. Each mat is a colony—a connected network of similar cells that shares resources across its extent. When the condensation comes, the entire mat drinks simultaneously through its whole surface, swelling subtly, softening from stiff to flexible, becoming temporarily Vital. In this state, it releases spores: microscopic propagules carried on the damp air that rises from the ravines at dusk, dispersing across the plateau to settle in other creases, other cracks, other folds where moisture might collect. Most die on the dry stone. A few find purchase. The mats expand by millimeters per year.

The older colonies develop phosphorescence—a faint, gray-green glow that is barely perceptible even after prolonged dark-adjustment. This is not display. It is chemistry: a byproduct of the colony's metabolic processes, a slow oxidation of the vent-exhaust compounds that the mats absorb from the air. The phosphorescence is, in effect, the mats *processing* the industry's waste—converting sulfur and carbon compounds into the light that they themselves cannot see. They are the plateau's lungs, breathing in the exhaust, breathing out a glow so faint it barely exists.

The dead mats become soil. Thin, dark layers of organic residue that accumulate in the ravine-bottoms, mixed with the mineral dust that the wind carries and the chemical compounds that the vents deposit. This soil supports the next generation of mats, which grow on the graves of their predecessors, which grew on the graves of theirs. The ravines are graveyards and nurseries simultaneously—places where the dead sustain the living in a cycle so slow that a human lifetime would see only the thinnine layering of new upon old.

---

**What lives on the Fume Plateau, ultimately, is a community of the patient.**

Not a community in the sense of interaction—there is no council here, no gathering, no social organization beyond the thermal-file of the runners and the vibration-network of the chitin-things. But a community in the sense of *co-dependence*: each species occupying a niche defined by the others, each process feeding into the next, the whole system sustained by the plateau's peculiar equilibrium of stone and warmth and damp and dim.

The runners crop the mats and spread their spores on their fur. The burrowers feed on the spores and are eaten by the hunters. The chitin-things clean the vents and are eaten by the shapes above. The mats process the industry's exhaust and provide food for the runners and substrate for the burrowers. The hunters cull the burrowers and the chitin-things, preventing overpopulation. The shapes above cull the hunters, perhaps—or perhaps the hunters are simply too scarce and too still to be found from the air. The plateau does not clarify its food webs.

And beneath it all, the industry runs—indifferent, enormous, ancient—providing the warmth and the exhaust and the vibration that makes the stone a medium for communication. The industry does not know that the plateau supports life. The life does not know that the industry is industrial. They have coexisted for so long that each is simply a condition of the other: the industry a source of heat, the life a thin film of organization on the skin of the stone. The distinction between natural and artificial has collapsed here, not because either has triumphed, but because both have persisted long enough to become inseparable.

What lives on the Fume Plateau is what has learned to live on a roof. What has learned to drink condensation and eat spores and navigate by warmth and communicate by vibration and hunt by stillness and survive by darkness. What has learned, above all, that the roof will not change—that the thrum will continue, that the vents will breathe, that the sky will stay iron, that the light will stay dim, and that the only strategy that matters is the one that allows you to be here tomorrow in the same form you are today.

They are not humble. Humility implies a comparison to something greater, and the plateau's residents do not compare. They simply are—dark and quiet and patient, making their modest way across the skin of something enormous, eating what the stone provides, drinking what the air deposits, and waiting, always waiting, through the long warm dim, for the condensation to come.
