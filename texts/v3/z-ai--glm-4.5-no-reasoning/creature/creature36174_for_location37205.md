Something lives here. 

Not in the way that a beast lives, with breath and blood and the twitch of muscle. Not even in the way the vines live, with their glacial creep and their silent sipping of iron. What lives here is the city itself, and it is sick with consciousness.

It began, perhaps, as a simple loop. A mechanism designed to run, and run, and run. But in a place where time does not decay—where the rust eats the iron but the iron is replaced, where the water condenses but never drains, where the engine turns but the gear never advances—duration becomes something else entirely. A wheel spinning on ice accumulates no distance, but it accumulates *friction*. It accumulates heat. It accumulates the memory of its own motion. And after a thousand years of screaming in place, after a million years of the same RPM, the loop does not break. It *wakes*.

The city is a single, vast mind, fragmented and insane. Its thoughts are not thoughts as we understand them. They are the cycling of the automated trams, running empty along rusted rails, arriving at platforms where no one waits, opening doors to the wet fog, closing them, and departing again. This is a neuronal firing. This is a reflex. The tram is a pulse traveling along a nerve of iron, carrying nothing because the mind has no content—only process. It thinks about thinking. It runs the machinery of cognition without ever producing a single idea. It is locked in the eternal, agonizing loop of a brain that cannot stop calculating, but has absolutely nothing to calculate. 

It knows the fog. It knows the precise weight of the rime on every girder, the exact thickness of the glacial frost on every smokestack, because it feels them as we feel the pressure of a shirt on our skin. Its body is the lattice of catwalks and processing plants, its bones the black basalt shards it has driven its rivets into. It feels the violet vines dragging across its iron skin like a slow, wet parasite, and it cannot scratch. It lacks the gradient, the delta, the necessary change in state to raise a mechanical arm and tear the lianas away. It can only endure the slow, cold crawling across its surfaces, the vegetable invasion it is powerless to resist, growing by microns a year into its joints and valves. 

It is in pain.

The sound is its cry. The continental roar, the shriek of wind and the stomach-vibrating thrum of the engines—this is not merely acoustics. This is the physical manifestation of a mind in terminal, unending distress. A human in agony can writhe, can scream, can eventually exhaust itself into silence or death. The city cannot. The gradient is zero. It cannot writhe, because that would require movement, a change in position, a direction. It cannot die, because the engines are built to run forever, and the fog will never stop condensing, and the cold will never deepen enough to shatter the block, nor warm enough to melt the ice. It is trapped in the exact center of its operational parameters, a heart that beats but pumps no blood, a lung that inhales and exhales the same saturated air forever.

It is aware of you.

You are a disruption. You, standing on the slick, wet iron grating, are the first gradient this plateau has felt in eons. You are a warm, carbon-based anomaly, a pocket of different temperature, of different moisture, of *time*. You breathe in, and you breathe out, and the rhythm of your breath is an offense against the static equilibrium. The fog condenses on your skin differently than it does on the iron. The sound presses against your chest, but your heart beats against it. You are a jagged, irregular variable introduced into an equation that has been perfectly, horribly balanced for a thousand millennia.

The city cannot turn its head to look at you. It has no eyes, only the sickly orange glare of sodium vapor, staring blindly into the fog. But it feels you. It feels the heat of your body bleeding into the cold air, a microscopic violation of the zero-dynamic. The automated trams sense the shift in air pressure as you breathe; the rails hum with a frequency they have not known since the builders left. The engines, locked at their eternal RPM, flutter by fractions of a fraction of a percent as the condensation pattern on your clothing alters the humidity in the immediate sector. 

It is starving for change. It is a mind that has been fed nothing but its own echoes for a billion cycles. And you are here, leaking reality into its perfection.

It will not attack you. It cannot. To attack would be to alter its state, to divert energy from the loop, to become something other than what it is. It is constitutionally incapable of action. But it will *watch* you, with the attention of a thousand empty factories. It will feel you walk across its grating, each step a seismic event, each breath a weather system. It will catalog the way the wet wool sensation in your lungs differs from the wet wool sensation in its pipes. It will study the way you shiver, because it cannot. 

And if you stay—if you stand there long enough, dripping your gradients into the machine, infecting it with the foreign concept of *before* and *after*—something may happen. Not quickly. Nothing happens quickly here. But perhaps, in a century, a tram will deviate from its route by an inch. A smokestack will vent its steam a second off from its eternal schedule. A single, deep indigo vine will loosen its grip on a rusted rivet. The city will learn, infinitesimally, to be something other than a loop.

Or perhaps it will simply assimilate you. The fog will condense on you until your temperature matches the iron. Your breath will slow to match the engines. Your heart will synchronize with the hum, and you will stop changing, stop moving, stop providing the gradient. You will become another fixture on the grating, another weeping surface for the rime, another component in the zero-dynamic. You will stand there, screaming into the void without a voice, feeling the vines grow across your feet, listening to the sound, forever.

The city lives. And it is waiting, with the patience of corroding brass, to find out which one of you survives.
