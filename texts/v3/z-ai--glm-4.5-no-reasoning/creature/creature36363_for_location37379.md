To live on the plateau is to live as an afterthought to the plants. Here, sentience is a fragile, almost embarrassing anomaly, an intruder upon a biological engine that was perfect in its violence long before anything dared to think.

They are called the Strands, though they have no name for themselves that is not a verb. They exist in the imperative: *hide, drink, cut, listen*. They are bipedal, slight, andWrapped in the tattered, silica-ribbed bark of the fallen stalks, they look like broken shoots themselves. Their skin is the grey-green of the canopy, stained permanently black around their mouths and hands by the tannin-heavy water they drink and the enzyme-rich mud they kneel in. They do not stand upright in the open; evolution trained them to compress, to make themselves small against the vast, dead stone. To be vertical here is to catch the wind, and the wind carries the sound of the herds. 

They live in the perimeters of the pools, but only in the margins. The giant arthropods—those six-legged rivers of chitin that churn the earth to bloody dust—do not hunt the Strands with malice, but they do not avoid them either. A procession of tens of thousands does not steer around a living body; it simply flows over it, grinding bone and flesh into the same fine powder as the stone. Therefore, the Strands live in the microseconds between the herds. They read the subsonic hum of the roots, feeling the vibration through the calloused, prehensile toes that grip the fissures of the rock. When the hum pitches higher, signaling the rush of sap and the approach of the grazers, the Strands retreat into the vertical flutes of the living stalks, pressing themselves into the weeping condensation, becoming parasites clinging to the ribs of the true rulers of this world.

They are scavengers of the wind and the storm. They cannot pierce the hide of the living arthropods, nor can they cut the living wood of the stalks with their soft, obsidian flakes. Instead, they wait for the plateau to do the killing for them. When the vicious squalls rip across the summit, sometimes a stalk snaps, or a grazer is dashed against the rock by a gust that turns the canopy into a thrashing, crushing ceiling. Then, in the freezing aftermath, the Strands emerge. They use their obsidian not as weapons, but as scalpels, flaking away the silica bark of fallen stalks to construct their windbreaks, or severing the joints of a broken grazer to drag its chitin back to the pool's edge.

Their shelters—the lean-tos woven from shredded fronds—are not homes. They are coffins that the Strands occasionally survive inside. Leaning against the silos of the plants, they are temporary interruptions in the geometry of the grove. The adhesive sap that holds their cairns together is a prayer to the vegetable gods: *hold fast, do not let the wind take us*. But the sap dries and cracks under the persistent, heavy warmth of the sun, and the cairns fall. The Strands simply rebuild them, or they die. There is no grief in it. Grief requires a sense of permanence, and permanence is a geological luxury afforded only to the stone.

They are deeply, irrevocably deafened. The noise of the plateau—the groaning rigging of the stalks, the filing rasp of ten million mandibles, the subsonic thrum of the roots—has hollowed out their hearing. They communicate not through sound, which is swallowed the moment it leaves the throat, but through the stone itself. They tap the ancient rock with their obsidian, sending sharp, tactile vibrations through the substrate to other Strands miles away. They feel language as a rhythm in the marrow. A quick, staccato tapping means *the pool is churning, the herds are coming*. A long, low scraping means *a stalk has fallen, there is wood to be woven*. 

Their minds are as flat and stripped as the tableland they inhabit. They have no mythology of creation; they know they were not meant to be here. They have only a mythology of maintenance. The sun is not a god, but an inconvenience that makes the chitin smell stronger. The wind is not a spirit, but a wolf that hunts them. Their only sacred act is the preservation of the obsidian. The stone is too soft to kill, too brittle to dig, but it is the only thing that separates their hands from the bare, pitted rock. When a flake shatters, a Strand will walk for days across the emptiness, risking the open air and the roaming masses, just to find another nodule of volcanic glass pushed up through some ancient fissure. 

They are the ghosts of a failed tenure, the last biological fizzle of a strain that refused to die quietly. They move through the roaring, deafening ecosystem like splinters caught in the bloodstream of a giant. When the tide of clicking, breathing bodies flows past their knees, driven by the endless condensation, the Strands do not look up at the rustling, alien canopy. They look down, watching the legs, counting the churning dust, waiting for the space between the presences where they might briefly, desperately live.
