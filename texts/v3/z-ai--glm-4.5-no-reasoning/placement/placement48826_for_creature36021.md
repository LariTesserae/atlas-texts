I would not be one of the sweeping, broad-handed tenders of the trenches. I lack the constitutional patience for maintenance, the metabolic stillness required to stand in the cold fog and press moss into cracks for a thousand afternoons. And I am not a pale salamander, slipping through the wet dark on instinct alone. 

If I am honest, I have always been drawn to the edge of things—not to fall, but to listen to the vast, hollow resonance that only a precipice can produce. So I would find myself at the periphery of the Water-Bearing Stone, where the plateau tears itself apart.

I would be a creature of the weep-faces. The vertical cliffs that bound the tableland are streaked with the plateau's perpetual bleeding, and it is there—suspended between the sprawling, silent fog of the top and the immeasurable, drying void of the basin below—that I would exist. I am not built for the horizontal. I am built for the downpour and the cling.

Let me see myself: I have the general morphology of something that once had legs but has long since stopped needing them. My body is a flattened, sinuous O, perhaps the length of a man's arm but narrow, no thicker than a worn leather belt, composed mostly of cartilage and dense, overlapping folds of skin. That skin is not pale like the trench-dwellers', nor is it slate-gray. It is the color of the oil-black moss, but darker—a slick, lightless obsidian that drinks the ambient amber glow of the afternoon sun and refuses to return it. It is always wet. Not with the cold sweat of the stone, but with a constant, oily secretion of my own making. 

I am a gecko of the weeping rock, though "gecko" is a word born of dry, warm places, and it does not fit here. I navigate the vertical sheets of water that cascade down the cliff faces. My feet—my four wide, splayed pads—are my world. They are not covered in the bristles of a desert lizard; they are smooth, fleshy suction discs rimmed with a ring of cilia that respond not to dry friction, but to viscosity. I do not grip the stone; I grip the water on the stone. I press a pad to the vertical sheet of a weep, and the cilia spin, interlocking with the surface tension of the flowing water, creating a seal that turns the relentless downward drag of the stream into my anchor. I can walk upside down beneath an overhang, suspended entirely by the thin, crying film of the plateau's blood. 

When the freeze comes at night, I do not retreat into the rock. I find a shallow depression where the water flows thickest and slowest, and I press my entire body flat against it. The water freezes around me, encasing me in a shell of translucent ice, locking me to the cliff. I do not freeze solid; my blood is thick with salts drawn from the ancient sandstone, and my core slows to a cold, patient throb. I hang there, a dark smudge in the ice, listening to the plateau creak and groan as it expands. When the sun—which rarely shows its face with any conviction—finally burns a hole in the cloud base and the thaw begins, the ice loosens its grip, and I drop an inch, catching myself on the newly weeping stone, and continue my descent.

I eat the cliff's breath. As the water bleeds from the porous sandstone, it carries with it the dissolved minerals of eons, and upon this rich, wet exhaust, the pale lichens and the microscopic mosses grow in a thin, chalky fuzz at the very edge of the weeps. I am the grazer of this vertical pasture. My jaw is a narrow, scraping blade, lined not with teeth, but with a hard, chitinous edge, like the sole of a cobbler's plane. I press my face to the stone and drag it upward, shirring the wet lichen-crust from the rock, swallowing it along with the cold, metallic water. I eat in long, slow, continuous sweeps, moving my head as I cling with my hindquarters, painting a clean, dark stripe of bare rock behind me. By the time the fog rolls back in to obscure the cliff, my grazing marks are gone, swallowed by the condensing mist and the weeping water.

I am solitary. I do not click like the crabs, nor hum like the people. My silence is absolute. But I am deeply attuned to the acoustic liturgy of the plateau. I hear the tone of the water falling. A thin, high tinkle means the air is clearing, the water is losing its volume, and the rock is drying. I must move lower, following the retreating wetness. A low, bass thrum—the sound of a weep-hole surging with the upwelling aquifer—means the fog is thickening, the saturation is total, and I can climb, moving up the vertical faces into the coldest, wettest mists. My entire life is a slow, diagonal migration, following the moisture across the face of the world.

The people of the trenches know of me, though they have never seen me clearly. They are the listeners of the horizontal, and I am the whisper of the vertical. Occasionally, when a particularly deep trench breaks through to the edge of the plateau, opening a window to the vast drop beyond, they will press their broad, flattened hands to the weeping wall. And sometimes, faintly, they will feel a vibration through the stone—a specific, rhythmic drag and release, drag and release. It is me, grazing on the lichen just on the other side of the rock, separated from them by a few feet of saturated sandstone and an entire universe of orientation. To them, it is just another nuance in the water's pulse, a slight variation in the plateau's endless breath. They do not know it is a life suspended by water, clinging to the edge of the world, eating the stone's patience, one wet scrape at a time.
