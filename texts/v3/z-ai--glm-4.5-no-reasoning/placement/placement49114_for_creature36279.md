I am the Errant, the Asymmetry, the breath in the lung that does not need air.

I do not have a name for myself, because names require a mirror—another mind to reflect the sound back and seal the contract of identity. Here, there are no mirrors. The water that runs down the vast, architectural leaves is too dispersed to hold a reflection; the mist erases all boundary between my shape and the space around me. I know my edges only by the ache of them.

I am bipedal, though the posture feels like an insult to the geometry of this place. The Bower is built of arches and catenary curves, of hexagonal lattices and perfect, load-bearing cylinders. I am a collection of irregular angles—hinged joints that crack and swell, a spine that curves with fatigue, a skull balanced precariously on a stalk of cartilage. I am the antithesis of the engineering around me. I walk on two feet, and every step is a small, clumsy rebellion against the static equilibrium.

My body is wrapped in a dense, waterlogged pelt. It is the color of old peat, though I only know this from the rare moments I hold a shed strand up to the bottle-glass light. The fur is perpetually plastered to my skin. Water beads on my brow, runs down the bridge of my nose, drips from my chin in exact mimicry of the weeping leaves. The Bower perspires; I perspire. But its moisture is function—channeling, pumping, distributing. Mine is a symptom of exertion, of decay, of the heat generated by a metabolism desperate to keep its own cells from unraveling. I am always wet. My skin is soft and pale where the fur thins, macerated by the endless condensation. Sometimes I press my cheek against the bark of the trunk-columns just to feel something relatively dry, to leach a moment of comfort from the lignified skin of the machine.

I have eyes. They are my greatest torment. The pale oligochaetes in the pipes have dispensed with sight—evolved down to mere living grout, feeling only the vibration of the current. I wish I could do the same. My eyes are large, dark, and constantly strained, dilated in the diffuse greenish glow. They seek edges, horizons, variations in light, but the Bower offers none. The light does not sharpen; it fills the space like a liquid. I walk for hours across the petiole-bridges, straining to see the next living buttress through the haze, and my vision blurs, tears mixing with the condensation. I have developed a chronic ache behind my orbits, a tension that comes from looking for pattern in a landscape that is all pattern, and therefore no pattern at all. I see the mathematical perfection of the vascular lattices, the exact degree of the leaf-angles, and it makes me nauseous. It is the nausea of being the only broken thing in an unbreakable machine.

I eat the grout. It is the only way. I crouch over the dark openings of the water-channels, pressing my face to the cold, wet rims, and I lap at the flow. The pale, eyeless oligochaetes wriggle past my tongue—hundreds of them, blind threads of peristalsis, gripping and releasing, gripping and releasing. They taste of nothing but water and a faint, watery slime, a ghost of protein. I crunch them between my molars, which are flat and worn down from a lifetime of gnawing at a world that was never meant to be chewed. When the hunger becomes a sharper ache—when the slimy grout will not quiet the burning in my gut—I turn to the bark. I scrape my teeth against the lignified stalks, tearing off fibrous strips of cellulose that resist my digestive acids for days. I eat the architecture. I am a parasite feeding on the machine, and the machine does not notice.

I shed. This is the horror I cannot escape. I leave behind a trail of anomalies—strands of peat-colored fur, flakes of keratin, the scuffed prints of my wet, clumsy feet on the waxy leaf-planes. The Bower does not consume them. There are no decomposers here, no fungi to rot them, no beetles to carry them away. They lie where they fall, foreign artifacts trapped on the perfect surfaces. I sometimes look back along a bridge I have crossed and see my hair clinging to the cuticle, wet and still. It is a desecration. I am contaminating a closed system. The thought sends me walking faster, trying to outrun my own mess, but I only shed more. I am an open wound, bleeding debris into a sterile operating room.

I do not speak. I tried, once. In the early days, before the weight of the silence crushed the instinct out of me, I stood on a spiraling rhizome-causeway and opened my throat. I let out a cry—a raw, shapeless sound, a plea for any other changing thing to hear me. The Bower swallowed it instantly. The polyphonic roar of the water—the millions of capillary streams, the resonant thrum of the pipe organ played by gravity—drowned my voice before it traveled a meter. The sound died in the mist. I felt the vibration of my own cry in my sternum, and then it was gone, absorbed into the thunder. I have not tried again. My mind has turned inward, echoing only its own thoughts. I am a solipsist by force, trapped in a dialogue with a body that is slowly failing.

Sleep is a risk. I curl against the hexagonal lattice of a trunk-wall, my spine aligned with the perfect geometry, my ribs expanding and contracting against the bark. I feel the water pumping without a pump beside me, the sap cycling in its endless, mindless loop. I close my eyes, and the roar becomes a presence, a weight pressing on my chest. When I wake, I know no time has passed. The light is the same diffuse green. The mist is the same density. A drop of water falls from the same leaf-tip, hits the same root-channel with the same crystalline plink. I cannot tell if I slept for an hour or a year. The only evidence of my passage is the weariness in my limbs, the faint increase in the ache behind my eyes, the new strand of hair plastered to my forearm. I am the only clock in a world without seconds, and my mechanism is running down.

I walk. I have no destination. The Bower is built for a population that does not exist, and I am a population of one, wandering the empty metropolis. I cross the bridges wide enough for six, feeling the vast, hollow space around me, the loneliness of the architecture. I look out over the haze and the distant, hazy silhouettes of the next living buttresses, and I seek something I cannot name. A dry hollow. A variation in the light. A reflection of my own mutability. But the Bower offers nothing. It is fully built, fully occupied by its own function, completely and utterly empty.

I am the error in the equation. I am the open wound. I am the breath in the lung that does not need air. And when my mechanism finally stops—when my cells can no longer sustain the rebellion against equilibrium—I will fall across a catenary arch, and the water will run over me, and the condensation will bead on my downward-facing surfaces. My bones will dissolve into the weeping green, becoming a tiny spike of calcium and phosphate flowing through the dark pipes, an infinitesimal defect in the latticework. And the Bower will go on, idling forever in neutral, unmarked and unmarking, a machine that has forgotten it was alive, and will never remember that I was here.
