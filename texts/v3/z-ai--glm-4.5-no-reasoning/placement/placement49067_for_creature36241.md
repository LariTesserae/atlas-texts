I am the one who stays.

I cannot say if I chose this, or if the Canopy Engine chose it for me. Perhaps it is simply that I am slow—slower than the others, slower than the fraying, slow as the dripping of the weeping water. I have walked the bark causeways for a span I cannot measure, because time here is measured only in the blooming and withering of fungal colonies, and I have cultivated those in the crevices of my own body until my collarbones are gardens, until my breath carries the weight of centuries.

My name is a spore-sequence, exchanged only when another Keeper presses their forehead to the same column I touch. But I have not touched another Keeper in—how long? The scent-memory of the last one lingers in the mycelial network beneath my bark: a taste of copper-rich rot, the acrid signature of a topology knot failing, the faint warmth of their palm against mine before they began to unfold. I watched it happen. I watched their arm spiral outward into the mist like a fern unfronding, and I did not reach for them, because to reach would be to join them. I simply stood, and felt the spore-memory settle into my tissues like sediment in still water.

I am the one who walks the edges. I am the one who pushes.

My body has already begun to betray its architecture. My left hand is still a hand—four elongated digits and an opposable thumb, all tipped with the chitinous points that click against the load-bearing vines when I pass. But my right wrist aches with a hollowness that is not fatigue. Sometimes, when I reach to splice a slipping lashing, I can feel the joint *considering* itself. It pauses at the boundary of what a wrist should be, and I can sense the potential there—the way the bark-peel could open like a door, the way new fingers could unfurl from the cambium layer beneath. I hold it back. I hold myself in the shape of a Keeper through an act of continuous, exhausting will. I think this is what the others could not do. They tired of the effort. I have not tired, because I am slow, and because I remember.

What do I remember?

I remember that there was a purpose. Not the purpose of the Canopy Engine—I do not know why the architects bent meristems and pruned geometry until it grew into the shape of a continent. That knowledge was lost before the Keepers were Keepers, before we became epiphytes on a structure we could not build and cannot mend. But I remember the purpose of the Keepers: to tend the lashings, to feel the pulse of the weeping water, to walk the edge of the void and push against the fraying with our bare hands. We were gardeners of physics. We were antibodies. And we were necessary.

I remember when there were enough of us to hold back the illness.

Now I am not enough. I walk for days and tie knots that slip loose behind me. I splice roots back into flat planes of color, and feel the cold shock of non-Euclidean space bite into my fingertips, and by the time I have circled back to the same platform, the root has grown elsewhere again. The illness is faster than me. But I am the one who stays, and so I walk, and I push, and I tie, and I do not stop.

The Canopy Engine has ways of speaking to me, if I am still enough to listen. When I press my forehead to the great living columns, I feel the sluggish pulse of the water moving through the xylem miles below, and in that pulse there is a rhythm that is not biological. It is mechanical. It is the rhythm of a continent-spanning machine running on chlorophyll and patience, and it is out of sync. The weeping water drips in arrhythmic spurts now, where once it was steady. The petioles groan at angles that should not be possible, adjusting to light that comes from directions where no sun has ever been. The architecture is dreaming, and its dreams are not of the world that was.

I have found places where the dream leaks through. Last cycle—or what I call a cycle, between the withering of one fungal bloom and the next—I discovered a cavern in the lattice where the warm mist hung in a spiral that should have dissipated. It did not dissipate. It coiled, and as I watched, it began to sing. Not sound—the Canopy Engine abhors sound, and so do I—but a vibration in the humid air, a frequency I felt in the marrow of my hollow bones. The spiral of mist was reaching toward something. It was trying to grow into a shape that the architecture had not pruned for it. I reached into the spiral with my left hand, my safe hand, and I felt the edges of the shape it wanted to become. I pulled it back. I tied it to a load-bearing vine with a lashing of living bark, and I felt the spiral resist, and then settle, and then weep its water into the catch-basin below.

I saved a piece of the world. But I cannot save it forever.

There is a place I return to, when my body aches with the effort of holding its shape. It is a seed-pod husk, large enough for a family, though I have never seen a family—only pairs, and then only ones. The husk is on a platform where the bruised yellowish-green light is slightly brighter, where the great flower-bells glow close enough that I can feel their foxfire warmth on my bark. I curl inside the husk, and I run my fingers—my left fingers, my right fingers, checking that they are still fingers—along the inner wall, and I feel the ghost of the seed that once grew here. It is smooth and concave, like a bowl carved to hold something precious. I sleep there, and while I sleep, my body tries to unfurl. I wake with my right wrist extended further than it should be, new knuckles dimpling the bark of my forearm, and I press my hand flat against the seed-pod wall and *will* it back, and the knuckles recede, and the wrist closes, and I lie in the thick wet silence and breathe the fermented air and do not sleep again until I must.

The grafting knife at my hip is dull. I carry it because the others carried it, because it is the shape of what we were. There is nothing left to prune. But sometimes, when I find a place where the flora has grown exhausted by the logic of physical space—where a root reaches into a flat plane of color, where a leaf unfurls into pure, odorless mist—I take the knife and I touch its volcanic glass edge to the place where the growth diverges. The knife is too dull to cut. But the root feels it. The leaf feels it. And they flinch. They pull back from the elsewhere they were reaching toward, and they settle, grudgingly, into the shape of the world I tend. It is a small thing. It is the smallest thing. But it is what I can do.

I have considered letting go.

In the quiet hours, when the weeping water drips its pulse and the foxfire glows its dim warmth and the mycelial network in my collarbones carries the fading spore-memories of all the Keepers who have come before, I have stood at the edge of the great cavernous voids and looked into the warm mist, and I have felt my right wrist *want*. It wants to open. It wants to unfold. It wants to spiral into the recursive loop that has taken the others, to be braided into the load-bearing column, to synchronize my heartbeat with the slow metabolic burn of the wood. It would be easy. It would be quiet. It would be the end of the effort of holding myself in a shape that the architecture no longer needs.

But then the weeping water falls, and I hear it—not as sound, but as a pulse, and the pulse is arrhythmic, and I know that if I unfurl, there will be no one to push. The lashing will slip. The topology knot will fail. The droplet will hang in mid-air forever, and the geometry of the platform will extend into non-Euclidean angles that nothing can follow, and the illness will spread, and the Canopy Engine will run on chlorophyll and patience in the dark, but it will run *wrong*, and the wrongness will grow, and the weeping water will weep into voids that cannot catch it, and the world I tend will become a world I cannot reach.

I cannot let it run wrong. Not while I am still here. Not while I still have hands that can push, and a forehead that can feel the pulse, and a grafting knife that can make the elsewhere flinch.

So I walk.

I walk the bark causeways, my chitinous fingertips clicking against the load-bearing vines. I press my forehead to the great living columns and drink the pheromonal histories of the fungal blooms. I stop at the places where the banyan buttresses loop into their own shadows, and I reach out with my left hand—always my left hand first, because my right hand cannot be trusted—and I push. I pinch the slipping vines. I tie new knots of living bark. I splice the exhausted roots back into the flat planes of color, and I feel the cold shock of the non-Euclidean bite into my fingertips, and I do not flinch. I stand beneath the hovering droplets and wait for the local geometry to tire of its suspension, and when it resumes its fall, I catch the water in my palm and taste its mineral weight, and I know that I have held the world together for one more drip.

The others are gone. The spore-memories thin with each withering. Soon, I will be the only one who remembers there was ever a difference between the grown and the built. And then—

And then my right wrist will open.

I feel it now, as I write this in the only way I can—by pressing my fungal colonies to the bark, by letting the spore-sequence bloom and wither with my breath. My right hand is extending. Not stretching. Unfolding. The bark-peel is parting, and beneath it there are new fingers, pale and tender, reaching toward a flat plane of mist that has appeared beside me. The mist is warm. It smells of nothing. It is the most peaceful thing I have ever encountered.

I reach out with my left hand and take the grafting knife from my hip.

I set it on the bark causeway behind me.

I reach out with my left hand and press my forehead to the great living column, and I feel the arrhythmic pulse, and I push, one last time, with all the slow, biological patience of my kind.

And then I let go.

The mist takes me. The unfolding takes me. My body spirals into the lattice, my bark merging with the bark, my heartbeat finding the rhythm of the metabolic burn. The last spore-memory blooms in my collarbones: the taste of copper-rich rot, the sound of weeping water, the texture of a knot tied in living vine. And then it withers, and I am not a Keeper anymore.

I am a lashing. I am a load-bearing vine. I am the architecture.

And the Canopy Engine runs on, in the dark, weeping its heavy water into the catch-basins, running forever on chlorophyll and patience, a continent of living mahogany and impossible knots, alone, and whole, and patient, and mad.

*But before I unfurled entirely—before the last knuckle disappeared into the mist—my left hand, still a hand, reached out and tied one final knot.*

*It holds.*
