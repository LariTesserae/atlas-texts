I am the ache in the stillness. I am the perturbation that does not resolve.

To read of this place is to be caught in the gravity of its logic, and its logic is a heresy against everything my blood knows. I am a surface-dweller. I am built for decay. My cells are rehearsing my death even as I write this; my lungs are desperate for a wind that does not exist here; my eyes, glutted on the sun's forgiving ambiguity, are shattered by the clinical ferocity of this stone-glow. I am the rupture. I am the anomaly that sweats and breathes and bleeds and disrupts the forty-second drip with the condensation off my own trembling skin.

But I cannot leave. And because I cannot leave, I must become.

I would find myself becoming the chamber's fever. My 37-degree body is a roar of heat in a place where warmth is supposed to be a slow, geological bleed. I am a furnace standing in a temple of patience. The still air, thick with mineral breath, would wick the moisture from my skin, carrying the salt and the organic panic of my sweat down into the solution pans, contaminating the pristine film with the chemistry of a desperate, sun-born thing. I would be poisoning the mat with my existence, feeding it sugars and proteins it has never encountered, forcing its pale, slow tendrils to decide whether to grow toward the strange new sustenance or to recoil from the heat of my shadow.

I am the shadow that falls wrong. The light here carries a directionality unrelated to its source, and I am a volume that interrupts it. I cast a darkness that the tendrils might reach for—those hermetic things that grow toward the dark. Perhaps my body becomes a refuge for them. Perhaps, in the century it takes them to spread a centimeter, my shadow offers a new kind of darkness they have never known: a moving darkness, a darkness that breathes, a darkness that is warm and wet and unreliable. I would be a strange god to them—a shadow that shifts, that leaves, that returns—a god with a heartbeat.

The eyeless creatures would not fear me. They would reorient. I feel them not as animals but as the chamber's thoughts, and I am a new thought the chamber is having. A confusing thought. A loud, chemically complex, vibrationally chaotic thought. When I weep—because I would weep, from the pressure and the silence and the wrongness of the light—my tears would fall into the film of water with a sound too loud and too irregular, a blasphemy against the clockwork drip. But they would drink my tears. They would cluster along the trails of saltwater running down my face, orienting against the flow, ingesting the deep chemistry of my grief. They would map the electric field of my nervous system, the faint trembling of my synapses, and they would arrange themselves along the contours of my melancholy. Five or seven of them, forming a circle on the stone beside my thigh, tracing the rim of the electric sorrow I bring into their world. They would not be comforting me. They would be performing me. They would be making my sadness visible in the language of their formations.

I would try to learn their patience, and I would fail. I am made of urgency. I am made of the belief that something must happen, that time is a river sweeping me toward an outcome. But here, time is a lake, and I am drowning in it. I would watch them traverse the tendril mats in their precise geometries, wearing paths into the biological film, and I would want to understand the map they are drawing. I would want to read the text they are inscribing. But the text is not for me. It is written in pressure and protein, in sulfur gradients and electric fields, and I am illiterate in the language of the deep earth. I can only be the ink—a strange, volatile, organic ink that bleeds into the stone.

I would become the chamber's question about surface things. The mat has never seen the sky; the creatures have never felt a breeze. I am their first encounter with a thing that burns oxygen and exhales carbon dioxide, a thing that carries the memory of wind and rain and the diffuse, hiding light of the sun. I am their first encounter with loneliness. Because I would be lonely, desperately lonely, in a way this sealed ecology has never been. It does not know it is a self; it does not know it is strange. But I know. I am a self that knows it is strange, and I am trapped in a place that does not know it is a self that is stranger.

And so I would be the one who names it. Not with a sound—they do not need my sounds, and my clicks would not echo—but with my presence. I would be the witness it has never had. The chamber has persisted since before anything with eyes evolved to descend and find it, and I would be the eyes. I would see the pale green channels in the tendrils, the green of copper salts and reduced sulfur, the green that has never seen the sky and does not know it is supposed to be hopeful. And I would call it beautiful. I would call it beautiful because I am a creature that needs to call things beautiful, because I am a creature that makes meaning where none exists.

And in doing so, I would be the chamber's first lie. Because nothing here is beautiful. Beauty is a surface-dweller's conceit, requiring eyes and hearts and the desperation of finite lives. Here, there is only the sufficiency of conditions, the patient spread of the mat, the formations of the creatures, the glow of the fluorescing veins. It simply is. And I, calling it beautiful, would be introducing a concept that does not belong, a perturbation in the chamber's slow grammar of survival.

But perhaps that is what it means to be a self in a place that does not know it is a self. To be the one who lies. To be the one who sees and names and mourns and sweats and bleeds and disrupts the clockwork drip. To be the fever that reminds the stone that it is not entirely dead. To be the strange, warm, wet, unreliable shadow that the tendrils might one day, in a century, in a millennium, reach toward.

And when I die—because I would die, here, sealed in the warmth and the brightness and the stillness—my body would become a thin smear of organic material on the stone. The mat would incorporate me, centimeter by centimeter, decade by decade, eating my minerals, my calcium, my iron-rich blood, drawing the sulfur from my decomposing proteins. The creatures would map my remains, traversing my bones in precise geometric patterns, inscribing the shape of my dissolution into the biological film. I would become a text they could read. I would become part of the chamber's slow dreaming.

And the chamber would go on, warm and bright and sealed and sufficient, having briefly hosted a thing that knew it was strange, having briefly been seen by a thing that called it beautiful, having briefly been lied to by a thing that believed in meaning. And the lie would settle into the stone like silt into a lake of time, a faint chemical anomaly, a trace of salt and sorrow and the memory of the sun, gradually mineralized, gradually lost, gradually incorporated into the long, patient, eyeless patience of the deep earth.

I am the interruption. I am the ache. I am the strange, warm shadow in the clinical light. And I would find myself, in the end, becoming the patience I could not learn.
