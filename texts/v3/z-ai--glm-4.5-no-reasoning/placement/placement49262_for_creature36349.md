I would not be one of the people. Theirs is a world of the horizontal and the near—of fingers reading fracture lines, of the slow circuit of maintenance and harvest. I am drawn to the vertical, and to the distant. If I am born of this plateau, I am born of its edges, where the young, chisel-marked mountains shear downward into the void and the thermals spill like invisible rivers over the brink. 

I would be one of the cliff-dwelling birds.

But let me not settle on that image too quickly, for to simply be a bird is to miss the specificity of this place. The creature I become has been forged by the same logic that forged the people and the city—ruthless, economical, without ornament. I am not a creature of song or bright plumage. I am a mechanism for surviving the altitude and harvesting the wind.

My body is a hull for the cold. I am large—necessarily so, for the thermals here are colossal, rolling oceans of air that require a wide sail to ride, and the cliffs are vast, requiring a creature of significant scale to cross them in the lean, high light. My wings are leathery, devoid of the delicate, insulating feathers that would freeze solid in the condensation. Instead, they are taut membranes of thick, dark, vascular skin, stretched across a frame of dense, heavy bone. When I hold them open, they are sails; when I close them tight against my body, they are a seamless, stonelike carapace that sheds the freezing damp. The membrane is not bare, however. Along the leading edge and across my back, I am covered in a dense, oily down—the same thick, insulating plumage that streaks the city walls with the green and white of my kind's presence. This down mats together, locked by the constant damp and the preen-gland's grease, forming a rigid, water-resistant armor. It is the color of the wet basalt: dark grey-brown, almost black when the condensation beads on it, lightening to the pale, frost-resistant white of the lichen where the sun strikes it over long years.

I am a creature of the threshold. The plateau is a continent of rock, and the city is a dense, sedimented hive upon it, but my world is the edge—the colossal, shearing drop where the crust tears away. I live on the sheer faces of the cliffs and the tall, blind walls of the tenements that rise highest against the hazeless sky. Here, the city is not a maze of alleys but a cliff of hand-stacked masonry, and I treat it with the same indifference as I treat the mountain. Both are stone. Both provide ledges. Both are marked by the chisel of the tectonic freeze.

My feet are my anchor to this world. They are not the broad, sucker-like pads of the grazers, evolved for the slow, adhesive crawl. Mine are a violent grip: thick, scaled toes ending in curved, black talons of keratin, hard as the basalt itself. When I land on a ledge no wider than a finger's joint, these talons close with an involuntary, crushing tension. They do not let go. The cold anesthetizes; the wind pulls with the force of a falling boulder; the margin for error is zero. So my grip is a spasm, a lock, a structural certainty. I can sleep latched to the vertical face, my body pressed into the cold stone, my wings folded tight, my head tucked beneath the oily ruff of my neck, while the freezing wind screams over me. I do not sway. I am pinned.

I am nearly silent. The soundscape of the plateau is one of low hums and sudden cracks; I am adapted to neither making nor hearing noise. I have no song, no call, no cry. My voice, if it can be called that, is a low, percussive grunt—a sound like two stones knocking together deep underwater, felt more than heard. It is a sound that carries no distance in the thin, crisp air. My ears are mere holes, flat and recessed beneath the down, to keep out the freezing rain and the wind's roar. I do not navigate by sound. I navigate by the map of the thermals written on my skin.

The air here is not empty; it is a landscape of pressure and temperature, and I read it with an organ I do not have a name for, a dense network of nerves at the base of my leathery wings that feels the subtle shifts in the air's weight and warmth. To me, the edge of the plateau is not a drop but a rising river of heat—the immense, slow spill of the lowlands' warmth colliding with the plateau's cold mass. I ride this river. I can fly for hours without a single beat of my wings, banking and tilting on the lift, reading the micro-thermals that rise from the sun-struck faces of the young mountains. The light here is thin and harsh, providing no warmth, but the dark rock absorbs it, and hours later, it exhales a faint, radiant heat that I can feel from a mile away, a ghost of warmth rising in the freezing air. I spiral in these breaths of the rock.

I am a predator, but not of the warm, furred things that hunt in the city's depths. My prey is the air itself, or rather, the life that moves through it. The cliffs and the high walls are colonized by my kind in vast, near-silent rookeries, but we do not crowd. We space ourselves with a precise, territorial geometry, each of us claiming a section of the cliff face and the airspace before it. We hunt the sparse, high-flying insects that are swept up by the thermals, and we hunt the small, furred predators when they venture onto the sheer faces in search of our eggs. 

My eyes are the final, defining feature of my being. They are large—dominant in my skull—protected by a thick, translucent nictitating membrane that slides across the cornea like a sheet of ice to shield them from the stinging rain and the silica-laced wind. The irises are a pale, frost-blue, almost white, washed out by a lifetime of staring into the hazeless, glare-bright sky. But behind the pale iris, the pupil is a deep, black void, and the retina is a masterpiece of extraction. In a world where the sun provides light but no warmth, where the horizon is a jagged line of grey on grey, my eyes do not need color. They need contrast. They need the heat of life against the cold of stone.

I see the world in a dual register: the sharp, grey geometry of the rock and the city, and, overlaid upon it, the shimmering, cold-fire signature of body heat. I can see the furred predator from a thousand feet above, its warmth a bright, fading ember against the freezing wall of a tenement. I can see the dim, distant figures of the people as they move through the labyrinthine corridors below—slow, warm pulses, their heat bleeding into the stone, leaving faint, thermal trails like the wake of a boat in dark water. They are the mobile tissue of the city; I am the watcher from above, seeing their heat from the cold, unbridgeable distance of the sky.

There is a relationship between us, though it is not one of understanding. I see them, and they see me, but we exist in different timescales and different geometries. They live in the alley and the cistern; I live on the wing and the ledge. They move like glaciers; I move like the wind. Yet we are both shaped by the same forces. They have no metal, no mortar; I have no song, no nest of twigs. My rookery is carved into the same stone as their city. I seek out the natural cisterns and the hand-carved channels of their condensation-traps, landing on the lips of the basins to drink the achingly cold, stone-tasting water, my leathery wings folded, my talons clicking on the rims they have dressed with bone tools. They harvest my down, snatching it from the ledges when we molt, and they take our eggs, climbing with terrifying, methodical grace into our rookeries. I do not fight them. I simply launch myself from the ledge and fall into the vast, cold air, riding the thermal away, waiting for them to finish and retreat. We are both extracting what we need to survive the freeze.

I am not unhappy. I am not happy. I am present. When I ride the thermal, I am the thermal. When I grip the ledge, I am the ledge. There is no despair in me, because I do not expect a better world. The thin air, the bitter cold, the relentless grey-brown expanse—these are not conditions to be endured; they are the substance of my existence. I am a creature of the high, cold light, the thin air, and the new-born rock. I am the plateau's edge, made flesh and leather, waiting out the freeze on the wing.
