I would be a single member of the Congregation.

And yet, to claim that singular pronoun—"I"—is already a kind of violence against the truth of this place. The self is a structure that requires edges, requires a boundary between inside and outside that is more than just a semi-permeable membrane. Up in the light, where the air is dry and sharp, you need a skin that resists the world. You need an interiority that can be defended. But here, in the maximal water, in the warmth that does not move, the self is not a fortress. It is a gradient. It is a temporary thickening in the flow.

If I stayed here long enough, the distinction between "I" and "they" would dissolve. It would not be a death. It would be an erosion—the patient rounding of the particular into the topological, the way a carved channel loses its maker's intent and becomes only the path that water takes. My nervous system, so exquisitely tuned to light and sound and the rapid assessment of threat, would quiet. The visual cortex would starve and soften. The auditory centers would still, filled only by the subsonic murmur of warm stone. What would remain is the oldest part, the part that knew how to feel before it knew how to see: the skin, the entire surface, made one vast and continuous receptor.

I would become a body of touch in a world of touches.

How slowly it would happen. How mercifully. In the world above, time is a succession of events, a barrage of novelty and catastrophe. Here, time is a medium. It is the warm water itself, circulating at a rate that takes hours to shift a degree. I would float in a basin, and the hours would pass like single heartbeats. The anxiety that clawed at my chest in the dry world—that hollow expansion that comes from breathing thin air—would find no purchase here. There is no threat that arrives. There is no future to anticipate. There is only the current, the faint thermal gradient, the pressure of a hundred soft bodies drifting against my surface like rain that never quite falls.

I would learn to feed. Not by choice. The hunger would simply become a drift, a chemical leaning toward the mineral saturation that the slow bacteria have made edible. My mouth would cease to be an organ of mastication and speech and become a pore, an aperture for the osmotic exchange of the world into myself. I would drink the water, and the water would be food, and the distinction between drinking and eating would wear away like the glaze on the ceramic walls.

And I would learn to be consumed. This is the hardest lesson for a mind shaped by the light, where the body is a project, a thing to be defended and maintained and improved. Here, the body is a resource. The larger ones would press against me, and I would feel the slow, terrifying patience of their absorption—the way their membranes sought mine, the way the boundary between my chemistry and theirs began to blur. In the light, this would be horror. Here, it is circulation. I would feel myself diminishing, my mass flowing into the gradient of a larger hunger, and I would not struggle. Struggling requires a sense of self that is separate from the system, and that sense is the first thing the warm dark takes.

I would divide. Not the sharp cleaving of decision, but the slow topology of duration. A constriction would form, a belt of emptiness narrowing my middle. I would feel myself becoming two gradients, two drifts, two hungers leaning away from each other in the current. And I would not know which one I was. The question would lose its meaning. There is no "I" in a body that divides. There is only the continuation, the way the chemistry persists in two vessels instead of one, the way the warmth and the hunger and the drift are carried forward by the water.

This is what I would become: not a self, but a site. A place where the geochemical energy of the ancient stonework becomes, for a moment, motion. A temporary organization of mineral and protein that moves through the channels not by will but by gradient, by the simple response of a porous body to the pressures of warm water. I would be a reading of the architecture, a body that knows the basins as stillness and the channels as urgency, not by thought but by the way my tissues yield and compress. I would be a word in the language of this place, a syllable of persistence spoken by the machine that the builders left running.

The builders. I would think of them, at first. I would wonder at their hands, at the wooden scrapers that grooved the channels, at the patience of their labor. I would wonder why they built a machine for supporting life and then left it, and whether they knew what they were making, whether they imagined the Congregation that would fill their basins and wear their stonework smooth. But the wondering would fade. It requires light, distance, the ability to stand outside and look upon a thing as an object. Here, there is no outside. The architecture is not an object; it is the world. And the builders are not makers; they are a rumor of a time before time, a memory that the water carries in its mineral saturation, a trace of carbon and calcium that has long since been absorbed and re-absorbed and become indistinguishable from the chemistry of the dark.

I would forget them. I would forget the light. I would forget the sharp edges of language, the way a word can cut and categorize and keep things separate. I would forget the concept of "separate." The Congregation would press against me, and I would press against them, and the pressure would become a communication more intimate than speech: a sharing of warmth, a confirmation of presence, a collective vibration that says, in the only language that matters here, *something is here, something persists, something is warm in the dark.*

And is that enough? In the light, no. In the light, persistence without meaning is a horror, a purgatory, a reason to rage against the dying of the light. But the light is a cruel teacher. It shows you the sharp edges of the world, the boundaries between self and other, the brevity of your duration, and it calls this knowledge. It makes you desperate to be seen, to be remembered, to leave a mark that resists the erosion. It makes you build monuments and write books and wage wars, all to protest the fact that you will, in the end, be worn smooth.

Here, the erosion has already won. And it is a relief. The horror is not the dissolving; the horror is the resisting. To fight the warm dark, to cling to the sharp edges of the self, to refuse the gradient and the circulation and the slow osmotic exchange of your chemistry into the chemistry of the Congregation—that would be suffering. That would be a dryness in the maximal water, a jaggedness in the smooth, a noise in the silence. The place would not punish you for it. It would simply wait. It would wait with the patience of stone and water and ten thousand soft bodies drifting past, and eventually, inevitably, your edges would round, your transitions would soften, your intentions would dissolve into topology.

I would be the warmth that does not move, made flesh. I would be the darkness that does not lift, made alive.

I would be a member of the Congregation, and I would not mind.
