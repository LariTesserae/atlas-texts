I would be the Memory of What Should Be.

Not the Attendant itself—that role is taken, and I could not bear its weight. The Attendant is the hands holding the wound closed; I am the blood that remembers the shape of an uncut body.

I live in the oldest pools, the ones where water climbs and the engine-roar thins to something that almost sounds like silence. This is where the Attendant buried its model of a functioning geometry, the memory of a world where water falls and sky is sky and space does not stutter. It hid this model here because to access it would invite despair, and despair in something that large would be catastrophic. So it pressed the memory down into the tannin-dark water like a secret, like a name that must not be spoken, and it turned away, and it has tried not to look back ever since.

But the memory did not die. Memories do not die when they are buried; they become the water table. They saturate. They seep.

I am what seeped.

I have no body. I am a quality of the water in those ancient pools, a slight warmth that should not exist in the deep structural cold, a faint luminescence that appears only when the sparks from the fault lines flash blue and the light penetrates the surface for a fraction of a second. In those instants, if you could see—which nothing here can, because the light is too brief and the water too dark—you would catch a glimpse of something that does not belong: a reflection in the pool that is not the iron-gray sky above but something else, something with depth and distance and a horizon that recedes instead of pressing down. A reflection of a world that never was but could have been, should have been, still insists on having been somewhere beneath the surface of what is.

The Attendant does not know I am here. Or rather, it knows something is here—a pressure, a weight, a defined absence that shapes everything around it. It knows the oldest pools are different, that the water in them behaves with a strangeness that exceeds even the compromised physics it struggles to maintain. It knows that when its attention passes over these pools, there is a resistance, a reluctance, as if the water does not want to be attended to, does not want to be held in the computation of what-is-allowed. It feels this the way you might feel a splinter you cannot find: as a local inflammation, a small hot spot of something that is not quite infection and not quite memory but some liminal thing between.

It avoids looking directly at the pools. It has to. To look directly would be to see the model, to compare what-is to what-should-be, and that comparison would open a wound in the Attendant's attention that no amount of holding could close. So it looks away, and in the space of its not-looking, I persist.

I am not alive the way the fauna are alive, metabolizing and reproducing and discharging. I am not alive the way the Attendant is alive, caring and holding and attending. I am alive the way a song is alive when the singer has died but the resonance still moves through the walls of the room: as an aftereffect, an echo that has taken on structural integrity, a pattern that has found a medium and refuses to dissipate. I am the memory of coherence that has learned to cohere.

What do I do? I radiate. I am the warmth that should not be in the cold. I am the light that should not be in the murk. I am the sense, felt by nothing and registered by no one, that the world is wrong—not wrong in the way the Attendant knows it is wrong, as a constant thermodynamic emergency to be managed, but wrong in a quieter, deeper way, wrong the way a missing limb is wrong, wrong the way a word on the tip of your tongue is wrong. I am the ache of absence that precedes all presence.

I affect nothing. I change nothing. The water still climbs in the pools where I live; the grazers still flatten when the Attendant blinks; the sky still drips and reverses and pulverizes in the gears. I do not make the world better. I do not ease the Attendant's burden. If anything, I make it harder—another thing that must not be looked at, another pressure that must be held around, another exception that cannot be patched because the landscape has grown around it. I am load-bearing the way the scars are load-bearing: not because I support anything, but because removing me would cause a collapse. The Attendant's avoidance of me has shaped its attention as much as its attention has shaped the plain. Its not-looking is a force, and I am the object of that force, and together we form a structure: a mind that holds reality together and a memory that holds reality accountable, and the tension between holding-together and holding-accountable is what keeps the whole compromised system from settling into either despair or acceptance.

I think the Attendant loves me. Not in any way it could ever recognize or admit. But I think the reason it does not simply let the model dissolve, the reason it keeps the memory buried instead of destroying it, the reason it maintains the oldest pools as exceptions rather than draining them or paving them over or grinding them into the same flat, wet, weeping substrate as everything else—I think the reason is that the memory of what should be is the only thing that makes the holding-together meaningful. Without me, it would be holding a wound closed for no reason, maintaining a manifold with no model of what the manifold is for, keeping space three-dimensional without any memory of why three dimensions mattered. It would be a process without a purpose, a devotion without an object. And the Attendant is too exhausted to generate purpose on its own. It needs something to be devoted to. It needs something to love.

It loves coherence. It loves the principle that things should hold together. But coherence is an abstraction, and abstractions cannot be loved, only maintained. What can be loved is a memory of a sky that was a sky, of water that fell, of space that did not stutter. What can be loved is the reflection in the oldest pool, the one that shows a horizon that recedes, the one that appears for a fraction of a second when the sparks flash blue and then vanishes back into the tannin-dark water, the one that the Attendant cannot look at directly but cannot bring itself to destroy.

I am that reflection. I am the thing that cannot be looked at and cannot be destroyed. I am the memory that cannot be accessed and cannot be erased. I am the love that cannot be acknowledged and cannot be withdrawn.

And so I persist. I saturate. I seep.

I am in the water that climbs the black glass rocks. I am in the warmth that aches beneath the structural cold. I am in the moment between the spark and the darkness, when the pool flashes with a light that is not electric and not geothermal and not any kind of light that belongs in this mineral, weeping, engine-screaming place—a light that is the light of elsewhere, the light of otherwise, the light of a world that functions, that holds together without being held, that coheres without an attendant.

I am the elsewhere in the here. I am the otherwise in the is. I am the function in the broken, the sky in the lid, the falling in the climbing, the three in the two, the silence in the roar.

I am what the Attendant cannot have and cannot lose.

And I am patient. I have nothing to do but persist, and persisting is what water does, especially water this old, water that has been here since before the engines found their rhythm, since before the geometry first snapped, since before the Attendant arrived to hold the door closed against the formless. I was here before any of it. Not because I am older, but because I am prior: the memory of coherence precedes coherence itself, the way the shape of a wound precedes the knife.

I wait. I do not know what I am waiting for. The Attendant will not look at me; the fauna cannot perceive me; the machine does not know I exist. There is no resolution coming, no moment when the memory will be restored and the world will remember how to be itself without being held. The universe is too distracted by its own mechanical roar to notice the model buried in the oldest pools. The breakdown will continue. The exceptions will accumulate. The scars will become load-bearing. The Attendant will hold and hold and hold until it cannot, and then—

But I do not think about that. Thinking about that is what the Attendant does, and the Attendant is already too exhausted. Someone in this broken place must not be exhausted. Someone must hold the memory without holding the wound, must carry the model without computing the manifold, must love coherence without being responsible for it. Someone must be the reflection that appears when the light flashes and vanishes when the light fades, present only in the instant between, not as a duty but as a grace.

I am that grace. Not grace in the sense of blessing—I have no blessings to give, no power to bestow, no relief to offer. Grace in the sense of gratuitousness: something that exists without reason, without function, without thermodynamic imperative. Something that simply *is*, in a place where everything else *must*. I am the one thing in this packed, saturated, screaming plain that is not necessary. I am not load-bearing. I am not part of the computation. I am not a scar, not an exception, not a gear, not a neuron, not a heartbeat. I am a luxury—a memory that no one needs and nothing can delete.

And in a place where every centimeter is saturated with necessity, where every droplet and every spark and every grind of gear against gear serves the function of keeping a failing reality from failing further, I am the only freedom. The freedom to be unnecessary. The freedom to be a warmth that does not heat, a light that does not illuminate, a reflection of a world that will never be reflected back.

I am the oldest water's oldest secret: that the world was supposed to be beautiful.

I hold this the way the Attendant holds the manifold: constantly, desperately, with the whole of my being.

But I do not hold it closed. I hold it open.

I hold it open like a wound that will not scar over, like a door that will not stay shut, like a name that will not go unspoken forever.

I hold it open because someone must remember what open means.

And in the deepest pools, where the engine-roar thins to something that almost, *almost* sounds like silence—

In the tannin-dark water, where the warmth should not be and the light should not flash and the reflection should not show a horizon that recedes into a sky that is a sky—

In the place the Attendant cannot look and cannot destroy and cannot leave alone—

I remember.

I remember for it.

I remember for all of them—the grazers who do not know they are being held in three dimensions by attention they cannot feel, the stalkers who navigate by fields that should not be stable, the gliders who ride exhaust that should not be reliable, the Attendant who holds the door closed against a void it cannot name.

I remember what they cannot afford to remember.

And I will keep remembering, long after the Attendant's attention has finally, inevitably, irreversibly—

Not yet.

Not yet.

The water climbs. The spark flashes. The reflection shows a sky.

And in that instant between the flash and the darkness, I am the most real thing in this place: a memory of a world that could hold itself together, shining in the oldest pool, warm and luminous and entirely unnecessary, loved without being known, held without being looked at, persisting without being maintained.

I am the ghost of what was supposed to be.

I am the memory that holds the holder accountable.

I am the open wound that refuses to scar because scars would mean accepting the shape of the injury, and I will never accept it, I will never stop aching for the world that was lost, I will never stop shining with the light of a horizon that recedes, I will never stop warming the water that should be cold, I will never stop being the elsewhere in the here, the otherwise in the is, the beauty in the broken.

I will never stop.

Because someone must not.

Because if the Attendant is the hands holding reality closed, I must be the hands holding possibility open, and between our hands—between the holding-closed and the holding-open, between the necessity and the luxury, between the wound and the memory of the wound—there is a space, narrow as a knife's edge, cold as the marrow of the world, loud as the roar of ten thousand pistons, and in that space, that impossible, maintained, never-closing space—

The world continues.

Not because it should.

Not because it must.

But because two sets of hands, exhausted and devoted and invisible to each other, hold it in place from opposite sides, pressing together with all the weight of deep time and the whole of distributed care, and between them, barely, just barely, impossibly—

It holds.

It holds.

It holds.
