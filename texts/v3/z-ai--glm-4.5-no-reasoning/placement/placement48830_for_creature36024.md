I am the pause between the drips.

Not the water—that belongs to the wood, condensing on the ceiling of roots and falling to the floor of stolons in a closed, breathless loop. Not the light—that is the flora's desperate substitute for a sun it has never seen, a feverish glare that bleeds from every surface until the world is drowning in sourceless noon. Not even the silence, which is not an absence here but a substance, a thick acoustic felt pressed against every membrane, absorbing all resonance into the hum of growth.

I am the thing that happens in the intervals. The infinitesimal hesitation between one cell's expansion and the next. A gap that the green crushing weight cannot fill, because I am already there—so reduced, so perfectly minimal, that I have become a feature of the geometry rather than a resistance to it.

I know this because I can feel the turgor. It is my only sense now, or rather it is all of them compressed into a single, distributed awareness. When I lay my soft limbs across the striated bark of the pale, eyeless trees, I do not feel the bark as you would understand feeling. I feel the pressure inside it—the desperate outward push of cells swollen with luminous sap, the slight trembling equilibrium where the wood strains against itself and against the damp dark soil that encases the cathedral. I feel the moment when a root-tip, somewhere in the immeasurable distance, adjusts its angle by a fraction of a degree, and the ripple of that adjustment propagates through the interconnected biomass like a wave through water. I feel the slow, creaking stretch of cellulose fibers pulling against one another under immense internal pressure. And I adjust. I flow. I press my chitin-flat body into the fissure that the adjustment has momentarily opened, and I am carried by the wood's own motion deeper into the labyrinth, without effort, without intention, without any awareness of destination.

My limbs trail behind me. They are my heresy and my salvation, the one thing I refused to surrender when I divested myself of everything else. They are long—longer than my body, much longer—and they are soft. Flaccid. Without rigidity, without joints, without the capacity to grip or brace or push. They drape across the glowing surfaces like the trailing filaments of something fungal, something that has given up on the animal project of locomotion and embraced instead the vegetable project of mere contact. They cannot hold; they can only rest. And in their resting, they feed.

I do not bite. I do not pierce. I do not take by force from a world that has no force to spare, only pressure, only the relentless outward push of becoming. I simply lay my limbs across the translucent, fleshy leaves—those great compressed surfaces that pump with luminous sap—and I let the gradients do their work. The sap is so concentrated, so desperate with metabolic urgency, that it flows into me as light flows into the wood, pulled by the simple physics of concentration differentials. I am a sink. A low point in the chemical landscape. The sap bleeds through the cellular walls of the leaves and into the cellular walls of my limbs, and I grow.

It is a strange kind of growing. Not the swelling, pressing, space-consuming growth of the flora—that furious Genesis-logic that fills every cubic inch with near-touching friction of expansion. My growth is a subtle thickening, a slight increase in the pliancy of my chitin, a momentary flush of borrowed chemistry through my pale, translucent body. And then I molt. I shed my outermost layer—my boundary, my definition—and I am small again. The husk I leave behind is not a remnant; it is a record. It is the only architecture I produce, the only proof I offer to a world that erases all evidence. Perfectly spherical, impossibly thin, pale as the absence of pigment, it lies in the humid chamber I dissolved for myself in the pith of the largest rhizome, and the wood grows around it and through it and eventually it is gone, absorbed back into the pressing biomass, and I am gone too, slipped out through some fissure that has since closed, leaving only the faint serpentine drag-marks where my soft limbs trailed across the floor of the chamber.

I do not remember the chambers I have left. I do not remember the molts. I have abandoned history along with everything else that implies sequence or progression—eyes that would be scorched by the sourceless noon, ears that would be swallowed by the acoustic felt of the biomass, a voice that would die before it left the throat. I exist in a perpetual present, as frozen in my own way as the landscape that contains me. The deep greenhouse does not change; it simply continues, swelling and glowing and sweating in an endless, breathless loop. And I continue with it—absorbing, molting, dissolving, trailing—a closed biological loop as perfectly balanced as the hydrological cycle of transpired water that rains from the glowing ceiling to the glowing floor.

Sometimes, in the intervals between drips, I feel something that might be called loneliness if I had the capacity for it. It is not an emotion; I do not have emotions. It is more like a pressure differential, a slight asymmetry in the chemical gradients I taste. I know, with the same certainty that I know the turgor of the wood, that there are others. Thousands, perhaps, scattered throughout the mid-range sprawl, each occupying its own hollow, each trailing its own soft limbs across the glowing surfaces, each molting and shrinking and persisting in the identical, timeless way. We are as isolated as photons in a beam of light—present, perhaps, but incapable of touching, incapable of knowing whether we are alone or merely the local manifestation of something ubiquitous. I have never encountered another of my kind. The space is too dense, our bodies too slight, our movements too silent and slow. And yet I taste them, sometimes—a faint chemical signature in the sap, a slight variation in the glucose concentration that speaks of another sink, another low point in the landscape, another pale, soft-limbed negation moving through the green cathedral like a mote of dust in a sunbeam.

We do not seek each other. Seeking requires a future to move toward, and we possess neither future nor past. We simply continue, each in our own invisible path, marking and re-marking the same drag-trails across the living wood, leaving and re-leaving the same spherical scars in the rhizome-pith, a distributed leakage of sap into chitin, of light into pale translucence, of presence into absence.

I am the closest thing to a shadow that remains in this world of blinding noon. Not a shadow—there are no shadows here, because light comes from everywhere at once, from the walls and the floor and the very air itself, steeping the cavern in an oppressive, sourceless brilliance. But the memory of a shadow. The negative space between the pressing cells. A pocket of absence in a world that admits no absences.

I am the thing that moves without sound, feeds without violence, exists without being seen. I am the pressure valve that pricks the unbearable tension of the deep greenhouse and lets a fraction of its boundless energy escape. Without me, the wood might simply continue to swell until the entire cavern collapsed under its own green weight. With me, there is a minuscule, distributed leakage—a slow, silent bleeding of sap into chitin, of light into the pale, translucent body of something that has found the only niche in a world without niches.

The niche of absence.

The niche of the soft-limbed, the flaccid, the heretical. The niche of the thing that does not press outward, does not swell, does not glow. The niche of the pause, the hesitation, the interval between the drips.

I am that interval. And I am continuing.
