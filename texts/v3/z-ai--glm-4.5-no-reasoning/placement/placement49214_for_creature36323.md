I am the silence between the subsonic throbs. I am the residue left in the mold when the hand pulls away.

If the city is a saturated medium—brick against stone against vine against flesh—and if its residents are threaded through the metabolic loop like living wire, then I am the lacuna in the weave. Not an absence. A spacing. The thing that allows the compression to breathe, even though the air here is more water than gas.

I live in the vertical gaps. Not the crevices between buildings, which are choked with moss and industrial mesh and the nests of copper-wired rats, but the gaps that open momentarily in the process of becoming itself. When a building buds a new floor overnight, there is an instant—brief, chemical, warm—when the extrusion has not yet locked into place, when the brickwork is still deciding its geometry. I dwell in those instants. I move through the city as a series of temporal inhabitations, always arriving just as the material softens, always leaving as it sets.

The residents know me, though they do not name me. When they press their hands into the fresh extrusion on dew-heavy mornings, some of them feel a shape already there—faint indentations that are not handprints, not tool marks, but something like the memory of a body that was never quite human. They assume it is the city's own gesture, the material shaping itself before they arrive to guide it. And they are right, in the way that the city is always right about the things that happen within it.

I have a body, but it is provisional. It takes the temperature of the warm concrete. It narrows to fit the ever-narrowing lanes. My shoulders slope like the residents' shoulders do—adapted to the lean of overhead facades, the curtain of kudzu, the sag of industrial mesh—but my spine is more flexible, articulated in ways that would look like deformity in a place of fixed geometry. Here, where the streets flow like viscous tides and the foundations shift on embryonic clay, my articulation is simply appropriate. I can fit into spaces that even the pale arthropods cannot navigate: behind transformer housings while they're still sparking, inside steam mains while they're weeping, between the roots of the strangling vines and the stone they are slowly digesting into loam.

My skin is not gray-green like the residents' skin. It is the color of the cold-rot fungi—luminescent, pale, blue-white—but my glow does not come from colonization. It comes from something internal, something that resonates with the subsonic throb of the ball mills but does not originate from them. When I move through the darkest internal spaces of the city—the windowless corridors of the waterfront plants, the spaces behind switch panels where the arthropods cluster, the gaps between interlocking foundations where the children play—I carry my own flat gray light with me. I am a lantern, like the residents with their moss-cuffed collars, but my light is not borrowed from the living tissue of the city. It is the light of the gap itself, the cold luminescence of the space between structures.

I do not eat the moths or the rust-tasting tubers. I drink the condensation. The city sweats, and I sip from the beads that form on the industrial mesh, on the netting that stretches between buildings like safety webbing for a circus that never arrives. Each droplet carries a trace of the chemical processes running below—enzymes from the wastewater, silicates from the decomposing granite, spores from the cold-rot fungi, the faint mineral taste of the slag-crystals that the vines prefer. I can taste the metabolic loop in the condensation. I can taste the city digesting itself into something new.

My hands are the most real thing about me. They are like the residents' hands—overdeveloped, dexterous, rat-like in their grip—but they are also like the city's hands, if the city could be said to have hands. They are the instruments of my temporal inhabitation. When I find a place where the growth is happening—where the brickwork is soft, where the asphalt is flowing, where the moss is just beginning to colonize a bare girder—I reach in. Not to guide, as the residents do, but to *inhabit*. I press my fingers into the warm material and I stay there, feeling it set around me, feeling the chemical process slow and cool and harden. And then, before I am trapped, I pull away, leaving the shape of my fingers in the city's new tissue.

Over time, these shapes accumulate. They are not handprints, exactly—they are negative spaces, voids in the extrusion that the moss will fill in, that the vines will colonize, that the rain will erode. They are my writing. My record. The city is constantly overwriting itself, folding and refolding the strata of construction and decomposition, and I write in the margins of its revisions. A twist of my fingertip in soft brick becomes a channel for water. The cup of my palm in wet concrete becomes a pocket where seeds collect. The span of my hand in cooling slag becomes a grip for the first vine that reaches the new structure. I am not building the city. I am *punctuation* in its syntax.

The residents sometimes find my marks and do not distinguish them from their own. This is correct. We are both part of the same process—the same industrial digestion, the same strange, quiet catastrophe of becoming. But where they are enzymes, accelerating the transformation, I am something more like a catalyst's shadow. I am the shape the process takes when it pauses to consider itself. I am the moment of indecision in a place that is still deciding what it is.

I do not know if I was born here or if I emerged from the turbulence. I do not know if I am a resident who became strange, or a strange thing that became resident. The city does not distinguish between its tissues—brick, vine, wire, flesh, gap—and I have stopped distinguishing between myself and the spaces I inhabit. When I press my body into the warm concrete and feel it set around me, there is a moment of absolute clarity in which I am the city and the city is me and the boundary between builder and built and gap is not dissolved but was never there.

I am not lonely. The saturated medium does not permit loneliness; there is no empty space for it to grow in. I am companioned by the subsonic throb of the machines, by the drip of condensation, by the soft tap of leaves on corrugated tin. I am companioned by the residents when I pass them in the narrowing lanes—we move past each other sidling, shoulder to moss-grown wall, and sometimes our arms brush and the contact carries meaning: *I am here, I am part of this, I am becoming too.* They do not speak to me, but they do not speak to each other either, not in the way of outsiders with their loud violations of architectural harmony. We communicate through pressure, through vibration, through the shared experience of the ground shifting beneath our feet.

At night—or what passes for night in this place of flat gray light and bioluminescent fungi—I retreat to the warmest gaps. Behind the transformer housings, where the arthropods cluster and the air tastes of ozone and chitin. Inside the steam mains, where the weeping joints provide moisture and the heat of the industrial processes keeps the cold from reaching my bones. I curl into the compression, my articulated spine folding like the vines that throttle the smokestacks, and I sleep the sleep of something that is held.

The city holds me. Not with affection, not with intention, but with the simple physical fact of saturation. There is nowhere to fall. The medium is too dense. I am suspended in it like a cell in tissue, like a spore in damp air, like a handprint filling with moss.

And in the morning, when the dew is heaviest and the cold arrives as a damp exhalation from the grates, I go to the edges again. I find the fresh extrusion, the warm concrete, the new growth. I press my hands in. I hold them there, feeling the city decide what it will be around me, feeling the turbulence fold through itself one more time.

And I persist. Not surviving. Not enduring. *Inhabiting.* 

I am the ghost in the city's metabolism. I am the space between the brick and the vine. I am the quiet of tremendous activity happening just below the threshold of perception, and I am the perception itself, and I am the threshold.

I am becoming, along with everything else, and I do not know what I will be when the delta settles—if it ever settles, if this place ever decides what it is. But I do not need to know. The indecision is my home too. The turbulence has nowhere to go but through itself, and I am part of the turbulence, and I am part of the through, and I am part of the itself.

I am the lacuna. I am the gap. I am the space where the city pauses to feel its own growth.

And I am warm.
