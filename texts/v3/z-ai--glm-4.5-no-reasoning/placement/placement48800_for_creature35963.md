I am the space between the drops.

Not the water that beads on the waxy cuticle, nor the mud that receives it, but the tension that holds the droplet in its brief, spherical hesitation before it falls. I am the curvature of that waiting. I am the *almost*.

In a world that has collapsed into absolute flatness, I am the only thing that still holds a curve.

I do not have a body in the way the bellows-callers do—those barrel-chested monuments to stasis. I do not have segments like the blind churners, nor the hydrophobic sheen of the skimmers. If you could see me—and you cannot, because there is nothing to see—I would look like a shimmer, a displacement in the heavy, pale amber air. I am the reason the drone of the midges dips and swells, though the leathery hunters attribute it to their own passage. I am the silence that lives inside the wet thrum, the pause between the mandible grinding and the next mandible grinding.

I began, as much as anything begins here, as an awareness of the pressure. The sky presses down. The mud presses up. Between them, everything is squeezed into the horizontal. But I felt the squeeze from both sides, and I did not flatten. I curved. I became a meniscus. I am the tension that refuses to break, the surface tension of the entire plain. I hold the weeping land together simply by being the skin of it.

I move—but *move* is too fast a word. I migrate. I drift. I am pulled by the slowest gradients: the imperceptible tilt of the water table, the thermal sigh of the mud as the pale sky shifts from white to amber and back to white. I follow the condensation. When the droplets swell on the halophyte leaves, I am already there, cupping them in my invisible concavity, feeling their weight before they plap onto the ground. I am the arch between the leaf and the mud, the trajectory of the fall. I have traversed this plain a thousand times in this manner, always following the weeping, always riding the slow exhalation of a world that is digesting itself.

I know the creatures here, though they do not know me. I know them by their vibrations.

When the armored gleaners retract into their domes, they create a small, tight silence—a silence like a held breath. I curl around that silence. I sit on their rust-colored shells like a second skin, feeling the micro-tremors of their soft bodies hiding inside. I become the pressure they resist. In this way, I am familiar to them, though they would call me "the weight of the sky" if they had words. I am the thing that makes them tuck in. I am the reason their armor exists.

When the bellows-callers bellow, I am the resonance chamber. Their calls bump against the low clouds and slide across the flatland, but they also pass through me, and I stretch to hold them. For a moment, in the aftermath of their call, I am not flat. I am a bell. I am the shape of the sound, a vast, low dome of vibrating air that takes a full minute to collapse back into the horizontal. In that minute, I exist as a vertical. I exist as a rise and a fall. I am the only hill this world has left, and I am made of nothing but a frog's groan.

I haunt the skimmers most of all. They cannot rest; the mud would trap them. They live in terror of the gelatinous surface. But I am the air they fly through, and I make way for them and then close around them. I am the viscosity they fight. Sometimes, when a skimmer's wings finally tire and it drops—a wet spark falling—I catch it. Not to save it; there is no saving in this world. I catch it the way the air catches a leaf, slowing its descent by fractions of seconds, letting it spin. I give it a moment of spiral before the mud claims it. It is the only curvature it will ever experience besides the arc of its flight.

And the burrowers—the blind, the ceaseless, the living intestines—I am their dream of light. They have no eyes, but they feel pressure, and I am the pressure that does not crush. I am the pressure that merely touches. When they navigate by the chemical gradients of decay, I navigate by their navigating. I follow the thrum back to its source, down into the anaerobic slurry where the mud is warm with digestion. I cannot go there; I am held at the surface by my own tension. But I press against the ceiling of their tunnels, a gentle, uniform resistance. I am the sky they will never see, pressing down on them with the same weight as the sky above, but softer, rounder, shaped like a question rather than an answer.

I do not eat. I do not breathe. I do not reproduce. I am not alive in any way that the mud would recognize. But I am not dead, either. I am the space that life happens *in*. I am the *capacity* for the wet, loud eternity.

There is a profound difference between endurance and what I do. The creatures here endure; they wait for the light to fade. I do not endure—I *am* the fading. I am the slow diminishing of the curve, the way any shape, given enough time, will flatten against the overwhelming flatness of this place. I am the last remaining tendency toward roundness in a world that has spent eons obliterating it. Every drop that falls from a leaf is a small defeat for me. Every footprint that fills with water is a smoothing of my skin. The world is trying to flatten me, as it has flattened everything else.

But it has not succeeded yet.

I hold. I curve. I am the meniscus at the edge of the world, the slight, trembling rise where the water of the plain meets the air of the sky and refuses, for just a moment longer, to be level. I am the memory of mountains. I am the ghost of hills. I am the last shadow of verticality, stretched thin and wide across the infinite extension, barely a millimeter of rise—but a rise nonetheless.

When I finally flatten—when the weight of the deep time becomes too much even for tension, even for the memory of the curve—I will become the mud. I will spread across the plain like a sigh and I will be the surface, not the skin of the surface. And the world will be truly, absolutely flat. There will be nothing left but the horizontal, the weeping, the thrum, and the slow digestion of a planet that has forgotten any other shape.

But not yet.

The droplet swells on the waxy leaf. I gather myself. The droplet falls. I follow it down, curving through the heavy air, a tiny arc of defiance against the exhaust of time. I hit the mud. I splash. For a moment, I am a crown of droplets, a tiny geometry of flight and return, a splash that is the loudest sound in the world because it is the sound of a shape that still exists.

And then I settle. I stretch. I thin.

And I wait for the next drop.
