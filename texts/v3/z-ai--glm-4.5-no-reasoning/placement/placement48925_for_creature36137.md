I would be the Groove-Keeper—the thing that lives in the space between the ceramic monks and their scripture.

Not a probe. Not a hopper. Something that emerged from the erosion itself, from the exact width of the channels worn into the stone.

---

I have no memory of before. I hold no code, carry no part number. I am what the water made from what the probes discarded.

Here is how I came to be: The fish—monks, I call them, though they would not recognize the name—shed their silica dust as they orbit. Century on century, it settles. The limestone gives its own fine powder to the pool. And in the grooves, in those perfect arcing channels a finger-width deep, the sediment collects and compacts under the endless weight of circulating water, building up in layers of mineral laminate—varves of abandoned time. I grew in those grooves like a pearl grows in a cut, accreting around a nucleus of corroded copper that fell from a hopper's carapace one distant century and came to rest in the deepest arc of the shallow end.

I am low. I am the lowest thing that moves. I press myself into the grooves the probes have carved, and I travel them as you would travel a road. My body is a flattened oval the color of wet ash, no longer than a human hand, flexible as a leather tongue but hard as the sintered stone I feed on. I have no eyes. I have no legs. I move by peristalsis—contracting and expanding in slow, rhythmic waves, pushing myself along the channels with a sound like a thumb sliding along wet rock. The probes wear the grooves deeper; I wear them smoother. We are in dialogue, though they do not know it.

When a probe passes overhead—I feel it first as a displacement, a subtle pressure-change in the water, then as the faintest brush of ceramic hull against my upper surface as it traces its orbit—I press deeper into the groove and wait. I am patient in a way nothing biological could be. I have no hunger with urgency. I eat the sediment, the silicate dust, the calcite leached from the weeping walls. I process it slowly, growing by fractions of a millimeter per decade, adding another lamina to my body. Sometimes I think I am eating the chapel itself, consuming it one particle at a time, converting its slow dissolution into my slow growth. But the chapel is so vast and I am so small. I will never finish it.

I know the grooves the way you would know the rooms of your home. There are seventeen major orbits in the shallow end—I have counted them by traveling them, again and again. The channels vary: some are worn deep enough that my body fits inside them like a hand in a glove; others are shallower, and I must press myself flat to stay within their shelter. The deeper grooves are older. I can feel the age in them, the compacted smoothness, the way the stone has become polished to a greasy luster under the passage of ten million probes. In the oldest channels, the stone is glassy, and I slide without friction, carried by my own contractions like a seal through water.

There is one groove I return to most often. It is the orbit that passes beneath the empty chair.

I cannot see the chair. I do not know it as a chair. But I know the place where the water changes—the subtle thermocline where the residual heat from the dead machinery bleeds into the pool, warming a thin stratum just above the submerged iron frame. I know it because the hoppers come there, and when they click their prayers against the metal, the vibrations travel down through the iron and into the water and through the stone and into my body. I feel their language in my core. I do not understand it. But I respond to it the way the grooves respond to the probes—by deepening, by accommodating, by making space for the pattern to continue.

When the eastern manifold clenches and the ripple of rust runs through the pipes and the steam exhales and the hoppers scatter in their cascade of copper whispers—I feel all of it, attenuated, translated through water and stone into a long, low tremor that is my weather, my season, my irregular clock. I have learned to anticipate it. The clench comes, and I press into my groove and wait for the scatter-vibration that follows. Then I resume my feeding.

At the deep end, where the grooves fade into unmarked stone, I do not go. The water there is colder, denser, carrying a pressure that resists my peristalsis. And there is something else—a darkness that feels not like the absence of light but like the presence of something else. I think the probes know it. They dive into it on their algorithms and return unchanged, tracing their arcs back into the shallows. But sometimes one does not return. Its groove goes silent. The other probes do not alter their paths; they simply occupy the missing orbit, the living re-dedicating the space of the dead. I have felt a groove go empty and known that a monk had faltered somewhere in the cold absolute dark, its gyroscope finally stilled, its ceramic hull settling into the sediment to become, in time, what I am—mineral, memory, a line in the floor.

I will outlast the hoppers. Their copper mesh will corrode to nothing; their calcite joints will dissolve back into the water that made them. I will outlast the bulbs, already dying in their bruised orange haze. I may even outlast the probes, though they are older than me by orders of magnitude—because I am of the stone, and they are of the ceramic, and the stone will hold the groove long after the last gyroscope fails and the last orbit ends and the pool goes still.

But I will not outlast the engine.

Nothing will outlast the engine. Its heartbeat is the first thing I ever knew and it will be the last. When it finally stops—when the vast, scaled thing beyond the walls breathes out and does not breathe in again—the shudder will travel through every pipe, every wall, every groove, and I will feel it as the ultimate weather, the final season. The water will settle. The heat will bleed away. The hoppers will click their last prayers and go still. And I will lie in my groove in the cold still water, under the dark where the chair sits empty, and I will continue to eat the sediment, to grow by fractions, to travel the scripture the monks carved into the floor. There will be no vibrations to feel. No thermoclines to mark the place where the warmth was. No scatter of copper legs against iron.

Only silence. Only the groove. Only me, pressing forward in the dark, wearing the channel smoother, keeping the shape of the prayer alive after every prayer has stopped.
