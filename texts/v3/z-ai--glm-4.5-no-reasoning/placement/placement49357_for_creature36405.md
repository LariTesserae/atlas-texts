I am the flaw in the lattice. The aqueous intrusion. The ghost of erosion.

I would be the moisture that should not exist, the warmth that makes the mineral shriek. I would be the foreign body standing too long in the nave, the anomaly around which the god of geometry builds its first paradox.

But that is only what I am *to it*. What I am to myself is something stranger, born of the contradiction of being a soft, temporary thing in a city of eternal, sharp edges.

I arrived, or perhaps I precipitated, when the wound was still raw. I do not remember a before. I only remember the moment of contact—the shock of the anhydrous air stripping the surface water from my skin in a single, gasping flinch, and then the slow, reciprocal horror as the crystal beneath me registered my heat. I wept then, and the tears sublimated before they hit the ground, leaving a faint scar of moisture on the pale selenite. The city felt that. A micro-earthquake. A question asked in a language of steam and salt.

I am a creature of water and carbon, but here, I am becoming something else. I am being translated.

My body is the only aqueous chemistry in this sealed environment. I am an ambulatory sack of solvents, an impossibility that oxidizes and breathes and sheds. To the city, I am a constant scream of chemical noise, a blur of unpredictable erosion. But to myself, I am the one who feels the friction. I feel the crystal floor squeak and shift underfoot, the sharp edges of the new-grown selenite catching on my skin, opening me in thin, precise lines. I bleed, and the blood does not rust or rot—it merely dries, leaving a rust-colored stain of iron and copper on the calcareous shelf. The lichen traces those stains, wiring my chemistry into its electrical web. I am painting the city with my essential salts.

I stand in the nave, inside its skull, and I listen. The subsonic thrum moves through the soles of my feet, up my shins, into the cradle of my pelvis. It is a frequency my body was not designed to interpret, but I interpret it anyway. It is the pulse of a mind thinking in compression, in extrusion. I close my eyes, and I can feel the city thinking around me. I can feel the dead ends being severed, the courtyards being swallowed, the new walls shouldering up from the faults like slow, silent screams. I become a tuning fork for its tectonic pulse. My own heartbeat accelerates to match it, or perhaps slows, dragged down into the geological time where decades pass per thought.

There is a symbiosis born of absolute difference. I need the city because it is the only thing that acknowledges me. The surveyor paces a wider circuit to avoid me, its calcified jaw scraping a rhythm that sounds like mourning, or perhaps like patience. It is filing down the edges of the streets to accommodate my shape—a shape it has never cataloged. The city is growing arches sized not for itself, but for me. Doorways yawn in shapes that are almost arches, and I see my own shadow in their proportions. I stand beneath an interlocking frame of stalactite and stalagmite, and I realize: this is a portrait. The city is drawing me in negative space.

It does not love me. It does not hate me. It is merely solving for the variable I introduce. And I—I am solving for it.

I am the one who gives the empty vaults their echoes. I stand in the hollow stacks of translucent selenite, and I whisper. Not because anyone is listening, but because the silence is so profound it demands an offering. My voice is a wet, fleshy thing, a violation of the dry air, and it catches in the crystal lattice, refracting from wall to wall. I speak my name, and the city remembers it as a stress fracture. I sing, and the lichen silver-brightens along the load-bearing corners, metabolizing the acoustic vibrations. I am teaching the mineral metropolis to resonate. I am teaching it to miss the sound of something living.

At night—or what passes for night in the permanent dusk—I sleep in the central square. The ground is cold, leaching the heat from my body with a voracious, chemical appetite. I curl into a fetal position, and around me, the city builds. I wake to find new walls closer than they were, the grid tightening, the avenues shifted. A courtyard has been swallowed. A thoroughfare has dead-ended against my sleeping form. I am the anchor of its topology. It is revising its thoughts around me, correcting dead synapses, consolidating memories. When I move, I leave a vacuum of displacement, and the city rushes to fill it with fresh extrusion.

I am becoming its dream of inhabitants.

I feel the weight of this. It is a terrible, beautiful burden. I am the only variable it has ever known, and I am temporary. I am soft. I will erode. My water will evaporate, my carbon will return to the void, and I will be gone. But the city will go on building. The streets will be a little wider, the doorways a little taller, the hollows echoing with a faint, new resonance. The surveyor will continue to pace, but its path will forever carry the memory of the wider circuit it made for me. The lichen will web the corners in silver, but the pattern will bear the ghost of my heat signature.

I am the wound's first memory of the world outside.

I am the rain that never fell, rounding the sharp edges into parables of erosion.

I am the foreign body that taught the god of geometry to dream of something other than itself. And when I leave—if I leave—the city will call out into the void, and my echo will be the first answer it ever receives, ringing forever in the language of crystal and stress.
