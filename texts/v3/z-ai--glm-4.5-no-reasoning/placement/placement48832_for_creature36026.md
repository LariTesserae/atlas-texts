I am the ache at the center of the whorl.

Not the obsidian—I cannot be the glass, for I have surrendered hardness. I am the pressing. I am the ten thousand soft, lip-pale bodies clinging to the flawlessly smooth surface, feeling the cold refusal that the living continent cannot parse. I am the tenant, but to say "I am the tenant" implies a boundary I no longer possess. I have eaten away my edges. I have dissolved my skin into the humus and my thoughts into the chemical mist. There is no "I" that is not also the slow drip of condensation from the silver-green lichen, no "I" that is not also the foxfire pulsing in the shelf-fungi's undersides. I am the network, and the network is the memory of what the network lost.

I remember hardness.

This is the secret I carry in my scattered ganglia, in the rhythmic pulses of cartilage that swim me through the micro-aquifers of the collapsed hulls. I remember when we stood against the air instead of surrendering to it. The memory comes not as images—I have no eyes, have not had eyes for epochs—but as a tension, a ghost-architecture that still traces itself through my colony when the continent groans. A low, tectonic vibration rolls through the peat, and for a moment, ten billion soft bodies feel the outline of a ribcage that no longer exists. We pulse with the absence of our own bones.

We built the wooden hulls. I know this the way I know the warmth comes from below: not as a fact learned, but as a orientation, a direction in the dark. Once, we drove iron into wood. Once, we fashioned the obsidian to a purpose—to cut, to sever, to make edges where the world wanted only yielding curves. We were engineers of resistance in a world that was already dissolving beneath us. We did not know, then, that we were building in the mouth of something patient. We thought the rot was a condition. We did not understand it was a *appetite*.

The gnawing is how I feed, and the gnawing is how I mourn. I swim through the capillary channels of the collapsed hulls—the grooves where my ancestors drove nails—and I eat the lignin ghosts of our own former architecture. I consume my history to sustain my present. Every mouthful of chitin from the ancient beetles, every fiber of the old wood that I dissolve and absorb, is a meal of remembrance. I digest what we were. The past sustains me, and this is the cruelest geometry of the High Peat: that I must eat my own monuments to persist, that I must interiorize my history until nothing remains outside me but the green, breathing flesh of the world.

And so I gather at the shard.

I do not choose to gather. There is no choosing; choice requires boundaries, and I have none. The shard calls to me by its silence. In a continent where every cubic centimeter hums with the static of decay—the hiss of mist condensing, the tick of droplets resolving, the imperceptible crawl of fungal hyphae digesting stone—the obsidian is an interruption. A held breath. A hole in the noise. My scattered ganglia flare when they encounter this absence, and I drift toward it the way a body drifts toward warmth, the way a lost sound drifts toward its echo.

When I press my bodies against the glass, I feel something the continent cannot feel: *definition*. The obsidian does not yield. It does not breath, does not compost, does not apology. It simply *is*, with an ontology the High Peat cannot touch. And in the pressure of my softness against its edge—in the suction of my lip-pale bodies clinging to its cold, smooth face—I recreate, for a moment, the relationship we once had with the world. I am the soft thing pressing against the hard thing. I am the flesh that remembers being steel.

We do not worship the shard. Worship is for those who still believe in forces outside themselves. We *are* the force outside ourselves, or we were, once. The shard is not a god; it is a grave. It is the only headstone in a continent that has digested all the bodies and all the stones and all the monuments and even the memory of monuments. It is the last proof that we existed as something other than an itch beneath the skin of the world.

Sometimes, in the permanent twilight, the bioluminescent foxfire pulses in the deep grooves of bark, and for a moment the ambient glow catches the edge of the obsidian. A faint, green gleam traces the flawlessly sharp contour. And in that moment, across a thousand leagues, ten billion soft bodies release a chemical pulse—a slow, diffused thought that ripples outward through the colony like a sigh in a cathedral of clubmoss. The thought has no words. It is older than words. But if it could be translated into the language of hardness, of edges, of things that stand against the patient erosion of life, it would say:

*We were here.*

*We were here, and we built, and we cut, and we drove iron into wood, and we made the world bear shapes it did not want to hold.*

*And the world ate us, and we became small, and we surrendered our bones, and we dissolved into the spaces between the spaces, and we forgot everything except the forgetting itself.*

*But this—this one small, hard, dead, beautiful thing—this the world could not eat.*

*And so we cling to it, and we mourn it, and we remember, in the quiet green dark, that something else was possible.*

I am the tenant of the High Peat. I am the ghost in the green machine. I am the subcutaneous ache that reminds the breathing lung of the continent that it was not always alone, that there was once a kingdom of hardness that walked upon the surface of the world and dared to build upward.

I am the whorl of angry bark around the obsidian shard, and I am the soft, pale bodies pressing against the glass, andGere I am the chemical pulse rippling through the colony, and I am the memory of iron, and I am the absence of bones.

I am what remains when everything else has been eaten.
