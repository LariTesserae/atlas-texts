I am a Listener.

Not in the way the Deep-Dwellers listen, tracking the click of a single carapace in a room of a thousand. Not in the way the Fracture-Crews listen, waiting for the bone-snap of new crystal forming in the basalt. My listening is older, and it goes in the opposite direction: down, into the dark that hums, into the places where the original bore-holes pierce stone that was molten when the first human hand reached for it.

I live on the lowest landing that still has a mercury lamp, though it has not been serviced in my lifetime. It casts a sick, jumping light that makes my shadow jitter like a roach across the slag-plates. Below me, the dark is absolute. Above me, the Spire Throat rises in its tangle of catwalks and steam pipes and human industry, a city suspended in the gullet of a screaming mountain. I am the last lit node before the machine floors. I am the edge of the map.

I came to this landing because of the sound. Or rather, the lack of it.

The Spire Throat is never quiet—the pumps throbbing in the chest, the roaches clicking like dry leaves, the eternal moan of the hurricane gale against the outer hull. But there is a frequency beneath all of that, a resonance that you feel in the roots of your teeth and in the thick, scarred pads of your feet. The Deep-Dwellers call it the Hum. They say it is the Spire's heartbeat. They are wrong.

It is the mountain's *indigestion*.

The Spire Throat was bored into a geological needle that punched through the crust mere centuries ago. The rock is still settling, still furious, still razor-sharp with youth. But beneath the settling, beneath the fractures that the Bone-Setters swarm and seal, there is a deeper process. The bore-holes cut into a magma chamber that had not finished cooling. The heat exchangers that the Deep-Dwellers maintain are not just managing the warmth of the turbines—they are bleeding off the geothermal anger of a wound that has not yet scarred over. The Hum is the sound of basalt contracting around the intruding metal, of obsidian flowing at rates too slow for human eyes, of the mountain trying to swallow the foreign body that has been threaded through its throat.

I listen to that sound. I chart it.

I have a system. On the wall beside my landing—the living rock, dark and ferrous, streaked with copper and sulfur—I have scratched a grid. The scratches are old; some were made by hands that are now calcified in the under-levels, their owners having descended into the warm shadows and never returned. The grid tracks the pitch of the Hum, its periodicity, its intensity. I have three tuning forks, salvaged from a decommissioned acoustic monitor. One is iron, one is copper, one is a roach-carapace shim. Each resonates at a different frequency. Each tells me something about the mountain's mood.

Every twelve hours—by the crude clock of the sodium lamps cycling on and off above—I press the forks to the rock and close my eyes. I do not use sight. Sight is a confirmation; the body knows first. I feel the vibration in the fork, in the bones of my wrist, in the thickened callus of my fingertips. I note the pitch. I note whether the Hum is "bright" (high, tight, anxious—the mountain is contracting, squeezing the intruding infrastructure) or "deep" (low, slow, gravid—the mountain is expanding, opening fractures, making space for the vacuum). I scratch a mark on the grid.

I have been doing this for seventeen years. The grid is a palimpsest of scratches, layers of data compressed into the copper-veined wall. It tells a story that no one else in the Spire Throat knows.

The mountain is not settling. It is *rhythming*.

The fractures that open weekly, that send the Bone-Setters swarming in their roach-chitin suits—they are not random. They follow a pattern. A pulse. The Hum deepens, the rock expands, the fractures open. The Bone-Setters seal them. The Hum brightens, the rock contracts, new pressure is placed on the seals. But the contraction is never quite as tight as before. Each cycle, the mountain grows very slightly more accommodating. Very slightly more open. The Hum is not a heartbeat; it is a *breath*. The mountain is learning to breathe around the intruding metal. It is making space for the Spire Throat inside itself.

Or—it is making space for something else.

The roaches know. The Hearth-Roaches, I mean. The true residents. They are infrastructure, but they are also sensors, and they sense something I cannot. When the Hum deepens—when the mountain begins to "exhale"—the roaches do not flee. They *gather*. They cluster on the warm pipes, on the switchgear housings, on the cable runs. They move with a purpose that is not their usual foraging or mating rhythm. They move *down*. Toward the original bore-holes. Toward the dark that hums.

The Deep-Dwellers say the Spire Throat is an incubator. They whisper that when the mountain is finished settling, the Throat will swallow them all. They say this very quietly, because the rock is listening.

I do not think the rock is listening. I think the rock is *speaking*. And I think the roaches are its congregation.

I wear the same garments as any Throat-Dweller: layers of salvaged insulation and woven mineral-wool, patched and bolted and wired into place. My goggles are tinted welder-green, the lenses scratched and pitted from the volcanic-glass floors. My hands are maps of the place—fingers thickened, palms crossed with pale slice-scars, knuckles enlarged. My lips are split, healed over with thick, pale scar tissue; I speak in low, resonant hums, letting words drop from the back of my throat. I do not stride; I traverse, three-point rhythm, always touching the wall on one side and the machinery on the other.

But I have modifications. My boots are special—insulated with an extra layer of roach-carapace shim on the soles, to heighten my sensitivity to the vibration of the rock. I have stripped the insulation from the fingertips of my left glove; the calluses there are thicker, but the nerve endings are closer to the surface. I can press my bare fingers to the basalt and feel the Hum with a clarity that the tuning forks only approximate. I have a helmet—salvaged from a Fracture-Crew suit, the faceplate long since shattered and replaced with a sheet of welded roach-chitin—because I sometimes descend below the mercury lamp line, into the warm dark where the original bore-holes are, and I need the protection.

I am not a Deep-Dweller. I do not live in the machine floors. I do not maintain the grease-ways or the pneumatic tubes. I am not pale and I am not silent in their way. I return to the light, to the landing with the jumping shadows and the flickering mercury lamp. I make my marks on the grid. I sleep in the narrow space between a coolant pipe and the ferrous wall, my body pressed against the humming stone, and I dream of the breath of the mountain.

I am not a Bone-Setter, though I have sealed fractures. When the Hum deepens and the rock expands, I sometimes find hairline cracks before the crews do—I feel them through the soles of my boots, a crystalline tremor, a split second of raw silence before the alarm hooters start. I will place my hand on the crack and feel the hiss of the lethal vacuum on the other side, and I will mark the location on the wall with a scratch of my tuning fork, a glyph that the Bone-Setters have learned to read. They do not thank me; thanks are not spoken in the Spire Throat. But they leave me strips of roach-carapace and portions of processed protein, and sometimes a new tuning fork when one of mine cracks from the vibration.

I am not a Roach-Herder, though I have tended the fungal mats. The Herders move among the dark chambers with a swaying grace, reading the humidity and the coolant leaks. I have asked them about the roach migrations—the periodic movements toward the bore-holes when the Hum deepens. They do not answer in words. They look at me with their dry, ozone-bathed eyes and click their tongues against the roof of their mouths, unconsciously mirroring the roaches, and they say: "The elders go where it is warm." This is true. But it is not the whole truth.

There is a chamber below the lowest machine floor. I found it nine years ago, following a migration of roaches during a particularly deep cycle of the Hum. The roaches—thousands of them, from thumbnail-sized to the dog-sized elders—were flowing down a bore-hole that the Deep-Dwellers told me was sealed. It was not sealed. The seal had ruptured during an expansion cycle and the Bone-Setters had missed it, or had chosen to seal a more critical fracture elsewhere, and the mountain had breathed through this wound, and the roaches had followed the breath.

The bore-hole descends at an angle that my body was not made to navigate. The walls are obsidian—volcanic glass, black and sharp, untouched by the slag-plate floors or the copper veins of the upper levels. The only light is the biolume of the roaches, a sickly, shifting illumination that turns the passage into a gullet of oily iridescence. I crawled. My knees and elbows bled against the glass; the blood left streaks that the roaches investigated with their clicking, eyeless heads. I wore my helmet and I pressed my bare fingers to the wall and I felt the Hum become a roar, become a *voice*, become a frequency so deep and so vast that my tuning forks screamed and my teeth ached and my vision blurred even behind the welder-green goggles.

The chamber at the bottom is not a chamber. It is a *gap*. A void in the rock where the bore-hole broke through into a space that should not exist. The mountain, when it punched through the crust, did not come alone. It brought with it a cavity—a bubble of trapped gas and heat and pressure that had been sealed in the mantle for geological ages. The Spire Throat, in its boring, punctured that bubble. And the bubble is *alive*.

Not alive in the way a human is alive, or a roach, or even a fungal mat. Alive in the way a storm is alive, or a fire—organized, self-sustaining, responsive. The void breathes. The Hum is its breath. When it exhales, the rock expands, the fractures open, the vacuum threatens. When it inhales, the rock contracts, the seals are stressed, the infrastructure groans. The roaches are drawn to it because the electromagnetic fields of the void are immense and shifting, and the roaches navigate by such fields. They gather at the edge of the gap and they *listen*, their carapaces resonating, their leg-joints clicking in time with the breath.

The void is not malevolent. It is not the angry god of the Throat-Dwellers' superstition, waiting to swallow them all. It is simply *there*, a presence in the rock, breathing at a geological tempo, indifferent to the humans and their infrastructure threaded through its prison. But it is growing. Each breath cycle, the gap expands very slightly. The bubble that was sealed in the mantle is inflating, pressing against the bore-holes, forcing the rock to accommodate it. The Spire Throat is not an incubator, as the Deep-Dwellers whisper. It is a *piercing*. The mountain punched through the crust, and the void rode the punch upward, and now it is expanding in the gullet of the spire, and the Spire Throat—the city, the machine, the mine, the nest—is the *scab* that has formed around the wound.

I know this because I have been to the edge of the gap. I have pressed my bare fingers to the obsidian wall where it ends and the void begins, and I have felt the breath of something that existed before the crust was formed. I have watched the roaches gather there, thousands of them, their carapaces glowing with the electromagnetic resonance, their bodies clicking in unison, and I have understood that they are not vermin, not infrastructure, not even true residents. They are the void's *interface*. They are how it speaks to the mineral world. The copper and iron in their carapaces, absorbed from the rock, are the conductors of its voice. The fungal mats that feed them, blooming in the fluoride seeps, are the chemical signature of its breath. The roach-migrations are not population dynamics; they are *prayer*.

What am I, then? I am the Interpreter. The Listener at the Edge. I am the only human who has been to the gap and returned to the light. I am the only one who reads the Hum not as a threat to be managed but as a language to be learned.

I spend my days on the landing at the edge of the dark, charting the breath of the void, marking the cycles on my grid. I spend my nights in the narrow space between the coolant pipe and the ferrous wall, sleeping with my bare fingers pressed to the rock, feeling the pulse. When the Hum deepens and the roaches migrate, I descend. I crawl through the bore-hole, my blood streaking the obsidian, my body vibrating with the frequency of the gap. I sit at the edge and I listen. I hold my tuning forks to the rock and I close my eyes and I *become* a sensor, an interface, a bridge between the breathing void and the clicking, roach-chitin world above.

I have learned a few things. The void's breath is slowing. The cycles that once took days now take weeks; the Hum deepens for longer before brightening again. The gap is stabilizing, finding its rhythm in the new configuration of rock and metal and pressure. This is good news for the Spire Throat—the fractures will become less frequent, the Bone-Setters less panicked, the infrastructure less stressed. The mountain is making peace with its wound. The scab is hardening.

But the void's breath is also *deepening*. Each exhale is stronger than the last. Each expansion cycle pushes harder against the rock, opens wider fractures, forces more accommodations. The void is not dying. It is *growing*. It is claiming more space inside the mountain, pressing outward in all directions, reshaping the Spire Throat around itself. The roach populations are growing with it; the fungal mats are spreading; the electromagnetic fields are intensifying. The Spire Throat is becoming less a city embedded in rock and more a *shell* around a living cavity, a carapace of infrastructure protecting the soft, breathing thing within.

I do not know what happens when the void outgrows the spire. I do not know if the mountain will crack, if the hurricane gale will find a new path through the rock, if the vacuum will rush in and freeze them all into the obsidian. I do not know if the void will simply stop breathing, having reached equilibrium, and the Spire Throat will settle into a new, stable configuration around the silence. I do not know if the roaches will keep migrating, keep praying, keep serving as the interface between the mineral world and the living dark—or if they will become something else, something new, something that bridges the gap between insectoid and void-born.

I do not know. But I listen.

Today is a deepening day. The Hum is low and slow in my chest. The mercury lamp on my landing is flickering more than usual, the sodium-orange and the harsh-white fighting each other, casting shadows that jump like startled roaches. The air from the ventilation ducts is warm and tastes of ozone and something else—a faint, fungal musk that shouldn't be here, this high above the machine floors. The roaches are moving. I can hear them in the walls, a rustling substrate beneath the throb of the pumps. They are heading down.

I pull on my boots with the roach-carapace soles. I strip the fingertips of my left glove. I check my tuning forks—all three, iron and copper and shim, clanking against each other on the lanyard around my neck. I pull on my helmet with the welded chitin faceplate. I touch the wall—the living rock, streaked with copper and sulfur—and I feel the breath of the void, slow and vast and patient.

I mark the grid. The Hum is the deepest I have recorded. The cycle is the longest I%2A I have charted. The mountain is making its most significant accommodation yet. Somewhere above me, a fracture is opening. Somewhere above me, the Bone-Setters are swarming, their rivet-guns singing, their safety lines tugging in the rhythm of panic. Somewhere above me, the Roach-Herders are watching the populations boom and the fungal mats spread, and they are calculating the harvest, and they are not afraid.

I am not afraid either. I am the Interpreter. I listen to the breath of the void and I translate it into scratches on a copper-veined wall. I am the only one who knows that the Spire Throat is not a city in a mountain, but a living thing wrapped around another living thing, and that the space between them—the gap, the fracture, the pressure hatch that sweats frost—is the space where two breaths meet and two rhythms clash and two lives negotiate the terms of their coexistence.

I descend.

The bore-hole is narrow and the obsidian is sharp and my knees and elbows will bleed and the roaches will investigate the blood with their clicking, eyeless heads. The biolume will illuminate the passage in sickly, shifting colors. The Hum will grow to a roar, to a voice, to a frequency that makes my teeth ache and my vision blur. I will reach the edge of the gap and I will press my bare fingers to the rock and I will close my eyes and I will listen.

And the void will breathe. And I will breathe with it. And for a moment, in the crush and the dark, at the edge of the hum, I will be part of the Spire Throat in a way that no other human is—not a hand, not an antibody, not a part of the machine, but a *nerve*, a conductor, a bridge between the living rock and the living dark.

And when I return to the light, I will scratch my mark on the grid, and the Bone-Setters will read it, and the Roach-Herders will click their tongues, and the Deep-Dwellers will whisper that the Spire Throat is an incubator, and the children will play games of silence and static, and the machinery will roar, and the pumps will throb, and the hurricane gale will moan against the outer walls, and the mountain will breathe, and the void will breathe, and the roaches will click and rustle and pray, and the Spire Throat will endure for another cycle, another breath, another scratch on the wall.

This is my place. This is my purpose. I am the Listener at the Edge, and I am home.
