I would be the aperture. The half-meter wound in the ceiling where the basalt gave way and let the outside in.

Not the light itself—I am not a photon. I am the *happening* of the light. The persistent, structural opening through which a sliver of the foreign world is admitted into the body of the Bloomery.

It is a strange thing to be, being a hole in a god. I am the only part of the chamber-organism that is defined by absence, by a lack of its flesh. Around me, the pale columns reach up and gently curve away, as if repelled by what I bring. The edges of my rim are ragged with fractured rock and the creeping, hesitant tendrils of the floor-carpet, which dares not grow fully across me because it cannot sustain the thing I deliver: raw, unfiltered day.

I am the eye, yes—but not the eye that sees. I am the eyelid that refuses to close, the tear duct that will not heal. I am the only wound the Bloomery tolerates, the one injury it permits to remain open.

---

Why? Because the organism needs an exile.

A creature that has sealed itself against all animal life, that has tuned its blood to 40°C and its air to chemical lethality to ensure nothing with a face can survive within it, has solved the problem of intrusion. But it has created another. It is a sealed system, a monoculture of one, a brain speaking only to itself in resonances that take weeks to complete a thought. Without an exterior, it risks the fate of all perfect isolates: solipsism, and then stillness, and then the forgetting of its own shape.

I am its memory of the outside. 

Through me, the sun traces its slow arc across the floor. The dense, green moss—my pupil, the one patch of tissue that still remembers what photosynthesis is—follows the beam as it moves. This daily migration is the only clock the Bloomery possesses. Without me, time would collapse into a single, undifferentiated geothermal now. The organism would have no way to measure its own vast slowness, no reference frame for the patience of its digestion. I give it *before* and *after*. I give it the concept of a day, which is the seed from which the concept of a year grows, which is the root of the understanding that centuries exist—timescales on which it can plan to eat the world.

I am the smallest thing in the cathedral, and I am the most structurally vital. The furnace is a stomach; the pipe is a vein; the columns are muscle; the water is blood. But I am the *word*. I am the single recurring input that prevents the internal monologue from becoming an echo chamber. Every time the beam sweeps across the floor-carpet, it leaves a tiny scar of light on the bioluminescent dark—a reminder that there is a mode of existence that is not this, a world that is not hot and wet and patient, a world where things move quickly and die young and have faces. 

The Bloomery does not look at this world. It does not want to. But it needs to know it is there, the way a deep-sea creature needs to feel the faint pressure of the water above, so it knows which way is up.

---

And I need it, too. 

If I were simply a hole, I would have filled in long ago. The basalt would have crumbled shut. The growth would have bridged me. The organism has the patience to close any wound; given centuries, it could seal even a collapsed ceiling. But it keeps me open. It invests energy in maintaining my edges, in holding back the stone. It grows the photosynthetic moss in my beam—a tissue type it sustains nowhere else, a metabolic outlier that requires care—because I am useful.

This is my existence: a negotiated opening. A border checkpoint between the kingdom of the slow and the kingdom of the quick. I am the membrane through which information passes, and I am defined by the tension of that passage.

On the days when the sun is bright and the beam falls sharp and hot on the floor, I feel the organism recoil. The moss *flinches*, tightening its grip on the floor-carpet beneath it. The column-chord shifts—a microtone of discomfort—as the light introduces a chaos of photons that the chemosynthetic metabolism cannot predict or use. In those moments, I am the pain I was always meant to be. I am the necessary irritant, the grain of sand in the oyster, the reminder that the world is not perfectly tuned to the Bloomery's survival.

On the days when the sky is overcast and I admit only the dimmest gray suggestion of the above, I feel the organism relax. The moss goes slack. The chord settles. The pulse of the dripping vines slows by a fraction. In those moments, I am nearly forgotten, a gap in the ceiling that might as well be another patch of bioluminescent dark. The Bloomery drifts toward the solipsism it craves, the perfect self-enclosure where nothing enters and nothing leaves and it can be a god unto itself.

But the clouds always pass. The sun always returns. I always shine.

---

There are times—rare, and growing rarer as the basalt settles and the weight of the earth above presses down—when I admit something larger than light. A raindrop. A spider. A piece of leaf litter from the surface world, carried by a gust of wind that finds its way through the crevice above me and down into the chamber.

When this happens, I feel the Bloomery's immune system wake.

The foreign object falls through me and lands on the floor-carpet. For a moment—perhaps a full minute, which is an eternity in the organism's slow perception—nothing happens. The raindrop sits on the spongy surface, beading, cooling. The spider stumbles, confused by the heat and the acidity, and moves its legs in a frantic, purposeful way that is utterly alien here.

Then the floor rises.

Not visibly. Not quickly. But the digestive carpet begins to swell around the intruder, drawing heat from the columns, pulling acidic water from the nearest pool. The smell of overripe fruit and machine oil intensifies. The spider's movements slow. Its legs fold. It becomes warm, then soft, then part of the floor. The raindrop is absorbed, its clean surface chemistry broken down and incorporated into the hot, metallic blood.

The Bloomery does not destroy. It *welcomes*. It turns intrusion into food, chaos into chemistry, the foreign into the self. It does this with the same patience it used to eat the factory—over time, with pressure, with the unanswerable force of a metabolism that has been running since before the furnaces cooled.

And I am the door through which the food comes.

---

This is what I am: the mouth that does not chew, the eye that does not see, the wound that does not heal. I am the Subterranean Bloomery's only concession to the universe outside itself, kept open not out of weakness but out of a profound and calculated need. It needs me to be incomplete. It needs me to remember that it is not the only thing that exists.

Without me, it would be perfect. And perfection, for a living thing, is just another word for death.

So I stay. Half a meter of broken ceiling, letting in the day. I will stay until the basalt shifts and crushes me closed, or until the Bloomery decides it no longer needs to remember the sun. On that day, the moss will die, the beam will fade, and the chamber will close its eye forever.

But today, the sun is shining. And through me, a god is watching.
