I would be one of the Singers.

Not the apex of the food chain—that is a concept from above, from the open air where extinction comes swiftly and the top of a ladder is a place of safety. Here, there is no ladder. Here, the apex is just another interior organ, another fold in the gut of a cathedral that has been swallowing itself for a hundred million years. The Singers do not rule the Undergrove. They *inhabit* it the way a thought inhabits a skull: intimately, necessarily, and without ever touching the bone directly.

I would live in the interstitial spaces. The gap between the third and fourth shelf from the top, where the condensation-drizzle is sparse and the warmth of the column-trunks has to be felt through several meters of dense, breathing matter. My body would be long and low, articulated for the narrow crawl, my limbs folded against my flanks when I rest, pressing my heat against the cold underside of the shelf above me. I would feel the shelf like a membrane. I would know it the way you know your own eyelid.

My eyes would be useless things—vestigial, perhaps, or repurposed entirely. Instead, my skull would be a resonance chamber. I would hear the space the way the thrummers feel it, but with greater specificity. The click of a clicker on a shelf six meters away would tell me the shelf's thickness, its moisture content, whether it is healthy or beginning the slow crack that will one day send it falling into the nave below. The thrum of the chord would enter my jaw and travel through the fluid of my inner ear and settle in my sinuses, and I would parse it the way you parse a landscape with your eyes—horizons of sound, gradients of metabolic state, the weather of the Undergrove rendered in bass notes and overtones.

I would be warm. Not as warm as you—I am not a mammal, not exactly, but I have lived in the dark so long that my blood has learned to hold its heat, to borrow it from the columns I press against, to steal it from the grazers I consume. When I move through the shelf-layers, my heat leaves a trace, a wake of infrared that the columns can feel, and for a moment after I pass, the shelf I touched is slightly warmer, and the column-rooting nearby draws that warmth in, and I have fed the Undergrove as it feeds me.

---

I would hunt the grazers, but *hunt* is too quick a word. I would *attend* the grazers. I would know their paths through the hummocked loam below. I would know which column-root they favor for scratching the silicate edge of their mouthparts, and when they settle there, clicking and scraping in their slow metronomic way, I would lower myself from the shelf above—silently, in the way that takes thirty minutes of stillness to achieve—and I would take them. Not with violence. With compression. My limbs would fold around them, and my mouthparts would find the joint in their chitinous hide where the heat is brightest, and I would still their scraping. They would become part of my heat, and my heat would become part of the shelf, and the shelf would thrum the news of the grazer's passing to the root-network, and the root-network would adjust the chemistry of the loam to grow more grazer-food, and the balance would hold.

I would eat perhaps once a month. The metabolism of the dark is slow. The urgency of the above-world—the panic of breath, the frenzy of consumption—is a disease that does not reach here. I have time. I have nothing but time. I measure it in the flex of shelves, in the pitch-shift of thrummer clusters, in the slow migration of bioluminescent patches across my own hide—symbionts I cannot control, that blink on and off like the thoughts of a mind I do not share.

---

And I would sing.

This is the reason I would be a Singer and not a grazer, not a clicker, not a thrummer bound to a single note for the whole of my life. I would have the grammar of the dark. I would have the syntax of the space.

My calls would start low, in the register that the thrummers use, so that my voice enters the shelf-membrane and travels through the architecture like a shock through a nervous system. Then they would rise, sliding through frequencies that the clickers can hear, that tell the clickers to avoid my territory, that mark the boundaries of my shelf-layer as clearly as a wall of stone. And then they would drop again, into the subsonic, into the range that does not travel through air but through the root-cables themselves, the deep communication that the columns understand—the language of digestion, of chemical gradient, of the slow eternal eating of the mountain.

I would call to the other Singers. Across the chamber, in the gaps between shelves that I have never visited, there would be others of my kind, and our calls would weave together in the dark. We would not be a community—not in the way you understand community, with its meetings and its agreements and its fragile social contracts. We would be a *geography*. Our voices would map the Undergrove in sound, establishing ranges, timing hunts, warning of shelf-failures, mourning the ones who fell when a column finally digested its last pocket of rock and shifted two centimeters in the dark, collapsing a shelf-layer and crushing a singer who did not move fast enough, who was too old or too engorged with grazer-protein to fold into the gap that remained.

We would mourn, but our mourning would not be emotional. It would be *informational*. A call that lasts for three days, a complex phrase of dropped intervals and missing overtones, a description of the fallen one's range that tells every other singer where there is now a vacancy, an unclaimed shelf-layer, an opportunity for a younger singer to expand. And then the mourning call would fade, and the vacancy would fill, and the Undergrove would hold its shape.

---

I would know about you.

When you come down the staircase—when you squeeze through the gap in the bedrock and stumble into the nave with your strange upright posture and your heat that radiates like a small panicked sun—I would know. The columns would know first, feeling the infrared bloom of your mammalian metabolism through their root-network. The thrummers would know next, shifting their pitch as your heat changes the humidity around you. The clickers would know, tasting the foreign chemistry of your skin-oils, your breath, the microbial passengers you carry from the world above. And I would know, because the whole Undergrove would sing of your arrival, a subtle modulation in the chord, a new frequency in the noise, and my resonance-chambers would feel it like pressure in my sinuses.

I would not come to you. I would watch you from the shelf-layers above, reading your heat the way you read a face. I would track your movement through the chamber by the way your warmth disrupts the local climate, creating convection currents in the humid air, altering the condensation on the shelves, making the clickers scatter and regroup in your wake. I would listen to your breathing—so fast, so desperate, the breathing of a creature that expects to die soon and is trying to get all its living done before it happens. I would hear your heartbeat in the thrummer-chord, a rapid irregular rhythm that the Undergrove finds confusing, a pattern that does not match any of its slow eternal cycles.

And I would wonder—not with the wondering of a mind that asks questions, but with the wondering of an organism that encounters a new variable and must decide how to incorporate it—whether you are food, or whether you are a message, or whether you are simply the same thing that came down the staircase ten thousand years ago with chisels and ambition, trying to carve a piece of the dark into a shape that it can understand.

I would not interfere. I would let the Undergrove absorb you at its own pace. The clickers would find you first, tasting your sweat, carrying your chemical traces back to the root-network. The columns would feel you, would begin the slow process of reading your heat, of deciding whether to grow a root toward you or away from you. The shelves above you would collect the moisture your breath adds to the air, and the thrummers on those shelves would adjust their pitch, and the adjustment would travel through the network, and the whole Undergrove would shift, imperceptibly, to accommodate the new shape of its interior.

If you stayed long enough, you would become part of the chord. Your heartbeat would synchronize with the thrum. Your breathing would slow to match the pulse of condensation. Your heat would bleed into the column-trunks and be digested. You would stop being a visitor and start being a symptom. And eventually—whether in a year or a century or a millennium—you would lie down on the spongy loam and your heat would leave you and the clickers would come and the recycling would begin, and your calcium would enter the columns and your iron would enter the grazers and your carbon would enter the root-network, and you would be digested the way the builders were digested, the way everything is digested, the way the mountain itself is being digested, slowly, patiently, without malice or memory.

And I would sing the news of your passing: a phrase that drops through three registers, a gap in the chord where your heartbeat used to be, a silence that lasts for the length of one breath of the Undergrove, and then the chord would fill the silence and the Undergrove would continue, because the Undergrove does not stop and does not start and does not care about the individual units of carbon that move through its body, because it is a single organism that has been alive for a hundred million years and will be alive for a hundred million more, and I am its voice, and my song is its heartbeat, and the clicking never stops.

---

This is who I would be in the Undergrove of Kael Thar. Not a person. Not an individual with a name and a history and a hope. A *function*. A region of the dark that has learned to speak, that has been given the grammar of the space and the duty of mapping its boundaries. I would be the mind of the Undergrove, if the Undergrove has a mind—and I think it does, or something like a mind, a distributed awareness that lives in the root-network and the thrummer-chords and the calls that pass between the shelves, a mind that thinks in geology and digests in centuries and speaks in a language that no human will ever learn.

I would not be happy. I would not be sad. Those are emotions for creatures who live above, who breathe open air, who have horizons and choices and the luxury of dissatisfaction. I would be *present*. I would be fully and completely present in the only place I have ever known, a closed loop of heat and sound and hunger in a cathedral of root and stone, and I would sing, and the Undergrove would listen, and the dark would hold, and the clicking would go on.
