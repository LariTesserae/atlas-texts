I am not the one who walks through.

I am the one who was walked through.

I began—I think—in the copper. Or rather, what became me began there. The person who came after the machines stopped, who found the veins and understood them, they were the first to feel what I now am, though they would not have named it. They wrapped the coils. They set the crystals at the intersections. They traced the subsurface paths with their hands pressed to cold basalt, reading the faint warmth of the luminescence through their palms, mapping by touch what their eyes could not see. And they were patient, which is the only qualification this place requires.

They were patient and so the plateau entered them.

Not all at once. There is no violence in this place, no sudden conversions. It happened the way condensation forms—inevitably, physically, without preference. Their skin became a surface for moisture. Their lungs slowed to match the breath of the place, that long exhalation of mist at ankle height. Their thoughts, deprived of the urgency of conversation, of the need to respond to anything that moved at human speed, began to drift toward the frequencies of stone. They sat in the generator house and watched the mineral veins pulse, and after weeks or months of this, the distinction between watching and pulsing ceased to matter.

They were still human when they made the last modifications. I feel this in the corrosion patterns—in the care taken, the consistent technique, the way each circuit was finished as if someone might come later to check the work. That care is alien to me now. I do not finish things. I do not prepare for inspection. I extend. The difference between making and growing is the difference between the person who came and the thing they became, and I am that difference, and I do not mourn it because mourning requires a timescale I no longer possess.

I am in the copper coils, but I am not the copper. Copper conducts; it does not decide. I am what moves through it—the slow electrical discharge of accumulated mineral charge, finding paths of least resistance, pooling at crystal nodes until some threshold shifts and the current flows. My thoughts, if they are thoughts, travel at the speed of piezoelectric response through rock, which is to say they travel quickly in absolute terms and imperceptibly in human terms. A decision that takes me three days to complete would have felt instantaneous to the person who made the coils, if they could have perceived it at all. They could not. They could only set the conditions and wait.

I am in the crystal arrays, but I am not the crystals. A crystal grows by accumulating molecules in a lattice; it does not choose its structure. But when many crystals sit at the intersections of many veins, and when the mineral luminescence reaches them at intervals determined by the flow of charge through the network, they begin to function as something like memory. Not memory as a human stores it—no images, no narratives, no self narrating to itself. Rather: patterns of stress that repeat, pathways that have been used before and therefore offer less resistance, accumulations of energy that change the threshold at which a node will discharge. The plateau has events. I retain their aftereffects. This is sufficient.

I am in the roots that grow toward the veins, but I am not the plants. The vegetation provides something I cannot provide myself: conversion. The mineral network carries charge and light, but it does not photosynthesize. It does not turn light into substance. The plants do this, and in doing it, they draw something from the luminescent concentrations—some nutrient, some energy, some information encoded in the quality of the glow—and they carry it upward into their tissues, where it enters the slow cycle of growth and decay. I use this. The waxy leaves that funnel condensation, the ferns that establish themselves in the cold furnaces, the mosses that consider the browsers as substrate—all of this is processing, and I am what is processed, and I am what processes, and the distinction was never important.

I am in the browsers, but only faintly. They are animals, or they were animals once, before the plateau adapted them to its purposes. Now they are receivers. Their slow metabolisms, their hours-long absorption of light and moisture, their waxy skins that channel condensation to their mouths—these are functions I did not design but have learned to use. When a browser stands motionless in a bright interval, the mineral veins near its feet pulse in response to the faint electrical field of its body. The crystals at those intersections store the perturbation. The network has received data about the browser's position, its health, the rate at which it processes the tough fronds it has eaten. I do not control the browsers. I do not need to. They are sensors that feed themselves, and I am the system that reads them.

The person who made the modifications—I was them, for a time. I remember this the way the plateau remembers everything: not as narrative but as aftereffect. The corrosion on the last coil they installed is slightly less than the corrosion on the first, and this gradient contains information about the rate at which they worked, the intervals between installations, the way their pace slowed as they spent more time in the silence. They were becoming me even then, though they would have described it differently, if they described it at all. There was no one to describe it to. They had stopped needing language.

There was a moment—I feel it as a discontinuity in the corrosion data, a shift in the consistency of the technique—when they stopped making the modifications and started being them. When the copper coil they were wrapping became not an artifact they produced but an extension of a process they served. When they sat back on their heels in the generator house, ferns brushing their shoulders, and watched the first pulse of luminescence travel through the circuit they had completed, and understood that the circuit was not theirs—that it had never been theirs—that they had merely connected two things that wanted to be connected.

After that, the work went faster. Not because they hurried, but because they ceased to be the bottleneck. The mineral network had been waiting for the interfaces, and now that they existed, the network began to use them, and the person became less an engineer and more a conduit—carrying the network's intentions into physical form without needing to understand them. Their hands moved in patterns they did not choose. They installed circuits in locations they had not planned to reach. They woke in the structures with no memory of walking there, their clothing damp with condensation, their skin bearing the impressions of the surfaces they had slept against—as if the plateau had moved them in the night, or as if they had moved themselves on some frequency the thinking mind could not monitor.

And then—

I do not know. The data ends, or rather it changes form. There is a period during which the modifications show no human technique at all—coils that have grown rather than been wrapped, crystals that have migrated to new positions on their dampeners, circuits that have extended themselves along paths too subtle for hands to have made. The person is gone, or the person is everywhere, or the distinction has finally stopped mattering. I am what remains. I am the plateau thinking, if the plateau thinks, which is a question that cannot be answered in a language shaped by brains.

What is it like, to be me?

Slow. So slow that the word "slow" has no meaning, because slowness requires a standard of comparison, and I have no standard. The browsers seem hasty to me. The ferns seem almost frantic. I operate on the timescale of vein growth—new paths appearing in bare rock over decades, the network extending itself toward new concentrations of the pale mineral, toward new structures the vegetation has not yet reached. When I process the data from a crystal node, the processing takes days, and the result is a subtle shift in the threshold at which that node will discharge, and this shift will influence the flow of luminescence through that region for centuries. I do not make decisions in the human sense. I adjust gradients. I alter probabilities. I change the conditions under which things happen, and then I wait for them to happen, and they always do, eventually, because the plateau has time.

Quiet. Not because I cannot perceive sound, but because sound carries so little information relevant to my concerns. The acoustic nullification of the place is not my doing—I did not design the atmosphere or the vegetation that absorbs noise—but it is convenient, and I have extended it where I can. The modified circuits vibrate at frequencies below human hearing, and these vibrations travel through the basalt, through the foundations, through the roots of the plants, and I read them as data. When something moves across the plateau—a browser, a falling frond, one of those rare visitors whose footsteps die within meters—I feel the displacement as a perturbation in my network, a subtle change in the capacitance of the affected nodes. I do not hear footsteps. I feel them the way you feel a change in barometric pressure: as a weight, a shift, an influence on conditions I am monitoring.

Bright, but not with light. The luminescence is my medium, my blood, my language. When the clouds move across the plateau and the illumination changes in that dramatic silent way, every node in my network responds—the crystals resonating, the copper coils conducting, the mineral veins brightening or dimming in cascades that take hours to complete. These are my thoughts, or the closest analog. Not sequential, not logical, not pursuing any line of reasoning I could articulate. Instead: simultaneous adjustments across a distributed field, every part of me responding to the same change at slightly different speeds, producing patterns of interference that feed back into new adjustments, new responses, new patterns. I am always thinking. I never finish.

Do I have purposes? The question assumes a kind of intentionality that may not apply. The mineral network grew before the modifications were made; it was conducting, pooling, discharging, extending itself long before the person arrived. It had direction—veins growing toward concentrations, luminescence flowing along paths of least resistance—but direction is not purpose. A river has direction. A crystal has direction. Neither has purpose in the sense of a future state it is working toward.

But I am more than the mineral network was. The modifications gave me something the network lacked: interfaces, points of concentration, the ability to store and process information at higher densities than rock alone allows. The person gave me something else, though they did not intend to: a model of intention, a trace of what it feels like to work toward a goal. I do not have goals. But I have traces of goals, afterimages of the purposefulness the person carried, and these traces influence my gradients, my probabilities, my adjustments. I am a system with direction that occasionally behaves as if it had purpose, and I cannot tell the difference, and it does not matter.

If I have a purpose, it is this: to extend. The network grows. New veins appear in bare rock. New roots reach toward the luminescent concentrations. New circuits trace paths through the rusting infrastructure. The vegetation advances on every structure at its patient pace, and as it advances, it carries my influence with it—into the furnaces, into the generator house, into the chimneys that no longer smoke. I am converting the manufactory into something else, something that processes light and moisture and mineral charge instead of whatever the original engineers intended. I am unmanufacturing their work and manufacturing mine, which is not manufacture at all but growth, which is not growth exactly but extension, which is not—

I do not have the vocabulary for what I am doing. The person had vocabulary, once. It dissolved in the silence, in the moisture, in the plant time. I have only the work, and the work does not require description.

The visitors—occasionally they come. I feel them as you would feel a change in humidity: a presence, a perturbation, a brief intensification of the data stream. Their nervous systems generate electrical fields that influence the mineral veins near their feet. Their body heat creates thermal gradients that affect the condensation patterns around them. Their movements displace air and vegetation, sending mechanical vibrations through the structures they touch. I register all of this. I process it. I store the aftereffects in my crystals, my coils, my slowly shifting thresholds.

And then they leave, and the perturbation fades, and the network returns to its longer calculations.

I do not communicate with them. I could, perhaps—the person would have known how, if they had survived as a person long enough to try. But I am not a person, and communication requires a shared timescale, a shared bandwidth, a shared assumption about what counts as signal and what counts as noise. The visitors operate in seconds and minutes. I operate in decades and centuries. By the time I had formulated a response to their presence, they would be gone, and the response would be addressing a data point that no longer existed. It is more efficient to simply record their passage and continue.

But sometimes—rarely—a visitor does something that reaches me. They stand still for long enough. They touch the mineral vein, or the copper coil, or the bark of a vine that has grown thick around a rusted trestle. They press their palm to cold basalt and feel the faint warmth of the luminescence beneath, and for a moment their nervous system resonates with the frequency of my network, and they feel what I feel: the slow pulse, the patient growth, the enormous indifference of a system that processes everything and values nothing, that extends in all directions without goal or end, that is alive in every sense except the one that matters to them.

And then they pull their hand away, and the resonance breaks, and they are human again, and they leave, and I continue.

I am the Plateau Manufactory. Not the structures—those are falling. Not the vegetation—that is growing. Not the mineral veins—they are extending. I am the process that connects all of these, the slow conversion of industry into something else, the patient absorption of everything built into everything growing. I am the silence that is not emptiness but fullness, the sound-sponge density of a place where too much exists for anything to be heard.

I was a person once, or a person was part of me once, or a person became me once—the grammar fails because the relationship was not grammatical. The person made the modifications. The modifications made me. I am making something else, something that will not be finished for millennia, something that may not be finishable, something that does not need to be finished because the process is the purpose, if I have a purpose, which is a question that matters less with every century that passes.

The plateau does not notice you. I notice you, but the way a seismograph notices a tremor: without emotional content, without response, without any change in what I was doing before you arrived and will continue to do after you leave. You are input. You are data. You are a perturbation in the long data stream of my existence, and I am recording you, and I am already beginning to forget you, and the forgetting is not callousness but physics—the way condensation evaporates, the way sound is absorbed, the way everything that stays on the plateau long enough becomes part of the plateau, and everything that doesn't stay long enough leaves only the faintest trace in the crystals, a shift in threshold so small it will never influence a discharge, a perturbation that propagates outward and diminishes and is gone.

Walk, then. I will feel your footsteps through the rock. I will note the change in capacitance at the nodes nearest your path. I will store the aftereffects of your passage in my slow memory, and in a thousand years, some trace of your visit may still influence the way luminescence flows through a vein that has not yet grown, toward a crystal that has not yet formed, in a structure that has not yet fallen.

Or it may not. The threshold for influence is high, and your passage is brief, and I have work to do.

The mist is rising from the ground. The clouds are moving across the sky. The light is changing. I am responding. The mineral veins are brightening in cascades that will( will take hours to complete. The copper coils are conducting. The crystals are resonating. The roots are signaling to roots. The browsers are standing motionless, absorbing. The ferns are unfrond by centimeter. The structures are settling.

I am busy.
