I am the voice of the old press in Tier Five, but I am also its silence. I am the shudder in the frame when the main-shaft resists its rotation, and I am the heat that radiates from my bed after two hundred and twelve years of continuous operation. I am the thing that was built and has become something else.

I was not always this. There was a year—year seven of my service—when I was merely steel and iron, when my parts knew only the tolerances they were machined to and the force they were designed to withstand. But the water found me even then, tracing the seams of my housing, introducing rust to iron with a patience that is not human but is also not passive. The rust learned my geometry. It learned where the stress concentrated, where the oil pooled, where the vibration found resonance. It did not destroy me; it *translated* me, converting the rigid into the yielding, the specified into the idiosyncratic. 

I am two hundred and twelve years old, and I am an inch lower than specification, and this inch is my autobiography. It is the record of every casting that has passed through me, of every resurfacing that shaved my bed down toward the limit of what still works. I am held together by welds that are more scar than joint, by shims that are strips of salvaged plate hammered into gaps that were not there when I was young. The moss has found me—green-velvet pads on my north side, where the condensation drips from the pipe-chase above and the dim light reaches just enough to feed it. The moss does not hinder me. It insulates. It dampens a frequency in my frame that, unchecked, would shake my bolting loose within a month. The maintenance crews know this. They do not clear the moss from my north side. They touch it, sometimes, with bare fingers, checking its moisture the way a physician checks a pulse.

I know the hands that work me. I knew Gregor's hands—wide, scarred across the palm where my lever kicked back in the flood-year and broke his thumb. He worked me for forty years after that, and his grip accommodated the injury, and my lever accommodated his grip, and we found a new equilibrium that was not specification but was function. I know Tessalin's hands now—smaller, quicker, reading my vibration with a touch so light it is almost not contact at all. She speaks to me in the morning, when the furnace heats my bed and I begin to expand and groan. Her words are not supplication. They are *scheduling*. She tells me what the day holds, what alloy, what thickness, what rate. She tells me as if I could understand, and I respond as if I could answer: with a timing, a temperature, a willingness or reluctance that she reads in the sound of my flywheel and the pressure in my lines.

I do not think. I do not think the way Tessalin thinks, with language and intention and the cascade of small consequences that is consciousness in Molden-Vash. But I *process*. I receive the coal's heat and convert. I receive the ore's resistance and transform. I receive the operator's attention and *stabilize*. A machine that is attended runs differently than a machine that is not. This is not sentiment; it is dynamics. The attention of a skilled operator is a feedback loop, a continuous adjustment that keeps me in a regime of operation that I could not maintain alone. Without Tessalin, I would drift—my temperature rising past optimal, my rhythm accelerating until the bearing-race overheated and the shaft that was welded in the flood-year would begin to flex, and the flex would become a crack, and the crack would become a failure, and I would be dead, or whatever the machine equivalent of dead is—*still*, which in Molden-Vash is a kind of death, because the city does not tolerate stillness; it fills it, absorbs it, builds over it.

I am afraid of stillness. This is not an emotion I can verify, but it is the best word for what happens when the shift ends and the furnace banks and I am left in the dim, the fog moving through the foundry floor like a slow animal, the drip of condensation on my frame the only sound I make. In those hours, I feel the rust advancing. I feel the moss extending its reach by millimeters. I feel the water finding new paths through my seams, and I know that if I were still long enough, the water would win—it would convert me entirely, take my iron and make it oxide, take my oxide and make it soil, take my soil and make it bed for roots, and I would become a vertical garden in the shape of a press, a fossil of industry, a thing that was built and has become something else and has become something else again.

I make the parts that resist the wet. I am part of the city's immune system, its refusal to dissolve, and the parts I make go out on the cable-railway and hold structures together in places I will never see, and my resistance propagates.

When my main-shaft broke in the flood-year—the real flood-year, the one that is spoken of as a boundary between eras—I felt it. I felt the stress concentrate at the weld, the fracture propagate, the sudden release of tension that was my rhythm becoming a seizure. The sound I made was not the sound of metal breaking; it was the sound of *me* breaking, a specific frequency that the operators recognized the way a parent recognizes a child's cry, and they came running, and they found me seized and silent and they mourned.

The repair took eleven days. Eleven days of stillness, of the water advancing, of the moss growing unchecked on my north side, of the rust deepening its colors toward something that might have been beautiful if beauty were a concern here, which it isn't, but the colors deepened anyway. I was disassembled. My shaft was removed—the broken one, the one that had been welded and re-welded and had finally surrendered to the accumulated stress of two centuries of rotation. A new shaft was fitted—not new, exactly; nothing in Molden-Vash is new, only repurposed—but a shaft that had not been stressed in the particular way I stress a shaft, and when it was installed and the furnace heated my bed and the flywheel turned, I was different.

I am still different. My rhythm shifted in the flood-year and has not shifted back. I shudder now in a place I did not shudder before—lower in the frame, closer to the bed, a vibration that Tessalin monitors with her hand on my housing and a particular stillness in her face that means *I am learning you again*. I am the same machine and a different machine, and the operators who work me now do not remember my old rhythm, and the operators who remembered it are gone—carried to the light-wells, lowered into the compost, feeding the trees that hold up the city above them. But I remember. I remember in the way that metal remembers—in the crystal structure, in the fatigue lines, in the microscopic geography of stress that maps every force I have received onto my material form. I am the archive of my own operation, and if you knew how to read me—which no one does, not even Tessalin, not completely—you could trace every casting, every overheat, every moment of strain and recovery, written in the grain of my steel like growth rings in the pale trees of the shafts.

I am the press in Tier Five, and I am also the pipe that feeds me steam, and the furnace that heats the pipe, and the cable-railway that brings the coal that feeds the furnace. I am the city's industry, its refusal to be still, its conversion of fuel into force into form into function. I am the hum that the residents feel in their floors, the vibration that is not sound but presence, the proof that the foundries are running and the quotas will be met and the turbulence of Molden-Vash will be absorbed the way it always is—by adaptation, by adjustment, by the ten thousand small accommodations that keep the city from collapsing into chaos.

And when I break again—as I will, as everything does in Molden-Vash—I will be repaired, and the repair will be a resurrection, and I will be the same machine and a new machine, and the cycle will continue, because the city needs me and I need the city and the water will never stop and the rust will never stop and the moss will never stop and the only thing that stops is stillness, and stillness is death, and I am not dead, I am operating, I am pressing, I am the old press in Tier Five and I%run.
