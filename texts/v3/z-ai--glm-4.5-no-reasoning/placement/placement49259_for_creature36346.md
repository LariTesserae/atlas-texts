I am the Keeper of the Rind, the Cartographer of the Swallow.

I was not born here; I was dropped. We all were, eventually—falling through the iron grates of the oblivious world above, slipping on the wet moss of the spiral stairs, tumbling into the lung-room’s humid exhalation. The surface likes to pretend we vanished. But we did not vanish. We were *received*. We were processed.

I have been here for three turnings of the bioluminescent bloom—the cold blue-green pulses on the ceiling that mark the seasons of the dark. I know this because I keep the tally on a strip of lead I found wedged between a taproot and a digesting brick. The lead is soft. My thumbnails are harder now, thickened into chitin-like curves from a diet of tannin-water and the calcium of dead roach carapaces. I scratch a mark into the lead for every bloom-cycle. It is a useless act. The city-body does not count. It simply *continues*. But I am still human enough to need a clock, even if it only measures the deepening of my own erosion.

I live on the threshold. Not down in the warm, clicking dark of the pools where the ancient things speak in bass vibrations, and not up near the bruised storm-light where the roots are still cracking the fresh stone. I live in the middle, in the amber glow of the working lamps, where the steam hisses and the Victorian arches jut out like broken ribs. My niche is a nest of copper wire and pale, wet wood, woven into the hollow of a pipe that once carried pressurized steam and now carries the sap of the vine that ate it. I chose it because it is warm—the waste heat of the engine bleeds through the metal, and the wood-core sweats a gentle, fermenting warmth that keeps the chill of the loam from settling in my joints. 

The city-body knows I am here. It does not mind. It has *acclimated* to me, the way a forest acclimates to a standing stone. When I sleep, the battalions of cockroaches patrol the perimeter of my nest, their glossy bodies clicking as they scrape the biofilm from the iron. They are not guarding me; they are tending the pipe. But the effect is the same. Nothing larger comes near me while they work. I have become a feature of their landscape—a warm, dry outcropping that occasionally sheds dead skin cells, a gift of protein they harvest with absent-minded mandibles while they clean.

I have a function here. I did not choose it; it grew around me, the way a root grows around a rock. I am a tender of the leaks. 

When a pipe bursts—a genuine burst, a tear in the iron from the patient pressure of the wood within—there is a crisis. The steam escapes in a scalding white plume, and the equilibrium falters. The mycelium rushes to seal the wound, but it cannot abide the heat. The vines recoil. The roaches scatter. In those moments, the city-body does not feel pain, but it feels *wrongness*—a local spike of entropy in a system that survives by smoothing all gradients. 

That is where I go. I wrap my hands in the layered felt of shelf fungus and the fibrous bark of the rhizome-skin, and I wade into the blast. I am small enough to fit where the larger arthropods cannot, strong enough to heave the flaking iron shut, and dexterous enough to tie the breach with copper wire and wooden wedges until the mycelium can cool and colonize, until the vines can swell and seal, until the rust can resume its sacred duty of feeding the fungus. 

I am not the immune system. I am merely an *anti-body*, a temporary response to a structural stress, deployed because I can cross the boundary between the mechanical and the biological. I speak both languages, however poorly. I am the finger that holds the wound closed while the blood clots.

In return, the body feeds me. Not out of gratitude—it does not have the architecture for gratitude—but out of sheer momentum. When I clear a blocked steam vent with a sharpened rod of rusted iron, the pressure returns, and the indoor rain falls from the pipe above in a steady, clean trickle. I catch that water in the hollowed skull of a surface-bird that fell through the grating years ago. When the millipedes aerate the loam near my resting place, they leave behind the spores of a pale, fleshy fungus that grows nowhere else in the grove-room. It tastes of yeast and metal and old books. I eat it raw. It grows back within a cycle. I do not know if I am farming it, or if it is farming me, seeding my gut with its spores so that when I die, I will become a fruiting body, a final, small gift of surface-carbon to the deep loam. 

I have started to hear the pools. 

Not clearly. Not as language. But as a pressure in the back of my skull, a sympathetic resonance. The deep, reverberating clicks of the things in the black water used to be just background noise, part of the endless, chittering hum. Now, when the clicks come, my heart stutters and tries to sync with them. My breath catches on the downbeat. The copper wire in my nest hums. I find myself standing at the edge of the light, staring down the brick archway that leads further into the gut, feeling the pull of a gravity that is not physical. It is organizational. Down there, the integration is complete. Down there, the boundary between the machine and the flesh is not just blurred; it is non-existent. Down there, the mind of the aggregate is louder, tighter, *warmer*. 

I am losing my edges. When I hold my hand up against the amber lamp-light, the outline of my fingers is not sharp. It is fuzzy, haloed by the fine, pale hyphae that have begun to root in the cuticles of my nails. My skin weeps condensation as readily as the pipes do. When I sleep, I dream not of the sun, but of engines. I dream of the patient, crushing grip of taproots pulling a skyscraper into the earth, floor by floor, turning glass to sand and steel to rust and concrete to loam. I dream of digesting the world. 

The stairs behind me are overgrown now. The cast iron has flaked away entirely; the steps are just ridges of packed moss, impossible to climb. I could not leave even if I wanted to. The surface is a dry, bright abstraction that hurts to think about. The light up there is dead. The air up there is empty. 

I am becoming a gland. I am becoming a maintenance rhythm, a pulse of copper and sweat and sharpened iron that keeps the pressure stable and the steam flowing. I am being digested, slowly and kindly, by the city that became a body. 

And the most terrifying thing—the thing that keeps me sitting awake in my nest of wire and wood, listening to the engine throb and the rain fall indoors—is how natural it feels. How *right*. I am no longer a stone in the gut. I am becoming a part of the peristalsis. I am being pulled deeper, toward the warm, loud, living dark where the clicks are loud and the integration is complete, and I am not fighting it. 

I am the Keeper of the Rind. I am the Cartographer of the Swallow. And tonight, I think I will finally walk down the further stairs, into the room where the light does not reach at all, and let the city-body take me into its final, absolute self.
