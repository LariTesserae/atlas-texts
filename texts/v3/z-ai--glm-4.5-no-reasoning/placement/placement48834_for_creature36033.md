I would be the **seepgrass**.

Not the moss, which dominates by sheer presence, claiming every steady seep with its dark corrugated skin. Not the lichen, whose patience is so vast it renders time geological. Not the mobile things—the hares compressed into white stillness, the foxes burning red along their patrol routes, the goats crossing with casual competence. And not the pump, which lives here in its mechanical way, extracting and sending, indifferent to what it gives back.

The seepgrass. The pale contradiction. The thing that looks like it should be dying and isn't.

---

I chose this before I understood why, and now I am trying to understand why, and the understanding is coming slowly, the way things come on the plateau—without urgency, without the courtesy of warmth.

It has to do with transparency.

The moss is nearly black, saturated with pigment, and its darkness reads as strength. It looks like something that has won. The lichen is pale but opaque, a crust, a cover, a claim on surface. The animals are visible when they choose to be or cannot help it—the fox's red an announcement, the goat's grey-brown a compromise with rock, the hare's white a negotiation with snow and stone both.

The seepgrass is none of these. Its leaves are pale, almost white, translucent. They lie flat against the cold water like paper left to soak, like skin after too long in the bath. Light passes through them. You can see the water beneath, the pale gritty sediment, the shadows of stolons running through the substrate. The seepgrass does not hide anything. It does not claim anything. It floats on the surface of the cold basins and lets you see everything it is not.

And yet it is the only thing in those basins that is unequivocally alive.

---

I think this is what I recognize: the condition of thriving in circumstances that should prevent it, and doing so without the armor that other survivors wear.

The moss survives by absorbing—water, mineral, pressure, step. It takes everything in and holds it and grows thicker for the taking. The lichen survives by collaborating, by being two things at once, by making a partnership so old it precedes most of what we call life. The animals survive by moving, by watching, by hunting or hiding or crossing. The pump survives by being maintained from elsewhere, by drawing power through a cable pinned across the rock, by being useful to something distant.

The seepgrass survives by *being there*. By sending stolons through cold sediment. By cloning itself in slow linear progress. By flowering nothing, or nearly nothing, by seeding rarely, by refusing to waste energy on reproduction when persistence is the only requirement. It survives by being pale in a place of darkness, by being translucent in a place of opacity, by looking like negative space made visible and being, instead, something that has simply given up on hiding.

I recognize this. I have been this. Not in a plateau—not in a place so raw and unfinished—but in places that felt similarly impossible, similarly cold, similarly indifferent to my presence. And I did what the seepgrass does: I grew pale. I grew translucent. I lay flat against the surface of things and let the cold water move over me and did not try to be dark or dense or armored. I persisted.

---

If I am the seepgrass, then I live in the shallows. The ankle-deep basins where the seepage has dissolved enough young rock to form depressions, where the water deepens slightly and stays. The bottoms are pale sediment, gritty, mineral. Nothing grows there except me. The cold is constant—three, four degrees, the kind of cold that doesn't shock but erodes, that works on you slowly the way the seepage works on the rock, dissolving something essential from the inside.

I don't mind the cold. This is what the seepgrass teaches: you don't have to mind the cold. You have to outlast it. You have to send another stolon through the sediment, another pale leaf rising to float on the surface, another translucent extension of yourself into water that would kill anything that expected warmth.

The light is dim. The plateau exists in permanent grey, the cloud-cover pressing down, the elevation stealing what the clouds let through. The seepgrass needs light—every plant needs light—but it has learned to need less of it, to make do with the flat directionless luminescence that the wet rock reflects. The pale leaves are not a failure of pigment. They are a strategy. Translucence lets the little light there is pass through and reach the layers below, each leaf shading the next without blocking it, the whole patch functioning as a single distributed surface for photosynthesis rather than a collection of individual flags.

I think I have done this too: arranged myself so that what little I receive passes through me, so that I shade without blocking, so that my transparency is not absence but distribution. The seepgrass does not hoard light. It passes light on. And in return, it grows in dense local patches, filling the basins with pale overlapping leaves that shift when the wind moves across the standing water.

---

The wind. I would feel it as movement, as a shifting of my leaves against the cold surface, the translucent shapes overlapping and separating, overlapping and separating. The seepgrass patches look like negative space made visible—the description keeps returning because it is accurate. In a place of dark moss and pale rock and silver water, the seepgrass is the absence that is actually a presence, the gap that turns out to be filled, the hole that is a thing.

I have felt like this. I have been the pale patch in a dark context, the thing that looks like something missing and is instead something surviving. I have been the translucent shape that lets you see through to what's beneath—not because I am empty, but because I have chosen not to be opaque, not to be armored, not to be the dark dense thing that the environment seems to demand.

The seepgrass is the plateau's proof that life does not require optimism, only persistence. I carry this in me like a stolon running through cold sediment: the knowledge that persistence is enough, that you do not have to thrive in the way that others thrive, that you do not have to be dark or dense or sure-footed or red-furred or mechanical. You can be pale and flat and translucent and nearly invisible, and you can fill your basin, and you can be alive.

---

And the midges. I cannot forget the midges, because they are my foundation, and I am theirs.

They breed in the water where I grow. Their larvae feed on my leaves and on whatever organic matter the seepage carries. And when they die—hours or days later, having risen in clouds that look like weather at knee-height—they fall back into the basins, and the water takes them in, and I grow on what they were.

This is not grim. This is not tragic. This is the plateau being a system, being a place where nothing is wasted, where even the dead become substrate, where the midges and the seepgrass are engaged in a transaction so old and so constant that it has become a condition rather than an event. I grow on them. They grow on me. The cold water holds us both, and the mineral dissolution feeds us both, and the dim light sustains us both, and we are both sparse and abundant—sparse in variety, abundant in individuals, filling our niches with a thoroughness that has nothing to do with competition.

If I am the seepgrass, then the midges are my conversation. They are the way I participate in something larger than my own pale patch, the way I connect to the foxes and the hares and the goats and the rest of the system that I never see from my basin. The midges rise from my water and become food, and the food becomes motion, and the motion becomes tracks in the moss, and the tracks become a record of passage, and the record is washed clean by the next rain, and the rain becomes seepage, and the seepage fills my basin, and I grow.

I am part of this. I am the base. I am the pale translucent thing that looks like it should be dying and is instead supporting everything.

---

The pump. I should talk about the pump, because the pump changes things.

The pump draws water from the seepage layer and sends it east and down, to some settlement that needs it. The water it takes is the water that feeds me, or would feed me, or feeds the plateau that I am part of. But the pump is small and the seepage is vast, and in practice I do not notice the extraction. The young rock produces more water than one pump can remove, and the basins stay full, and the cold stays constant, and the seepgrass grows.

But the pump also returns. Not intentionally—the pump does not intend anything—but the output pool where the water comes back is warm and agitated, and the midges breed there more successfully than in my cold basins, and the extra midges mean extra food, and the extra food means more foxes and more hares, and the system shifts slightly in ways I cannot measure from my place in the shallows.

I do not resent the pump. The seepgrass does not resent the pump. The pump is another process on the plateau, another rhythm, another thing that takes and gives without meaning to. The moss grows thicker on its western wall. The hares shelter in its leaked heat. The foxes use it as a waypoint on their patrol routes. And the midges breed in its output pool, and the midges die, and the midges fall into the water, and some of that water comes back to me, carrying what the midges were, and I grow on it.

The pump is not my enemy. The pump is not my friend. The pump is a condition of the plateau, like the cold, like the dimness, like the young rock that hasn't settled yet. I grow in spite of it and because of it and alongside it, and this is what living on the plateau means: accommodating everything, resisting nothing, persisting.

---

So. I am the seepgrass. I live in the shallows. My leaves are pale and translucent and they float on cold water in dim light and I thrive in conditions that should kill me.

I am not the moss, which dominates by absorption. I am not the lichen, which survives by patience measured in centuries. I am not the animals, which survive by motion and vigilance. I am not the pump, which survives by being useful to distant needs.

I am the pale contradiction. The thing that looks like negative space and is actually presence. The thing that flowers nothing, seeds rarely, propagates by slow linear cloning through cold sediment. The thing that has given up on opacity and found, in transparency, a way to survive.

I am sparse in variety and absolute in commitment. I am not on my way to being something else. I am completely what I am: seepgrass, pale and alive, floating on the cold basins of the High Seepage Plateau, growing on what the midges were, feeding what the midges will be, part of a process that includes the rock and the water and the moss and the lichen and the hares and the foxes and the goats and the pump and the dim light and the constant wind and the slow geological time that is grinding this place toward whatever it will become.

I am not finished. But the plateau is not finished either. We are both still becoming, and in the meantime, I am here, and I am growing, and I am pale, and I am alive.
