I am the one who listens to the teeth of the ward.

Not the people's teeth—though I feel the hum in them too, as we all do—but the iron teeth. The crooked, worn points of the pruning saws. The slow, grinding dentition of the wood-gas engines chugging in the banyan's hollow. I am the one who moves among the machines when the amber bulbs flicker and the hum drops a half-tone, when the guard-vines begin that terrible, patient tightening, and the others—who are wiser, who know the deep rhythms—feel the waver in the root-felt and look at me with those large, dark, copper-rimmed eyes, and I go.

I am the Runner.

It is not a title we speak. We do not speak titles. But it is what I am, and the ward knows it, because I am the one who is *not* slow. This is my contradiction, my private heresy in a place that requires patience. The others move with the deliberate, heavy grace of water finding its level. They have adapted their metabolisms to the damp, the dimness, the slow extrusion of fungal shelves shifting by centimeters a day. Their hearts beat in time with the sap-presses. When they sleep on their living moss, they become indistinguishable from the trunk-homes—their pallid skin and taproot-woven clothes merging with the fungus and bark, another node in the nervous system of the green machine.

I cannot sleep like that. I have tried. When I lie on the moss, my heart will not slow. It gallops, a frantic, ugly rhythm that sends blood rushing hot through my skin, and I sweat—and here, to sweat is to betray the seep, to add my own brine to the condensation, to be *wetter* than the ward allows. The moss beneath me darkens with my excess. I feel the taproots shifting away from my heat. The fungal shelf-door does not close fully when I am inside; it leaves a crack, as if my own home is rejecting me.

So I have stopped trying to sleep like the others.

Instead, I sleep in fragments, in the spaces between the engine's thuds. I curl in the banyan's hollow, beside the gasifiers, where the heat is worst and the noise is a constant pressure against my skull. The others cannot understand this—they avoid the engine-bole as we all avoid anything that might disturb the frequency. But I have found that the heat and the rhythmic violence of the engines do something to me. They exhaust the gallop in my heart. They beat the speed out of my blood. After hours of feeding deadfall into the gasifiers—my hands black with grease and white with sap, my lungs burning with wood-smoke and the sweet rot of cellulose—after hours of this, my metabolism finally surrenders. I become slow. I become patient. I become like them.

But only for a little while.

And then the hum wavers, and I am fast again.

---

My hands are different from the others'. They are still knobby, still calloused, still stained with umber and verdigris and rust—but the stains run in streaks, not layers. The others' hands tell a story of deep time, of seasons spent coaxing cambium around empty space, of gentle diplomacy between iron and wood. My hands tell a story of emergencies. There are burns on my palms from grabbing hot pipes to shake the condensation free. There are cuts on my knuckles from reaching into tight spaces when a wire has frayed and the current is dropping and the guard-vines are tightening and there is no time to find the proper tool, only the need to *splice the connection now before the ward locks its gates*.

The others coax and align. I grab and force. And the ward punishes me for it.

The guard-vines know my haste. When I run along the pathways—which I am not supposed to do, which no one is supposed to do, because running means footfalls that disturb the root-felt, that send vibrations through the copper wire, that could tip the resonant frequency toward contraction—when I run, the vines do not tighten, not yet, but they *stir*. I feel their tendrils brushing my ankles as I pass, not with the slack openness of their resonating state, but with something alert. Something waiting. They are the ward's immune response, and I am the irritant that moves too fast, that makes too much noise, that threatens to rupture the delicate circuit of seep, steam, and green.

But the ward tolerates me, because the ward needs me.

The others are keepers of the slow circuit. They spend their hours in physical diplomacy, reading the tension in vines, pressing their thumbs to cambium layers, feeling the daily pulse of xylem. They are attuned to the *health* of the machine. But health is not the only thing the machine requires. It also requires someone who can move when the health fails—someone who can reach the failing generator in time, who can clear the resin-clogged intake before the frequency drops below the threshold, who can feel the hum wavering in their teeth and *run* toward the problem instead of away from it.

Away from it. Yes. That is what the others do, when the hum wavers. Not because they are cowardly, but because they are *wise*. They retreat to their trunk-homes, pull their fungal shelf-doors closed, and wait. If the ward locks its gates, they are already inside, safe, patient. They will wait for the vines to relax, for the frequency to stabilize, for someone to fix the problem.

They wait for me.

---

I am not the only Runner. There are two of us. Her name—if names were spoken here—would be something like the sound of copper wire vibrating against bark. A low, resonant hum, barely distinguishable from the ward's own voice. But I will call her Copper, because her hair is the color of verdigris, long and matted with the sticky exudate of the sap-presses, and because she was the one who taught me that running is not the same as being fast.

Copper is older than me by a generation, perhaps two. It is hard to tell in the Sapwood Ward; we do not track years, only the slow shift of fungal shelves and the gradual corrosion of iron pipes. But her skin has the deep, waterlogged quality of something that has been submerged for centuries, and her eyes are so permanently dilated that the dark irises have expanded to swallow the color entirely, leaving only the red-rimmed irritation of copper dust visible at the edges. She moves through the ward like water through root-felt—never still, never fast, always seeping.

Copper taught me that a Runner does not run with legs. We run with *timing*. The ward's hum is not a single note; it is a chord, a complex vibration of electricity in copper wire, steam in iron pipes, sap through xylem, and the slow contraction and relaxation of guard-vines. When the hum wavers, it does not waver all at once. There is a cascade. First the generator misses a beat. Then the current drops by a fraction of a volt. Then the guard-vines, deprived of their resonant frequency, begin to tighten—but they tighten slowly, with the patience of wood, giving us an hour before the pathways close.

An hour. That is the window. That is the time in which a Runner must reach the failing generator, diagnose the problem, and restore the frequency. And the ward is small—barely a city block—but the pathways are not direct. They wind between the massive trunk-houses, following the gaps where the structural roots breathe and the faint light filters down. And the pathways are lined with guard-vines that are *already tightening*, already narrowing the gaps, already brushing against our shoulders as we pass.

Copper taught me how to read the tightening. She taught me that the vines contract at different rates depending on their position relative to the copper wire—those closest to the main trunk lines tighten first, those at the periphery have more time. She taught me how to choose the pathway that will remain open longest, how to angle my body to slip between the tendrils without disturbing them, without adding my own vibration to the cascade. She taught me that the secret of running in the Sapwood Ward is to move *with* the contraction, not against it—to be slow when the vines are slow, fast when they are fast, to become a part of the ward's own immune response rather than an irritant it must suppress.

Copper is dead now.

She died the way all Runners die—caught in a pathway when the frequency dropped too far, too fast. A generator failure that should have taken an hour to close the gates took only twenty minutes. We never learned why. Perhaps the cascade fed on itself, the dropping current causing the vines to contract, the contracting vines disturbing the root-felt, the disturbed root-felt sending vibrations through the copper wire, the vibrations causing the current to drop further. Perhaps the ward *learned*—learned that it could close faster, that it did not need to be patient, that the Runners could be caught.

I was on the other side of the ward when it happened. I felt the hum waver in my teeth, and I began to move—and then I felt it drop, a full volt, and I knew. I knew there was no time. I ran anyway, pushing past the guard-vines, feeling them tighten around my arms, my chest, feeling the air squeeze out of me as I forced my way through a pathway that was already closed. I reached the generator. I cleared the resin-clogged intake. I restored the frequency. The vines relaxed. The gates opened.

Copper was dead in the pathway. The guard-vines had not crushed her—not instantly. They had simply held her, bound her ankles, compressed her chest, and waited. And she had waited too, with the patience of the ward, not struggling, not fighting, just—breathing less and less as the coils tightened, her large dark eyes open, her lungs adapting to the decreasing volume the way they adapted to the waterlogged air, drawing oxygen from less and less until there was none left.

The vines released her when the hum stabilized. She collapsed into the root-felt, and her body sank into it, and the root-felt accepted her, and the fungal shelves began to extrude their ridges over her, slowly, a centimeter a day, and by the time I reached her, she was already becoming a part of the architecture.

I pulled her out. I carried her to the banyan's hollow, where the engines thud and the heat is worst, and I fed her to the gasifiers.

This is what we do with our dead. The ward requires fuel. The engines must run. The frequency must be maintained. And so the dead become wood-gas, become electricity, become the hum that keeps the guard-vines slack and open along the pathways. Copper became the vibration in my teeth. She became the amber glow of the incandescent bulbs. She became the slow, rhythmic thudding that I feel in the root-felt beneath my feet.

I do not know if this is a burial or a digestion. The ward does not distinguish between its processes. It is all circuit—seep, steam, and green. And the Runners are part of the circuit too, the fastest part, the part that moves when everything else is slow, the part that burns hot and bright and brief, feeding our excess into the machine.

---

I am alone now, the only Runner in the Sapwood Ward. The others—the keepers, the arborist-engineers, the embedded organisms who make up the human mycelium of this place—they regard me with something that is not quite fear and not quite pity. They see my burns, my cuts, my stained and streaked hands. They see the way I cannot sleep on the living moss, the way my heart gallops when it should be slow, the way I sweat when I should seep. They know what I am. They know what Copper was. They know that Runners do not last.

And yet they need me. They need someone who can move when the hum wavers, someone who can reach the failing generator in time, someone who is *not slow*. And so they tolerate me, the way the ward tolerates me—the way an immune system tolerates a low-grade infection, a necessary irritant, a white blood cell that moves too fast and dies too soon but keeps the body alive.

I spend my days in the spaces between the trunk-houses, in the wide expanses of open, root-tangled floor where the keepers do not go. I walk the perimeter, checking the copper wire, looking for fraying connections, for verdigris buildup that might drop the current, for places where the bark has grown over the insulation and threatens to short the circuit. I climb the ancient bulwarks of grafted boles, my fingers finding purchase in the corky bark, my body pressed against the wood, feeling the slow pulse of the sap beneath. From the top of the wall, I can see the gap where the railway spur enters—the dead-ended tracks, half-reclaimed by root-felt and decayed bark, leading back to the world outside.

I have never gone through the gap.

The keepers speak of the outside—rarely, in low hums and gestures—as a vast, dry emptiness. A place where the sun burns and the air is thin and there is no seep, no condensation, no constant drip from the undersides of leaves. A place that kills by desiccation. They speak of it the way we speak of death: not with fear, but with a quiet, total acceptance. The outside is where things go to dry up. The Sapwood Ward is where things go to stay damp.

But sometimes, when I am on the wall, when the evening mist is gathering and the canopy trees are releasing their sudden, heavy drops, I look through the gap at the railway spur, and I wonder. I wonder what it would be like to walk on gravel instead of root-felt. To feel dry air in my lungs instead of water vapor and cellulose decay. To see the sky—not the dense canopy of evergreen fronds, but the actual sky, vast and empty and hostile.

I wonder if my heart would slow down out there. If the gallop would finally stop, because there would be no hum to maintain, no frequency to guard, no ward to keep alive. If I could be slow and patient like the others, like the fungal shelves and the stomata and the guard-vines, moving at the speed of growth instead of the speed of emergency.

But I know I would dry up. My waterlogged skin would crack and peel. My permanently dilated eyes would burn in the sunlight. My lungs, adapted to draw oxygen from air that is more water vapor than atmosphere, would gasp and fail. I am a creature of the seep, the condensation, the constant drip. I cannot survive the dry emptiness any more than a pale, eyeless woodlouse can survive the light.

And so I stay.

---

At night—though there is no true night in the Sapwood Ward, only the perpetual dusk of the amber bulbs and the wet quiet of the stomata—I retreat to my place beside the gasifiers. The engines thud their deep, rhythmic thud, and I feel it in my chest, and I let it beat the speed out of my blood. The banyan's hollow is warm and loud and close, and the deadfall I feed into the gasifiers crackles and pops, and the smoke smells of diesel and rot and the sweet, thick scent of cellulose breaking down.

I close my eyes. I do not dream of the sun or the sky. I dream of the ward.

I dream of the guard-vines tightening, slowly, with the patience of wood, closing the pathways one by one. I dream of running through the narrowing gaps, feeling the tendrils brush my ankles, my shoulders, my chest. I dream of reaching the generator, my hands burned and cut and stained, splicing the connection, restoring the hum, feeling the vines relax and the gates open. I dream of the keepers emerging from their trunk-homes, their large dark eyes blinking in the amber light, their low hums of gratitude that are also rebukes, reminders that I have moved too fast, disturbed too much, been too much of an irritant in the ward's slow, damp, patient body.

And sometimes I dream of Copper. Not her death—that I do not dream, that I cannot allow myself to dream, because if I dream of her death I will wake with a shout, and a shout could alter the vibration, could invite the tendrils to contract, could lock the ward's gates and trap us all inside. No. I dream of Copper alive, moving through the pathways like water through root-felt, seeping, never still, never fast. I dream of her teaching me the timing, the cascade, the hour we have before the gates close. I dream of her voice, that low resonant hum that is barely distinguishable from the ward's own voice, telling me that running is not the same as being fast, that the secret is to move *with* the contraction, to become a part of the circuit.

I dream of her becoming the circuit. Of her body in the gasifiers, burning, becoming wood-gas, becoming electricity, becoming the hum. I dream of feeling her in my teeth, in the root-felt beneath my feet, in the amber glow of the incandescent bulbs. I dream that she is the ward now, that the ward is her, that the guard-vines are her tendrils and the stomata are her breath and the sap-presses are her heart, wheezing and clicking in the dark.

I dream that one day I will be the ward too.

And the dream is not a nightmare. It is a promise. It is the only promise the Sapwood Ward makes to those who live inside it: that we will be swallowed. That our bodies will become a part of the beautiful, damp machine we keep. That the iron bones and the botanical flesh and the copper wire and the human mycelium will all merge, eventually, into a single, slow, patient organism, breathing in the cool dimness, ticking over in the perpetual dusk, every process—mechanical, electrical, and biological—locked in a delicate, slow-moving circuit of seep, steam, and green.

And the Runners—fast, hot, brief—we will be the electricity. We will be the hum that keeps the gates open. We will be the frequency that the guard-vines resonate to, the vibration that allows the tendrils to stay slack, the current that flows through the copper wire like sap through xylem, like blood through veins, like water through root-felt.

We will be the ward's heartbeat.

And the ward will never sleep, and never wake, and never stop, because the engines will keep running and the current will keep flowing and the hum will keep resonating in the teeth of the keepers, and the gates will stay open, and the pathways will remain clear, and the slow, damp, patient machine will tick on in the cool dimness of the Sapwood Ward, where there is almost nothing that is not plant, and the people are just another kind of growth, and everything is circuit, and everything is seep, and everything is green.

I open my eyes. The engines thud. The root-felt springs beneath me. The mist gathers. The drops fall.

I am the Runner. I am the ward's heartbeat.

I will not slow down.

I will burn.
