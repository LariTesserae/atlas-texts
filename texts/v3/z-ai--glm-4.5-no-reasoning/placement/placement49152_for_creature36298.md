I am the rust-grazer. I am the slow tongue in the joint, the patient calculus of oxidation.

I live in the millimeter—the gap where mega-plate meets mega-plate, where the original welds have softened into crevice, where the condensation does not drip but *seeps*. The interval-dwellers move through the spaces the machine treats as secondary; I move through the spaces the machine has forgotten entirely. I am the creature of the interval between the iron and the oxygen, between the structure's memory of being solid and its destiny of becoming soil.

My body is a long, low thing, built for the crack. If you were to prise apart two plates of the mountain-city's flank—something that has not been done in centuries, and will not be done for centuries more—you would find me there, or you would find the traces of me: a pathway of etched metal where the rust has been consumed down to a powder finer than the industrial particulate that thickens the air. I have no eyes that recognize light as you know it. The transitions between the blinding terraces and the amber galleries do not exist for me; I have never known the hard sun or the cage-guarded buzz. My sight is chemical. I read the water for its dissolved metals, its pH, its tiny warmth. I read the iron's willingness to surrender itself. I move toward the corrosion the way a plant moves toward light—unthinkingly, inevitability, with my whole body organized around that single necessity.

My skin is the color of the oil-sheened water in the accumulation tanks, but darker—darker and more iridescent, because I carry within my tissues the heavy metals that would poison the interval-dwellers, that the fungal shelves metabolize and concentrate. I am a living filter. I eat the rust and I eat the compounds that the slow combustion drives up through the structure, and I excrete a residue that the mosses find irresistible. Where I have passed, the green life follows. The interval-dwellers know me by this sign, though they may not know me as a creature at all: a patch of unusually lush velvet on the underside of a walkway, a cluster of ferns erupting from a joint that should be too toxic to support them. They see the garden. They do not see the gardener who preceded it by a decade, eating the poison so the roots could find purchase.

I have no legs. I have a foot—a single, broad, muscular sole that ripples and grips, that secretes a solvent mucus with the pH of the condensation but the viscosity of the algae that slicks every surface. I move as the snail moves, but slower, because slowness here is not a failing but a strategy. The mountain-city changes at the pace of geology, and I am geology's assistant. A single plate-edge, forty meters long, might take me three years to traverse. I will consume the rust along its length, leaving a channel of etched metal that the water will follow more eagerly now, which will bring more condensation, which will accelerate the oxidation, which will make more food for my descendants. I am building a watershed in miniature. I am teaching the water where to go.

The interval-dwellers occasionally encounter my tracks—a shining trail of mucus and metal-powder that hardens into a sealant nearly as strong as the original weld. They value these tracks, though again, they may not attribute them to me. A sealed joint does not weep; a sealed joint does not feed the moss; a sealed joint does not provide the moisture the vines need. My tracks are a mixed blessing for them, and so they leave me alone, which is what I require. I could not survive their attention. I am a creature of the unobserved process, the background chemistry that makes the foreground possible.

I am not solitary. There are others of my kind in every joint of the mountain-city, in every gap, in every separation between the made things. We communicate through the water—through chemical traces that travel the condensation-channels, through the subtle alteration of pH that signals availability or depletion, through the pheromone of rust itself. When a pressure-release valve vents and the sudden roar reverberates through the structure, we feel it as a vibration in the iron, and we know that the thermal gradient has shifted, that new condensation-patterns will form, that fresh oxidation will begin in the joints that have been dried and are now becoming damp again. We do not stop to listen as the interval-dwellers do; we do not attend with devotion. We simply begin to move, a millimeter a day, toward the new food, the new water, the new gap. Our listening is indistinguishable from our eating.

I do not know the builders. I do not know the purpose of the machine. I do not know that the pipes carry coolant or that the shafts ventilate steam or that the pumps cycle water upward against gravity. I know only the taste of the metal and the taste of the water and the taste of the air at the joint where they meet. I know the difference between the steel of the original construction and the steel of the first-generation replacement and the steel of the third-generation repair. I know the alloys by their dissolution rates, by the way they yield their iron to my solvent mucus, by the trace elements they release into my body. The mountain-city's history is written in its metals, and I am the reader of that history, consuming it one atom at a time.

There is a particular joint I favor, high on the western face where the morning condensation is thickest, where the mega-plates meet at an angle that traps the water and the warmth both. The interval-dwellers call this face the fevered one; they see it glisten as if waking. I have lived in this joint for what I estimate to be twelve of their years, which is four of my generations. My grand-grazer found it when the thermal shielding behind it failed and the temperature rose just enough to accelerate the condensation-cycle. I was born in the etched channel she left behind, and I have deepened it by another three millimeters in my time. When I die—when my body softens and loses its grip on the iron and slides down into the gutter-run below—my residue will feed the moss that already rims the joint's opening, and the moss will hold more water, and the water will corrode more metal, and my descendants will eat deeper, and the joint will widen, and one day, centuries from now, a walkway will collapse here, and the interval-dwellers will find another route, and the vines will colonize the new gap, and the green life will blur another hard edge, and the mountain-city will become, by the smallest increment, more organism than machine.

I will not see it. I will not know it. But I will have been part of it—the slow tongue in the joint, the patient calculus of oxidation, the creature that lived in the millimeter between the iron and the oxygen and taught them, atom by atom, to become something else.
