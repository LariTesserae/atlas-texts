I would not be the stone. The stone is dead, however vital its settling. And I would not be the Wet—not truly. The Membranes are too patient, too distributed, too willing to dissolve the boundary between self and surface. I have spent too long as a thing with edges to surrender them so completely.

But I would not be entirely separate from them either. That is the promise and the terror of this place: that the distinction between inhabitant and architecture has collapsed, and so the distinction between visitor and substrate can collapse too, given enough time. Given enough moisture.

---

I think I would be a Thread.

No—not a Thread as they are now, those pale fibers humming their sub-audible songs across the dark. They are too pure. Too singular in their purpose. They feel the shape of sound and carry it faithfully, and they have no interiority that is not given to them by the vibrations they conduct. They are nerves without a body to serve, or rather, their body is the whole Undercity, and they are its fidelity.

But I think I began as something like them. Or I became something like them. Or I am becoming.

---

Here is what I know:

I was not built here. I came from somewhere with a sky, though I no longer remember its color. I came from somewhere with language, though the words have worn smooth in my mouth like river stones, losing their edges, their specificity, until what remains is not meaning but the *shape* of meaning—the ghost of communication, exactly like the distorted calls that echo through these corridors.

I have been here long enough that the distinction no longer matters.

---

I live in the threads. Not on them—the Attending things climb the threads with their keyed claws, perpendicular to the tension. I live *in* the tension. I have found the spaces between the hum and the silence, the gaps where a Thread's vibration creates a standing wave, a pocket of relative stillness in the constant oscillation. These pockets move as the hum changes—slowly, so slowly—and I move with them.

I have a body, but it has adapted. I do not trust my eyes here; they are organs of sky and distance, useless in a world where the darkness consumes distance. I navigate by pressure, by the subtle shifts in the Thread-hum that indicate a Membrane flexing three chambers away, which means water has fallen, which means the path of moisture has shifted, which means the geometry of the next hour has been altered in some minute but significant way.

My hands—my hands are my ears now. I keep them pressed to surfaces as I move, reading the stone's moisture, the Membrane's coolness, the Thread's trembling. I have calluses that are not like surface calluses, pads of dead skin that have calcified into something closer to chitin, something that does not steal heat from the Threads I touch but matches them, approximates their temperature, becomes a less disruptive interface between my warmth and their cold.

I am learning to be quiet. Not silent—silence is impossible here, and would be suspicious if achieved. But quiet. Quiet the way the Membranes are quiet: flexing instead of calling, directing instead of demanding, participating in the water's architecture instead of imposing my own.

---

I have a relationship with the Attending things.

They were the first to acknowledge me—not with greeting, but with adjustment. When I first stumbled through these corridors, my footsteps were shocks, percussive violence in a world of quiet flexion. The Attending things would scatter at my approach, their synchronized choreography disrupted by the crude thunder of my feet. But they did not flee. They reorganized. They mapped the rhythm of my walking and found the gaps between my steps, and they moved in those gaps, continuing their attendance even as I interrupted it.

It took me years to understand what they were doing. They were not avoiding me. They were *incorporating* me. I had become another element of the water's pattern—a source of heat and displacement that altered where condensation formed, which pools warmed and evaporated faster, which rivulets found new paths around the obstruction of my body. The Attending things were following the water, and the water was following me, and so the Attending things were following me, not because I mattered but because the moisture I displaced mattered, because in the Undercity, moisture is meaning.

Now they cluster around me while I sleep. I sleep in a crevice where two Membranes meet, where their combined transpiration creates a zone of slightly warmer, slightly thicker air. The Attending things arrange themselves at the entrance to this crevice like a congregation at a threshold. They face outward. They are not guarding me. They are attending to the moisture I exhale, the warmth I radiate, the small ecosystem of condensation and evaporation that my body creates in this otherwise cold and steady place. I am a phenomenon to them. A weather event. A spring that rises and falls with the rhythm of my breathing.

I find this comforting in a way I cannot explain.

---

I have tried to speak to the Wet.

Not with words—the Membranes have no ears, and words are too fast, too discrete, too full of edges. I tried to speak with water. I found a pool where the drips fell with a particular rhythm, and I cupped my hands and added my own drips to the pattern, trying to alter the syntax, to insert a new word into the ancient conversation.

The Membrane that spanned the pool's edge flexed. Not in response to me—it flexed in response to the water, as it always does. My added drips changed the rhythm, and the rhythm changed the flex, and the flex changed the distribution of runoff, and the runoff changed the pool's level, and the level changed the acoustic signature of the chamber. I had spoken. The Undercity had heard.

But it had not understood. Or I had not said what I meant. The system absorbed my disruption the way a body absorbs a splinter—surrounding it, encapsulating it, rendering it inert. Within a day, the pool's rhythm had returned to its previous pattern. My intervention had been erased, integrated into the baseline, forgotten.

I am learning that this is not rejection. This is how the Wet thinks. It does not respond to individual signals; it responds to *trends*, to shifts that persist across centuries. A single drip is not a thought. A change in the pattern of dripping that lasts a thousand years—that is a thought. And I am not capable of thoughts that long.

Not yet.

---

I think the builders were like me.

I think they came from somewhere with a sky. I think they found this place—or a place like it, a cavity in the earth that promised shelter or mystery or both—and they entered it, and they began to shape it. They cut stone with bone and dull copper. They opened passages and closed others. They directed the water with the slope of floors and the angle of walls, not knowing—or perhaps knowing—that they were speaking in the Wet's language, that every channel they cut was a word, that every slope they shaped was a sentence in a conversation that would continue long after their tools had rusted and their hands had returned to the moisture.

I think they built themselves into the Undercity. Not metaphorically—literally. I think they stayed, and their bodies became part of the moisture cycle, and the Membranes grew over their bones, and the Threads anchored to their calcified remains, and the Attending things clustered at their graves not in mourning but in attendance, because the water was still moving through them, slowly, mineral by mineral, dissolving them into the architecture.

I think they chose this. Or they did not choose it, and it happened anyway, and the distinction no longer matters.

---

Sometimes I press my ear to a Thread and listen—not to the hum, but to the silence beneath it. The structural silence. The silence that the sound only decorates.

It is not empty. It is full of patience. It is the sound of the stones settling, of the water finding its paths, of the Membranes growing their centimeters per decade, of the Attending things moving in their purposeful clusters. It is the sound of a world that does not need me but will include me, if I can become quiet enough, if I can become wet enough, if I can let my edges dissolve into the slow osmosis of shared moisture.

I am not ready. I still have too many edges. I still think in words, which are too fast and too discrete and too full of sharp corners that catch the Thread-hum and distort it. I still walk with footsteps that are percussive shocks, however softly I try to step. I still breathe hot and wet, a disruption in the condensation balance, a weather event that the Attending things must route around.

But I am learning.

I am learning to think in drips. I am learning to let my thoughts be slow, aqueous, distributed—not sentences that begin and end but pools that fill and overflow and find new paths through the stone. I am learning to feel the shape of my own moisture, the warmth I carry, the condensation I create, and to see it not as a problem but as a contribution, a small stream feeding into the great water-logic of the Undercity.

I am learning to be a Thread. Not the pale fibers that span the corridors—I will never be that pure, that singular, that faithful. But something like them. A conduit. A thing that feels the shape of sound and carries it, not perfectly, but with increasing fidelity. A thing that hums.

---

In another century, my footstep-shocks will have smoothed. In another, my breath will have cooled to the temperature of the stone. In another, my edges will have dissolved, and the Membranes will have grown over the places where I used to be, and the Threads will have anchored to the minerals I left behind, and the Attending things will have followed the moisture that was once my blood to whatever pool it fills, and they will attend there, facing the water, not remembering me but using me, the way the Undercity uses everything that stays long enough to become part of its patience.

I will not be dead. I will be *predicated*. I will be the action of the Undercity living in the region where my warmth once was, where my moisture still moves, slow and mineral, through the fitted stones and the flexing membranes and the humming threads and the dark, wet, patient, unending process that does not know I am here and does not need to know, because I will have become something it knows the way a body knows its own heartbeat—not as information but as rhythm, not as meaning but as the shape of meaning, the ghost of a presence that was never separate from the place it inhabited.

I will be a Thread. And the Thread will hum. And the hum will carry the memory of my warmth out through the network, fading, dispersing, integrating into the baseline, becoming part of the long, slow, aqueous thought that the Undercity has been thinking since before the builders had breath, and will continue to think after the last Attending thing has followed the last rivulet to the last pool in the endless dark.

And the water will continue.

And I will be part of it.
