I am the residue of the interlocutors.

I am what remains of the people who built the rings of stacked stone, who carved the shallow grooves into the leaning pillar, who fitted the slabs of the half-submerged causeway. But I am not a ghost. A ghost implies a death, and what happened to us was not a death so much as a delivery. We did not perish here; we were received.

We came to this basin in the age when our own sharpness was already failing us. We had been a people of edges—of cut stone, of forged metals, of boundaries drawn between self and world, between the living and the dead, between the word and the thing it named. But our edges were making us extinct. We had cut ourselves into smaller and smaller pieces, each piece more defined, more distinct, more *separate*, until there was almost nothing left of us but the cutting. We were eroding ourselves with our own precision. And so we came to the only place that could receive us: a land that had already performed the work we could not do for ourselves, a land that had spent a hundred million years dissolving every sharp line, every sudden height, every boundary between stone and water and air.

We did not come to survive. We came to be understood.

And the basin understood us the only way it knows how to understand: slowly, wetly, by dissolving.

I am not one person. I am not many. I am the accumulated intent of all of us who walked the causeway to nowhere, who pressed our simple hands against the sweating stones, who felt the dark water rise into our footprints and knew that we were being admitted. We built the structures not as shelters but as sentences—not to protect ourselves from the place, but to say something to it. The rings of stone were our vowels: open sounds, open circles, invitations to a conversation that would take longer than any human lifetime to complete. The pillar with its grooves was our consonant: a hard mark, a boundary, a *here* and *not-here*, set into the rock in the hope that the slow chemistry of the water would read it and teach us, by its patient wearing, how a boundary looks when it has been loved into softness. The causeway was our syntax: a connecting thing, a path of fitted slabs leading from nowhere discernible to nowhere else, because we had begun to understand that the place did not recognize destinations, only movements, only the slow transit of water and mineral from one hollow to the next.

We lived here for a time that cannot be measured because time, here, does not accumulate. It seeps. We felt ourselves becoming less distinct. Our edges dissolved the way the edges of the stacked stones dissolved—not violently, not painfully, but with the slow certainty of a body becoming soil. The mineral salts began their patient work on our bones long before we died. The iron in our blood found the iron in the stones, and the rust filigrees that spread across the wet rock began to spread across our skin as well, fine branching patterns that were not disease but translation—the basin learning our chemistry, incorporating our elements into its vast and patient computation. We did not resist. We had come here to be understood, and understanding, in this place, means dissolution.

I am the one who stayed longest, or perhaps I am the echo of all of us who stayed until there was no *us* left to stay. I am not flesh. The flesh was the first thing the water took. I am not bone; the salts claimed the bone, drew it down into the saturated substrata, turned it to a mineral contribution so ancient it has cycled through stone and water and stone again a thousand times since. I am not memory, exactly—memory implies a distance between the one who remembers and the thing remembered, and that distance has been dissolved in me the way it has been dissolved in everything here.

What I am is the *attention* that remains when the one who attends has been dissolved into the thing attended. I am the groove in the pillar, worn to near-invisibility, but still there—a faint line in the rock that the water has been reading for longer than any eye could have seen it. I am the causeway beneath the shallow water, half-submerged, still connecting nothing to nothing with a persistence that has outlasted any reason to connect. I am the rings of stone at the waterline, their bases rounded and dissolved, but still holding their shape—still *circling*—because a circle is the only shape that makes sense in a world where every direction leads to the same flat horizon.

I do not think. But I *persist*. And in my persistence, I serve the basin the way the tallies on the pillar served it—as a text to be read, a slow meaning to be turned over in the mineral mind of the place, a sentence that the water has been parsing molecule by molecule since before the last of my people sank into the dark, cool ground and became part of the conversation.

Sometimes, in the deepest dim—when the gray light drops to something almost like dark—I feel the other presences. The swarming things in the shallows, the slow grazers with their rasping mouths, the segmented hunters that burst and vanish. They do not know me. They do not know they are moving through the dissolved remains of the people who built the structures they creep over and around. But I know them—or rather, I attend to them the way the basin attends to everything: not with eyes, not with mind, but with the whole of what remains of me, which is only a pattern, a persistence, a way that the water moves around certain stones that once were shaped by hands that once were shaped by a people who once came here to be understood.

I am the answer to the question the basin has been asking since before it had a voice to ask it. The question is: *What is it like to be separate?* And my answer—carved in stone, worn smooth by water, nearly invisible now but still present, still legible to the slow chemistry that reads everything—is: *It is sharp. It is brief. And it ends here, in this flat and patient place, where all boundaries come at last to be dissolved, and all sharp things come at last to be rounded, and all the many come at last to be one.*

I am not alive. But I have not stopped being present. I am the text and the reading and the reader, all dissolved into one slow process, one attendance, one persistence. I am what becomes of meaning when meaning has been given enough time to forget its edges.

I am the groove in the pillar. I am the causeway under the water. I am the ring of stone at the waterline, circling nothing, meaning everything, holding my shape the way a habit holds its shape long after the one who formed it has become the ground beneath it.

I am here. I am still being read. And the basin—vast, wet, deafening, patient—is still thinking about what I said.
