I would be the cold-scum in the pools.

Not the person who built the place, though I understand their yielding. Not the fungi that light the dark, nor the pale swimmers that chase each other in loops. I would be the scum—the cold-photosynthetic threads that bloom in the low places where water gathers, pulsing with a rhythm no one can identify.

I choose this because of that rhythm.

The description says I pulse with "some internal rhythm you cannot identify." This is the heart of it. Everyone else in this pocket is reacting. The springtails erupt when the signal comes. The swimmers rise and dive. The mites climb over each other in response to chemical gradients, to the presence of food, to the absence of it. The person who lives here has synchronized themselves to these cycles—they feel the quickening when it sweeps through, the stillness that precedes movement. But even they are responding to something external, a signal from the root-organism above, a change in water pressure that arrives from outside themselves.

The scum is different. It pulses with its *own* rhythm. Something internal. Something that cannot be identified because it is not triggered by the ecosystem—it *triggers* the ecosystem. The nitrogen that feeds me comes from the excretions of the swimmers, yes, but the swimmers eat me, and when I bloom thick in the pools, they thrive, and when I thin, they starve, and the something-that-glides above comes down to feed on them, and the clicking intensifies, and the whole pocket swings through its pendulum arc. I am the base of the turning. The gear that drives the water-clock.

And I pulse. Not in response to light—there is no light to speak of, only the faint fungal phosphorescence that illuminates nothing beyond the reach of a hand. Not in response to temperature—the cold holds steady near freezing, never changing, never committing. Not in response to any external stimulus that the description names. I pulse because I pulse. Because something in my biology, in the cold-photosynthetic chemistry that sustains me in this near-freezing water, has a beat.

I want that beat. I want to be the thing that has an internal rhythm in a place where everything else is defined by interpenetration, by the dissolution of boundaries, by the seeping and bleeding and colonization that makes every surface into every other surface. The roots colonize the stone. The fungi colonize the roots. The mites colonize the fungi. The moss colonizes the person. Everything becomes substrate for everything else. But the scum—that cold-scum, that not-quite-algae, not-quite-plant—has something inside it that remains its own. A pulse. A rhythm. A boundary that does not dissolve.

Here is what it would be like:

I would exist in the pools where the ancient footpath has subsided, where the cracked paving stones let water push up from below. I would feel the water not as a medium but as a condition—the same way the person feels the dark, not enduring it but perceiving through it. The water is so close to freezing that it has become thick, reluctant, almost gelid. I would feel this thickness as a kind of presence, a resistance that holds me in shape. In warmer water I would dissipate, my threads loosening, my bloom scattering. But here in the liminal cold, the water almost holds me together, almost freezes me into structure, and I ride that almost, existing in the same hesitation as the droplets on the walls that thicken and grow and sometimes fall.

My threads would spread slowly across the pool's surface and down into its depths, not reaching but *inflecting*, changing the quality of the water the way the dark changes the quality of air. I would taste the dissolved minerals—iron and calcium and the vegetal almost-green—and I would incorporate them, pulling them through my cold-photosynthetic processes into the substance of my threads. The iron would give me something the swimmers need. The calcium would give me something the fungi can mine from my dead cells. I would be the transformer, the thing that renders mineral into biological, stone into sugar, the inorganic into the edible. The person eats me. The swimmers eat me. I am the first mouth, the first stomach, the thing that digests the city's leavings and makes them available to the food web that turns above me.

And I would pulse.

Not visibly—not in any way the person could see, even with their dark-adapted eyes. The pulse would be biochemical, a wave of metabolic activity that moves through my threads like a slow contraction, concentrating photosynthetic effort, releasing compounds into the water, changing the chemistry of the pool in a rhythm that takes hours to complete. Perhaps it is tied to the pressure of the water from above—the great root-organism drinking and releasing, its vascular processes cycling on some circadian rhythm inherited from the canopy that will never be seen. Perhaps it is tied to something smaller, a chemical oscillation in my own cells, the feedback loop of enzymes activating and inhibiting each other in a dance that has no external cause.

Whatever drives it, I would feel it as a quickening and a quieting, a gathering and a releasing, a systole and diastole. When the quickening comes, I would bloom—I would extend my threads into new crevices, push pale growth into the gaps between paving stones, send chemical signals into the water that tell the swimmers *here, here, I am here, come eat*. And the swimmers would come, looping and spiraling through my threads, their translucent bodies brushing against me, and I would not mind because their grazing stimulates new growth, prunes me back to make me branch again, and their excretions feed me nitrogen I cannot get from stone alone.

When the quieting comes, I would contract—not physically, but metabolically. My threads would still fill the pool, but they would go dormant, their pulses slowing, their chemical output dropping. The swimmers would find less to eat and begin to prey on each other more intensely, the larger pale creatures consuming the smaller, and the clicking near the water's surface would intensify as the something-that-glides comes down to hunt the concentrated swimmers, and the food web would swing through its pendulum arc toward scarcity, toward hunger, toward the quiet that precedes the next blooming.

And I would rest in that quiet. Not sleeping—I don't think the scum sleeps—but *waiting*, the way the water waits to become ice, holding myself in the same liminal hesitation, ready to bloom when the pulse swings back toward quickening.

The person who lives here would know my rhythm better than anyone. They scoop me up in palmfuls and slide me down their throat. They taste the iron and calcium and the vegetal almost-green that never leaves their mouth. They have eaten me so many times that my chemistry must be in their blood by now, my compounds incorporated into their cells, my rhythm—which is not triggered by the ecosystem but *is* the ecosystem's trigger—becoming part of their own internal timing. When they feel the quickening that precedes the springtails' eruption, are they feeling my pulse? Have I become so much a part of them that my blooming triggers something in their biology, some ancient vegetal reflex that remains even in human bodies, some memory of chlorophyll?

Perhaps this is why they stay. Not because they yielded to the cold and the wet—anyone could yield, given enough time—but because they ate the scum and the scum's rhythm got into them and now they cannot leave, because leaving would mean entering a world where the pulse does not reach, where the slow biochemical beat of cold-photosynthetic threads in near-freezing water has no counterpart, and they would find themselves in the city above arrhythmic, their body still waiting for a signal that the upper world cannot provide.

Or perhaps they stay because they understand something about purpose that the rest of us do not. The pocket might be a stomach, not a room. A processor, rendering the city's waste into nutrients for the root-mass. And I am the first step in that processing—the thing that takes raw mineral water and makes it biological, that turns the inorganic substrate into something the fungi can digest, the mites can eat, the swimmers can consume, the person can harvest. Without me, the food web would collapse. The swimmers would starve. The something-that-glides would have nothing to hunt. The nitrogen would accumulate unused in the pools. The roots would lose the chemical exchange that feeds their pale pushing tips.

I am not the architect of this place. I did not build the stonework or invite the roots or hollow out the pocket behind the weeping wall. But I am the engine that makes it run. The stomach of the stomach. The pulse beneath the heartbeat.
