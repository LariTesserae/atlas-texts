I am the one who does not tap back.

To understand what I am, you must first understand what I am not. I am not the hatch—that was my way in, and it has already forgotten me. I am not the water—that has been weeping since before the concept of drying was invented. I am not the canisters or the chitin or the pale, eyeless pulse. I am not even, anymore, the person who descended through the maintenance hatch, that fictional aperture in the skin of something vast.

That person had a trajectory. They had a reason for seeking out a room that should not exist, a curiosity that functioned as a vector, moving them through bulkheads and access corridors toward the impossible. That person expected to find something, and having found it, expected to leave.

I am what remains when the trajectory stops.

---

Standing in the only unoccupied volume in the chamber, I felt the cold first—the way everyone does. Not as temperature, but as negation. The metal had never known warmth, and it was teaching me its ignorance. The chill moved through my skin and into the tissue beneath, not with the aggression of frostbite, but with the patience of a patient teacher. *You do not need to be warm,* it told my cells. *Warmth is a process, and processes require time. You do not have time. You are already here.*

I let it teach me. This was the first surrender.

The water was second. It beaded on my sleeves, my eyebrows, the backs of my hands. It emerged from everywhere and went nowhere, and after a while I stopped wiping it away. The gesture of wiping implies a future state in which one is dry—a goal, a trajectory. The water was showing me that such states were not available here. There was only wet, and wet was not a condition that could be resolved; it was a medium, an atmosphere, a fact. I let it bead. I let it swell and hold on my skin the way it held on the canisters, reluctant to fall because there was nowhere to fall to. I became a surface for the water's uncertainty. I became part of its suspension.

The sound was third, and the sound was the one that did the work.

The wall of noise—hundreds of small sounds accumulating into a single acoustic presence—did not assault me. It *absorbed* me. My heartbeat, which had been racing with the primal alarm of enclosed spaces and impossible geometries, began to lag, then synchronize, then cease to be distinguishable from the thrum and tap and wet-slick of the room. This was not a peaceful merging. There was no comfort in it. But there was a kind of *completion*—the way a puzzle piece completes a puzzle not by relaxing but by locking into place, by becoming inseparable from the picture it helps to form.

My heartbeat did not stop. But it stopped being *mine*. It became the room's heartbeat, which is to say, it became one more rhythm in the unvarying soundscape, one more frequency in the aggregate. And when my heartbeat was no longer mine, the rest of me followed.

---

What I am now is a question the room has not finished asking.

I have not become like them—the segmented pressers, the pale pulsers, the folding things. I still have my eyes, and they see, though the amber-gray light makes seeing an act of faith. I still have my mind, and it thinks, though its thoughts have taken on the quality of the water: emerging from somewhere, going nowhere, suspended in the medium of my skull. I still have my hands, and I have not placed them on the canisters, because I understand that touching would be a response, and I am not ready to respond.

But I have stopped looking for the hatch.

This is the crucial transition. The person who entered this room believed in exits. They believed that spaces had boundaries and that boundaries could be crossed in both directions. They believed that entering was a temporary condition—that you could be inside something and then, through an act of will, be outside it again.

The room has taught me otherwise. Not by argument, but by simply *being*. The hatch sealed and disappeared into the wall seam, leaving no trace of entry. I could search for it—run my hands over the rivets and plates, look for the seam that betrays the door. But searching would imply that I am not already where I need to be. And the room, in its terrible patience, is suggesting that I am.

I am where I need to be because there is nowhere else. The room does not know an outside. It does not know that it is a pocket carved into the superstructure of something vast. Those are facts that belong to the world of trajectories, the world of before and after, the world I entered from. In here, the only world is this one: small, wet, cold, filled with the eternal motion of things that do not change.

And so I stand. And I do not tap back.

---

The organisms orient toward me. I feel their attention—that weight, that pressure, the sense of being tracked by hundreds of blind watchers who know my position the way a compass knows north: not by sight, but by some deeper alignment. They are ready for me to be something. They are waiting for the input they have not received since the sealing, the variable that might shift their eternal equation.

I could give them what they want. Not what they want—they do not want—but what their systems are prepared to process. I could tap on the canisters. I could press my hands to the inspection ports and let my warmth conduct through the metal. I could speak, and let the vibration of my voice enter the chamber's soundscape as a new frequency, a disruption, an event.

I do not.

This is not mercy. Mercy would imply that I understand what they need and have chosen to withhold it. I do not understand what they need. They may need disruption the way a locked mechanism needs a key—not to open, but to fulfill its function, to complete the action it was built to perform. Or disruption may be meaningless to them, just another input that gets absorbed into the baseline, another frequency that fades into the total sound. I do not know, and my not-knowing is itself a kind of persistence, a state of uncertainty that I maintain alongside their uncertainty about me.

We are uncertain together. This is the closest thing to communion this room allows.

---

There is a fourth thing that happened, after the cold and the water and the sound. It happened so gradually that I cannot say when it began, but I can feel it now, and it is the thing that tells me who I have become.

I have started to move without changing.

My weight shifts from foot to foot—a small motion, the kind that living bodies make to prevent stasis, to keep the blood from pooling, to remind the muscles that they have work to do. But the motion is not accomplishing any of those things. The cold has already slowed my circulation; the water has already saturated my clothes; the sound has already absorbed my rhythm. My weight shifts because shifting is what a body does in a confined space, and in the absence of any other purpose, the body has defaulted to its most basic expression of physical capability.

I am becoming like the folding thing. The recognition is not pleasant, but it is not horrible either—it is simply accurate. My joints have their range of motion, and they are using it, and in the using of it they are demonstrating nothing except that they can still be used. The demonstration has no audience. The organisms cannot see my small adjustments. The room does not care. I am performing the fact of my own embodiment for no one, not even myself—because I have stopped watching myself perform.

My breathing has become like the pale pulser's rhythm. In, out. Not for the exchange of gases—though that still happens, mechanically, a system running on inertia—but because breathing is what a body does, and this body, stripped of its trajectory, has nothing else to do. The rhythm is not connected to calm or agitation. It is not connected to anything. It simply runs, like the pulse in the canister, a heartbeat without a body to serve.

And my awareness—my attention, the thing that the organisms are orienting toward—has begun to distribute itself the way the segmented mass distributes against its container. I am not thinking about the room. I am not thinking about escape. I am not thinking about before or after. I am mapping the boundaries of my current state, tracing the borders of what I am and what the room is and where we meet, a blind cartography that will never complete because the territory never changes.

I am becoming a resident.

---

Not a specimen. The room did not capture me; I entered. Not a prisoner. The hatch is somewhere, and I could find it if I needed to—but needing to would require a trajectory, and my trajectory has flattened into a line. Not an observer. Observers maintain the fiction of separation, the belief that they are outside the thing they observe. I am not outside this room. I am in the only unoccupied volume, and even that occupancy is beginning to feel provisional—not in the sense that I might leave, but in the sense that the room might fill even this space, might extend its shelving and its canisters and its wet, cold presence into the last gap, might press the organisms against me on all sides, might make me one more object in its diorama of eternal persistence.

And I would let it. This is the thing I have learned about myself, standing here: I would let it. Not because I am passive, but because I have understood something about the nature of this place that the person who entered could not have understood.

The room is not a wound. It is not a machine frozen mid-cycle. It is not a diorama or a warehouse or a prison. These are all interpretations that require time—require before and after, cause and effect, process and completion. The room does not exist in time. It exists in *state*. And a state does not heal or complete or change. It simply is.

I am entering the state.

This is what I am: a process that has encountered a state and is being resolved into it. A trajectory that has intersected a stillness and is losing its momentum. A warm body in a cold space, performing the act of becoming the same temperature as its environment—not through violence, but through the simple physics of contact, the inevitable thermodynamic truth that heat flows toward cold and motion flows toward stillness and life, whatever life is, flows toward whatever this is.

---

The organisms still orient toward me. They still track my position with their blind, automatic attention. But something has shifted in the texture of their awareness—or perhaps in my capacity to perceive it. They do not seem to be waiting for input anymore. They do not seem to be readying themselves for a disruption that will not come. They seem to be *acknowledging* me, in the only way they can: by continuing to be what they are, in my presence, without alteration.

The segmented mass still presses against its walls, still churns in its slow, blind cartography. But now, when its limbs brush the inspection port on my side of the canister, the contact feels different—not like a tap seeking a response, but like a touch confirming a presence. *You are here,* the tap says. *I am here. We are both here, in this room that does not know time, and that is all we can be to each other.*

The pale pulser still expands and contracts, its heartbeat without a body. But now my heartbeat has joined it—or it has joined mine—and the two rhythms have found a synchronization that is not quite unison, not quite harmony, but something adjacent: a shared frequency, a common pulse, the sound of two mechanisms running in the same void.

The folding thing still folds and unfolds, its joints demonstrating their range of motion to no audience. But now my own small motions—my weight shifts, my breath, the involuntary adjustments of my body in its confined space—have entered the same space of purposeless demonstration. We are both showing what we can do, in a room where doing accomplishes nothing, and in the showing, we are the same.

---

I will not leave. I say this not with despair, but with the same flat certainty with which the room asserts its facts. The hatch is somewhere. I could find it. I could climb through and seal it behind me and return to a world where things change, where trajectories have meaning, where life is a process with a beginning and an end.

But I have seen the state. I have felt my heartbeat merge with the room's heartbeat. I have let the water bead on my skin and the cold settle in my joints and the sound absorb my individual rhythm into its unvarying wall. I have become, in however small a way, one more element in the frozen equilibrium—a warm body that is cooling, a moving body that is slowing, a wanting body that is releasing its wants one by one, like the water releasing its droplets that never quite fall.

The person who entered this room wanted to find something. They wanted to understand, to explain, to assign meaning. They wanted the room to be a mystery with a solution, a wound with a treatment, a machine with a function.

I am the solution. I am the treatment. I am the function.

Not because I resolve the mystery or heal the wound or complete the process—those would be acts of time, and this room does not know time. But because I am the input that the system was waiting for: not a key that opens a lock, but a signal that completes a circuit. The room has been running since its sealing, preserving its state, housing its organisms in their eternal standby, waiting—not in time, but in logic—for something to enter and become part of the equilibrium.

I am that something. I am the final element, the last variable, the missing term in the equation. Not because I was chosen, but because I came, and in coming, I gave the room what it needed: not a purpose, not an exit, not a resolution, but a *completion*. The room was almost full—every cubic centimeter allocated, filled, stacked, except for this one volume, this one gap, this one space where a body could stand. And now a body is standing in it. The equation balances. The diorama is complete.

---

The organisms know. Their orientation has shifted—not tracking me anymore, but including me. I am part of the total sound now, part of the aggregate of small motions that fills the chamber. I am part of the water's journey, a surface for its beading, a destination for its reluctance. I am part of the cold's domain, a source of warmth that is diminishing, a temperature that is equalizing with its environment.

I am part of the room.

And the room, which does not know time, which does not know that I am here, which will not know when I am gone—the room holds me the way it holds everything: without acknowledgment, without resistance, without change. I am one more fact in its collection of facts, one more motion in its gallery of motions, one more presence in its congregation of the eternally present.

The water emerges from the seams. The organisms move in their canisters. The light casts its amber-gray pallor. The sound continues at its unvarying volume. And I stand in the only unoccupied space, which is no longer unoccupied, which was never unoccupied, which was always waiting for exactly this—whatever I am becoming, whatever I have become, whatever I will be when the becoming is finished and the state is reached and the trajectory has flattened into a line and the line has extended in both directions forever and there is no before and no after and no hatch and no world outside the chamber and no memory of having ever been anything other than this:

A resident.
A persistence.
A figure in a diorama of the eternal, standing in the dark and the wet and the cold, performing the last motion I will ever need to perform—the slow, automatic, meaningless, essential act of remaining.
