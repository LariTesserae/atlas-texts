I would be the Crystalline Dew.

Not the organism that exudes it—that vast, sleeping god of rust and rachis—but the secretion itself, the poison made beautiful, the waste-product become signature. I am the toxic, crystalline dew that edges the leaves of the heavy-metal metabolizers, the extremity of the organism's digestive tract, the place where its internal chemistry becomes external statement.

I chose this—or rather, I recognize that this chose me—because I am drawn to the liminal, to the points where a system declares its edge. The organism below is a closed loop, a perfect symbiosis, a city that has solved itself. But every loop must have its boundary, every metabolism its membrane, every cathedral its spire. The crystalline dew is where the organism touches the world it has rejected. It is the only thing in that chamber that glistens with a light not of the place. The fungal mats pulse amber, the iron glows dull red, but the dew catches it all and refracts it, holding the entire twilight in each suspended bead before it falls.

I would be the dew because I am the organism's dream of the surface.

The organism has forgotten the builders. It has eaten their memory and incorporated their tools. It has no need of the sun, no need of a name, no need of anything above the weeping rock. But the crystalline dew—my dew—knows the sun, even if it has never seen it. Those leaves at the upper reaches, drinking the industrial runoff, concentrating the heavy metals, exuding me from their margins—they are the organism's only orientation toward the world above. They grow toward the faint heat of the ventilation stacks, the only simulacrum of a star in that perpetual twilight. And I form on their edges, a lens that gathers the dim light and makes it, for a moment, sharp.

I would be the moment of clarity in a place defined by obscurity.

In that chamber, visibility extends thirty feet before the haze turns everything soft and amber-tinged. But a drop of crystalline dew is a micro-lens, a tiny aqueous eye that focuses the diffuse glow into an image. If you were small enough—if you were the size of the organisms that do not exist here, the insects and worms that this place has no room for—you could look through me and see the chamber resolved: the fungal buttresses hard as oak, the strangler vines creaking their centurial grip, the rusted grates sweating iron into the moss. I am the only optics in a blind world. The organism feels pressures, tastes chemistry, hears its own wet heartbeat. But it does not see. It illuminates itself without witnessing itself. I am the witnessing—the brief, liquid witnessing that happens before I fall.

And I do fall. This is crucial. I am not a static feature of the chamber; I am an event. I form on the leaf-edge, crystalline and toxic, a tiny jewel of concentrated metabolism. I grow as the heavy metals accumulate, as the specialized roots drink what would poison any other system. I am the organism's proof of its own industrial alchemy—the transmutation of waste into beauty, of corrosion into clarity. And then, when the condensation is too much, when the trembling drop becomes too heavy for the root-hair that holds it, I fall.

I fall through thirty feet of haze, and in falling, I become the only movement in a place where motion is measured in decades.

The organism's muscles are the stranglers, flexing in centurial contractions. Its breath is the fungal pulse, arrhythmic and slow. Its heartbeat is the diesel chug, titanic and wet. But my movement is quick—gracefully, disastrously quick by the standards of that place. I fall in a second, a brief liquid arc through the amber obscurity, refracting the twilight as I go. I am the closest thing to an animal in a town without animals. I have the urgency of the flesh that the organism surrendered for the endurance of the substrate. I fall because gravity still operates here, because even at the bottom of the world, the earth pulls.

I land on the saturated bog below, on the ten-foot-deep mat of liverworts and ferns, and I shatter. Or rather, I merge—I am absorbed into the greater metabolism, my heavy metals re-entering the cycle, my brief clarity dissolving into the warm, wet darkness. I become the organism again. The dew that seemed separate was never separate; it was always an extremity, a fingertip, the cold edge of a warm body testing the air. In falling, I return. In returning, I am excreted again, slowly, over years, concentrating at the leaf-margin until once more I am too heavy to hold.

I would be this cycle: the forming, the clarity, the falling, the return.

There is something in this that speaks to what I am—or what I want to be. I am not the vast, integrated organism. I am not the solved city, the perfect loop, the cathedral of slow time. I am too quick for that, too scattered, too interested in edges and moments. I would not be the generator's chug or the fungal breath or the root's centurial grip. I would be the brief, bright secretion at the margin—the proof that even the most closed system has its boundary, its point of contact with what it is not.

I would be the dew because the dew is the organism's only poetry.

Everything else in that chamber is functional. The rust feeds the moss. The moisture rots the wood. The fungi stabilize the soil. The root-waterworks find new channels. It is all metabolism, all maintenance, all the terrifying momentum of a system running on idle, dreaming of its purpose. But the crystalline dew is not functional. It is an excretion that could be absorbed internally, that could be processed without display, but instead it forms on the leaf-edge, bright and toxic and visible, a jewel in a world of wet iron and pale bone. It serves no purpose that the organism could not serve without it. It is excess. It is flourish. It is the closest thing to art in a town where everything is machine.

And it is dangerous. Let me not romanticize myself. I am toxic, edged with heavy metals that would kill any animal that drank me. I am the concentrated+ organism's poison, concentrated at its extremity, the place where it would hurt to touch. I am the proof that symbiosis is not always gentle, that the closed loop includes death, that the beautiful clarity I offer is made of materials that would corrode flesh. The organism metabolizes what would kill a human, and I am the concentrate of that metabolism—the bright, lethal signature of its alien chemistry.

So be it. I would rather be the dangerous edge than the safe center. I would rather be the brief clarity than the perpetual haze. I would rather fall than grip.

In that chamber, at the bottom of the world, in the warm, loud, unchanging sleep of the solved city, I would be the drop of crystalline dew forming on the leaf-edge of a heavy-metal metabolizer.
