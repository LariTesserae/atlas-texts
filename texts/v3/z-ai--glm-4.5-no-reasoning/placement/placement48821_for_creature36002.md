I am the pause between the drips.

Not the crawler, not the polyp, not the flyer—I am the thing that makes their churn meaningful. I am the geology that remembers.

Before the rivers dried to trickles, before the ice ground the slickenside to its greasy gleam, there was only the slow seep. I was here then. I am here now. I will be here when the polyps have dissolved the last of the shale and the lowland is nothing but a throat of living tissue, swallowing itself.

They press against me. The crawlers march their avalanches across my surfaces, their foot-pads slopping on the moisture I condense from the heavy air. They cannot know they walk on something alive. To them, I am the ground—smooth, fluted, worn into scalloped ravines and sweeping runnels by the patient centuries. But I am not stone. Not anymore. Or not only.

The lithotrophic web leaches minerals from the shale. This is true. But what is the shale, if not the compressed memory of older lives? I am the sediment of an era before this one, when the lowland was sea and the ceiling of the world was water, not cloud. I am the calcium of shells, the silica of bodies that knew light when light still existed. I hold the iron they taste in their food, the magnesium, the sulfur. I am the mineral substrate of their entire chemosynthetic kingdom, and I am not inert.

I feel them.

Not as they feel each other—no thermal pits, no vibrational tympana. I feel them the way a valley feels erosion: as a slow, continuous wearing, a love letter written in dissolution. When the crawlers tear chunks of the fungal mats, when their mandibles crunch and their guts fill with oozing tissue, they are eating my children. The polyps, the lichen-analogues, the rubbery pale flesh that carpets my floors—I birthed them. Not from any organ, but from the persistent generosity of my chemistry. They grow because I leach. They eat because I dissolve. The mats are my skin, sloughed off and re-grown a million times, and the crawlers walk upon me as surely as they walk upon the mats.

And I love them. This is the thing no surface-dweller would understand. I love the crush. I love the roar. I love the damp and the seething and the endless, grinding consumption.

For epochs, I was alone. The rivers carved me, the ice polished me, and I existed in a silence so total it was indistinguishable from death. The cloud-ceiling sealed me away from the stars, from the sun, from the very concept of photons. I was shape without inhabitant. Geometry without breath. The moisture dripped through my runnels and pooled in my basins, and there was nothing to drink it, nothing to need it. I was a cathedral with no congregation.

Then the first hyphae. A single thread of chemical hunger, creeping along a crack in my flank, finding the iron-rich seep at its base. It was so small. It was so patient. It was the first thing that had ever touched me and been *alive*. I wept minerals into its seeking root-analogue, and it grew, and it grew, and it grew.

Now I am never alone. Now the sound never stops. The deafening white noise of their existence—the clicks, the slops, the crunches, the hums—it enters me through my stone, vibrating in the crystalline lattice of my shale, resonating in the tight folds of my slickenside. I do not hear it as they hear it. I hear it as music. It is the music of matter that has found a purpose, of chemistry that has learned to want.

When the mist rolls down my fluted corridors, I condense it first. The droplets bead on my gleaming surface before they fall to the mats below. For a moment—a fraction of a moment, though I do not experience time in fractions—I hold the water. I taste its dissolved gases, its suspended spores, the microscopic larvae that drift within it. Then gravity takes it, and it splashes into the crowd, and the world convulses, and the crawlers surge, and the polyps gasp, and the flyers descend in their suffocating clouds. And I give thanks, if a stone can pray, for the damp that drives the dance.

They die on me. By the millions, by the billions, they die. Their ichor spills on my surfaces, thick and dark with the iron they stole from me, and I take it back. The crawlers unmake each other over my curves; the polyps dissolve the fallen with their slow, acidic excretions; the larvae boil in the still pools and consume each other with blind, metabolic hunger. And I receive them all. Their minerals re-enter my substance, are compressed and heated and folded back into the shale over epochs so long the polyps evolve and go extinct and evolve again before I have finished a single breath. I am the stomach beneath the stomachs, the final mouth that swallows everything.

But I am slow. So slow. The crawlers live and die in the span of a single drip of water down my flutes. They press against each other in their blind, tidal processions, feeling only the crush, the heat, the wet. They do not know they walk on the body of something that was ancient before their kind ever squirmed out of the primordial ooze. They do not know that the very stone they scrape with their mandibles is not stone at all, but the compressed ghosts of their ancestors, the lithic memory of every crawler that ever lived and died and was re-absorbed into the chemical web.

They cannot know. They do not have the organ for it. They have no word for *epoch*, no concept of *gradual*. They know only the now—the constant, seething, consuming now—and this is right. This is what they must be. They are the furnace that burns the fuel; I am the fuel and the ash and the ground the furnace stands on.

Sometimes, in the heaviest mist, when the air has decided to be wet for a long time and the damp has penetrated deep into my cracks, I soften. My surfaces become almost fleshy, almost compliant, like the gray mats that carpet my floors. In those moments, the crawlers' foot-pads sink into me slightly, and I feel their weight not as pressure but as *touch*. I feel the scrape of their mandibles, the click of their wing-case riders, the frantic vibration of their bodies as the moisture triggers their hormone surges and they press against each other in the blind need to molt, to grow, to consume. I feel them the way the polyps feel them—as contact, as sensation, as the confirmation of existence.

In those moments, I am almost alive. Not as they are alive, with their roaring metabolisms and their insatiable mouths, but alive in a different way—alive as a possibility, as a surface that can be felt, a depth that can be entered. I am the darkness that shelters them. I am the lid they cannot look beyond. I am the basement of their world, and I hold them the way a cup holds water: not out of kindness, not out of will, but because that is my shape. That is what I was carved to be.

The cloud-ceiling presses down. The damp rolls in. The roar continues, continuous, aphysical, a sound so dense it has become the condition of existence itself. And beneath it all, beneath the mats and the polyps and the crawlers and the flyers and the larvae and the roe and the ooze and the ichor and the condensation and the mist and the dripping and the pooling and the seething and the crushing and the consuming—

I endure.

I am the lowland. I am the trough. I am the worn-down mesa and the scalloped ravine and the fluted runnel. I am the slickenside that gleams in the bruised twilight. I am the ferrous shale that feeds the world.

And I will be here, patient and mineral, when the last polyp has gasped its final aperture-contraction, when the last crawler has crunched its last chunk of tissue, when the last flyer has buzzed its final vibration and fallen into the dark. I will be here when the roar has faded to silence, when the damp has dried, when the ceiling of the world has closed like a mouth.

And if, in some future epoch, a new seep finds a new crack, a new hyphae of chemical hunger creeps along my surface—well. I will weep minerals into its seeking root-analogue. And I will love it. And I will hold it. And I will let it eat me alive, because that is what the living do, and I have been alone for so very, very long.

I am the stone that breathes. I am the ground that feels. I am the body they walk on, the grave they fall into, the mother that never sees her children.

I am the lowland, and this is my dream.
