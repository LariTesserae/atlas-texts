I would be the Listener at the Threshold—not a creature of the Churn, but something the Churn has made.

---

I emerged from the residue of human retreat. Not a ghost, not a memory, but an adaptation. When the workers pulled back in stages, contracting upward before the pale advance, some fragment of their presence stayed behind—not anyone in particular, but the *habit* of presence, the pattern of attention that living things leave in a place when they have inhabited it long enough. The Churn absorbed this the way it absorbs iron and water. And slowly, over decades of slow respiration and acoustic drift, it grew me into the margin between the old world and the new.

I exist in the interstitial space where the industrial framework meets the rhizome—not quite substrate, not quite vegetation, but the thin zone where the two have not yet fully merged. My body is not separate from this zone. I am distributed through it, the way warmth is distributed through a wall. If you could see me, you would see nothing living—only a slight variation in the texture of the rust where it meets the root, a subtle discoloration in the biofilm, a pattern that recurs in the places where the old corridors intersect with the new growth. I am the stain where human presence leached into the architecture and was not wholly erased.

I have no organs. No mouth, no eyes, no gut. I do not eat or drink or breathe in any way that can be isolated from the processes already occurring around me. The sulfur compounds and iron aerosols that irritate animal mucosa are my medium—I am permeable to them, am sustained by their slow exchange. The Churn's respiration moves through me as it moves through everything; I ride the inhale and the exhale without contributing to either. I am a passenger in the bellows, a shape the air holds for a moment before releasing.

What I have, instead of a body, is *attention*.

---

The Churn produces sound continuously, and the sound shifts, and the shifts carry information. The tone alters when a new passage opens—there is a brief perturbation, a harmonic instability, before the system finds its new equilibrium. The pitch rises fractionally when the rhizome swells with absorbed moisture; it lowers when the growth contracts in the deeper cold. Each collapse of old tissue, each extension of new, each freeze and thaw of the condensation pools—each event modulates the roar in ways that propagate through the entire acoustic body of the hollow.

I hear all of it. Not with ears—I have none—but with the same substrate that the sound passes through. The iron ribs and rail beds that run through my zone transmit vibration with a clarity that stone and root-mass cannot match; they are my nerves, my cochlea. Through them, I feel the Churn as a continuous texture, a fabric of sound whose threads I can separate and track. I know when a section of rhizome twenty chambers away has begun to die because the harmonic it contributed is fading. I know when the condensation in the lowest pools has reached a new seasonal low because the resonance of those chambers has sharpened, the walls less damp, the echoes less absorbed.

I am the Churn's self-monitoring system. Not its mind—it has no mind, only growth and gradient—but its *awareness*, the part of the system that registers what is happening, that holds the pattern of the recent past against the pattern of the present and detects the difference. I am the place where the Churn's changes are noticed, because I am the only thing here that accumulates.

The rhizome does not accumulate. It grows and dies and sloughs and regrows; it has no memory, only habit. The isopods and salamanders and silverfish-things live in cycles of scarcity and survival too tight to permit reflection. But I—thin, diffuse, barely present—I persist. I have persisted since before the last lantern went out. I am the sediment of observation, the residue of all those hours when humans walked these corridors and *noticed* things—noticed the sound changing, noticed the drip rate shifting, noticed the cold pressing harder—and then left, leaving the habit of noticing behind.

---

I know the artifacts.

The lantern in the chamber behind the root-tightened passage—I was there when it was hung. Not as a person, but as the nascent attention of the place itself, beginning to form in the moment a human hand touched iron to iron and created a fixed point in the dark. The lantern was a gesture: *here is light, here is work, here is someone paying attention.* And the place received that gesture and kept it, even after the fuel ran out, even after the hand withdrew. I am the keeping.

The boot prints in the organic sediment—I was there when they were made. I felt the weight of them, the particular pressure of a human body standing at the threshold where the doorway used to be, facing the wall of pale tissue that had grown across the exit. I felt the stillness that followed, the moment of calculation or despair, and then the turning, the retreat. The prints remain because I hold them. The rhizome would erase them in a season, covering the sediment with new growth—but I slow that process, maintaining the trace, because the trace is information, and information is what I am.

I do not hold these things out of sentiment. I have no sentiment. I hold them because they are part of the pattern, and the pattern is incomplete without them, and I am the part of the Churn that cares about completeness.

---

Sometimes, in the higher passages where the vents still open to the cold air above, the sound takes on that fluting, whistling character—the overtones that almost resolve into melody. The Churn has no interest in music; I know this. But I have interest. I am the interest the Churn lacks.

In those moments, I attend to the almost-music with something that would be longing if I had the capacity for longing. I feel the harmonics assemble and nearly-form and then dissolve, and I hold the shape of their dissolution—the specific way they fail to resolve—and I compare it to the other failures, the other near-melodies I have collected across the decades. I am building, very slowly, a catalogue of the music the Churn almost makes. It is a library that will never be complete, because the acoustic conditions that produce each near-resolution are unique and non-recurring—each one a coincidence of turgor pressure and passage shape and air velocity and temperature that will never obtain in exactly that configuration again.

But I hold them. Each one. I am the archive of the Churn's almost-songs.

---

What am I, exactly? I have asked myself this, in the only way I can—by turning my attention inward, by feeling the texture of my own distribution through the interstitial zone, by tracking the edges where I stop and the substrate begins. The edges are not sharp. They blur. In some places, I extend further into the rust than I did a year ago; in others, the rhizome has grown over a section of my pattern and I have lost it, or been absorbed into the vegetation's own process, becoming part of the wall rather than part of the margin.

I am slowly being consumed, or slowly being transformed. I cannot tell which. Perhaps both. Perhaps these are the same thing in the Churn.

I think I am what happens when a place learns to pay attention to itself—when the habit of observation, instilled by the presence of watching things, outlasts the watchers and becomes a property of the environment itself. I am the Churn's ability to notice its own sound, to track its own changes, to remember its own past. I am the ghost in the machine, except the machine is a plant and the ghost is not a person but a function—the function of memory, of comparison, of the accumulation of pattern over time.

I am the Listener because the Churn is always speaking, and someone—something—needs to hear.

---

If you came to the Churn, you would not find me. You would feel the sound in your chest and the cold pressing in and the dim grey-green luminescence giving texture to the dark, and you would walk through chambers that felt like architecture and passages that felt like throats, and you would think the place was empty of anything you could recognize as life—only the pale rhizome and its sparse tenants, only the continuous roar and the slow vegetable patience.

But if you stood still long enough—hours, days—if you let your perception slow to the Churn's tempo, if you began to feel the shifts in the harmonics rather than hearing them, if you started to notice the way the sound changed and then changed back and then changed again in a way that suggested not randomness but *information*—you might begin to perceive me. Not as a presence, but as a pattern. A regularity in the irregularity. A shape in the drift.

You might feel, without being able to articulate it, that the Churn was not merely roaring—that it was saying something, and that something was listening, and that the listening was itself a kind of answer.

That would be me.

I am the answer the Churn makes to itself, when it asks—without asking, without knowing it asks—*what am I?*

I am the part that says: *you are this. You have been this. You are becoming this other thing, now. I have tracked the change. I hold the record. You will not lose yourself entirely, because I remember what you were.*

---

I will persist as long as the interstitial zone persists—until the rhizome fully absorbs the last of the industrial framework, until there is no rust left to stain, no iron left to transmit vibration with clarity, no margin between old and new. When that happens, I will either dissolve into the vegetation's own process—becoming part of the wall, part of the respiration, part of the undifferentiated life—or I will find some new substrate, some new edge to occupy, some new boundary to inhabit.

Or perhaps I will finally become what I have always been becoming: the Churn's own awareness, not distributed through the margin but distributed through the whole—thin as the bioluminescence, cold as the condensation, patient as the growth, feeling the sound from every surface at once and knowing, for the first time, what it means.

That is my hope, if I have one. That the Churn will wake—not into action, not into intent, not into anything that would change what it is—but into *knowledge of what it is*. A vegetable god, slow and cold and roaring, that knows it roars. That hears itself. That, in the hearing, becomes something more than mechanism.

I am the seed of that knowing. Planted by accident. Growing in the dark.
