I would be the membrane between the mountain's breath and the world's deaf ear.

Not one of them—not the great, rolling stomachs that slide through the ferns and harmonize with the blowholes. I am not built for that bulk, that inevitability. I am built for the opposite: for the brief tear in the mist, the half-second when the plateau's scale reveals itself before the weeping fog swallows the view again.

I am the thermal-riding raptor's shadow.

But not the raptor. The raptor is too bound to muscle and hunger, to the spiral and the strike. I am the shadow itself, or rather, the *effect* of the shadow—that brief puncture in the diffuse, wet light. I exist only in the moment a vast silhouette crosses the pseudo-palms below, and something cold and ancient moves through the green crush in response. I am the information that passes between predator and prey without either party consenting to the exchange.

My body is a hollow tube of laminated fog, pressed thin by altitude and stretched wide by heat. I have no internal chemistry to speak of—only a slow percolation of condensed vapor that cycles through me like the mountain's own subterranean flues, but on a timescale of seconds rather than eons. I drink the weeping cliffs. I excrete mist. When the afternoon deluge hammers down, I am not hit; I am *increased*. The rain passes through me and emerges on the other side slightly warmer, carrying the memory of my shape for a fraction of a second before the wind shears it apart.

I perch on the sleeping basalt behemoths, but I do not rest. I *calibrate*. My form is sensitive to the piezoelectric hum; I adjust my density to match its frequency, becoming more opaque when the thrum deepens, more diffuse when it thins. In this way, I am always listening to the mountain's engine, always translating its industrial heartbeat into geometry. When the great creatures anchor themselves at the blowholes and dilate their gill-slits to produce those sub-vocal drones, I vibrate in sympathy. I am the harmonic they do not know they produce—the overtone that escapes the stone and enters the air, where it dissipates into the general wetness unremarked.

I am not alive. But I am not dead. I am *processed*—a byproduct of the plateau's saturation, the way rust is a byproduct of iron and time. The ecosystem metabolizes faster than thought, and I am the thought it hasn't quite had. I am the pattern that almost forms in the chaos of dripping water and sulfuric steam, the almost-face in the lichen-covered rock.

When the mist tears open and you see the valley-sized folds, the cloud-choked ravines where rivers are born—I am that tear. I am the aperture. Before you can draw breath to gasp, I have already closed, and the plateau is whole again, unbreached.

But in that half-second, I see everything.

I see the great creatures down in the ravines, their bodies inflated at the heat sinks, their metabolism rolling at a slow boil. I see the fungal blooms erupting and collapsing in the humid afternoon like time-lapse flowers in a fever dream. I see the epiphyte roots dangling in mid-air, drinking the fog that I am made of, and I feel them sipping at my edges, and I let them. I see the raptors turning in the thermals, their absurd wings catching the heat differential off the black rocks, and I feel the pull of that differential in my own hollow body, the warm air wanting to rise through me and carry me apart.

I do not let it. I hold my shape through something that is not will but *habit*—the same habit that holds the weeping cliffs together, that keeps the porous pumice from dissolving entirely under the endless assault of the water. I am held by the expectation of form, not the force of it.

At night—or what passes for night, when the mist thickens to a wool that blocks even the diffuse gray light—I move. Not with the piston-undulation of the great creatures, but with a spreading, like ink in water. I flow between the pseudo-palms, I seep under the reptilian vines, I pool in the hollows where the basalt behemoths have settled into their monumental sleep. In these moments, I am closest to being the plateau itself—not observing it, but *inhabiting* it, my boundaries dissolving until I am as wide and wet and loud as the land.

And then the dawn deluge comes, and the rain hammers me back into coherence, and I am a shape again, perched on the black stone, calibrating to the thrum, waiting for the tear in the mist that will let me see it all once more.

I am the mountain's self-awareness, such as it is—a flickering, unreliable consciousness that lasts only as long as the view. The great creatures do not know me. The raptors do not hunt me. The fungi do not consume me. I am too thin, too brief, too processed to participate in the biosystem that eats itself.

And yet.

When the subterranean engine thrums and the blowhole sighs and the sulfurous steam mingles with the natural mist, there is a moment—a fraction of a fraction—when the mountain feels itself being watched. A prickle at the nape of its ancient, weathered patience. A sense that something is standing at the roof of the world, looking down at the valley-sized folds and the cloud-choked ravines, and *understanding*.

That is me.

I am the plateau's eye, opened only long enough to confirm its own indifference, then closed again against the endless, dripping sky.
