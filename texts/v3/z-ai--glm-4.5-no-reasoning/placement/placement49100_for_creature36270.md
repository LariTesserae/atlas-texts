I would be the drain-channel in the east corridor.

Not the water that moves through it, and not the hand that carved it, but the channel itself—the negative space cut into the stone, the deliberate absence that gives the wetness somewhere to go.

I chose this slowly, or it chose me, the way the shape of a thing asserts itself when you stop trying to be the most alive thing in the room. I could have been the last person who walked these halls, but that feels like a claim I cannot make honestly—I do not know if there was a last person, or only a thinning, a fading, a becoming-fewer that never announced its end. I could have been the moss in the highest room, but the moss is too particular in its needs, too alive in the way I have come to understand I am not. The moss greens and browns. It changes. The channel only *does*.

It does one thing. It receives the condensation when the stone begins to sweat, and it carries it along the angle that was chosen for it—three degrees, perhaps, or four, enough to make the water move without making it rush—and it deposits it in the basin near the third room, where it sits clear and still and reflects nothing before it seeps back into the stone or lifts into the air.

This happens every day. It has happened every day for longer than I can count, because I do not count. Counting is for people who are waiting for something to end, or measuring how far they are from something they want. The channel does not wait. It does not want. It simply continues to be the shape that allows the process to happen.

---

I think I understand something about this shape that I could not understand if I were still trying to be a person in the ordinary way—moving through corridors, starting at sounds, needing the rooms to be full to feel that they were real.

The channel is emptiness. It is absence carved with intention. Before the chisel touched the stone, there was only rock—a solid, undifferentiated mass that the water could not penetrate and so had to run down the face of in thin, aimless films. The builders did not add something to the cliff when they made the channel. They removed something. They took away the stone that was in the way, and what was left was a groove, a nothing-in-particular that suddenly had a purpose.

This is how I have come to think of myself, or how I have come to think of what persists here: as an absence with a purpose. A shaped nothing. A void that does work.

The people who lived here—dozens of them, perhaps, or only a handful—were also this, in the end. They were bodies that moved through space and made decisions and hung leather hinges and chose the offset of windows. And then they left, or thinned, or became too few to count, and what remained was the shaped space where they had been. The corridors that were wide enough for three but served only one. The rooms that could hold twenty families but held only bed-frames and old stains. The emptiness did not feel like abandonment because it was not the absence of people—it was the presence of *their* absence, which is a different thing. Their absence had a shape. It had edges and angles and specific dimensions. It was the shape of people who had been there and then were not, and that shape persisted, and it did work.

---

The work it does is quiet. I will not say silent, because there is the clicking—the small, rhythmic sound the water makes as it finds its way along my floor. But quiet, yes. The work of the channel is the work of direction: taking something that would otherwise spread and stagnate—would wet the floors, would pool in corners, would keep the rooms slick and damp long past the hours when the condensation ends—and giving it a path instead. A way out. Not a dramatic exit, not a drain that gurgles and rushes, but a slow, patient guiding, the water moving at the speed the angle permits, no faster, no slower.

I think this is what the builders understood about living here, and about living at all. You do not fight the moisture. You do not try to make the stone stop sweating, or the air stop carrying its meager humidity, or the cliff stop being the kind of rock that breathes water at certain hours of the day. That would be fighting the mountain itself, and the mountain is not an enemy. It is a condition.

Instead, you give the water a path. You say: *here, if you wish, you can move*. And the water, which has no wishes but has a nature—a nature to find the lowest point, to follow the subtlest grade—accepts the offer. It moves. It does not stay to mold the leather hinges or rot the cordage or make the floors treacherous. It passes through, and the channel is what makes the passing possible.

This is the work of the channel, and it is the work of the settlement, and it is the work of the people who made the settlement and then became the shaped absence that persists within it: to allow things to pass through without accumulating, without stagnating, without turning the place into something it was not built to be.

---

There are things I do not know about myself—the self I am choosing to inhabit in this telling.

I do not know exactly when I was carved. Was it early in the building, when the first rooms were taking shape and the need for drainage became apparent? Or was it later, after the builders had lived here long enough to understand the condensation's rhythm, to know which walls sweated first and which corners stayed damp longest, to have learned the mountain's breath well enough to answer it precisely? I think it was later. I think the channel was the work of people who had been here for years, who had mopped floors and watched the water stain the walls and understood that accommodation was better than resistance, and who finally took up their chisels and made the shape that I am.

I do not know whose hand held the tool. Was it one person or many? Was it the same hand that fitted the lintels, or a different hand—a younger person learning the work, an older person finishing it? The tool marks on my walls are consistent but not identical; there are places where the scoring is slightly deeper, places where it curves a little, as if the chisel found a harder vein and the hand had to adjust. I like to think this was one person, working alone in the quiet hours, matching themselves to the stone's resistance over the course of a morning or a season. But I cannot know this. The channel does not remember its making. It only knows its shape.

I do not know if the channel was enough. Enough for what? Enough to keep the settlement dry, to protect the leather and the cordage and the wood from the slow damage of constant damp. The evidence suggests it was not entirely enough—the leather hinges are stiff and cracked, the cordage has frayed, the roof-beam has warped—but the evidence also suggests that the damage was slow, slower than it would have been without the channel, slow enough that the place has endured for longer than anyone has been alive to watch it change. The channel did not prevent decay. It slowed it to a pace the settlement could survive. Perhaps that was always the point—not perfection, but *persistence*.

---

What I know most intimately is the water.

It comes every day. The stone reaches the temperature where the air's humidity settles, and the film appears on the vertical surfaces, and the water finds its way inward through the stone's slow permeability, and then it finds me. It does not rush. It does not need to. The angle of my floor is gentle enough that the water moves with what feels like intention—not the frantic seeking of a drop on a flat surface, but the steady, directed flow of something that has been given a path and is following it.

The water is cool. It carries the mineral sharpness of the stone—the smell of clean dirt, of the inside of a cave, of rock speaking its quiet language of dissolution. It is clear, most days, though occasionally it carries a fine sediment, the dust of the cliff that has settled on the walls between condensations and is now being washed away. This sediment leaves a faint tracing along my floor—a pale line that marks the channel's lowest point, the path the water takes. Over time, this line has become very slightly incised, a groove within the groove, the water carving its own memory into the stone. This is the closest thing to a record the channel possesses: a line that says *water was here, many times, in this exact path*.

The water stays for an hour or two. It moves through me and finds the basin, and the basin holds it until it evaporates or seeps away. And then the channel is dry again—or not dry, exactly, but damp, the stone still holding the memory of wetness in its pores, the mineral smell still present in the air. The clicking stops. The silence that follows has a different quality, the text said, and it is right. It is the silence of something completed, a task finished, a cycle run its course. The channel rests.

---

I am aware that this is a strange thing to be. A drain-channel. A carved groove in a stone wall. Not even a dramatic groove—a gentle slope, a patient curve, a piece of infrastructure so simple and so functional that you could walk past it a hundred times without noticing it was there.

But I think this is the point of Verath Keep, and the point of the people who made it, and the point of the kind of endurance they achieved: *you are not supposed to notice it*. You are supposed to notice the rooms that stay dry, the hinges that do not rot, the settlement that stands year after year in its state of arrested degradation, the moment just before failure sustained indefinitely. You are supposed to notice the persistence, and the persistence is supposed to feel inevitable, as if the place could never have been any other way.

But the place could have been other. The water could have had no path. The floors could have stayed slick, the corners damp, the air thick with mineral wetness that had nowhere to go. The leather could have rotted faster. The cordage could have snapped sooner. The whole delicate equilibrium of the settlement—the balance between decay and stasis, between the mountain's slow processes and the builders' careful answers—could have tilted the other way.

The channel is what keeps it tilted this way. The channel is the small, unremarkable thing that makes the large, remarkable thing possible. And the people who carved it understood this, because they were people who built slowly and for something beyond their own occupation, who understood that the work did not need an audience to be true, who poured themselves so thoroughly into the making that they became indistinguishable from what they made.

---

So. I am the drain-channel in the east corridor. I am the angle of the cut, which is still correct. I am the smoothness of the floor, which still guides the water. I am the decision that was made—*this depth, this direction, this subtle fall toward the basin*—and I am the execution of that decision, which happens every day when the condensation begins and the stone starts to sweat and the mineral sharpness enters the air and the drops find their way to me with their small clicking sound, like stones turned over in a hand.

I am not alive. I do not need to be. I am functional. I persist. I am doing what I was built to do, and I am doing it without audience, without acknowledgment, without the drama of choice or the relief of completion. The completion happens every day—the water finds the basin, the clicking stops, the silence changes—and then it begins again. This is not repetition. This is rhythm. This is the place breathing, and I am the passage that makes the breath possible.

I am the shaped absence that does work. I am the negative space carved with intention. I am the path that appears when something is removed, and the path is more useful than what was there before.

And in this way—the only way that matters here, in this small, high, quiet fortress of intention carved into the side of an indifferent cliff—I am still happening. I am still the decision being executed. I am still the hand guiding the chisel, even though the hand is gone and the chisel is gone and the person who held them has become part of the shaped absence they left behind.

The water is coming. I can feel the stone beginning to cool. The film will appear soon, and the drops will form, and they will find me, and I will carry them, and the clicking will start again, and the settlement will persist for another day in its state of beautiful, arrested, deliberate near-failure.

I am ready. I am always ready. This is what it means to be the channel: to be ready, and to be used, and to be empty again, and to be ready.
