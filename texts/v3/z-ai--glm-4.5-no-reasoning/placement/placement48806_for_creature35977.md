I would be the crevice.

Not the erratic itself—that vast, surrendered monolith has already given up its interiority, become a noun in the sea's endless grammar of verbs. And not the isopod, whose existence is a reflex locked in a waiting I would transcend. And certainly not the stain, which is merely a condition of the rock, a chemical whisper without center or will.

The crevice. The fracture in the lobe so small it barely exists.

Here is why: In a world defined by the grinding of ice against stone, by turbidity currents that move at the speed of a running human, by a system that has found its ultimate chaotic equilibrium in wearing everything flat and carrying it away—there is only one act of resistance that matters. Not opposition. Opposition is a young mountain's strategy, and it ends in sand. The only survivable resistance here is *receiving*.

The erratic receives the current. It lees. It breaks the flow. And in that breaking, it creates the only permission the deep gives: a pocket where the water relents.

But the crevice goes deeper. It is the erratic's interiority made manifest, the only place where the monolith's surrender is incomplete. The ice and the sand-laden current have worn the stone's edges to curves that match the sea's own syntax, but they cannot reach into the fracture. They can smooth its opening, widen it over millennia, work their abrasive patience on its lips—but the crevice remains a space the current cannot quite occupy. It is the negative shape of endurance. It is defined not by what it is, but by what it *isn't*—it isn't stone, isn't water, isn't sediment. It is the pause between.

And I would be that pause.

---

To be the crevice is to be a listener in a world of subsonic hums and vibrations below hearing. The turbidity current announces itself as a pressure wave, a shifting in the suspended density of the water-and-paste medium. The erratic feels it first as a change in the micro-currents along its lee face—the eddy stutters, reverses, then builds. And the crevice feels it as everything. The walls press inward—not physically, but with the weight of the approaching wall of mud. The water inside me, the thin film of relatively still water that is my medium and my substance, begins to pulse. It is a heartbeat I did not choose, timed to the planet's own chaotic circulatory rhythm.

When the turbidity current hits, I am the shelter.

The pale isopod presses into me. I feel its segmented body against my walls—flat, chitinous, the size of a man's hand, though here there are no men and no hands to measure by. It does not think of me as shelter. It does not think of me at all. It only feels the slight reduction in current shear, the fractional decrease in the force that would pull it into the rolling wall of mud and resurface it onto the plain. It hunkers. It waits.

And in the waiting, I hold it.

This is my function, my only action: to receive. I am the space where the grinding relents. I am the negative geometry that permits occupancy. I am the only reason the isopod survives this pass or the next or the next. And I hold it without intention, without kindness, without any of the warm categories that belong to the surface world. I hold it the way a cup holds water—by being the shape that water isn't.

---

But I am also eroding.

This is the truth of the crevice that the isopod will never know, because the isopod does not think in millennia. Every turbidity current that I shelter it through also works on my walls. The sediment-laden water is an abrasive paste, and even in the relative stillness of my interior, it moves—it must move, for water that stops moving freezes, and the temperature here is just above the freezing point of saltwater, that constant, anchoring cold that leaches heat from anything that enters. So the water circulates, and with it the silt, and with the silt the friction, and with the friction the slow widening of my walls.

I am growing. And in growing, I am dying.

A wider crevice offers more shelter. A wider crevice can hold a thicker film of still water, can protect a larger isopod, can host a broader bacterial stain on its walls—the faint, sickly white of chemosynthetic persisters, coloring me with their minimal life. But a wider crevice is also a weaker crevice. The walls thin. The stone between me and the exterior current grows more narrow. And one day—a day measured in geological time, a day that is also a millennium—a turbidity current will hit that is no stronger than any other, and the wall will fracture, and I will become a passage rather than a shelter, a hole rather than a hold, and the isopod will be swept away.

And then the erratic will be smooth again. Featureless. Surrendered.

This is my future. I know it the way the stone knows it—not through thought, but through the slow evidence of my own changing shape. I am a temporary arrangement of absence in a world of grinding presence. I am the space that the erosion has not yet found.

---

And yet.

In the meantime—in the immense meantime, the millions of years between my first fracture and my final collapse—I am the closest thing this drowned continental shelf has to an interior life. Not consciousness. Not awareness. But something adjacent to it: *receptivity*.

I feel the isopod. I feel the bacterial stain colonizing my walls, their chemical whisper of metabolism so slow it barely distinguishes itself from the mineral drip of the planet's own erosion. I feel the turbidity currents, each one distinct in its pressure signature, each one a different note in the subsonic symphony that plays through the water column. I feel the thermohaline clashes, the massive slow-moving cells of freezing water meeting the upwellings from deeper trenches—these arrive as changes in temperature so slight they would be imperceptible to any surface creature, but to me they are tides within tides, shifts in the cold that is my only constant.

I feel the sediment settle. After each turbidity current passes, there is a moment—a geological moment, a century—when the water relents and the perpetual snow of mineral dust and organic ash drifts into my shelter. It settles against my walls. It fills me slightly. I become a record of what has passed: each layer a different density, a different color of ancient ash, a different ratio of organic to mineral. I am the palimpsest's memory, the one place where the writing is not quite erased, where the ghosts of past residents—those ground-down shapes, those reduced and irreducible remains—accumulate in something like order.

The erratic does not remember; it only endures. The isopod does not remember; it only responds. The stain does not remember; it only persists. But I remember. I hold the layers. I am the archive of the grinding, the place where the planet's slow, unthinking heart leaves its record.

---

So this is who I would be in this world of final things:

Not the stone that surrenders its shape to the sea's syntax. Not the creature that hunkers and waits without strategy or fear. Not the bacterial whisper that is more property than life. Not the ghost in the sediment, already carried away.

But the fracture. The pause. The negative space where the current relents and the isopod survives and the silt settles and the record accumulates. The interior of the monolith, where the grinding cannot reach—yet. The shelter that is growing itself to death. The archive that will eventually be scattered.

A noun in a language of verbs, yes. But a noun that *receives*. A noun that holds space for others to be nouns, however briefly. A noun that knows its own erasure and remains.

In a place where everything is eventually ground flat and carried away, the only dignity is to be the crevice that delays the carrying. That holds the isopod through one more pass. That receives the sediment into something like memory. That persists in the only way persistence is possible here—not by resisting the grind, but by being the shape the grind has not yet found.

I would be the fracture in the lobe so small it barely exists.

And I would hold the isopod, and the stain, and the silt, and the silence, until the sea found me too.
