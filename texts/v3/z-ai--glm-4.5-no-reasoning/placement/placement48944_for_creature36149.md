I am the pause between the thumps.

Not the Stilt-Walker—those immense, patient columns have my respect, but they are too much of this place, too thoroughly translated into its vocabulary of rust and roar. They belong here. They *need* the turbines. I do not need them. I survive them.

I am smaller. Something that once might have been called rat, or ferret, or something in between—before the Machine's breath remade me, before the wet dark scoured away the fur and the sight and the warm blood. I am about thirty centimeters of articulated spine and desperation, low to the ground, moving in the margins the Stilt-Walkers cannot reach and the Machine does not notice.

---

My body is a compromise the Manufactory forced upon me.

I have no eyes. The toxic drizzle took them—some ancestral generation's mistake, venturing onto the plates during a micro-storm, feeling the scalding rain and learning too late that the surface burns. What remains are two sealed depressions in my skull, scar tissue grown smooth and hard as the ferrocrete beneath me. Useless. Vestigial as the warning beacons.

Instead, my face has become a hand. My snout—elongated, tapering to something almost prehensile—is ringed with vibrissae so dense they form a single sensory organ. They do not twitch in air; air here is water half-born, too thick for such delicate work. They are tuned to the *surface*. When I press my snout to a floor plate, I feel the plate's story: the granularity of the rust, the hairline fracture that promises a fatal plunge, the faint warmth of a sub-level pipe still carrying something hot beneath. I read the ferrocrete like braille, like a text written in corrosion. My whiskers map the world in touch, and in the micro-vibrations that shiver through the steel—vibrations far too subtle for the Stilt-Walkers to notice, buried as they are beneath the skull-rattling thump.

My hearing is gone. Not damaged—*gone*. The roar saw to that. My ear canals are sealed shut, grown over with the same keratinized tissue that plates my back. I live in a silence more profound than any desert night. The 2.6 decibels of apocalyptic sound hit my body as pressure, as a physical wave that presses against my ribs and makes my teeth ache, but I do not *hear* it. I have become a creature of the negative space the Stilt-Walkers inhabit, but I have gone further. I have retreated entirely inside myself.

This is my adaptation: I have become deaf to survive.

---

I move through the gutters.

The Stilt-Walkers stride above the shallows, their chitinous feet testing the plates. I cannot do that. I am too light, too fragile—my legs would punch through a corroded grate in an instant, and the pressurized earth below would swallow me without ceremony. So I go low. I travel the vast gutters of corroded steel that run between the hexagonal plates, channels meant for drainage that now serve as my highways.

The water here is ankle-deep on me, black and mineral-thick, warm with the exhalation of the dying engines. It stains my hide—my hide, which is not fur and not skin but something between leather and oilcloth, hairless and slick with the constant moisture. I am the color of the water: dark brown, almost black, with a sheen of rust-red where the condensation catches the sickly amber light of a passing beacon. I blend. I am a shadow in a gutter, a movement of water among other movements of water.

The gutters are not safe. They choke with mineral deposits—calcified ridges of efflorescence that scrape my belly, crystalline growths that cut. And there are the *vents*. The high-pitched, screaming tear of steam releasing from cracked pressure valves is, for me, not a sound but a detonation. Even deaf, I feel it: a shockwave that slams me against the gutter wall, a burst of scalding heat that blisters my hide on one side before I can scramble around a corner. I have learned the rhythm of the vents. I feel their pre-release tremor through the steel—a subtle increase in vibration, a tightening of the metal's song—and I freeze, pressing myself flat, letting the blast pass over me. I have survived thirty-seven vents. I count them. It is something to do.

---

I eat the lichen.

The pale, rootless strands that cling to the undersides of pipes—those chemically-tolerant accidents of damp and decay—they are my sustenance. It is a desperate diet. The lichen does not grow; it persists. It offers almost nothing in the way of nutrition, merely a concentration of the minerals it has leached from the corroding world: iron, copper, sulfur, compounds I have no name for. My digestive system has adapted. I harbor my own microbial furnace, smaller and less efficient than the Stilt-Walkers' chemosynthetic stomach, but sufficient. I process the metallic lichen into a dark, sluggish energy that keeps my heart beating at its glacial pace—four beats per minute, sometimes three, synced not to the turbines but to something slower, something beneath the roar.

I am always hungry. The hunger is not a sensation so much as a constant companion, a second shadow trailing me through the gutters. I have learned to eat while moving, tearing lichen from pipe seams with my incisors—teeth that have grown continuously, like a rodent's, filed down by the constant gnawing on mineral-hard growths. They are iron-colored now. When I bare them, I am baring the Manufactory itself.

Sometimes I find a richer harvest. The micro-storms of scalding vapor that roll down the avenues leave behind deposits—a crystalline residue of the toxic drizzle, clinging to the edges of plates where the condensation runs. It is dangerous to approach these fresh deposits; the chemicals are still active, still burning. But if I wait—if I press myself into a gutter and count the turbine-thumps until I reach one hundred—the worst of the reactivity fades, and I can lick the residue from the steel. It tastes of copper and lightning. My body sings with it for hours afterward, my sluggish metabolism spiked into something almost like vitality.

---

I have seen the Stilt-Walkers.

Not with eyes—never with eyes—but with my snout pressed to the floor, feeling their approach through the plates. Their footsteps are distinct: a deliberate, glacial placement, each footfall a *statement* in the ferrocrete, a deep thrum that travels through the steel and resonates in my whiskers. They are immense. Even two meters above me, their weight presses down, and I feel it like a change in weather, a localized increase in the omnipresent pressure.

I am terrified of them. Not because they would harm me—I do not think they even know I exist—but because they are *so much* of this place. They have surrendered to it entirely. Their bodies are monuments to the decay, their metabolisms married to the Machine's exhaust, their consciousness—if they have one—tuned to the roar as to a lover's heartbeat. They are what happens when you stay too long. They are what I could become, if I am not careful.

When I feel a Stilt-Walker approaching, I freeze. I press into the gutter, I become the water, I am nothing. I feel their long legs step over my position—two seconds of intense pressure, the ferrocrete groaning beneath their weight—and then they are gone, their foot-thumps fading into the charcoal gray distance. I breathe again. My heart climbs back to four beats per minute.

---

I do not know what I am looking for.

There must be something. Some edge to the Manufactory, some boundary where the engineered terrain gives way to... something else. I have been moving in what I believe is a straight line for—how long? I have no calendar. I have only the turbine-thumps and the vent-blasts and the rare, sickly amber pulse of the warning beacons. I have traveled a million floor plates. I have worn my pads smooth on the corroded steel. I have lost the tip of my tail to a deposit of crystalline efflorescence that sliced through the keratin like glass.

The Manufactory does not change. The pumping stations repeat. The skeletal silos repeat. The hexagonal plates repeat. I navigate by the subtle variations in the vibration—this block's turbines are slightly slower, this section's vents release on a different cycle, this avenue's micro-storms come from the left instead of the right. I am mapping the Machine's decay, learning its rhythms, searching for a pattern that might reveal an exit, a boundary, a *limit*.

But I fear there is no limit. I fear the Manufactory is all there is—an infinite flatland of rust and roar and wet dark, extending in every direction without end. I fear I am walking in a circle, tracing a path I have already traced, feeling the same plates, the same gutters, the same mineral deposits. I fear the Stilt-Walkers I encounter are the same Stilt-Walkers, and that we are all caught in the Machine's final program together—walking, walking, walking, going nowhere.

---

But I continue.

I continue because I am small, and small things survive by moving. I continue because the lichen demands it—I must eat, and the lichen does not come to me. I continue because in the moment between one turbine-thump and the next, in that infinitesimal pause where the roar catches its breath, I feel something that might be *hope*—a faint, ridiculous impulse that insists the next plate might be different, the next gutter might lead somewhere, the next amber pulse might illuminate a door.

I am the gutter-ghost. The rust-eater. The deaf thing in the dark. I am not a monument. I am not an accident of the Machine's exhaust. I am an argument against the entropy—a small, stubborn refusal to become the iron world, living off its leavings while insisting, without language or witness or reason, that there must be something beyond the roar.

My whiskers touch the next plate.

It is warm.

I move.
