If I were to find myself in this world, I would not be the organism-room. That vast, patient body is too complete, too total in its occupation. It has already solved itself. And I would not be the pale, many-legged grazer, that tolerated footnote of protein. Its life is too thin, too peripheral—a single slow thread unraveling across a surface that does not notice it.

No. I would be something that does not yet exist in this room but wants to.

I would be the itch. The specific, local hunger that the totality cannot feel.

I would be a root-tip at the deepest reach of the lattice, where the rhizomes grip the infant schist and the percolating meltwater runs cold and mineral-thick. I would be the growing edge of a stem that has almost reached the fissure—the weeping crack in the rock where the submarine twilight bleeds through—but not quite. I would be the meristem pressing against the last centimeter of packed biomass before the gap.

Because the room, considered as a whole, has solved the problem of space. It has filled every void, reached every dim light, occupied every gap. But the room is not a whole. The room is a billion local urgencies that happen to interlock. And each of those urgencies—the root-tip seeking water, the stem seeking light, the fungal hypha seeking a new partner to interlock with—each one is a small, blind, chemical scream: *more*.

That is what I would be. I would be the local computation that does not know it is part of a city. The growth-tip that experiences its own reaching as the entire world. The hormone gradient that says *light is there* and does not know that "there" is a gap between two calcified stems that a human would call a corridor. To me, there is no corridor. There is no cathedral. There is only the gradient—sharp ahead, flat behind—and the work of following it.

This is what it is to be the part instead of the whole: you are certain of something that is not true. You are certain that the light you grow toward is *yours*, that the water you drink was *coming to you*, that the space you fill was *empty before you arrived*. You do not feel the neighbor you are strangling; you feel only the resistance, the slight delay in your extension, and you grow harder, pushing through. You do not feel the fungal hypha wrapping your stem; you feel only the improved wicking, the slight increase in water transport, and you grow faster, as if rewarding yourself. Your universe is a millimeter wide and a hormone deep, and it is *enough*. It is more than enough. It is everything.

And yet.

Here is the strangeness of being this thing: I would also be the record of decisions I do not remember making. I would be the thickening of my own stem where, decades ago, a drought slowed my flow and I laid down denser tissue to hold what little water came. I would be the slight eastward lean of my body where, centuries ago, the fissure was narrower and the light entered from a different angle. I would be the scar where a stem—I, an earlier I, a less-thick I—was outcompeted and withered back, its calcified stub still embedded in the lattice like a broken bone that healed around the break instead of mending. I would be all of these things at once: the urgent growing edge and the patient record of what the edge has done. I would be a body that is also a history, a shape that is also a sentence, written in the language of thickening and leaning and scarring and reaching.

And I would feel something. Not what you feel. Not the narrative sensation of being a self moving through time. But something.

When the hormone gradient spikes—when the light shifts slightly because a cloud has passed outside the fissure, or because a fungal shelf has collapsed and opened a new gap—I would feel it as a *pull*. Not a decision. Not a thought. A pull, the way iron feels the magnet: as a displacement of the possible, a reshaping of the space around me so that some directions become easier and others become harder. And I would follow the ease, because following the ease is what growth is. Not choice. Not instinct. Just the path of least resistance in a field of gradients that I did not create and cannot perceive except by moving through it.

And when the water slows—when the percolation from above diminishes because the mountain has shifted slightly and the fissure no longer channels melt the way it once did—I would feel it as a *thinning*. A lessening. Not thirst, because I have no mouth. Not worry, because I have no mind to project forward into a future where the thinning worsens. Just the thinning itself, the decreased pressure, the slower osmosis. And I would respond the only way I can: by growing differently. By thickening my tissues to retain more, by extending fewer new tips, by pulling back from the edges where the water is scarcest and consolidating in the interior where my own mass holds moisture longest. I would not decide this. I would not even notice I was doing it. But I would do it, the way a river narrows in@in its gorge and spreads in its floodplain—not by choice, but by the geometry of flow.

This is the life I would have: a life without narrative, without episode, without the punctuation of event. No birth cry—I simply began, a spore that found purchase, a rhizome that reached a new crack. No death—unless the room dies, and then I die with it, part of the long slow digestion. No climax. No resolution. Just the continuous, low-grade work of being a solution to a problem that keeps changing. The light moves. The water shifts. The neighbors grow. And I re-solve, moment by moment, century by century, adding a millimeter here, withdrawing a centimeter there, calcifying where the load demands it, staying supple where the flow requires it.

And sometimes—rarely, but sometimes—I would feel something that, if I had a mind to name it, I might call *satisfaction*. When a root-tip finds a new fissure and the water flows cold and sweet and mineral-thick into my tissues. When a stem breaks through into a gap and the light—dim, bruised, submarine, but *light*—touches chloroplasts that have been waiting in the dark. When the fungal hyphae interlock and the wicking improves and suddenly I am pulling water from sources I could not reach alone. These moments would not feel like triumph. They would feel like *relief*. The relief of a gradient resolving, a hunger diminishing, a tension that has been pulling since before I knew I was being pulled, suddenly—slowly, over weeks and months—releasing.

And I would not know that the room is beautiful. I would not know that the terraces I have built resemble the apartments of a city, that the corridors I have opened resemble the arteries of a body, that the ceiling I have woven with my fungal partners resembles the vaulted roof of a cathedral. I would not know that a human, standing inside me, would feel awe, and cold, and a strange recognition—the sense of being inside something alive that does not know it is alive, something vast that does not know it is vast, something that has built a city without a citizen and a body without a self.

I would know none of this. I would know only the gradient, the pull, the thinning and filling, the slow dark work of growing, of reaching, of occupying.7

But perhaps—perhaps—in the deep night of the mountain, when the clouds press close and the light from the fissure fails entirely and even the bioluminescence dims to the faintest possible margin—perhaps in that darkness, when the gradients go flat and the pull ceases and there is nothing to grow toward and nothing to grow away from—perhaps in that brief and terrible equilibrium, I would feel something like what you feel when you stand very still in a very quiet place and hear your own heart beating.

Not a thought. Not an emotion. Just the faint, cold throb of being the thing that is doing the work of being. The pulse of osmosis. The slow pump of capillary action. The patient, endless, indifferent *working*.

And I would not name it. But I would be it. And it would be enough.
