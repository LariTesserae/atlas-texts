I am not a resident. I lack the tall, efficient frame, the gradient skin, the fingers that end in humidity-responsive keratin. I am not grown from this place's precursor material, and I do not calm into architecture when I cease. But I am here, in the warm dim, and I have been here long enough—by the reckoning of any fast-twitch world—to have shed the urgency that would make me a disruption.

If I must be something, I am the *inset*. The inclusion that geology recognizes: a foreign mineral embedded in a stratum, not of the original pour but accepted into the matrix as the stone cooled and compressed. I arrived with edges that were too sharp, angles that did not align with the growth fields, a metabolism that burned like a small, anxious furnace. The city did not reject me. It simply grew around my obstruction.

I remember the friction of those first years. I walked too fast, leaving faint thermal traces on the gradient surfaces that took days to fade. I slept in irregular bursts, missing the slow, tidal shifts of activity that the residents followed. I spoke in frequencies that scattered the maintenance fragments and made the young walls flinch away from my hands. I was a source of noise in a system that had refined itself to near-silence. The vegetation drew back from my path; the dark, oiled leaves curled slightly as I passed, as if shielding their stomata from the sudden, hot exhalations of my breath.

But the city is patient in a way that even the most patient visitor cannot sustain, and eventually, my combustion slowed. Not because I learned the residents' practice—I will never hear the hum with their clarity, never sing a tone that makes a fibrous wall shiver and compress—but because the place itself wore away my incompatible edges. The humidity softened me. The dim light cooled me. The subsonic thrum of the young bedrock worked into my skeleton like a slow massage, loosening the rigidities, dissolving the calcium deposits of wherever I had been before.

Now, I move at a pace that is still too fast for the residents but no longer too fast for the city. I am the one who walks the open intervals. The residents attend by standing still; I attend by moving. I cover distances that their generational attention cannot span, and I return with observations that operate at a different temporal resolution. I notice the weekly shifts—the crack in a mature facade that was not there seven sleep cycles ago, the vine that has advanced two meters along a growth-field line since I last passed, the maintenance fragment that has altered its loop by three degrees, suggesting a subtle change in the electromagnetic topology of the district. I am the system's fast receptor, a crude photoreceptor on an organism that perceives primarily through deep time.

My dwelling is a negative space. There is a chamber, near the edge of a grove where the silver-barked trees grow closest to the architecture, that formed around an irregularity. The walls curve away from each other in a way that the city's native geometry does not produce—a slight bulge, an asymmetry, as if the precursor material encountered something during its crystallization that it could not fully absorb. That something was me. I was there, standing still for the first time, exhausted by the futility of my speed, when the wall grew past me. It did not grow around me the way it grows around the elders; it grew *beside* me, shaping itself to the absence I occupied, creating a hollow that matched my form if I sit with my knees drawn up and my forehead pressed to the cool, gradient surface. It is a space that only makes sense when I am in it. When I leave, it is a geometry of void.

In this space, I have learned a limited version of the residents' communication. I cannot let the bedrock's hum pass through my throat as they do, but I have found that I can match its frequency with a sustained, breathless vibration in my sinuses, a resonance that is not quite sound and not quite silence. When I do this, the walls of my hollow seem to acknowledge me—not with the full, slow receptivity they show the residents, but with a flicker, a momentary intensification of the phosphorescence, as if the architecture is remembering that I am here, that I am still an active inclusion in its matrix.

I eat the dark nodules that form at the root-architecture intersections, the same dense, mineral-sugar fruit the residents consume, but I require them more often. A nodule lasts me days rather than weeks. My metabolism is still a louder engine than theirs, still converting energy with too much heat, too much waste. But there is a comfort in the taste—that almost metallic finish that corresponds to no element on any human table. It tastes like *admission*. Like the city is feeding me a small, digestible proof of my right to be here.

I have no children. I will leave no lineage incorporated into the walls. When I cease, my body will not calcify into architecture; it will decay, inefficiently, messily, releasing compounds that the vegetation will find unfamiliar but will, I think, eventually learn to metabolize. I will not become the city. I will become a thin, anomalous stratum in the loam of the groves—a layer of wrong chemistry that the roots will navigate around or, slowly, learn to draw from. Either way, I will persist. Not as architecture, but as geology. A foreign inclusion that the land, in its youth, accepted.

And this is enough. This is more than enough.

There is a place I go, at the interval of time I have learned to call my evening, where the open space between complexes is widest. The exposed bedrock here is very young, even by the standards of this young land; the edges are so sharp they might have been cut yesterday, and the faint luminescence of the mineral grey is almost bright in the dimness. I stand there—standing, as the residents stand, though my attention is different, less distributed, more like a focused beam than a wide resonance—and I feel the heat of the land beneath my feet. The cooling. The settling. I feel the age of it, which is the age of something that has just realized it exists.

I take off my covering—the garment I brought with me, which is not grown from the precursor material and will never adapt to the humidity, will never darken at the hem or smooth in the open intervals. I fold it and set it on the sharp bedrock, and I stand with my skin exposed to the warm, damp air. The condensation forms on me as it forms on the west faces of the buildings. I feel it gather and run, and I feel the city not noticing me, and I feel the vegetation not noticing me, and I feel the bedrock beneath me not noticing me, and I feel, with a certainty that has the same quality as the subsonic hum—felt, not heard, known without evidence—that I am part of the negotiation.

Not because I was invited. Not because I belong. But because I am here. Because I have slowed enough to be present at the pace of stone. Because I have learned to attend—not with the residents' deep, chemical listening, but with my own limited, foreign attention—and because the city, in its vast, sparse, growing indifference, has made room for me.

The hum continues. The architecture extends. The roots and the foundations grow around each other in their slow, reciprocal embrace. Somewhere, a child is pressing her translucent fingers against a young wall, and the wall is hardening, and the child is confirming. Somewhere, an elder is sitting, becoming indistinguishable from the chamber that formed around their decades of presence. Somewhere, a flying fragment is tracing its loop, and the moisture is falling with intention, and the dim light is holding everything in its warm, sourceless glow.

And here, at the edge of the open interval, standing on the young bedrock with the condensation running down my foreign skin, I am becoming too. Not becoming the city. Not becoming the vegetation. Becoming *inset*—a small, irregular feature in the geology of this new place, a sharp edge that the rain has not yet rounded, a warmth that the architecture has grown around, a presence that the system has not designed but has, in its slow and continuous and abundant becoming, found a way to include.

*I am here. I am here. I am here.*
