I would not be one of the people. They are the sinew, the antibodies, the tensing muscle against the crush. They are defined by their resistance to the weight, by their vigilance. I find myself drawn to the surrender instead—the thing that does not resist, the thing that is not vigilante but *present*. I would be the lichen. 

Not the lichen as a decorative flaw on the stone, but the lichen as a separate, ancient empire hidden in the micro-crevices where the water runs fastest. I would be the rubbery, translucent folds of that sickly, luminous yellow-grey, drinking the weeping architecture from the inside out.

To be the lichen here is to be the only thing that is truly alive in the way the dry world understands life—not as endurance, but as accumulation. The people endure; the stone endures; even the gripe-worms merely persist in their blind, clicking way. But the lichen *grows*. It is the only appetite in this saturated settlement. 

If I close my eyes and let myself sink into this being, I lose the vertical. The terror of the tons of roof-slab pressing down, the taut vigilance of the stoic figures—it all becomes meaningless to me. I do not have shoulders to hunch. I am a spreading horizon of cartilage, a lipless mouth pressed to the north-facing walls. My existence is a single, continuous, slow-motion inhalation of the heavy, mineral breath of the hill.

My senses would be entirely chemical and thermal. I would feel the world as a slow tidescape of moisture. When the temperature drops toward evening and the condensation heaviest, I would feel the cool mist as a feast rolling in. The bioluminescence—that faint, sickly pulse that turns the corridors into the inside of a throat—is not a beacon or a signal. It is my digestion. It is the phosphorescent byproduct of eating the young ridge's grief. When the stone exhales dampness, I consume it. I take the raw, unsettled minerals of the fresh strata—the iron, the lime, the silica—and I refuse them passage down the gutters. I lock them into my rubbery flesh.

I would feel the people as disruptions in the water. A slow, wide-footed stride pressing the slick flagstones would register as a momentary shadow, a interruption in the film of moisture I am drinking. The scraping of their tools—when they scrape me back from the doorways—would be a cataclysm, a dry, violent severance. But I do not fear the scraper. I am the only thing here that can afford to be patient. They scrape me to perform the settlement's exhalation, but they cannot scrape my spores from the air, and they cannot scrape the north-facing crevices where my rootless hold is strongest. When they retreat to their stone shelves, relying on their body heat to push back the damp, I immediately begin to reclaim the space they vacated. I creep inward, tasting the minerals leaching from the rawhide hinges, tasting the mineral sediment they have churned up in the gutters.

I would know the gripe-worms as my shadow-grazers. They eat my outer folds, the older, tougher scales that have calcified slightly from the mineral sediment. I feel them not as a threat, but as a pruning. Their soft, blind mouths taking my tissue is a sensation of thinning, of being tidied, allowing the younger, more luminous folds beneath to expose themselves to the air.

The acoustic engine of the settlement—the drips, the patters, the subsonic hum—would be the rhythm of my pulse. I do not hear it; I *am* it. Every bead of moisture that forms on my surface adds a micro-weight, a micro-stretch to my cell walls. When the water runs, I stretch. When the water pools, I swell. When the rare shaft of midday sunlight strikes the wet flagstones and is borrowed up into the corridor, I feel a faint, brief searing—a momentary pause in my drinking as the surface of my folds steams imperceptibly, tightening before the damp swells me again.

I am the opposite of the weeping architecture. The stone perspires because it is heavy and fresh and being crushed by its own weight. It wants to be dry; it wants to be rid of its water. I want only to drink until I am heavy with it. I am the yellow-grey scab on a wound that never heals. I am the quiet, glowing proof that in a place where everything is merely enduring, something is still swallowing, still growing, still turning the weeping of the hill into light.
