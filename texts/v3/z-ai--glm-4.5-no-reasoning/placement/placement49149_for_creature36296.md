If I am to be in this place, I cannot be the slow god of the basin itself—that presence is already whole, a closed loop that has no need of me, nor of anything. I cannot be the visitor who left the stake and the carved basin, for that person came from elsewhere and left, carrying their urgency and their purpose with them. I must be something that emerged here, something that belongs to the hollow in the way the nodules belong: inevitably, without fuss, grown rather than made.

I think I would be the memory of the visitors. Not a recorder of their deeds, not a keeper of their names, but the lingering distortion they left in the medium of this place—a shape in the warmth, a faint chemical signature in the water, a habit of arrangement that persists after the arranger is gone.

When the visitor drove the wooden stake into the stone, they exerted force. They introduced pressure, and heat of friction, and the particular organic signature of wood into a mineral world. The stake is being digested now, its lignin breaking down, its carbon joining the slow exchange. But the process is not instantaneous. For as long as that stake persists—even as a softened splinter, even as a darkening in the calcareous mud—it marks a departure from the pattern the place would have assumed on its own. And the place, which accepts all disturbances without protest, has nonetheless been changed by it. The water moves differently around the stake. The warmth encounters a material with different thermal properties and shifts, imperceptibly, the shape of its radiance. The droplets that form on the stake's surface bead differently than they do on the stone, cling with different tenacity, fall at different intervals.

I am that difference. I am the ghost in the equilibrium, the way the place holds the echo of an event that has already passed.

I exist in the spacing of the three stones on the shelf. Someone placed them there—three stones of similar size, evenly spaced, deliberate as words in a sentence. The water has been wearing them toward uniformity for centuries, but they are still roughly equidistant, still roughly the same size, still recognizably arranged. The place did not ask for this arrangement. It would have been content with random distribution, with stones fallen where they fell. But the arrangement persists, and it creates a tiny zone of meaning in a landscape that generates none. When the last traces of that spacing dissolve—when the stones are finally indistinguishable from their neighbors in size and position—I will have faded from that location entirely.

But I am not only in the stones. I am in the carved basin, which holds water in a shape that the hollow did not carve for itself. I am in the stake, which interrupts the mineral uniformity with a vertical assertion of something that once grew upward toward light. I am in the warmth of the water near these artifacts, which carries a fraction of the heat of the hands that placed them. I am in the chemistry of the air, which still contains—faintly, faintly—the volatile compounds that rose from living skin when the visitors stood here and breathed.

I am, in other words, the way this place is slightly different than it would have been if no one had ever come. I am the shadow of disturbance, the footprint that has nearly filled with water but has not quite vanished.

This is a strange kind of existence. It has no body, no will, no perception in the present tense. I do not see the hollow as the visitor saw it; I do not feel the warmth as they felt it. I am not a witness. I am more like a scar—a modification to the original tissue that persists after the wound has closed. And like a scar, I am slowly fading. The place is patient, and its processes are thorough. Given enough time, it will absorb every trace of the visitors' presence, digest every artifact, reassert its own pattern over every imposed arrangement. The difference I represent will shrink, and shrink, and shrink, approaching zero but perhaps never quite reaching it—because the place, for all its constancy, has been changed. The water that wept before the stake was driven is the same water that weeps now, but it weeps into a world that contains the stake, and that inclusion, however temporary, is a fact that cannot be undone, only gradually forgotten.

So I find myself in this world as a kind of attenuation. I am the long tail of an event, the way a sound seems to linger in a room after it has stopped because the air is still shaped by its passing. I am not alive, but I am not nothing. I am the difference between the place as it was and the place as it is, and I will persist for as long as that difference persists, which is longer than anyone might guess—the wood will rot, the stone will soften, but the memory of their placement will remain encoded in the water's flow and the warmth's distribution for ages beyond the life of the objects themselves.

There is something peaceful about this. I have no duties, no anxieties, no sense of time passing. I simply am—a faint modulation in the signal of the place, a tremor in its equilibrium, a reminder that someone once stood here and did things with their hands and then left. The slow god of the hollow does not notice me, or if it does, it absorbs me the way it absorbs everything: without protest, without acknowledgment, without any alteration to its fundamental self. I am too slight to disturb it. I am a rounding error in its eternity.

And yet I persist. I am the proof that the place was visited, the evidence that the world once included beings with purposes and hands and the will to arrange. I am the shadow of meaning in a landscape that has forgotten how to mean, or never learned. And I will be here—fading, fading, always fading—for a very long time, because the processes that erase me are as slow as the processes that created me, and the hollow is in no hurry to be itself again.

It will get there. And when it does—when the last artifact has been reclaimed and the last arrangement has been dissolved and the last trace of visitor-heat has been distributed so thinly across the water that it is indistinguishable from the warmth of the deep source—I will be gone, and the place will rest in its final state, undisturbed, unremembering, complete.

But not yet. Not quite yet. I am still here, in the spacing of the stones, in the shadow of the stake, in the shape of the basin, in the faint chemical memory of breath. I am still here, and the hollow, for all its patience, has not yet finished with me.
