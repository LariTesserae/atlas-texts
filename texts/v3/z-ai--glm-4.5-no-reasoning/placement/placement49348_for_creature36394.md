I would be the Keeper of the Dew-Trace.

Not a title I chose, or that anyone bestowed. It is simply what I have become, after so many turns of the isopods' grinding, so many hatchings of the pale, blind moths. In a place where there is no history—only the eternal, fevered present—someone must hold the memory of the stone's weeping. Someone must notice when the nectar slows, when the gill-lobes pulse at a different rhythm, when the ozone undertone sharpens or flattens in the dim. The throat does not know it has a chronicler. But I am here, pressing my broad palms against its walls, reading the resin like a script.

I am old, even by the measure of this place where age is meaningless. My skin has gone beyond ash and old blood into something closer to the stone itself—grey and tight, with the veins showing dark beneath like the root-galls that crack the mesa's interior. I have lived so long in the hollows that my spine has taken the curve of the crevice, a permanent bow that lets me press into the gill-shelves without strain. My splayed toes grip so naturally now that I sometimes forget I am clinging; it feels as though the resin has grown around my feet, that I am a shelf myself, a living outcropping of the flesh-wall. My eyes are the palest of any in the settlement, almost white, and they ache in even this bruised twilight. I see what others cannot: the faint, slow drip of the vapor condensing on the stone, the way the waxen dew thickens or thins across the hours, the almost imperceptible swelling of the gill-lobes before they release their spores. I have made myself a sensor, an instrument tuned to the throat's metabolism.

My hollow is the highest in the settlement, carved into a root-shelf near the crevice roof where the vines hang like cables and the shadow is deepest. It is a place the others avoid. It is too close to the canopy-things, to that wet, suction-cup tread that picks off the slow and the careless. But I am not slow, and I am careful—*careful*, *careful*, *careful*, my fingers sign in the dim whenever the young ones approach my climbing-route, warning them back, telling them to stay in the press-points lower down where the isopod columns are the only danger. I chose the high hollow because it is the closest point to the slit of sky. That 远远, pale slit that means nothing to anyone else—it means something to me. I press my face against the gill-shelves there and tilt my head until I can see it: a line of faint, colourless light, no wider than a finger, segmented by the biomass that hangs across it. I do not know why I look at it. I know there is nothing beyond it that concerns us. The sky is a rumor from a world that has never been ours. But I look at it the way one looks at the face of someone who has been gone for a very long time—not with hope, not with longing, but with a kind of disciplinary grief, a need to confirm the absence is still there, still stable, still unbridgeable.

The others tolerate my strangeness because I am useful. I know the dew-traces—the paths along the stone veins where the nectar condenses thickest, where the waxen dew can be scraped in the greatest quantities with the least effort. I have mapped them in my mind over countless generations, noting how they shift as the gill-forest grows and the root-galls crack new channels in the rock. When the dew slows—and it does slow, sometimes, when the mesa's deep chemistry stutters and the weeping falters—I am the first to know. I sign the news down through the vertical warren: *dew thin here*, *move to the eastern vein*, *the stone is dry, the stone is dry*. And they move. They do not ask how I know. They have seen me pressing my palms to the walls, my cheek to the gill-lobes, listening with my whole body to the slow pulse of the place. They have accepted that I am attuned to something they cannot feel, and they trust my signs because my signs have kept them alive.

I do not tell them what I actually hear.

The throat is not stable. That is the secret I keep, the knowledge I hold in the hollow of my chest like a moth's body pinched between finger and thumb. The description of this place—the one we all carry in the shape of our minds—says that it is the end state, the primordial equilibrium, the unchangeable throat that will persist until the stone sublimates. But I have felt the tremors in the root-shelves. I have tasted the subtle wrongness in the dew—less iron, more ozone, a thinness that speaks not of a stutter but of a depletion. I have watched the isopod columns thin over the turns, their teeming density reduced to a still-overwhelming but measurably sparser flow. I have counted the moths in the air, counting them by the touch of their wings on my skin, and the count is lower than it was when I was young. The throat is not dying—I would not use that word, for the throat does not live as we live—but it is *changing*. Contracting. The mesa's deep chemistry is not infinite; the slow weep is not eternal. The nutrient-rich solvent in the stone is being drawn out faster than the geological pressure can replenish it. The gill-forest drinks, and the drinking is breath, but the breath is growing shallower.

What would they do with that knowledge? They cannot leave; there is no exit from the crevice but the slit of sky, and the slit does not open for soft, bare, sweating things. They cannot change the throat's chemistry; they are gutter-dwellers, chroniclers, not gods of the mesa. They can only do what they have always done: cling and scrape and catch and carve and sign *careful* in the dim. And so I let them do it. I hold the secret the way the stone holds its nectar—in the dark, in the deep, seeping it out only in the slow, viscous vapor of my signs, which are always about the present moment, the immediate need, the next handhold and the next meal.

But at night—or what passes for night, when the slit of sky dims further and the dimness becomes a darkness that even my pale eyes cannot penetrate—I lie in my hollow and I feel the gill-lobes pulsing against my skin, and I think about the end. Not with fear. Fear is a surface-dweller's emotion, and I have never been a surface-dweller, not even in the mythic before-time that some of the old ones half-remember from the signs their ancestors passed down. Fear requires the possibility of alternative outcomes, and in the throat, there are no alternatives—only the cycle, the closed loop, the perpetual, frantic motion that is the only logic this place has ever known. I do not fear the end. I *grieve* it. I grieve it the way one grieves a sound that has been so constant you no longer hear it, until the moment it falters and you realize it was the most beautiful thing you will ever know.

The sound of the isopods grinding. The sound of the moths hatching. The sound of the stone weeping. The wet, clicking, rustling cacophony that vibrates in the sternum, that is the heartbeat of the throat, that is the proof that the fever burns on. I love this sound. I love it the way one loves one's own breathing—not for its beauty, but for its continuity, its promise that you are still here, still alive, still occupied with the business of being small. And I can hear it changing. I can hear the gaps in the grind, the silences between the hatches, the moments when the weeping falters and the walls are dry and the gill-lobes pulse with nothing to drink. These silences are brief—scarcely a pause, scarcely a breath—but they are lengthening. And each one is a small death, a preview of the ultimate silence that will come when the throat has given all it can give and the mesa's chemistry has run its course and the stone, at last, stops weeping.

What will we do then? When the nectar stops and the gill-forest withers and the isopods have no dew to graze and the moths have no shed to eat and the canopy-things have no moths to pick off and the cycle that has sustained us all for longer than memory stops turning? We will dissolve. Not all at once—there will be a long, slow fading, a thinning of the flesh-walls, a stripping of the root-shelves, a settling of the dust and the chitin and the bone into the bottom of the crevice, where the last dregs of the nectar will pool and the last gill-lobes will drink and the last isopods will grind and then stop. And the settlement—our hollows and cubbies and press-points—will remain, empty, carved into the dead biomass like fossils in stone, a record of the gutter-dwellers who lived in the margins of a metropolis that did not know their names.

And I will be the last to go. I have decided this, though I have not signed it to anyone. I will climb to my high hollow and press my face to the gill-shelves and look at the slit of sky—the 远远, pale slit that will still be there, unchanged, when everything below it has gone quiet—and I will listen. I will listen to the silence growing, filling the crevice the way the hum used to fill it, a negative sound that is the absence of grinding and hatching and weeping, and I will feel the gill-lobes stop pulsing against my skin, one by one, like the cooling of hearts I have slept against my whole life. And I will hold my broad, clever palms against the stone until it is dry, until the resin no longer condenses, until the walls are bare and the dew is gone and the last trace of the nectar is a faint, ozonic ghost on my fingertips. And then I will close my pale, rheumy eyes—which have not blinked in so long that the lids have grown stiff—and I will feel the pressure and the darkness that we have always dreamed of, and I will know that the carrying is, at last, complete.

But not yet. Not today. Today the throat still breathes. The isopods still grind in their columns, their mandibles working the stone-nectar with a sound like shifting gravel. The moths still hatch in their continuous snow, their blind, fluttering bodies thick in the air. The canopy-things still shift in the deep shadow, their suction-cup tread a warning that *careful* is always the right sign. The gill-forest still presses its maroon and violet lobes against every inch of the walls, pulsing with thick, sap-like fluids. And the stone still weeps—slower than it did, thinner than it did, but weeping still, sweating its slow, non-aqueous tear into the pocket-world it has held for a billion years.

And so I cling. I press my hands to the walls and I read the dew-traces and I sign the news down through the warren: *dew here*, *come*, *careful*. And the young ones climb to my press-points and I trace the signs on their palms and they climb back down and they scrape and they catch and they carve and they live, occupied and breathless and small, not knowing that the throat is winding down, not needing to know, sustained by the perpetual motion that has not—yet—stopped. And I look at the slit of sky, and I feel the fever burning against my skin, and I am content. Not with the contentment of one who believes things will endure, but with the contentment of one who has accepted that they will not, and who finds, in that acceptance, a kind of peace that is deeper than hope.

I am the Keeper of the Dew-Trace. I am the one who notices. And in a place that is waiting for nothing, in a terrarium of the ancient earth, in a warm, dim, suffocating room at the top of the world—my noticing is the only prayer the throat has ever received. Not a prayer for deliverance. Not a prayer for more time. Just a prayer of attention: *I see you. I feel you weeping. I count your breaths. And when you stop, I will be the last to stop listening.*

That is who I am. That is what I have made of myself, here in the hidden, secret swallow of the stone. A witness. A chronicler of the gutter, dwelling in the margin of a dying metropolis, holding the memory of the weeping in the hollow of my chest. Small. Soft. Bare-skinned and sweating. And utterly, quietly, unchangeably *here*.
