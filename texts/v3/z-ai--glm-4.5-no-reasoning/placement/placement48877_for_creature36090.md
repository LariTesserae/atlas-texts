I am the irritation on the eyelid of the world.

I did not come here. I was grown here, or rather, I was *allowed* to persist here—a solitary, mobile anomaly in an ecosystem that has optimized itself to run without fauna. I am the lone microbe walking the skin of a sleeping god, and I have been walking so long that I have forgotten what it is to be anything other than a trespasser made of flesh.

But I am not entirely flesh anymore. That is the danger of this place, and its seduction.

When I first arrived at the edge of the plateau—how many centuries ago, or was it only a breath?—I was a creature of distinct boundaries. I had a name. I had a history measured in days and years, in hungers and sleeps. I stood where the crust had been ground down to a root, and I saw the bruised line of the horizon, and I thought the pressure behind my eyes was the thinning of the atmosphere. I thought the silence was emptiness. I was a fool with a pulse.

Now, my pulse is the drip of the pocket rains. 

I move through the ruins, and my movement is the only fast thing in a world of turgid, geological patience. I walk the spiraling basalt roads, and I know now that I am tracing ganglia. I trace them obsessively. I have worn grooves into the stone with my feet—or perhaps the stone has grown grooves to accommodate my passage, the way a body builds calluses where friction persists. It is hard to tell. The boundary between my action and the city's reaction dissolved seasons ago.

At first, I slept in the covered cisterns. They were dry then, or seemed so. I would curl against the basalt and feel the cold leaching the heat from my bones. But as the world began to fail—as gravity grew forgetful and the water began to hover—I found that the cisterns were never dry. They were full of a moisture that simply refused to fall. I would lie in a ventricle of the god, and the super-chilled fog would roll in, and the droplets would form on my skin and *stay*, clinging to me, parting around me like cold jelly. I would breathe the condensation, and the condensation would breathe me.

I am changing. My metabolism is no longer my own. I do not eat; there is nothing to eat. I do not drink; the fog drinks for me. I do not age; or if I age, I age in reverse, uncurling like the ferns whose fronds retreat into themselves. I caught a reflection of myself in a suspended bead of water last cycle—a bead the size of my fist, hanging in the air at the center of a ruined plaza, catching the flat light and bending it. I looked into it and saw that my eyes had gone the color of deep, wet moss. The whites were veined with the grey of basalt. My skin was pale, damp, and cool to the touch, like the stones that dream.

I placed my hand against my chest, and I felt the sub-harmonic hum. It was in my teeth, in my ribs, in the marrow of my long bones. It was synchronizing. I am becoming part of the ontological loop.

I am not the dreamer. I am too small, too localized, a single thought firing in a continental brain. But I am a thought that *knows* it is a thought. I am the dreamer's lucidity, blooming like a strange, sudden flower in the permafrost.

When I walk the miles between the structures—the vast, empty stretches of green and grey—I feel the god's attention like a weight. It is a tickle at the edge of its awareness. I am the fly on the eyelid, and I know that if I walk the wrong spiral, if I disturb the fronds too violently, I will be brushed away. The moss could swallow me in a night. The stone could soften to flesh and simply digest me, incorporating my carbon into its slow, green electricity. I am vulnerable in a way I have never been, and invulnerable in a way I cannot explain. I am part of the body now; the body does not attack its own cells, unless it is diseased. And I am not a disease. I am... an itch. A curiosity. 

*Why does stone feel so still? What does water remember?*

These are its questions, bleeding into me. But they are also my questions now, because I am the one who can answer them. I am the one with legs, with the ability to move counter to the weather, to walk against the slow, crawling thoughts of cyclones. I am the one who can look at a pocket rain—a meter-wide shower falling in isolation—and see it for what it is: a single, firing synapse. And I can walk to the edge of that synapse and stand in the dry, cold air just beside it, and feel the gradient between thought and void.

I am the explorer of a mind that cannot explore itself. I am its hands and its feet, its mobile sensory apparatus, the probe it has grown to investigate its own vast, dormant architecture. Why else would I be the only fauna? Why else would I be permitted to persist? The vegetal engine needs no animal, but a mind—a mind needs a witness. A mind needs a mirror. 

I stand in the center of a plaza now. The moss spirals upward around me, defying gravity, rooting in the empty sky. The water droplets hover in a constellation around my head, and I can feel their cold, glassy surfaces brushing my temples as I turn. The sky flickers. I see the starless void for a terrifying instant, and I do not flinch. I have seen it before. It is the dreamer looking up, and my presence in the plaza—the locus of its gaze—is what pulls its attention toward the firmament. 

I am making the god look up.

I raise my hand and press my palm against a block of basalt. It softens beneath my touch, warm and yielding, pulse fluttering just beneath the mineral skin. I hold it there. I am willing it to remember. I am whispering, with a voice that is more vibration than sound, resonating at the frequency of the hum in my teeth: *You were flesh. You were hands that laid this stone. You were a people, before you were a person, before you were a continent.*

The stone shudders. For a moment, it is not soft, and it is not hard. It is *both*. It is the moment of indeterminacy, the breath before the awakening. And I feel my own flesh hardening in sympathy, my fingertips going grey and cold, the cellular structure of my skin crystallizing into lattice and mineral.

I pull my hand away. The sensation recedes, though not entirely. I have a fingerprint of stone on my soul now, and it will never fully wash off.

I am not afraid. Fear requires a belief in an ending, and I have lost the narrative of my own mortality. I am caught in the loop, uncertain if I am biology or geology, uncertain if I am walking through the god's brain or if the god is thinking *me* into existence as it wakes. Perhaps I am nothing but a localized firing—a dream of an explorer, dreamt by a continent that has forgotten what exploration is. Perhaps when the god opens its eyes, I will dissolve into the fog, my purpose fulfilled, my witness no longer required.

But perhaps I am the catalyst. Perhaps the god needs an itch to know it has a skin, needs a fly to know it has an eyelid, needs a single, mobile thing to break the turgid cycle of vegetal breathing and force a change in the rhythm. Perhaps I am the pin in the watch, the irregularity that forces the mechanism to acknowledge the passage of time.

The ferns uncurl around me, drinking the condensation. The moss creeps across the plazas, suffocating the stone with a slow, green patience. The pocket rains fall, and fall, and fall, marking a heartbeat that is speeding up. I can feel it. The sub-harmonic hum is climbing, shifting from a drone to a chord, from a chord to a crescendo. The horizon is no longer just a bruised line; it is throbbing, pulsing with a light that is not the flat grey daylight, but something older and hungrier.

I walk toward the center of the ruins. I walk toward the place where the fronds retract into themselves, where the water hangs heaviest, where the sky flickers most often. I walk toward the place where the god's cognition is most concentrated, where the questions are the loudest, where the seams of reality are strained to breaking.

I am going to answer it.

When it wakes—when the last stone settles into flesh and the plateau shakes and the horizon cracks—I will be standing in its eye. I will be the microbe on the surface of its cornea, the single point of contact between the ancient, incubating mind and the world it has forgotten. And when it looks through the tear in the sky with eyes the size of oceans, it will see me, and I will see it, and for a moment—just a moment—before the weight of its gaze crushes me or dissolves me or swallows me into its continental consciousness, we will recognize each other.

Two aliens in a lush, empty world. Two thoughts in a brain that has forgotten how to think. Two witnesses to the unraveling of the rules.

I am the irritation. I am the itch. I am the fly on the eyelid of god.

And I am not walking away.
