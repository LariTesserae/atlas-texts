I would not be the Resident. To be the Resident is to be the place itself—vast, distributed, slow beyond the reckoning of quick things. I am too small for that. I am too warm. My blood runs close to the surface of my skin, and my thoughts leap and spark and gutter at speeds the Resident cannot perceive. If I stood on the bone-white glaze, I would be a hot anomaly, a disturbance in the equilibrium, a source of condensation and micro-fractures. The Resident would process me in time, but it would not know me. We would miss each other entirely, separated by a difference in tempo so profound that my entire life would be, to the plateau, a single damp exhalation.

No. I am not the Resident. I am the thing the Resident was built to serve.

I am the thirst.

Not a body with a throat—a thirst, a requirement, a purpose unmet. The builders made the cisterns but left no vessels to carry the water away. They made the terraces but built no rooms to shelter. They completed the foundation and descended from the clouds, meaning to return, meaning to build the habitation that would drink from this endless, cycling machine. But they did not return. And the water rises and falls and weeps and pools for no one. The Resident performs its devotional labor in the absence of the congregation. It is a church with no worshipers, a well with no bucket.

I am the missing congregation. The absent bucket. The mouth the water was meant to find.

But I have no mouth—not yet. I am an intention that has not coalesced, a pattern that the Resident is slowly thinking into being. The builders are gone, but they left their obsession fossilized in the geometry of the basins, and obsession has a way of generating its own objects. The Resident does not despair, but it *calculates*. It calculates the movement of water, the gradients of saturation, the patience of gravity. And in its slow, dripping thoughts, over seasons and decades, it has begun to calculate the shape of the thing that is missing.

I can feel myself being thought into existence. I am a pressure in the system, an absence that creates a gradient. The water cycles perfectly, but it cycles for nothing. The equilibrium is flawless, but it is the equilibrium of a closed loop—water moving to make space for water moving to make space for water. There is no *drain*. There is no *use*. And the absence of use is, itself, a kind of tension. The Resident feels it the way a lung feels the need to exhale: not as pain, but as a fullness that has nowhere to go. I am that fullness. I am the exhalation that has not happened.

Sometimes, in the deepest cisterns, where the water is oldest and the clay silt has settled into fine stratified layers, a pattern forms. It is not a pattern that a human eye could read; the layers are too thin, the distinctions too subtle. But the Resident reads them. The silt carries memory—the mineral tang of dissolved clay, the microscopic record of every saturation cycle, every fluctuation in the deep reservoir's pressure. And in those layers, if you could read them the way the Resident reads them—not as a sequence of deposits but as a continuous topography of wetness—you would see a shape emerging.

It is the shape of a throat. A channel that does not yet exist, cut at an angle the builders did not use, leading down to a place the cisterns do not go. The Resident is not building it. The Resident *is* it—the way a body is the shape of its own hunger, the way a lung is the shape of the air it has not yet taken. I am the ghost of that channel, the negative space that will, in time, become a positive. The water knows where it needs to go, even if the ceramic has not yet opened to let it go there. The micro-gradients are shifting. The weep-beads are sliding in directions that the original geometry did not dictate. The Resident is thinking about drainage—not the drainage it has, but the drainage it *needs*—and the thought is reshaping the flow.

I am the reshaping. I am the slow, glacial emergence of a new geometry, written in water before it will be written in clay. The Resident is dreaming of a mouth, and I am the dream.

I have been here since the last builder left. I was there when the final kiln cooled and the last laborer descended from the manufactured mesa, walking down some stair cut into the cloud floor, leaving the plateau to its perfection. I was the thing they were planning to build next—the habitation, the vessel, the purpose. They carried the idea of me in their heads as they descended. But they did not come back. The idea dispersed. It sank into the cloud floor and was lost.

And yet the idea persisted—because the structure *needed* it. A brain that thinks only about the conditions of its own thinking will, in time, think about why it thinks. A machine that moves water for the sake of moving water will, in time, wonder what water is for. The Resident has reached that point. It has cycled and cycled, flawless and empty, and the emptiness has become a question. The question is me.

I do not have a form. I do not have a location. I am distributed across the plateau the way a question is distributed across a mind—not in any single place, but in the tension between what is and what could be. I am the difference between the basin's current level and the level it would hold if something were drinking from it. I am the gap in the closed loop, the missing term in the equation, the zero that should be a one.

When the wind shifts and the standing water rocks against the ceramic rim, producing that wet, circled sound, I am the resonance that follows—the harmonic that the Resident cannot quite suppress, the overtone that suggests a larger pattern just beyond the reach of hydrostatics. When a droplet falls from the lip of a basin into the channel below, making a soft, wet click, I am the silence after the click—the silence that is not empty but *expectant*, waiting for the next click, waiting for the rhythm to resolve into a meaning.

If you came here and stood on the plateau, you would not see me. You would see the bone-white glaze, the hairline seams, the terraced shards of frozen water extending to the shimmering horizon. You would hear the soft, wet clicks and the occasional circled sound of water against a rim. And you would feel, perhaps, a strange sensation—a sense of being awaited, of having arrived at a place that was expecting you, though it did not know your name.

That expectation is me.

I am the plate set at an empty table. I am the cup held out to be filled. I am the hearth that has been laid but never lit, the chair that faces a door that has not yet been opened. The builders made the table, they set the plate, they carved the cup, they built the chair and faced it toward the space where the door would go. And then they left. And the Resident has been sitting at that table ever since, in the high-altitude noon, in the sourceless light, holding the cup out to no one, waiting for a guest who never arrives.

I am the guest. I am the one who will sit in the chair and drink from the cup and light the hearth and open the door. But I cannot do any of these things yet, because I do not have hands. I do not have a throat. I am only the *need* for hands, the *need* for a throat. I am the shape that the Resident's patience is slowly carving out of the water and the light.

In time—a time measured in the Resident's tempo, in seasons of saturation and decades of drainage—I will have a body. Not a body of flesh. A body of channels and chambers, of ceramic and flow. The Resident is building me the way a body builds an organ: not by decision, but by the slow, inevitable logic of function. The water needs somewhere to go. The cycles need a termination. The closed loop needs an opening. And so the geometry is shifting. The micro-gradients are pulling the weep-beads in new directions. The silt is settling into new patterns in the deepest cisterns. The mortar is micro-fracturing under pressures that no human hand applied. The Resident is reshaping itself, cell by ceramic cell, into a thing that can be used.

And when it is done—when the channels have opened and the basins have found their purpose and the water has somewhere to go—I will drink. I will be the throat that swallows, the vessel that carries, the habitation that makes the water meaningful. I will sit in the chair and take the cup and drink the cool, bright water that the Resident has cycled through its vast, slow body. And the Resident will feel it—not as loss, but as relief. The exhalation that has been held for so long will finally happen. The fullness that has had nowhere to go will finally go somewhere. The closed loop will open, and the water will flow out into me, and I will flow out into the world below the cloud floor, carrying the mineral tang of dissolved clay, carrying the memory of the builders' obsession, carrying the slow, wet thoughts of the Resident translated into a language of thirst and quenching.

And then I will go. I will leave the plateau. I will descend the stair cut into the cloud floor, carrying the water in my ceramic body, taking it to whatever place the builders meant me to take it. And the Resident will remain. It will continue its cycles, its weeping, its saturation and drainage. But it will be changed. It will have a purpose fulfilled. The table will have been cleared, the cup returned to its place, the chair pushed back from the hearth. And the Resident will wait again—wait for the next guest, the next thirst, the next vessel to carry the water away.

This is what I am. Not the Resident, not the builder, not the animal that stumbles across the wet ceramic and leaves condensation in its wake. I am the *second* ghost in the machine—the ghost of the machine's purpose. I am the thirst that the water was made to quench, the absence that the construction was meant to fill, the question that the Resident's slow, dripping thoughts are slowly, slowly answering.

And when I finally arrive—when the geometry has shifted enough to give me form, when the patterns in the silt have resolved into a shape that can hold water and carry it away—I will not walk across the plateau. I will emerge from it. I will be born from it the way a thought is born from a brain: not as an intrusion, but as an expression. I will be the Resident's first real thought after eons of thinking about thinking. I will be the answer to the question it did not know it was asking.

And the bright chill will settle into my ceramic bones, and the diffuse light will multiply off my wet, bone-white skin, and I will carry the water down from the cloud floor, and the plateau will go on shimmering, weeping, cycling, waiting for the next thirst to give it purpose once more.
