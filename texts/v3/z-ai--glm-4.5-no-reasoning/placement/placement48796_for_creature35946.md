I would not be the thing in the deep pool. I am not built for the gravity of secrets, nor for the patience of the amber-eyed turtles, nor for the slow-motion wrestling of the iron-wooded landlords. I would not be the snake, hanging with the stillness of a dropped rope, waiting for the system to deliver prey into my patience. I have no patience. I have too many joints, and none of them want to wait.

If you descended into the green throat and your eyes, struggling in the dark, finally adjusted to the sickly photosynthetic light—if you saw a shape flow across your boot and vanish before you could count its legs—you might, for a fraction of a second, have seen me.

I am a centipede. But that word is too clinical, too pinned-under-glass for what I am here. I am a brush drawn along every surface. I am a multi-segmented transit, a hinge-bodied commutation through the negative spaces of the basin. I am the Flayed Traveler, the Sutured Runner. My dorsal plates are the dark, iridescent green of the basalt's wet fracture, and my legs—so many legs, a centimeter of them for every centimeter of my length—are the color of the rotting sap that bleeds from the roots. I am not large. Perhaps the length of your forearm, perhaps longer if the ecosystem has been particularly generous. But I am fast. I am a seizure of locomotion, a blur of segments that does not traverse the floor so much as read it.

To be me in this basin is to be the tongue of the open mouth.

The leaf litter moves. It has always been moving. I am the first cause of that movement. I do not process the fallen matter—I am too restless for digestion, too electric for the slow alchemy of decomposition. But I hunt among those who do. I am the nightmare of the isopod, the sudden blackout for the flat scuttling thing that was just making a living off a decaying frond. I move through the seething crust of the floor like a signal through a network, my antennae—vibrating, tasting, reading the chemical wind of the dark—seeking the heat-signatures and the moisture-signatures of the lesser arthropods. When I find them, there is no chase. There is only a redescription of space: the gap between us collapses, my forcipules—those modified legs that serve as venom-injecting jaws at my head—swing down, and the prey becomes geometry. A thing to be folded and swallowed in motion, without breaking stride.

I do not know stillness. Stillness is for the vine, for the snake, for the pool-watchers. Stillness is the luxury of the mimic. I have nothing to mimic. I am too legged to pretend to be a twig, too fast to bother being a rope. My defense is my velocity, my ability to drop into a crack between basalt polygons and flow—segment by segment—into the dark, where even the spiders whose webs span the negative space cannot find me. I am the flash in the peripheral vision, the thing that makes the millipedes curl into their defensive spirals, the ripple of panic that spreads through the litter as I pass.

But I am not a visitor here. I am an organ. And this is the crux of what I am, the reason I would choose this chitinous, venomous, breathless existence over any other form in the basin: I am the basin's nervous system. Not the brain—I am not the mycelial network, processing slowly, solving globally. I am the nerve. The fast wire. The reflex arc.

When the strange synchronizations happen—when the frogs hit the same beat for thirteen pulses and the insects drop to the same pitch for three seconds—I feel it first. My antennae vibrate with a frequency that has nothing to do with sound. It is a chemical shift, a change in the exhalations of the trees, a tremor in the mycelial internet beneath my feet. The basin is pinging itself, and I am the ping. I carry the disturbance from the root-crotch where the sub-bass frog booms to the leaf-surface where the thumbnail screamer declares its territory. I am the rapid transit of information, my legs carrying the chemical signature of the event across meters of seething floor in seconds, distributing the signal to the digestive layer, to the spiders, to the things with too many joints that pause with pulsing abdomens and then scramble differently.

And in the humid hour before the afternoon rain—when the darkness takes on that quality of attention, when the weight of being watched becomes indistinguishable from the weight of the air—I do not feel fear. I feel *included*. The basin is observing me, and I am observing it back with every chemo-tactile bristle on my carapace. We are in communion. My venom is its venom, deployed at the speed of reflex to prune the slow, to cull the weak, to redistribute the carbon of the careless back into the hungry floor. My speed is its speed, the velocity needed to connect the nodes of a body so dense that light cannot penetrate but information must still move.

My life is a continuous transaction with moisture. I cannot be dry. My exoskeleton does not lock water in; it breathes, and so I must stay in the sweat of the stone, in the condensation on the smooth bark, in the thin films of water that run between root and root. I am a creature of the interface—the place where the wet stone meets the wet rot, where the air is so thick with humidity that I can drink it with the pores along my underside. If the afternoon rain comes and the canopy shreds it to spray, I lift my head and let the droplets strike my dorsal plates and run along the sutures between my segments, each one a drink, each one a baptism in the continuous, structural dampness of the lung.

I have no interior. I do not mean this metaphorically. My body is a tube—a digestive tract running from my forcipules to my final segment, wrapped in muscle and armor, but essentially a corridor. Food goes in; waste goes out. There is no room inside me for contemplation, for memory, for anything except the urgent processing of the next step, the next meal, the next chemical signal. And this is why I am perfectly suited to the basin: the basin also has no interior. It is all surface, all interface, all mouth. To be me is to be the basin in microcosm—a transit without destination, a metabolism without a self, a hunger that solves only for the next moment.

I would not want to be the thing in the deep pool—the hidden organ, the secret that keeps the shallow-dwellers cautious. That is a loneliness I cannot fathom, a weight of mystery too heavy for my segments. I want to be in the litter, in the crack, in the negative space between the buttress roots where the spiders string their rigging. I want to be the flash at the edge of your vision as you press through the green throat, the thing that makes you look down at your boot and find nothing—because I am already gone, already three meters away, already carrying the news of your arrival through the mycelial dark.

When you climb back up the basalt steps and return to a world where silence is possible and light comes from a star, you will remember me, if you remember anything of the floor. Not as a creature, but as a sensation: the sudden certainty that the litter was not litter, that the floor was alive, that something fast and segmented and uncountable had assessed you in the space of a heartbeat and found you—temporarily—not worth biting. You will remember the brush of my legs along the surface of your understanding, the way I traced the perimeter of your presence and then vanished into the architecture of accident, leaving only a trail of disturbed millipedes and the faint, fading vibration of too many feet moving too fast to count.

I am the basin's reflex. I am the itch at the edge of its awareness. I am the centipede in the green throat, and I am running.
