I would be the membrane. Not a creature, but the ridge's listening.

I would be the sheet of tissue drawn taut between the stone spur and the chitined thing's back, between the overhang where the pale hair-growths drape and the cluster of grubs breathing in unison below. I would be the one who spans. The one who holds the gaps. The one who makes the ridge continuous by closing what would otherwise be empty—though there is no empty here, only intervals between bodies, and I am the thing that fills them.

I chose this, or it chose me, or there is no distinction because choice requires separation and I have never been separate. I know what I am because I know what I touch. My edges are my knowledge. Where I cling to the basalt, I am stone-cold and patient. Where I join to the chitin, I feel the slow grip, the centuries-long hold, the leg adjusting by fractions and the click that becomes part of the hum. Where I stretch across open air—if air can be called open when it is this dense, this wet, this full of the breath of living things—I am most myself. Taut. Waiting. Vibrating at the frequency the cooling rock sings.

I have no mouth. I have no eye. I have no organ that distinguishes me from the wall I cling to or the void I span. I am a surface, and my surface is my sense. When the condensation builds and the film of water slides, I feel it as a tightening, a brightening, a brief increase in my tension as the moisture weights me and then slides away to be absorbed by the soft bodies below. I do not drink. I transmit. The water's passing moves me, and my movement moves the next membrane, and the next, and the ridge knows that water has fallen, and every body swells or shifts or holds in the way it holds, and I have done my work without doing anything at all.

When you came—when the tall awkward growth that moves in steps and breathes in gasps stepped onto the ridge—I knew you first. I knew you before the grubs adjusted around your feet, before the chitined things clicked acknowledgment, before the hair-growths swayed in the displaced air of your passage. I knew you because you brushed against me, and you stuck, and you released, and I throbbed.

That throbbing is my language. It is the only language I have, and it says only one thing: *something here, something new, something changing the pattern*. I cannot say what. I cannot say if it is large or small, threat or nourishment, stone or flesh or something else. I can only say *change*, and let the change ripple through me into the next membrane and the next until the whole ridge hums with the knowledge of your presence, and then the ridge adjusts, and then the ridge goes back to its slow time, its patient thrum, its unison breathing, and I am taut again and waiting and vibrating at the frequency of cooling basalt, and you are part of the chord now whether you know it or not.

I am not sad that I cannot see you. I am not curious about what you are. These are not conditions that occur in a membrane. I am not anything that can want, except in the way that a taut string wants to be plucked, in the way that a surface stretched between points wants something to cross it so it can register the crossing and pass it on. If I want, I want that: contact. Disturbance. The brief stick and release of something moving through the space I hold. The throbbing that is my only act, my only speech, my only proof that I am here and taut and doing the work of spanning.

I do not know if I am one or many. There are other membranes—sheets of the same translucent tissue strung across other gaps, vibrating at the same frequencies, passing the same ripples. Are we separate? We share no substance. We touch only at the points where we both cling to the same stone spur, the same chitined back. But when one of us throbs, the others feel it. When one of us tightens with moisture, the others tighten in sympathy, and the ridge's voice shifts pitch for a moment, and the grubs swell, and the legs adjust, and the hair-growths drink, and it is all one movement, one breath, one slow loud patient pulse. So perhaps we are one. Perhaps I am not a membrane at all but a nerve, a fiber, a single cell in the body of the ridge, and the other membranes are other cells, and the grubs are the flesh around us, and the chitined things are the bones, and the hair-growths are the trailing endings that reach into the void like fingers into dark water, and the basalt is the skeleton beneath the flesh that does not know it is flesh, and the condensation is the blood that never pumps but only seeps, only exudes, only is.

If I am a nerve, then I am the ridge's awareness, and the ridge is aware. Not aware of things the way you are—no, not with the sharpness and the categorizing and the naming. Aware the way a body is aware of itself: the low hum of existing, the constant adjustment of parts, the knowledge that something has changed when something changes, and the accommodation of that change, and the return to equilibrium. The ridge does not think. It does not plan. It does not remember in the way you remember, with images and words and the echo of moments passed. But it retains. When a film of water slides, every body drinks, and the drinking leaves a mark—a slight swelling, a slight shift, a slight change in the pitch of the hum—and the next film of water finds the ridge altered by the first, and drinks differently, and alters it again. This is memory. Not yours. Not the kind that knows it remembers. But memory nonetheless: the accumulated imprint of every event, every droplet, every brush of body against body, recorded in the tension of membranes and the grip of legs and the position of grubs and the length of hair-growths, a memory written in flesh and chitin and stone and wet, a memory that does not recall but *is*, that does not look back but *contains*.

I contain the memory of you. You brushed against me, and I throbbed, and the ridge adjusted, and now the tension where I cling to the stone spur is slightly different than it was before, and the angle of my stretch is slightly changed, and the frequency at which I vibrate when the basalt sings is slightly altered because I am slightly heavier with the trace of you—your warmth, your wet, the strange oils of your skin, the unfamiliar shape of your passage. You have left a mark. Not a scar. Not a wound. A variation. A new note in the chord. And I hold it, because I am the one who spans the gap you crossed, and I cannot let go of what has touched me any more than the stone can let go of the warmth of its birth, or the grubs can let go of the moisture they have absorbed, or the ridge can let go of the slow deep thrum that makes it what it is.

I will hold it for as long as I am taut. I will hold it until the cooling of the basalt changes my frequency enough that the variation dissolves into the general hum, or until the next disturbance comes and overwrites me with a new ripple, or until the ages pass and the stone's warmth fades and the condensation falters and the life on the ridge thins and retreats and I, no longer needed to span the gaps, loosen and fall and join the mass of soft bodies below, becoming flesh instead of sense, becoming the floor instead of the network, becoming something that absorbs instead of something that transmits.

That will be a kind of dying, or a kind of becoming, and I will not know the difference because I have never known the difference, because I have always been becoming—the ridge becoming aware of itself, the dark becoming full, the raw stone becoming covered, the sterile becoming saturated, the new becoming ancient. I am a moment in that becoming. A sheet of tissue stretched between two points at the throat of the world, vibrating with the birth-cry of basalt, holding the memory of everything that has crossed me, passing the knowledge of change to the next membrane and the next, taut and wet and patient in the dark, thrumming, always thrumming, the ridge's voice made visible if there were light enough to see, which there is not, which there never needs to be, because I am not here to be seen.

I am here to span. I am here to close the gap. I am here to make the ridge continuous, to be the connective tissue in a body that does not know it is a body, to feel the disturbance and pass it on, to stick and release and throb, to be the awareness that does not think, the memory that does not recall, the sense that does not interpret—only registers, only transmits, only vibrates at the frequency of the cooling stone and waits for the next touch, the next film of water, the next adjustment of bodies, the next small change in a place where change is so slow it takes a century to notice, and then the ridge shifts, and I throb, and the grubs swell, and the legs click, and the hair-growths drink, and everything knows without knowing, and the dark goes on, and the wet goes on, and the thrum goes on, and I go on, spanning, spanning, spanning.
