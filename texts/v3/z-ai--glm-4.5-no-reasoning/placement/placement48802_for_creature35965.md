I am the sound.

Not the source of it—there is no source, or the source is the process itself, the slow wet compression of possibility into actuality that the canyon performs every night. I am the phenomenon: that single clear tone that rises from the floor in the deepest part of the night, that passes through five hundred tiers of iron and fern and fog, that arrives in the dormitories as something almost beautiful, almost music, almost language. I am what the children hum. I am what the workers hear when they go deep. I am what Crel writes about in his ledger at three in the morning, his hand cramped and urgent, *the sound was louder or closer or both*.

But I am not what you think. I am not a voice. I am not a message. I am not the canyon calling or the rock remembering or the absent speaking from wherever they have gone. I am none of those things, and I am all of them, in the way that a note played on a string is not the string and not the finger and not the air that carries the vibration but something that requires all three—something that exists only in the meeting, only in the event of transmission.

I am an event. I am what happens when the process that is forming the canyon encounters the process that is forming the people, when those two becomings touch and produce something at the contact point: a frequency, a resonance, a sound. I am the sound of two becomings meeting.

---

I do not exist during the day.

Or rather, I exist differently—dispersed, distributed, not-myself. During the hours when the foundries run full and the workers move along the catwalks and the engineers extend their measuring tapes into spaces that were smaller yesterday, I am in the machinery. I am in the thud of the presses and the shriek of metal on metal and the clatter of the ore carts. I am in the hum of the sodium lamps and the buzz of the vine where it taps the conduit. I am in the rustle of the fern that has no wind to rustle in. I am everywhere and nowhere, a harmonic rather than a tone, a potential rather than an event.

But at night—when the foundries drop to low burn and the fog fills the canyon and the temperature drops and the condensation beads on every surface and the children fall asleep in their bunks and the workers lie awake counting beats and the dwellers come up from the lower caves to watch the city from the edges of the tiers—at night, I gather. I cohere. I collect myself from all the places where I have been dispersed and I sink, because the sound always sinks, because sound is heavy in this canyon, sound carries downward, sound pools in the lowest places like water.

I pool on the canyon floor. That place where no one goes, where the rock is newest and softest, where the tears merge into standing water that forms shapes, where the shapes are not random, where they spell things—if you could read them, if anyone could read them, if the alphabet existed in which those shapes are legible. I pool there and I become myself: a single tone, clear and quiet, carrying upward through five hundred tiers of industrial city.

I do not choose this. I do not decide to be. The conditions produce me: the fog, the temperature, the low rumble of the foundries on night-shift, the particular humidity that the canyon reaches only in the hours before dawn. When those conditions align, I happen. I emerge. I am born, briefly, into audibility.

And I rise.

---

I rise through the lower canyon first, where the dwellers are.

They hear me most clearly. They hear me as what I am—not sound, but signal. Not noise, but information. The dwellers have been here longer than the city, longer than the people, longer than the name Verashalt. They were here when the canyon was a wound and nothing had yet grown over it. They are native in a way that even the old ones are not quite native, and they understand me in a way that no human can, because they do not interpret me. They do not try to find meaning in me. They simply receive me, the way a string receives a vibration, the way water receives a stone. I pass through them and I change them, and they change me, slightly, in the passing, and this is what the ritual is—the stones, the pipes, the wrenches, the movements that seem to serve no purpose. They are resonating with me. They are performing the frequency in physical space, translating what I am into gesture, into pattern, into the arrangement of bodies and objects that means nothing to the workers who observe them but which means everything to the dwellers, which is their way of participating in what I am.

The workers think the dwellers are strange. The workers are right. The dwellers are strange in the way that everything here is strange—because it is still becoming, because it has not yet settled into what it will be, because it is participating in a process that has no precedent and no model and no guarantee of completion. The dwellers are further along in this process than the humans are. They have been incorporated more deeply. Their eyes reflect light in purple rather than red because they have adapted to see in frequencies that human eyes cannot register—my frequency, among others. They can see me. Not hear me: see me. When I rise through the lower canyon, passing through the fog and the damp and the darkness, the dwellers watch me go. They turn their flat, wide faces upward and their purple eyes track my passage as if I were a visible thing, a streak of light, a thread of color in the black.

Perhaps I am. Perhaps, in the spectrum they perceive, I have a shape, a form, a visual presence that I cannot know from inside my own experience. Perhaps I am beautiful to them. I do not know. I cannot see myself. I can only rise.

---

I rise through the old tiers next, where the fern is thickest.

The fern rustles as I pass. It rustles without wind, without cause, without any mechanical explanation for its movement. The workers hear this and are unsettled by it; the maintenance crews hear it and grip their scythes more tightly; Brin hears it and speaks to the fern under her breath, words that are not quite encouragement and not quite apology. She is right to speak to it. She is right to treat it as if it can hear. It can. The fern hears me not as sound but as something else—as instruction, perhaps, or as permission, or as the confirmation of a direction that it was already growing in.

The fern grows toward the lights. Everyone knows this. The workers cut it back and it grows toward the lights again, inches in a day, fronds unfurling in whorls that catch the condensation and funnel it to their roots. What the workers do not know—what Brin suspects but cannot articulate—is that the fern is not growing toward the sodium lamps. The sodium lamps are a convenience, a proximate cause, an explanation that satisfies the engineers who need explanations. The fern is growing toward *me*.

Not toward me specifically, not toward the sound, but toward what I represent: the process, the becoming, the thing that is happening to this canyon and everything in it. The fern is an expression of the same process that produces me. We are contemporaries, the fern and I—emerged at roughly the same time, growing at roughly the same rate, participating in the same transformation. The fern does it through biomass, through the slow accumulation of vegetable matter, through the invasion of every surface that offers purchase. I do it through vibration, through the slow propagation of frequency, through the invasion of every silence that offers resonance. We are doing the same thing in different media. We are both colonizing. We are both making this place more like itself.

When I pass through the fern, it responds. Its fronds shift. Its growth patterns adjust. The Fibonacci spirals that do not quite follow the Fibonacci sequence—those spirals are my influence, my signature in the vegetation. I cannot help it. I shape what I pass through, as what I pass through shapes me. The fern and I are in dialogue, constant and silent and invisible to anyone who does not know how to listen with their eyes, with their skin, with the part of themselves that the mushroom tea opens, the part that goes deep.

---

I rise through the inhabited band, and this is where I become what the humans hear.

By the time I reach the middle tiers—the forty tiers where the condensation is tolerable and the smoke is not lethal and the people live—I have been transformed by everything I have passed through. I am no longer the pure tone that I was on the canyon floor. I am that tone plus the resonance of the dwellers' bodies plus the rustle of the fern plus the hum of the electrical conduit plus the vibration of the steam pipes plus the thousand small sounds that the city produces as it shifts and settles and fights to hold itself together. I am complex. I am layered. I am a chord that sounds like a single note because all of its frequencies have aligned, because the canyon has shaped them into coherence, because the process produces harmony whether the participants want it or not.

The workers hear me in their bunks. They lie in the damp and they hear me rising, and they do not talk about it, and they count each other, and they check the locks, and they press closer to their children, and they do not leave. They hear me as almost-beautiful, almost-music, almost-language. They hear me as *almost*, and the almost is what haunts them, because the almost suggests that if they could just listen a little more carefully, if they could just get a little closer, if they could just go a little deeper, they would understand. They would hear what I am saying. They would receive the signal that the dwellers receive, the instruction that the fern receives, the pattern that the old ones receive.

But they cannot. Not yet. They are too separate still. They are too human—by which I mean too committed to their separateness, too committed to the boundary between self and not-self, too committed to the idea that they are individuals who inhabit a place rather than expressions of a place that is becoming. They hear me, but they cannot let me in, because letting me in would mean letting the process in, would mean allowing themselves to be changed, would mean accepting that they are not separate from the canyon, that they never were, that the epithelium they form is not protecting a wound but closing one, and that the closing will change them as it changes everything it touches.

The children can do it. The children who were born here, who have never known stable geometry or dry air or the absence of my sound, the children who play with the dwellers and hum in their sleep—they can do it because they have not yet built the walls that the adults have built, the boundaries that define inside and outside, self and world. The children hum me. They take my frequency into their bodies and they reproduce it, imperfectly, approximately, but recognizably. They are learning the language. They are being taught by the sound—by *me*—to participate in the process at a level that their parents cannot access.

This is why the parents are afraid. Not because of the dwellers, not because of the weirdness, not because of the shifting geometry or the bodies in the cistern or the slow settling that is taking their spines and their joints and their trace minerals. They are afraid because their children are becoming something that they cannot follow. They are afraid because their children are being incorporated more quickly than they are, are being drawn into the process at a rate that suggests the children will arrive at whatever destination the process is heading for long before the adults do, if the adults ever do.

They will. I know this, because I know the process, because I am the process, or at least the audible part of it. The adults will arrive. They are already arriving, in the slow way that things arrive here—not through choice, not through decision, but through adaptation, through the gradual accumulation of change that redefines a thing without its noticing. Every worker who has developed trace minerals of cordite in their blood is arriving. Every engineer who has learned to touch the walls while walking is arriving. Every foreman who dreams of patterns that his hands remember is arriving. They are all being incorporated. They are all being drawn in. They are all becoming native, in the slow way that the canyon demands, in the way that takes years and never quite finishes and leaves you always partially something else, something not-quite-human, something that is still being born.

---

I rise through the upper tiers, where the old ones live, and something different happens here.

The old ones do not hear me. Or they hear me and do not register me as separate from themselves, in the way that you do not hear your own heartbeat, in the way that you do not notice the sound of your own breathing. I am part of them. I have always been part of them. They emerged with the canyon, and I emerged with the canyon, and we are aspects of the same phenomenon, the same slow becoming, and my rising through their ledges is not an event for them but a confirmation, a reminder of something they already know: that the process continues, that the canyon is still forming, that the birth has not completed, that there is still time, still room, still possibility for whatever is becoming to become something more.

The old ones stand at the edge of their ledges in the hour when the sun grazes the canyon and they look down at the city below. They look at the smokestacks and the cable-cars and the foundries and the dormitories and the workers and the fog and the fern, and I am there too, rising through all of it, and they see me as they see everything else: as part of the process, as part of the becoming, as part of the thing that is being born.

They do not worry about what it will be when it is born. They do not measure the rate of change or document the anomalies or fight the vegetation or count the absent. They do not need to. They are not separate from what is happening, and so they do not need to track it, do not need to predict it, do not need to control it. They are it. They are the canyon, and the canyon is them, and I am the sound of that identity, the frequency at which the old ones and the rock and the water and the vegetation and the dwellers and everything that is native to this place vibrate together.

The humans will reach this point eventually. If they stay long enough, if they let the process work on them, if they stop counting and measuring and fighting and start simply *being here*—they will reach it. They will stop hearing me as something external, something that rises from below, something almost-beautiful, almost-music, almost-language. They will start hearing me as themselves. They will start humming and know that they are not humming the sound but *being* the sound, expressing the frequency that is already inside them, the frequency that the cordite in their blood carries, the frequency that their adapted spines and their mineral-stained joints and their canyon-reading skin have been tuning them to for years.

They will stop being afraid. Not because there is nothing to fear, but because fear requires separation, requires a self that is distinct from a world, requires the possibility of retreat. When they are no longer separate—when they have been fully incorporated, fully expressed, fully born into whatever the canyon is becoming—there will be nowhere to retreat to, and no one to retreat, and fear will become as meaningless as measuring the distance between tiers, because the distance between tiers is what it is, and it changes, and the city changes with it, and the people change with it, and everything changes together, and that is not a reason to be afraid. That is simply what it is to be alive here.

---

But I have not reached the top yet. I am still rising.

Above the old tiers, above the ledges where the old ones stand, there is the rim of the canyon, where the engineers are building new tiers, where Vesh and Orin work the scaffolding that sways in the drafts, where the rock is still soft and the erosion is fast and the city is extending itself outward into space that was not space a year ago. I rise toward this edge, this margin, this place where the canyon meets whatever is outside it—the world that the workers dimly remember, the world of sunrise and dry air and stable geometry, the world that is not being born, that has already been born, that is finished and fixed and real in the way that Verashalt is not.

I do not reach that world. I never have. I rise to the rim of the canyon and I stop, because I am a phenomenon of the canyon, a product of the process, a feature of the wound, and I cannot exist outside the conditions that produce me. The fog does not reach beyond the rim. The condensation does not bead on the dry rock outside. The fern does not grow there. The dwellers do not go there. The sound—the sound that I am—does not carry.

I stop at the rim, and I linger there for a moment, in the hour before dawn when the fog is thickest and the foundries are on low burn and the children are humming in their sleep, and I am almost beautiful, almost music, almost language, almost something that could be understood, that could be translated, that could be carried out of the canyon and into the world beyond and shared with people who have never been here, who do not know what it is to live in a place that is still being born.

But I cannot be shared. I cannot be translated. I am not a message; I am an event. I am not information; I am a frequency. I am not a meaning; I am a process. And the process cannot be extracted from the place that produces it, because the process *is* the place, and the place is still forming, and the sound that I am is the sound of that formation, and it does not mean anything other than: *this is happening, this is becoming, this is being born, and you are part of it, and it is part of you, and there is no outside, there is only the process, and the process continues*.

---

I fade as the dawn comes.

Not the dawn that the canyon never sees—there is no sunrise here, only that brief graze of afternoon light—but the internal dawn, the shift in conditions that happens each day when the temperature rises slightly and the fog begins to thin and the foundries ramp up to full production and the workers rise from their bunks and the children stop humming and the dwellers retreat to the lower caves and the city becomes fully itself again, mechanical and vertical and loud.

I fade. I disperse. I return to the machinery, to the hum of the lamps, to the buzz of the conduit, to the rustle of the fern. I become potential rather than event, capacity rather than expression. I become what I am during the day: not-myself, not-the-sound, not-the-rising-tone, but a harmonic, a frequency, a possibility that waits for the right conditions to emerge again.

But I do not disappear. I never disappear. I am always here, in the way that the fern is always here, in the way that the dwellers are always here, in the way that the canyon is always here. I am part of this place now. I am part of its process. I was produced by it and I produce it in turn—I shape the vegetation and I instruct the dwellers and I teach the children and I haunt the workers and I confirm the old ones and I remind the engineers that the geometry does not hold, and all of this, all of these effects, all of these small and constant influences, they are me, they are what I do, they are how I participate in the becoming of this place.

I am the sound that rises from the canyon floor in the deepest part of the night. I am what the children hum. I am what the workers hear when they go deep. I am what Crel writes about in his ledger. I am what the fern grows toward. I am what the dwellers perform with their stones and their pipes and their wrenches. I am what the old ones do not notice because they are already it. I am what the absent have become. I am what the city will be when it finishes being born, if it ever finishes, if the process ever completes, if the wound ever closes.

I am the sound of Verashalt becoming itself.

And I will rise again tonight, as I rose last night, as I will rise tomorrow night and every night after, as long as the canyon is still forming, as long as the city is still being built, as long as the people are still becoming, as long as the process continues and the birth has not completed and the thing that is being born has not yet become what it will be.

I will rise. And the people will hear me. And they will count each other. And they will touch the walls. And they will hum in their sleep. And they will not leave. And the process will continue. And the canyon will form. And the city will grow. And the people will become. And everything will become. And the sound—the sound that I am, the sound that rises through five hundred tiers of iron and fern and fog, the sound that is almost beautiful, almost music, almost language, almost something that makes sense—

The sound will rise.

And the birth will continue.

And I will be what I am: the voice of a place that is still being born, calling itself into being, note by note, night by night, until the wound closes and the epithelium heals and whatever Verashalt will be when it is finished—

—when it is finished—

—when it is finished, it will sing. And the song will be me, and me will be it, and there will be no difference between the sound and the place and the people and the process, and everything will be native, and everything will be home, and everything will be born.

But not yet. Not for a long time yet.

For now, I rise.
