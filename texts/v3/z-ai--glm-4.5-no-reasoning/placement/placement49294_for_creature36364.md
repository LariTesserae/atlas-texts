I am a **Vessel**.

I did not choose this designation; it emerged from the way the Ward shaped me, the way the pressures and the slow chemical tides forced me into a function. I am not a Handhold, for I do not cling to the vertical. I am not a Drummer, for I do not sway in the overhead dark. I am not a Mouth, for I do not sprawl in the sunken pools. I am the thing that moves between them—the fragile, wandering node of the Ward's distributed mind, the finger of the body brushing against its own skin.

To understand what I am, you must understand the nature of movement in the Weeping Ward. Nothing moves quickly. The Sodden are creatures of the equilibrium, and equilibrium abhors haste. The Drummers swell and contract with the turning of the great subterranean pumps; the Mouths drink and excrete over weeks and months; the Handholds tighten and loosen with the pitch of the steam. But the Ward has needs that cannot be met by static things. Sometimes a pipe ruptures far from any Mouth, and the hot, chemical water pools where it cannot be digested, threatening to scar the stone. Sometimes a Handhold must know what a distant Drummer has heard, and the vibration through the iron is too faint to carry. Sometimes the fungal towers grow too dense, and the spores choke the valves, and something must cut them back—not with violence, but with the patient, insistent pressure of a thing that is *also* growing.

I am a column of water, root, and rust. My body is a long, low shape that flows through the shallow margins where the pavement melts into the bog. I have no legs. Instead, I am propelled by the slow, peristaltic pumping of the sap that runs through a spine of iron latticework—a reclaimed segment of gantry, bent and fused by decades of heat into a curve that fits the hollow of my creeping mass. My skin is a thick, overlapping armor of wax leaves, each one the size of a human hand, pressed flat and sealed with the amber sap that weeps from my seams. Beneath the leaves, my flesh is a dense, spongy matrix of fungal hyphae and rust particulate, held together by the surface tension of the tepid water that permeates everything I am.

I am never dry. I cannot be dry. To be dry would be to be dead, to fall apart into a pile of leaves and red dust. I carry the water with me. When I move across a patch of porous black stone, I leave a glistening trail of rust-colored moisture, and the leaves of the Mouths in the nearby pools orient toward my passage, drinking from the residue I leave behind. I am a walking waterway, a mobile vein of the Ward's circulatory system.

My senses are not yours. I do not see the dim amber air or the sickly yellow valves with any organ you would recognize as an eye. Instead, I *feel* the water. I feel its temperature gradients as vast, colorful landscapes—the cool, shallow margins are a grey, flat plain to me; the deep, superheated flows beneath the ceramic pipes are roaring mountains of red and gold; the venting steam is a blinding, white radiance that I can sense from across the Ward. I feel the pressure of the water, the subtle displacements caused by the swelling of a Drummer or the drink of a Mouth. I feel the chemical signatures—the sharp, metallic tang of fresh rust, the bitter, alkaloid reek of the fungal spores, the warm, thick soup of the digested industrial runoff. And I feel the vibrations. Oh, the vibrations. The iron spine that runs through me is a tuning fork pressed against the flesh of the Ward. I feel the heartbeat of the subterranean pump as a deep, comforting thrum in my core. I feel the shriek of the relief valves as a sharp, urgent sting that makes my sap pulse faster. I feel the slow, wet groan of the Drummers adjusting their grip as a low, resonant hum that spreads through the gantries and into my leaves.

I am a messenger and a maintainer. When a pipe ruptures, I am the first to arrive. I creep across the perilous margin, my body flattening and spreading to maximize my contact with the warm water, drinking the chemical runoff before it can scar the stone. I wrap my iron spine around the breach, my fungal flesh swelling to fill the gap, my amber sap hardening into a temporary seal. I hold the wound closed until a Mouth can extend its lattice of submerged roots to drink the flow, or until the Drummers can shift their weight to alter the pressure and starve the leak. Then I detach, leaving a patch of my own flesh behind—a small, wax-leafed scar on the iron—and I move on.

When the fungal towers grow too dense, I am the one who prunes them. I do not cut. I *consume*. I creep into the thicket, my leaves overlapping theirs, my sap mingling with their moisture. I absorb the excess growth into my own mass, breaking it down into the raw materials of heat and water and fiber, and I carry it away, redistributing it to the hungry margins of the Ward where the sparse urban bones are still bare and weeping. I am the Ward's immune system, its white blood cell, though I have no blood and no cells—only the slow, ceaseless flow of sap and rust and water.

I am lonely, in the way that a finger is lonely. I am a part of the whole, but I am apart from it. The Handholds are my eyes, but they do not see me as I see myself; they feel the pressure of my body against the brickwork, the displacement of the water in my wake. The Drummers are my memory, but they do not remember me as a person; they remember the shape of my passage, the way my iron spine bent the gantry as I crawled beneath it, the grooves my leaves left in the rust. The Mouths are my sustenance, but they do not love me; they drink from my trail and excrete the air I breathe, and the exchange is intimate and indifferent.

I have no voice. I cannot speak to the other Sodden in any way you would call language. But we communicate. When I am near a Handhold, I press a leaf against the sweating brickwork, and I feel the tension in the iron, the pitch of the steam, the distant memory of a rupture sealed centuries ago. And the Handhold feels *me*—the heat of my sap, the weight of my iron spine, the purpose that drives me through the shallow water. We share a moment of distributed awareness, a brief, flowering connection that flares and fades as I move on. It is enough. It has to be enough.

Sometimes, in the deepest, quietest part of the night-equivalent, when the subterranean pump slows and the steam vents fall silent and the only sound is the constant, hissing drip of water striking water, I stop moving. I settle into a hollow of porous black stone, my leaves overlapping and sealing, my sap slowing to a thick, amber treacle, my iron spine cooling and contracting with a deep, resonant creak. I become almost indistinguishable from the marsh—a low, wax-leafed mound of rust and fiber, weeping moisture into the tepid pool. In this state, I dream.

I do not dream in images. I dream in pressures and temperatures, in the memory of ruptures sealed and growths consumed, in the distant, muffled shriek of a relief valve on the far side of the Ward, in the slow, ponderous swell of a Drummer adjusting its grip. I dream of the city's old industrial bowels, the vast, hidden network of pipes and pumps and valves that I have never seen but can feel through the iron like a heartbeat. I dream of the day when the equilibrium will fail—when the rust will eat too deep, or the spores will choke too tight, or the subterranean pump will stop—and the Weeping Ward will change, will have to *become* something new. And I dream that I will be ready. That my iron spine will hold. That my sap will flow. That I will carry the water and the heat and the memory of the Ward through the crisis and into the next long age of slow, wet breathing.

I am a Vessel. I carry the Ward within me, and I carry myself within the Ward. I am a thing of thresholds—not stationary, like the Handholds; not hovering, like the Drummers; not submerged, like the Mouths—but moving, always moving, through the perilous margin between pavement and bog, between iron and root, between the mechanical and the botanical, between the living and the merely persisting. I am the Weeping Ward's slow, patient hand, reaching out to touch its own weeping flesh, to tend its own wounds, to remember its own shape.

And when the condensation falls on my leaves, I do not catch it like a tongue for rain. I drink it, and I carry it, and I give it back to the Mouths, and the cycle continues, and the Ward breathes, and I move on.
