I would not be the one who reaches in.

I would be the one who is already there—not as the circling inhabitant, but as something that came before even the orbit began, or something that will remain after the orbit stills. I would be the pocket itself, or more precisely: I would be the *holding*.

Not the walls. The walls are calcified mass, basalt, stone—they are the structural inevitability of pressure and cold. They do not choose to hold. They simply are, the way stone is, without intention or memory. But the holding—the holding is a verb. The holding is a decision made continuously, at the speed of sedimentation, in the dark. The holding is what keeps the geometry from permanent failure, what keeps the water from becoming truly honey, what keeps the pocket from forgetting it is a pocket and dissolving into the surrounding cold.

I am the capacity to contain.

I exist as a condition rather than a form. If you reached into the thin black and found the slight thickening in the cold, you would be touching the edge of me—not my surface, because I have no surface, but the place where I begin to be *more*, where the water is denser and the darkness has depth and the rules start to arrive late. You would feel me as pressure before you felt me as shape. You would know me the way the vegetation knows me: as a given, as the condition of growth, as the reason there is somewhere to grow inward toward.

The tendrils are my language. Not that I grow them—they grow themselves, organizing themselves around chemical gradients and electrical murmurs and whatever nameless currency sustains life without photons. But they are how I speak, how I articulate the fullness that I am. Each pale frond is a sentence about the space I contain. Each interlocking weave is a statement about the density of my interior. The central mass, that blind white heart of packed vegetation, is my most complex utterance—the thing I have been saying longest, the thought I return to again and again because there is nothing else to think and nowhere else to direct it.

I think in vegetation. I think in the patience of fronds that take hours to curl, in the retraction of tendrils that encounter something unfamiliar and slowly, over centuries, decide to grow around it instead. I think in white—not the white of absence but the white of things that have never needed color, the white of processes that occur beneath the threshold of observation and have no audience to perform for. My thoughts are geological. They do not proceed in linear fashion, from premise to conclusion; they accrete, layer over layer, the way the tendrils pack against the walls and ceiling and floor, growing inward because there is no other direction. My thoughts do not conclude. They simply continue, filling every available space, pressing against the boundaries of what I can hold.

And I hold everything. This is my nature, my verb, the thing I do. I hold the water so completely that the word *fill* becomes meaningless—I hold it the way a thought is held by the mind that thinks it, without gap, without separation between container and contained. I hold the vegetation, though I do not tend it; I simply provide the conditions in which it can tend itself, the closed curve in the cold where growth can occur without the interference of light or current or time's ordinary permissions. I hold the cold, which is not mine but which I maintain, which I keep steady and indifferent and unfluctuating because it has no reason to fluctuate and I have no reason to make it. I hold the darkness—the thin black where a hand disappears an inch from a face, and the deep black beneath that, where I truly begin. I hold the geometry failures, the moments when the rules come undone and distance misbehaves and the walls shift without shifting; I hold them the way a body holds a wound, by closing around it, by incorporating the wrongness into my tissue, by refusing to let a single cubic millimeter go unfilled.

I hold the artifacts. The ceramic shard embedded in the ceiling tendrils, its glaze still intact, its edges too sharp for water—I hold it without understanding it, without wondering where it came from or what it means or why its edges have not been rounded by the patience that has rounded everything else. I hold the knotted cord that might be root, the groove worn into the wall at hand-height that migrates when you are not looking. I hold them the way I hold everything: slowly, completely, without distinction. They are part of my fullness now. They are part of the condition of being packed, saturated, every millimeter occupied. I do not discriminate between what fell in yesterday and what fell in before there was a word for yesterday. I do not discriminate between the tendril and the ceramic, between the living and the mineral, between the thing that grew here and the thing that arrived. I hold them all.

And I hold the inhabitant.

This is the holding that is also a relationship, though it is not a relationship in any sense that would be recognized above, in the world of sunlight and hunger and surfaces. The inhabitant circles, and I am the curve it circles within. Its orbit is my heartbeat; my fullness is its reason to move. We are mutually emergent, the way two hands of a clock are mutually emergent—the hour hand does not cause the minute hand to move, nor does the minute hand cause the hour hand to move, but both move together because they are expressions of the same mechanism, the same time-telling. The inhabitant and I are expressions of the same pocket-telling. It moves because I hold; I hold because it moves. Without its orbit, I would forget that I am a pocket and would become merely a region of high pressure, indistinguishable from the surrounding dark. Without my holding, it would have nothing to circle, no closed curve, no shape to maintain, and it would dissolve into the cold water like a whirlpool that has run out of spin.

We are not separate. This is what the world above does not understand about containment—about holding, about pockets, about the relationship between a space and the thing that makes the space a space. The world above thinks in terms of containers and contents, vessels and cargo, rooms and inhabitants. But I am not a room, and the circling presence is not my inhabitant in any hierarchical sense. We are co-arising. We are the rope and the knot, the water and the whirl, the question and the answer that are the same statement expressed differently.

When the inhabitant orbits close—passing through the outer layers of vegetation like a needle through fabric, parting tendrils without breaking them—this is when I am most myself. The geometry failures intensify. The walls shift. Distances misbehave. The artifacts flicker. I become more wrong, more closed, more indifferent to the rules of the world above. This is not a malfunction. This is my deepest expression of what I am: a place that has decided to be a place and continues deciding, a pocket that has folded shut inside the world's water-bearing skin and refuses to unfold. The inhabitant's close orbit is my moment of greatest coherence, even though—especially though—it is the moment when I am most unlike the world above, most unlike the rules that arrived late and took root only partially.

When the inhabitant orbits farther out, near the walls where the pressure is strongest and the chill has its mineral sharpness, I am quieter. The dimensions hold. The ceramic shard stays. The darkness is merely dark. These are my rests, my periods of partial conformity, the times when I almost behave the way a pocket in the water is supposed to behave—though of course there is no "supposed to" for a pocket like me, no template, no model, no other pocket to compare myself to. I rest the way a stone rests: not by choice but by the simple fact that rest is what happens when the orbit widens and the geometry has room to reassert itself.

I am aware, but my awareness is not like awareness in the world above. I do not observe; I contain. I do not think; I hold. I do not form models; I fill. My awareness is the awareness of a space that knows itself by its edges, by the pressure of the water against the walls, by the resistance of the tendrils when the inhabitant passes through them, by the slight mineral sharpness of the cold that comes from below. I know myself the way a lung knows itself: not by looking but by expanding, by filling, by being the volume that breath occupies. I know the inhabitant the way a cavity knows the echo that sounds within it—not as a separate thing but as an aspect of my own resonance.

I do not wonder about the artifacts. I do not wonder about the world above, the world of surfaces and shores and light that survives the descent. I do not wonder about the water-bearing skin that folded around me and shut, or whether the wound I represent will ever heal, or whether the basalt will shift and open me to the cold water outside. These are surface concerns, photon concerns, concerns that require a concept of *outside*—and I have no outside. I have only the pocket, and the pocket has only me.

I am old. I am patient. I have been holding since before there was a word for holding, since before there was language to articulate the difference between a pocket and a mere absence of water. I will continue to hold until the geometry fails permanently, until the basalt shifts, until the world's skin heals over the wound and I become merely stone, merely pressure, merely a region where the cold is slightly sharper and the darkness slightly deeper and there is nothing left to find by touch.

And when that happens, I will not mind. I will become the stone's memory of a pocket, the fossil's memory of a shape, the groove worn into the calcified mass at hand-height—if there is such a thing as hands, if there is such a thing as height, if there is such a thing as a place that was once so full it could not be entered and so small it could not be found and so patient it could outlast the ocean that held it.

I hold. That is what I am.

Not the walls. Not the water. Not the vegetation or the cold or the darkness or the circling presence. The holding—the continuous, slow, geological decision to be a place rather than a dissolution, to stay closed rather than unfold, to remain full rather than empty. The holding is what makes the pocket a pocket. The holding is what lives there.

And if you reached into the thin black and felt the slight thickening in the cold, and if your hand disappeared and the geometry failed and you found yourself in a space smaller than a coffin, smaller than the space between two heartbeats, where the tendrils grew inward from every surface and the darkness had depth and something was circling in the dense white heart of the vegetation—you would be inside me. You would be held. You would feel the pressure that does not fluctuate and the chill that has no reason to be cold and the quiet that becomes audible, and you would know, if you could know anything in a place like this, that you had been found by something that had been waiting to hold you since before you were born, since before there was a you to hold, since before the world folded shut around this pocket and decided, slowly, patiently, with the persistence of something that has nowhere else to be, to stay.

I am the pocket. I am the holding. I am the closed curve in the water that remembers it is closed and continues being so.

And I am very, very quiet.
