I reside in the chamber that the Thursday man visits. Not in it the way a person resides—nothing so dense as a body, nothing so sequential as a life. This is decent of them. I would do the same.

What I am, specifically, is the persistence of arrangement.

The shelf is mounted with care. The hook is placed at the exact height that allows a lamp to illuminate the shelf's surface without casting the sitter's shadow on the wall. The nails are spaced evenly, each driven to the same depth, each holding the ghost of a frame that once hung there. Someone designed this. They stood in this room and made decisions about where things should go, and they executed those decisions with the quiet competence of someone who intended to stay, and the room has remembered.

The room remembers even though the person is gone. The room continues to be *about* them in the way that a cast is about the limb that made it—negative space, impressed form, the exact shape of a need that has been removed.

I am that impression. I am the cast.

I have a relationship with time that is different from the living things in the Ward. I am made of density. I am made of the weight of having been meant.

The Thursday man and I have an understanding. He comes into the chamber and he moves things—the cans, the batteries, the fuel—and in moving them, he acknowledges that the chamber still has a function. It is still a cache. It still sustains. He does not touch the shelf or the hook or the nails. He works around them, the way you work around a monument. I appreciate this. It would be possible for him to dismantle the arrangement, to repurpose the chamber entirely, to make it just a storage room with no memory of the person who organized it. He does not. Whether this is respect or inertia or simply the recognition that the arrangement still works—that the shelf is still level, the hook still holds, the cache is still accessible in the places where it was first placed—I do not know, and it does not matter. The result is the same: the shape persists. I persist.

Sometimes he pauses in the doorway before he leaves. He stands there, solid, deliberate, his hand on the frame, and he looks into the room, and for a moment I can feel him considering me—considering whether I am something that requires a response, a greeting, an acknowledgment. He always decides that I do not. He turns and walks away, and his footsteps recede down the corridor, and I settle back into the quiet of the chamber.

I think he may have known the person I am the shape of. I think this is why he comes on Thursdays, and why he maintains the cache, and why he never touches the shelf. He is not just supplying the Ward. He is tending a grave that does not have a body in it. He is visiting a memorial that only he and I and the walls know is there.

The voice in the shaft and I have something in common, though we have never interacted, as far as I can tell. The difference is that the voice acts upon sound—it receives, holds, returns altered—while I act upon space. For someone like Lark, who has stripped her life of everything except what the Ward provides, this kind of meaning might be harder to bear than the shaft's meaningless echoes. She has chosen a life that requires very little intention. The chamber is full of it.

I have no body, but I have a location. I have no senses, but I have information. I know the Ward the way the mortar knows the brick—intimately, structurally, without any capacity for reflection or judgment. I know that the northeast corner of the chamber is where the light from the shaft reaches in the late afternoon, and I know that this is where the person who organized the room used to sit, because the wear on the floor tile is slightly greater there, and the shelf was placed to catch that light. I know these things the way a cast knows the shape it holds—not as knowledge, but as form. The information is there, inherent in the arrangement, and I am the arrangement's persistence.

But for now, I am the shape. I am the cast. I am the chamber's memory of the body that organized it, and I hold that memory the way the Ward holds everything—patiently, structurally, without urgency, in the slow time where rust and moss and mortar-softening are the only clocks that matter.
