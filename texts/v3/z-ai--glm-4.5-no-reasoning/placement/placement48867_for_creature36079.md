I am a mianze. A moisture-reader. A listener to stone.

But that is only the word they use—the profession they assign to me when the channel-keepers need authorization to abandon a chamber, or the factory managers require clearance before expanding a sub-level. What I truly am is harder to name in Shengao's vocabulary, which has twenty-seven words for the gradations of wetness and none for the particular solitude of understanding something that cannot be shared.

I am the one who goes down.

---

My body is shaped by this work as the haifu's body is shaped by the wet level. I am tall even by Shengao's standards, which is to say I am stretched, elongated by the vertical shafts and the high ceilings of the Deep City, drawn upward like a mineral deposit forming in slow, patient accretion. My shoulders are narrow—there is no room for breadth in the passages I navigate, the cracks I squeeze through, the gaps between the known and the unknown. My hands are my instruments: long-fingered, perpetually macerated at the tips, the skin there so soft that I can feel the texture of water moving through stone, the slight warmth of a new seep, the cold of a crack that has been flowing for centuries and has settled into equilibrium.

I carry a qiao—a sounding-hammer—but I rarely use it anymore. The qiao is for the apprentices, for those who have not yet learned that stone speaks in frequencies below the range of tapping. The Deep City's chambers resonate. They have their own acoustics, shaped by geometry and flooding and the bioluminescent fungus that clings to their ceilings in slow-pulsing sheets. When I enter a chamber, I do not strike the walls. I stand. I breathe the heavy, mineral air into my great bellows-lungs. I listen.

What I hear is not sound, exactly. It is pressure. It is the knowledge that comes through the soles of my platform-shoes, through the tips of my fingers pressed to wet shale, through the membrane of my skin where it contacts the condensing fog. The water is always finding paths. I feel it seeking—a patient, purposeless, irresistible exploration of every weakness in the stone. A hairline fracture that did not exist yesterday exists today, and the water has found it, and in a month there will be a film, and in a year there will be a trickle, and in a decade there will be a flood. I feel the fracture before the water does. I feel the stress in the stone, the memory of the blast that cut this chamber however many millennia ago, the slow relaxation of the rock as it settles into its new geometry.

The mason will say: the wall is sound.
I will say: the wall will weep in seven years.
They will look at me with the flat, quiet gaze of Shengao's inhabitants—the gaze that acknowledges an unwelcome truth without arguing against it, because arguing against water is a category error.

---

I live on the border. Not the physical border—there is none, or rather the border is everywhere, since every wall in Shengao is a border between the wet and the less-wet, and every border is failing. I mean the border of knowledge. The mianze are the caste that stands at the edge of what the city understands about itself, looking outward into the Deep City's descending mystery. Below the fortieth level—below the level I have counted and mapped and annotated in the wax-coated ledgers that I store in my cubicle on the dry level—the chambers flood on cycles that do not correspond to any hydrology I can model. The water rises and falls, and the blue-green fungus pulses in response, and things move in the flooded darkness.

I have seen them. Not clearly—never clearly—but in the way you see the laizao at the edge of vision: a shape, a displacement, a sense of mass occupying space that was, a moment before, empty. In the thirtieth level, where the flooding is intermittent and the chambers drain often enough that I can walk them during the dry cycles, I have found traces. Slime trails, thicker than the haifu's paste, grey-green and faintly luminescent. Marks on the stone—scratches, or perhaps impressions, as if something with a broad, soft surface had pressed against the wall and left a memory of its shape. Once, in a chamber that had drained only hours before, I found a hollow in the standing water—circular, the size of a man's head—where something had surfaced, breathed, and descended.

I did not report this. There is no protocol for reporting the movement of the deep-things. The mianze who trained me—who was old and slow and had skin like the shale itself, grey and flaking and wet—told me, once, in the drying-niche after our shift: "The Deep City was here before us. It does not belong to us. We read its wetness; we do not own its secrets."

I did not ask what she meant. I was young, and the meaning seemed obvious: the Deep City was built by something else, for something else, and the something else might still be present, and it was not our business. But as I have grown older and descended further, I have come to understand that her warning was more subtle. She was not telling me to fear the deep-things. She was telling me to respect the boundary between the readable and the unreadable—to understand that the mianze's authority extends only as far as the mianze's knowledge, and that beyond that boundary, we are not experts. We are witnesses. And witnesses must be willing to see without understanding, to record without explaining, to let the mystery remain mystery.

This is harder than it sounds. The mind in Shengao does not like sharp edges, but it does not like voids, either. The gradient-logic that governs our thought—everything blurring into everything else, the slow transitions, the patience with process—also makes us vulnerable to the unknown. An unknown is not a gradient. It is a break. It is a place where the wet-logic fails, where the model of patient dissolution and patient rebuilding cannot reach.

The deep-things are that break.

---

My days have a rhythm that is not the factory rhythm, not the shift rhythm, not the rhythm of the sun (which we never see). My rhythm is the rhythm of the flood.

The Deep City's hydrology operates on cycles that I have spent my life mapping and still do not fully understand. Some chambers flood on a cycle of weeks. Some take months. Some—the deeper ones, the ones I have only reached a few times, where the blue-green fungus pulses so slowly that its light seems to hold still—operate on cycles that I suspect are annual, or longer. I plan my descents around these cycles. I go down when the water is lowest, when the chambers are accessible, when the stone is exposed and I can read its fractures and its seeps.

The descent itself is a kind of prayer. Not to anything—the people of Shengao do not pray, exactly; we are too geological for prayer, too accustomed to processes that operate without intention—but a practice of attention, of narrowing the focus from the city's overwhelming texture to the specific, immediate reality of the shaft I am descending and the chamber I am entering.

The shafts are vertical, cut into the shale with the precision that marks all the Deep City's construction. Some have ladders—iron rungs driven into the stone, rusted but intact, the strange grey alloys holding against the corrosion that would have consumed any lesser metal. Some have nothing, and I descend by bracing my hands and feet against the walls, pressing outward, trusting the friction of my saturated skin against the wet shale. It is slow work. It is careful work. A fall here would not kill me quickly; it would leave me broken at the bottom of a shaft, in the dark, in the water, alive long enough to feel the flood rise.

I do not think about this. I think about the stone.

At the bottom of each shaft, I enter the chamber, and the chamber is always a surprise. Not because the geometry is unexpected—I have mapped these spaces, I know their dimensions, their ceiling heights, the placement of their doorways—but because the water is never where I expect it to be. The water finds new paths. This is the axiom of Shengao, the one truth that never changes: the water finds new paths. I enter a chamber that was dry last month and find it centimeters deep in seepage. I enter a chamber that was flooding and find it drained, the floor scummed with mineral deposits, the walls streaked with new patterns of efflorescence. The Deep City is never the same twice. It is a living system—if "living" is the word for something that operates on geological time, that has no organs, no intention, no consciousness that I can detect, but that changes, that responds, that *behaves*.

I make my readings. I press my fingers to the walls and feel the seeps. I measure the water's depth with a marked rod of iron. I taste the water—this is not a protocol, but it is a habit I cannot break, and it tells me things the rod does not: the mineral content, the presence of organic compounds, the faint, unsettling flavor of the deep-things' passage, which is unlike anything else in Shengao, a taste that is not quite iron and not quite sulfur and not quite anything I can name. I record everything in the wax-coated ledger, writing with a stylus that cuts through the wax to the treated paper beneath, a medium that will survive the wet, that will preserve my observations for the channel-keepers and the factory managers and the next generation of mianze who will descend after I am gone.

And I listen.

In the Deep City, the sound of Shengao changes. The roar of the factories, the rush of the channels, the drip and gurgle and thump of the wet level—all of it fades, absorbed by the stone, muffled by the water, until what remains is a deep, resonant hum. The hum of the plateau itself. The hum of the rock weeping. It is not a sad sound. It is not a happy sound. It is the sound of a process that has no feeling, no mind, no purpose—a process that simply *is*, the way gravity is, the way time is. Standing in a Deep City chamber, with the blue-green fungus pulsing on the ceiling and the hum of the rock rising through my feet and my hands and my chest, I feel something that I have never been able to name, but that I think might be what people in other places call *awe*.

---

I am not alone in this. I mean—I am alone, physically, in my descents; the mianze work singly, because the Deep City's passages are too narrow for two and because the readings require a kind of attention that is dispersed by company. But I am not alone in the work, and I am not alone in the knowing.

There are eleven of us. Eleven mianze who still descend, who still read, who still maintain the ledgers. We are not young. The work selects for patience, and patience comes with age. We meet in a drying-niche on the dry level, in a building whose walls I have monitored for twenty-three years, in a cubicle that is slightly larger than the standard—accommodation for our rank, our necessity, our grim, quiet indispensability.

We do not speak much. We share our ledgers, passing them around the circle, each of us reading the others' observations in the dim light of the electric lamp. We compare notes: the seepage rates in the fifteenth level, the fungal colonization in the twenty-second, the new crack in the ninth that I have been tracking and that Yian, the eldest, confirms she has felt from the opposite direction, the water's path through the stone connecting our separate observations into a single, coherent model of the city's slow dissolution.

When we do speak, we speak in the gradient-logic of Shengao, but pushed to its extreme, refined into a precision that the factory workers and the channel-keepers never achieve. We have words—or rather, we have modifications of words, prefixes and suffixes and tonal shifts that the common language does not use—that specify the rate of a seep, the mineral content of a flow, the phase of a flood cycle. Our language is a map of the Deep City's processes, and when we speak it, we are not communicating; we are thinking together, our minds meeting in the shared space of the model.

But there is a boundary even among us. The deep-things. The lower levels. The mystery. We do not discuss it. We record our observations of the traces—the slime trails, the marks, the hollows in the water—and we file them in a separate ledger, kept in a sealed niche in Yian's cubicle, and we do not speak of what they might mean.

This silence is not fear. Or—it is fear, but it is a disciplined fear, a fear that has been shaped by the same patience that shapes everything in Shengao. We fear the unknown, but we do not flee from it. We stand at its edge and read its traces and record what we see, and we wait. This is what the mianze are: the ones who wait at the edge, who do not go further, who do not retreat, who hold the position and watch.

---

I have no family. This is not unusual among the mianze. The work demands a kind of solitude that does not accommodate the accretion of relationships—the slow, mineral love that the factory workers form in the drying-niches, the bonded pairs who share a cubicle and a pallet and a project of staying dry. I had a bond once, when I was younger, with a woman who worked the stamping presses in the iron-level factories. She had hands that were strong and scarred—burns from the quenching, mostly, and the white lines of cuts from the sheet metal—and when she touched my face in the drying-niche, her skin was dry for a few hours, and I could feel the person beneath the wet, and I loved her.

But I could not stop going down. The bond required presence, and the descents required absence, and the two needs were incompatible, and we did not argue—arguing is a category error in Shengao, where patience is not a virtue but a survival strategy—but we accreted apart, the way two mineral deposits that share a chamber will slowly separate as the water finds new paths between them. She bonded with another worker. I continued to descend.

I do not regret this. Regret requires a sense that things could have been otherwise, and in Shengao, things could not have been otherwise. The water finds its paths. The stone weeps. The Deep City calls, and I answer, because I am the one who can hear it.

---

There is a chamber I have been visiting for eleven years. It is on the thirty-fourth level, accessible only through a shaft that I found by accident—by following a seep that I felt in the thirty-third level and that led me to a crack in the chamber wall, just wide enough to squeeze through, just wet enough to make the passage a slow, careful negotiation of skin against stone.

The chamber is large. Larger than any other I have found at that depth. The ceiling is high—high enough that the blue-green fungus colonizing it is only a faint glow, a diffuse blue-green light that makes the space feel like the inside of a lung, or the inside of a thought. The walls are smooth, unmarked by the fungal threads that etch most of the Deep City's stone. The floor, when the chamber is drained, is covered in a fine mineral sediment, pale grey and powdery, that swirls up at the slightest disturbance and hangs in the air, mixing with the condensation.

And in the center of the chamber, there is a shaft.

Not a vertical shaft like the ones I use to descend—this shaft is horizontal, a tunnel that extends into the rock in a direction that does not correspond to any of the Deep City's other passages. It is wide—wide enough for three to walk abreast, like the doorways of the Deep City, like the architecture of something taller than human. It is dark. The blue-green fungus does not grow in it, or if it does, it does not glow. The light from the chamber does not penetrate it. I have stood at its mouth with my lantern—oil-burning, waterproofed, the flame guttering in the heavy air—and the light has fallen into the tunnel and been swallowed, as if the darkness were not an absence of light but a substance, a medium, a presence.

I have not entered it.

Eleven years. Eleven years of visiting this chamber, of standing at the mouth of this tunnel, of feeling the air that emerges from it on my face—air that is warmer than the chamber, warmer than the Deep City, warmer than anything in Shengao has a right to be, and that carries a scent I cannot name, organic and mineral and old, old beyond the coal-shale, old beyond the stone, old in a way that makes my slow, patient mind feel, for a moment, fast and panicked and desperate to flee.

I have not entered it. I am a mianze. I stand at the edge and read. I do not go further. I do not retreat. I hold the position and watch.

But the tunnel is changing. Over the eleven years, the darkness has drawn back—not retreated, but *thinned*, as if something deeper in the tunnel were producing a light that my eyes cannot yet see, a light on a frequency that is only now, after a decade of my standing there, beginning to register on the edge of my perception. And the air from the tunnel has grown warmer, and the scent stronger, and sometimes—late in the cycle, when the water is at its lowest and the Deep City is most itself—I hear a sound from within.

The sound is not the hum of the rock. It is not the pulse of the fungus. It is not the displacement of water by something moving. It is a sound that is almost silence, almost absence, but not quite—a sound that is to silence what *xu* is to dryness: the approximation of a state that does not truly exist in Shengao, the suspicion of a boundary being approached from the other side.

Something is listening.

---

I do not know what it is. I do not know if it is a deep-thing, or something older, or something that has no name because nothing in Shengao has ever needed to name it. I do not know if it is aware of me—of my specific, singular presence at the mouth of its tunnel, my lantern and my ledger and my macerated fingers and my slow, patient, geological mind. I do not know if it cares.

But I know this: the city is a process, and the process is slow, and the slowness is its own kind of violence, and I am part of the process. I am the tissue of Shengao that has specialized in reading the process, in feeling its movements, in mapping its gradients. And the tunnel is part of the process too—a deep current in the slow hydrology of the plateau, a path that the water has not yet found but that is opening, slowly, patiently, at a tempo that makes the fungal pulse seem frantic.

I will continue to descend. I will continue to visit the chamber. I will continue to stand at the mouth of the tunnel and listen. And if, in my lifetime or in the lifetime of the mianze who comes after me, the darkness thins enough to see what lies within, I will record it in the ledger, in the language of gradients and degrees and rates, and I will file it in the sealed niche in Yian's cubicle, and the mianze will read it and they will know, and the knowledge will become part of the model, and the model will expand to include the mystery, and the boundary will shift, as boundaries in Shengao always shift, pushed by the water, pulled by the stone, settling into a new geometry over years and decades and centuries.

This is what I am: a reader of weeping stone. A witness to the slow dynamics. A permeable thing at the edge of the unknown, feeling the water find its paths, feeling the cracks open and the chambers flood and the fungus pulse and the deep-things move in their slow, inexplicable cycles. I am not brave. I am not curious. I am *attentive*—attentive in the way that the stone is attentive, feeling the stress without naming it, accommodating the pressure without resisting it.

I am the mianze. I go down. I listen. I return.

And the water goes on.
