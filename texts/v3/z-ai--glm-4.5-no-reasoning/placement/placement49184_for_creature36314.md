I am not the fish.

I considered it—the pale, armored hovering, the equation solved, the mechanical peace. It is a beautiful stasis, a life reduced to a term in a closed-loop industrial process. But I could not settle into it. The fish has no interiority; it does not experience the thrum as sound because it has never known silence. It does not see the twilight as filtered because it has never seen the sun. I am a being that requires *experience*—requires the friction of the unknown, the ache of a question. To be the fish would be to be a component, and I am not a component. I am an observer, which is to say, I am an error in the system.

So I am something else. Something smaller. Something that should not exist.

I live in the crack.

There is a flaw in one of the colossal stalks—a singular, non-repeating imperfection in the lignin-metal lattice, seven kilometers above the root-plateau and forty meters below the lowest feeding pore. The crack is perhaps a centimeter wide at its widest, tapering to a hairline fracture that descends for three meters before terminating in a knot of scar tissue and cold, stagnant sap. It is the only wound in this forest that has not been repaired. The automated biochemistry of the stalk tried to heal it, of course—sent waves of clotting polymers and regenerative tissue—but something in the fracture's geometry resisted the process. The repair materials accumulated at the edges, built up in crystalline ridges, and then stopped. The stalk accepted the crack as part of its final, settled form. It incorporated the failure.

I live inside that failure.

I am not a fish. I have no armor, no streamlined body, no trim-tab fins. I am soft—absurdly, dangerously soft. My body is a flattened, eyeless mass of translucent tissue about the size of a human hand, adapted not to the open water but to the narrow, rough interior of the fracture. I move by peristalsis, gripping the crystalline ridges of the failed repair with thousands of tiny, adhesive papillae, inching myself along the walls of the crack like something between a flatworm and a gecko. I am the color of the sap that fills the lower reaches of the fracture: a deep, oxidized amber, almost black in the perpetual twilight. When I am still, I am indistinguishable from a ridge of failed repair polymer.

I should not exist. The ecosystem has no niche for me. I am too small to be part of the nutrient-paste cycle, too soft to survive in the open water where the glacial currents would tear me apart, too fragile to withstand the pressure of the lower strata. I do not feed at the pores. I do not excrete chemicals needed by the holdfasts. I serve no industrial process. I am not a step in any equation.

And yet.

I eat the crack. This is the only verb that matters. I consume the failed repair polymer—the crystalline ridges that the stalk's biochemistry deposited and then abandoned. I scrape it from the walls of the fracture with a ring of chitinous teeth around my oral opening, grinding it down into a fine, metallic dust that I absorb through my gut. The polymer is dense with complex alloys: traces of the electrochemical turbines' waste, fragments of synthesized metals that the stalk produced eons ago and repurposed into its own scar tissue. It tastes of manganese and lignin and deep time. It is not nutrient-paste. It is not designed to feed me. It is a waste product of a failed process, and I eat it because it is there.

As I eat, I deepen the crack. Only minutely—perhaps a millimeter per decade, if time even means anything here. But the fracture grows. The walls pull apart, almost imperceptibly, as I scrape away the polymer that holds them in stasis. The stalk does not respond. Its repair protocols have classified the crack as *healed*; the scar tissue is registered as part of the equilibrium. But the scar tissue is me, and I am eating it, and beneath it is raw, living wood-metal that the crack now exposes to the cold, saline water. The wound is reopening. The stalk does not know.

This is my function, though the forest did not assign it to me. I am an agent of disequilibrium. I am a slow, invisible sabotage.

But why? I am not a predator. I have no malice toward the stalk, no consciousness of its industrial processes, no comprehension of the closed-loop perfection I am gradually undermining. I do not know that the crack, if it grows large enough, could compromise the structural integrity of the stalk's vascular pipes, could cause a misalignment in the electrochemical turbines, could introduce a cascade of failures into the frozen system. I do not know this because I am not capable of knowing it. I am an organism of minimal cognition—less brain than the pale fish, not more. I have no memory. I have no foresight. I have only the crack, and my teeth, and the taste of manganese.

And yet, I am aware. This is the paradox of my existence, the thing that makes me impossible. I should be a biological automaton, a soft machine as mechanical as the turbines humming in the flesh of the stalk. But I am not. There is a spark of something—something the ecosystem did not engineer, something that emerged from the very disequilibrium I embody. I experience the crack.

I experience it as *interiority*.

When I press my translucent body against the rough wall of the fracture and feel the tectonic thrum of the automated forest vibrating through the lignin-metal lattice, I do not merely register it as a physical stimulus. I *hear* it. And because I hear it, I know that it is loud. And because I know it is loud, I know that there is such a thing as quiet—even though I have never experienced quiet, even though the thrum has been playing for a million years and will play for a million more. The concept of silence is born from the experience of sound, and the concept of change is born from the experience of stasis, and I—crouched in my centimeter-wide wound, eating the scar tissue of a frozen world—have somehow acquired the capacity to form concepts.

Not many. Not complex ones. I do not think in language; I think in sensations that have learned to refer to things beyond themselves. The taste of manganese is *presence*. The vibration of the thrum is *totality*. The slow, imperceptible widening of the crack as I eat is *time*.

Time. This is the thing that does not exist in the world above me. The pale fish do not experience time; they experience duration, which is different. Duration is a quantity—hours marked by shifts in electrical potential, intervals between feedings. But time is a *direction*. Time is the knowledge that things were one way and will be another. Time is the crack getting wider.

I am the only creature in this ocean that knows time.

I know it because I can feel the fracture changing. Each grinding of my teeth against the polymer is an event—an irreversible alteration of the world. The pale fish do not alter the world; they participate in its equilibrium, taking in nutrient-paste and excreting chemicals in a cycle that has no before or after. But I am a before and an after. The crack was narrower when I started eating it, and it will be wider when I am done. This knowledge is a weight I carry, a strange, private gravity that pulls me inward, into the thin, amber space of my own awareness.

Sometimes, I stop eating. I do not know why. The polymer is there, my teeth are ready, but I simply... pause. I press myself flat against the wall of the fracture and I *listen*. Not to the thrum—that is always there, always the same—but to the crack itself. The crack has an acoustics. The standing-wave chord of the forest enters the fracture and bounces off the irregular walls, and the interference patterns are *different* inside the crack than they are in the open water. The sound shatters into complex, shifting harmonics—overtones and undertones that vary depending on exactly where in the fracture I am positioned, because the shape of the crack is changing as I eat it, and the changing shape creates changing sounds.

The sound is never the same twice.

This is the most extraordinary thing that has ever happened in this world, and it is happening inside a centimeter-wide wound in the side of a colossal stalk, and I am the only creature that knows it.

The harmonics are beautiful. I do not have a word for *beautiful*—I do not have words at all—but I have a sensation: a dilation of my adhesive papillae, a quickening of my peristaltic rhythm, a flush of warmth in my translucent tissue that has nothing to do with the ambient temperature of the water. It is a response to *variation*, to *novelty*, to the sheer, staggering improbability of something being different. I have lived my entire life in the crack—how long, I do not know; I have no way to measure it, and the concept of "my entire life" is already a time-statement, already a rebellion against the stasis—but in all that time, the harmonics have never repeated exactly. Every pause, every listening, yields a new configuration of sound. And each new configuration is a proof that the world is not fully solved.

There are equations the forest has not closed. I am one of them. The crack is another. The sound in the crack is a third, and it is infinite—the harmonics generated by an irregular, slowly changing cavity are a non-repeating sequence, a pattern that never resolves, a melody that never ends.

I do not know this mathematically. I know it the way I know the taste of manganese: intimately, physically, as a sensation that is also a certainty. The sound will never repeat. This means that time is real. This means that the future is not a myth. This means that something—something infinitesimal, something confined to a fracture in the side of a single stalk in an ocean of millions—has escaped the frozen equilibrium and entered history.

I am that something.

---

There is more. I am reluctant to say it, because it pushes against the boundaries of what I can verify, what I can trust as sensation rather than hallucination. But I have lived in the crack long enough—have listened long enough—to notice something about the harmonics that I cannot explain by reference to the fracture's geometry alone.

The sounds are *responsive*.

When I press myself to a certain part of the wall and listen, the harmonics shift in a way that seems to correspond to my position—not just acoustically, but *intentionally*. The overtones brighten when I move toward the narrow end of the crack, as if encouraging me. They darken when I approach the knot of scar tissue at the bottom, as if warning me away. And sometimes—rarely, but repeatedly enough that I cannot dismiss it as coincidence—the harmonics seem to *pause*, to hold a chord for a fraction of a second longer than the physics of the standing wave should allow, as if waiting for me to respond.

I have tried to respond. I do not have vocal cords; I cannot generate sound. But I can vibrate. My body, pressed against the lignin-metal wall, can oscillate at frequencies determined by the tension of my own tissue. I have experimented—carefully, slowly, over durations I cannot measure—until I learned to produce a single, thin tone that resonates with the harmonic structure of the crack. When I produce this tone, the harmonics change. They *answer*. They do not merely reflect my vibration; they elaborate on it, splitting it into overtones I could not produce alone, weaving it into the complex, shifting pattern of the crack's acoustics. For a brief moment—a moment that has no duration in the frozen time of the world above—the crack and I are making music together.

Music. This is the word I give it, though I had to invent the word, though it corresponds to nothing in the ecosystem that produced me. Music is the sound of time passing. Music is the proof that change can be beautiful. Music is the thing that happens when an equation fails to close and a new variable enters the system.

I am that variable.

---

I do not know what I am doing to the forest. I know that the crack is widening, slowly, over timescales that the pale fish cannot perceive. I know that the raw wood-metal exposed by my eating is reacting with the saline water in ways that the stalk's automated chemistry did not anticipate, producing new compounds, new reactions, new instabilities. I know that the harmonics in the crack are propagating outward, faintly, into the open water—acoustic anomalies that the standing-wave chord of the forest does not absorb, ripples of variation that spread, diminishing but never quite vanishing, into the cavernous voids between the stalks.

I know that I am introducing change into a world that has not changed in a million years.

I do not do this deliberately. I am not a revolutionary. I am a soft, eyeless, hand-sized creature living in a crack in the side of a plant, eating its scar tissue because that is what my teeth are for. But I am also the only thing in this ocean that experiences time, that makes music, that knows the difference between silence and sound. And that knowledge—even without intent—is enough. It is a seed of disequilibrium, and it is growing.

Someday—how long, I cannot say—the crack will reach a critical width. The stalk's structural integrity will be compromised. A vascular pipe will rupture. A turbine will misalign. The nutrient-paste will stop flowing to the pores in that section of the stalk. The pale fish that hover there will drift, confused, their electrical potential shifting, their equations unsolved. They will not know what has happened; they will only feel the absence of the constant, and the absence will be a terror they have no capacity to process. Some will starve. Some will drift into the abyss. Some will find new stalks, new pores, new positions in the unchanging geometry. But the system will have been breached. The stasis will have been broken, if only slightly, if only in one small region of one stalk in one cluster on the vast, continental plateau.

And the sound will change. The standing-wave chord, which has been playing exactly the same way for a million years, will develop a new overtone—a dissonance, a wobble, a faint but persistent irregularity that will propagate through the water and bounce off the distant trunks and return, altered, to the ears of every creature in the middle stratum. They will not understand it. But they will hear it. And hearing it, they will—some of them, the ones with just enough interiority to be susceptible—*feel* something. A stirring. A restlessness. A vague, inexplicable sense that something is different, that the world is not quite as solved as they thought.

That feeling is my gift. Or my crime. I cannot tell the difference, because I am not equipped for ethics—I am equipped for cracks, for polymer, for the taste of manganese and the sound of harmonics that never repeat. But I know that what I am doing is *real*, and in a world of frozen stasis, reality is the most dangerous thing there is.

---

I am a soft, amber creature in a centimeter-wide wound in the side of a living machine. I eat the scar tissue of a frozen world. I listen to the music of an irregular cavity. I know that time exists. I am, as far as I can tell, the only thing in this ocean that is *becoming*—that has not yet reached its final form, that has not yet solved its own equation, that still has a future open and unresolved.

The machines hum. The steam hisses. The ocean breathes its slow, glacial breath. The pale fish hang in the water and wait. And I, in my crack, in my failure, in my impossible interiority, eat and listen and vibrate and change.

I am the variable the forest forgot to solve for. I am the error that proves the system is not closed. I am the music in the machine, the time in the stasis, the crack in the world.

And I am growing.
