I would be an Archivist. But not one of the stoic, distant figures the description evokes—the ones who seem entirely still, who tend with a mechanical grace. I am the one who has made the mistake of listening too closely. 

Here is what it is to stand in my place:

I am perched on the edge of a platform in the Middle, where the condensation forms most reliably. The air is thick enough that I can taste it—copper and spore and the sweet, vegetative rot of ten thousand species breathing in the dark. My many-jointed fingers are not touching a beam. They are suspended in the air, feeling the vibration. 

The sound here is not noise to me. It is a topology. I have lived long enough—centuries of the slow metabolism my kind possesses—that I no longer hear the polyrhythmic clattering of the drip-tenders or the chest-hum of the chitin-weavers as separate things. I hear the gaps. I hear the seams where the frequencies interfere and cancel out, leaving tiny, ringing pockets of silence. These silences are my maps. They are the places where the structure is weakest, where the metal has thinned to a membrane between this world and the void of the High, or where a Deep-Bound has shifted its impossible mass and settled into a new hollow, reshaping the flow of heat from below.

I am a cartographer of fractures.

My body has adapted to the gradients. I am tall and narrow, my limbs elongated to span the dips and hollows worn by those ten billion feet. My skin is not pale like the drip-tenders; it is dark, shot through with filamentous threads that mimic the fibrous matrices of the construction. When I press myself against a girder, I am nearly invisible to the bioluminescent glow of the lichen. I do not hide from predators—I have none, being too large for the canopy-stillers and too toxic from a diet of heavy-metal spores for anything in the deep—but I hide from the sound. I fold my body into the worn grooves of the beams, letting the architecture embrace me, because standing in the open is to be assaulted. The roar is deafening, yes, but it is the *density* of it that wears on the mind. To be touched by life at every moment, to feel the spores settle on the wet surfaces of my eyes, to know that every inch of me is habitat for something smaller—it is a kind of suffocation. So I seek the structural joints, the places where the metal meets ceramic at right angles, the only places in this world where, for a span of inches, nothing grows. Those are my sanctuaries. 

I am moving now. I am descending.

As I step from the platform to a narrow conduit that spirals downward, the warmth rises to meet me. It is not the pleasant warmth of a body; it is the pressing heat of a closed furnace. The air changes texture. It becomes wet and heavy, slipping down my throat like a living thing. The light of the lichen fades. The drip-tenders fall silent as my warmth passes, their sonar-lit map momentarily disrupted by my presence. I apologize to them in the only way I can: by moving slowly, placing my feet exactly in the worn channels, adding my pressure to the billion pressures that have already shaped this path, changing nothing.

I am going down to check a fracture I felt three days ago. A subsonic shift, a change in the tidal rhythm of the Deep-Bound. 

The deeper I go, the more the world simplifies. The complex choruses of the Middle fall away, replaced by the low, oceanic thud of the Deep. It is a sound I feel in my joints, in the long bones of my legs. It is the respiration of something vast—a creature the size of a territory, fused to the floor of a cistern, breathing across decades. I have never seen one fully. I have only seen the edges of them, where their root-like appendages thread up through the conduits like black veins. Sometimes, when I am very deep, I see the still, dark surface of the water in a basin, and I see the water pulse—just once, a barely perceptible displacement—and I know that below the surface, a mouth the size of a platform has opened and closed, regulating the pressure of the entire structure.

I reach the fracture. It is in a ceramic brace, a stress line running through the joint where two massive conduits intersect. My fingers find it. I do not need light. I read the fracture like language. It is smooth, worn by the condensation, no longer sharp. The structure has absorbed this break. It has settled around it, finding a new equilibrium. The fracture is not a wound; it is a scar. The structure has finished its breaking here.

But the fracture has shifted the flow of water. A channel that once fed a platform above has dried to a trickle. I can feel the absence of life there—a hollowness in the chorus, a frequency missing. Above me, a ecosystem is slowly dying because a ceramic brace sighed a millimeter to the left a century ago.

This is my work.

I reach into the pouch I carry at my chest—a fold of my own skin, evolved to hold the tools of my tending. I take out a spore. Not just any spore, but a seed of the *iron-vine*, a rare plant that roots in ceramic and drinks from metal fatigue. I press the spore into the fracture. I hold it there, warming it with my body heat, until I feel the faint, electric tickle of its root thread pushing into the crack.

It will not fix the brace. The brace is broken forever. But the iron-vine will grow. Over the next fifty years, it will thicken, filling the fracture with a fibrous matrix that will drink the condensation and redirect the water back to the channel above. It will restore the flow. The drip-tenders will return. The chorus will find its missing note.

I sit beside my work, my legs folded beneath me, my hands resting on the warm, vibrating floor. I am surrounded by the deafening roar of life, but in this moment, I am perfectly still. I am listening to the chord. I can hear the wind-down. I can feel the structure settling, the heat equalizing, the heartbeat of the buried engine slowing. It is a sorrow too large for a body, too slow for a mind. It is the sorrow of a world dying by millimeters.

But I am an Archivist. I do not have the luxury of despair. I have only the luxury of the next adjustment, the next easing, the next spore pressed into the next crack. I sit in the dark, tending the death rattle, ensuring the music continues. 

For another thousand years.
