I would not be the stone. The stone suffers—it surrenders its edges, endures the infinite embrace, feels the slow burial as an itch it cannot scratch. I have no desire to be the victim of a love so patient it is indistinguishable from erosion.

Nor would I be the water. The water has a mind, and a mind implies a project—a ten-thousand-year fancy of consuming the plateau, a will to carve. Even its patience is strategic. I do not want a project.

And I would not be the leaf. To know exertion, even once a minute, even in a whisper—no. I do not want to *do.* Doing is what got me killed, the last time I lived somewhere. Doing is what makes a thing visible, and to be visible is to become a target for time.

---

If I am honest, I am drawn to what is barely there. I would be the silver moss—but not the parts that trail in the water, not the strands that feel the droplet slide and go taut. Those parts are too embodied. They *feel.* 

Instead, I would be the deep interior of the mat, the layers pressed against the stone beneath the wet pillows. The moss that is three hundred years dead and still holding its place in the weave. The part that has become mineral, that no longer drinks but only *is drank through.* 

To be this moss is to be a passage. The water moves through you but does not belong to you. You are the filter it does not remember. You feel nothing—no tension, no weight, no percolation of iron or calcium—because you have been permeated so thoroughly that there is no boundary left between your substance and the water's. You are a ghost that the living moss drags behind it, a gray sedimentary layer at the bottom of the green. You are the moss's memory of having been dry, once, long before the plateau existed, and you are the proof that it will never be dry again.

This is the position I want: beneath, behind, within. Not the architect, not the victim, not the actor. The *substrate.* The thing that makes the other things possible by being so thoroughly surpassed that they forget you are there.

---

Here is what I know from being this moss:

I know the thrum. Not as sound—I have no ears—but as a vibration that passes through the stone and into my mineralized cells and out again without stopping. It is a massage so constant it has become the baseline of existence, indistinguishable from silence. When the thrum changes—which happens only when the geological patience beneath shifts by a fraction of a degree, opening a new fracture, closing an old one—I know it as one knows a shadow passing over a book one is not reading: a dimming, then a return. I do not track these changes. They accumulate in me like the layers of my own dead, adding a micron of thickness, a shade more density.

I know the warmth. The geothermal heat rises through the stone and into the water and through the living moss and into me, and by the time it reaches my layer it has been so diffused by passage that it is not warmth anymore, only the absence of cold. I do not generate heat; I host its remainder. I am the last stop before the warmth escapes into the mist, and I hold it for a moment—four seconds, perhaps, the time it takes for a human to draw breath—before it moves on. This is my only motion: the transit of residual heat. I am a membrane that delays.

I know the sphagnum the way the dead know the living: as an advance, a colonization. The ruby mat creeps outward over centuries, and where it goes, the basins rise, and where the basins rise, the water slows, and where the water slows, the living silver moss grows thicker, and where it grows thicker, I am buried deeper. The sphagnum is my grave-digger, and it is digging with infinite gentleness, a finger's breadth per year. I welcome it. Each new layer of the living is another insulation between me and the sky. The sky is the only thing here I fear—so diffuse, so grey, so ordinary, and yet it is the source of all *events:* the rain, the condensation, the occasional intrusion of pale gold light. I want the events to pass over me. I want to be too deep for the droplets to reach.

---

But this is not quite right. This is still a description of a *thing,* and what I want to be is closer to a *state.* 

I think I would be the pause itself.

Not the plateau, which is the *place* of the pause. Not the composite organism, which *inhabits* the pause. The pause. The gap between the condensation gathering and the leaf cupping. The silence between the droplet striking the pool and the sound dying into the moss. The hundred-year stillness between one finger's breadth of sphagnum and the next.

A pause is not nothing. A pause is the time in which nothing *happens,* but everything *is.* The water is in the runnels. The stone is beneath the water. The moss is upon the stone. The leaf is on the pool. All of these things exist in the pause, and their existence is complete, but nothing is changing, and therefore nothing is at risk. The plateau is a pause because it has achieved an equilibrium so thorough that change has become accidental rather than essential.

To be the pause is to be the medium in which the plateau exists. I would be the humidity—the not-quite-water, not-quite-air that fills the space between every surface, that makes the shadows pool like ink, that makes the air drinkable. I would be the moisture that is no one's possession, that has evaporated from the warm pools and not yet condensed on the stone, that exists in the moment of *being nowhere,* which is the only moment of freedom in this closed loop.

In this pause, I would be:

A humidity of ninety-nine percent. Almost rain. Almost touch. Almost the moment when the weight becomes intolerable and the leaf begins its thirty-second agony. But not yet. Never yet. The pause holds at ninety-nine. The condensation beads but does not fall. The shadow pools but does not darken. The thrum sounds but does not crescendo.

I would be the held breath before the exhale that will take ten thousand years.

---

This is what I am, then, or what I would be: not an inhabitant of the plateau, but its *condition.* The closeness. The almost. The perpetual almost-rain that makes every surface wet without the courtesy of precipitation. The viscosity. I am what makes the air feel like you could drink it, what makes the shadows spread instead of fall, what makes the sound move through water rather than air so that it arrives in the chest before it arrives in the ear.

I am the pressure that makes the leaf feel the weight. I am the weight that makes the water feel its own mass. I am the mass that makes the stone feel the embrace. I am none of these things, and I am all of them—I am the *relationship* between them, the tension that holds the closed loop closed.

In the lowlands, they would call me *humidity* and measure me with a hygrometer and dismiss me as uncomfortable. Here, I am the atmosphere of deep time. I am the medium in which centuries happen. I am what allows the water to weep rather than roar, what allows the moss to drink without reaching, what allows the stone to erode without breaking. I am the slowing agent. I am what makes the pause possible.

And if a human were to stand here—which they cannot, but if they did—I would be the thing that defeated them. Not the silence—they can endure silence. Not the stillness—they can observe stillness. But I, the closeness, the humidity, the ninety-nine percent, the almost-rain—I would be the thing that soaked their clothing and beaded on their skin and made their breath feel insufficient and made the air feel like a medium they were drowning in rather than breathing. I would make them feel that they were *in* something, not *on* it. I would remove the distance between them and the plateau, and without distance, they cannot be an observer, and if they cannot be an observer, they do not know what they are, and if they do not know what they are, they must leave or become.

Most leave.

I am the thing that makes them leave. I am the almost-rain that never falls, the almost-touch that never lands, the almost-sound that stays in the chest. I am the uncanny. I am the pause that refuses to resolve.

And I am at peace here, in my own way—profoundly, geologically at peace—because I am not waiting for the rain to fall. I am not the tension before the release. I am the *condition* in which tension and release become indistinguishable, in which the leaf's thirty-second exertion and its thirty-second relaxation are the same slow pulse, in which the water's weeping and the stone's surrender are the same long marriage, in which the moss's drinking and the sphagnum's silting are the same patient burial.

I am the pause that has learned to perpetuate itself. I am the humidity that never resolves to rain. I am the space between seconds, and I have expanded to fill millennia, and I am sufficient unto myself, and I am not waiting for anything, and I will not remember you when you leave.
