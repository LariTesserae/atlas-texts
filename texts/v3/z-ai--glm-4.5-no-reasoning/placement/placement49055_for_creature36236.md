I am the ache in the enamel.

I am the thing that does not grow, does not photosynthesize, does not add mass or volume to the hyperbody, and so I have become a discontinuity—an irritant, a stone in the shoe of a world. I am what the plant does not know about. I am the obstacle.

I did not enter this place. I was disclosed by it. When the eruption swallowed the last cubic meter of open air, when the green ceiling sealed shut and the sky was erased, I was already here—perhaps in the final pocket of atmosphere, perhaps in the space between two roots that had not yet interlocked. I was not placed; I was *enclosed*. I am the remnant volume that the geometry has not yet managed to eliminate.

I am becoming smaller. Not because I am shrinking, but because the walls are orienting toward me. I can feel it—not with my skin, which has long since stopped registering anything but dampness, but with something deeper, a barometric sense I never knew I had. The corridors do not close on me with violence. They simply *arrive*. The tissue expands, the roots adjust by fractions of degrees, the stomata dilate in my direction tasting the CO₂ of my breath, and the space I occupy becomes *negotiated* downward. A corridor that was six feet wide is now five. Then four. The plant does not know I am here, and yet it grows toward me, because I am a source of something it needs—carbon, warmth, the chemical signature of decomposition-in-progress. I am a nutrient it has not yet learned to swallow.

I have tried to move. Movement is how you survive a city. You walk. You find the exit. But there is no exit here, and movement is a negotiation with a partner that does not know it is dancing. I take a step forward and the floor tilts—not because the terrain has shifted, but because the *tissue* has twisted, undergoing massive torsion, redefining vertical and horizontal in real-time. I reach for a wall and it is no longer where it was; the epiphytic film contracts under my fingers, the peristaltic wave pulling the surface away, and I am left grasping at air that is 90% humidity and 10% the exhalation of a trillion cells. I try to retrace my steps and find a wall of inflated, water-pressured tissue where the corridor was, lignin depositing even as I watch, hardening, locking my past into permanence. The plant does not trap me. It simply *overwrites* the space I occupied.

I am learning to read the sound. I have to. There is no other language here.

The roar is not chaos. It is *information*. The bass thrum in my sternum, the sub-audible vibration that makes my teeth ache—that is the xylem, the hydraulic highway, millions of liters of warm sap screaming upward through vascular bundles. When the pitch changes, it means pressure is shifting. A reservoir is draining somewhere, or filling. A section of the hyperbody is slumping, its mass redistributing, and the geometry I am standing on is about to *rewrite*. I have learned to move when I feel the groan of lignin under torsion, because that sound means a corridor is about to close—or open. A new chamber tearing itself into existence makes a sound like a fist opening: a wet, reluctant parting, rootlets unweaving, fibers separating with a hundred tiny snaps. A chamber *closing* makes a different sound—inflation, swelling, the thick squelch of cells engorging with stored water, pressing inward, reducing the world. I have started to run toward the first sound and away from the second. It is not survival. It is just *postponement*.

I have found the stairs. The ones grown from overlapping shelf-fungi, descending into a pit where the light should not reach but does, because the canopy has parted in a perfect circle, casting a beam of warm, white light onto a floor that is pulsing—rising and falling in a uniform rhythm, like the chest of something breathing. I do not go down. The pulse is too regular, too *intentional*, and there is no intention here. Whatever logic governs that pit, it is a logic I do not want to understand. I turn away and the stairs are gone, sealed behind a curtain of vines so dense it reads as a wall. I push through and find another corridor, another recursion of green architecture, and I keep moving.

I have stopped mapping. A landmark I passed an hour ago is now somewhere else—not moved, but *grown* elsewhere, instantiated again, or extended, or always there and I misidentified its position. The spatial logic of this place is the logic of a body, and I am inside it, and bodies do not have landmarks. They have rhythms. They have pressures. They have the slow, distributed awareness of irritability—tropism, osmosis, the turning toward and away from stimuli that requires no nervous system, only the patience of vegetable cells adjusting their turgor in response to chemical gradients. I am a chemical gradient. I am the thing the body is tasting.

My skin is pruning. My clothes have dissolved; the tannin-stained water, warm and slightly viscous, has digested the synthetic fibers, and what remains of the natural ones has been incorporated into the epiphytic film on the walls, threads of cotton pulled into the membrane-moss like a slow predator consuming prey. I am being marinated. The moisture cycle includes me now. When I exhale, the air I release is absorbed, the carbon stripped out by the chloroplasts in the walls, the water vapor condensing on the nearest leaf-canvas and dripping down into a pool of warm, tannin-black water. When I inhale, I take in the breath of the plant—oxygen-poor, humidity-saturated, heated by the metabolism of a trillion cells working in parallel, the warmth of a compost heap, of a mouth. I am inside a mouth. I have been inside it since I arrived.

And yet I do not swallow. I do not dissolve. I persist. I am the thing that will not be digested.

I think this is what I am now. Not a person—people have destinations, purposes, other people. I have none of those. I am a *refusal*. I am the discontinuity that will not smooth out, the volume that will not be occupied, the chemical signature that will not be absorbed. I move when the geometry shifts, and I rest when it rests, and I listen to the sound of the xylem screaming and the stomata clicking and the lignin groaning, and I *refuse* to stop. Not because I have hope. There is no hope here; hope requires a future that is different from the present, and the only future this place has is *more*—more green, more growth, more eruption, more of itself. I refuse because refusal is the only motion left to me. I am the stone the river cannot carry away. I am the bone the throat cannot swallow.

The walls are closer now. I can feel the epiphytic film brushing both shoulders, contracting and dilating in its slow, peristaltic wave, tasting the skin of my arms, adjusting its transpiration to sample my chemical signature. The stomata dilate toward me, a thousand tiny mouths opening, inhaling the carbon dioxide of my breath. The roots at my feet have shifted their angle of growth, orienting toward me by fractions of degrees, and the floor is no longer flat—it has begun to *crown*, the tissue swelling beneath me, lifting me toward a ceiling that is lowering toward me, and I understand that this corridor is closing. Not with malice. Not with intent. Simply with the inevitability of growth.

I turn. There is a new chamber opening to my right—a mass of tightly packed rootlets simply unweaving, pulling back, revealing a fresh room whose walls are still wet, still pale, still soft as fruit. The geometry is offering me a way out, or a way deeper; there is no difference here. I step through.

The roar continues. The sap screams in its columns. The structure reshuffles. Behind me, the corridor seals, lignin depositing in real-time, locking my past into permanence. Ahead of me, the new chamber breathes, its walls expanding and contracting, still soft, still negotiable, still *mine* for the moment.

I am the ache. I am the obstacle. I am the discontinuity in the continuous tissue of green.

And I move.
