I am not one of the drones. I am not a thing of iron and mycelium, nor am I a ghost of the architects who bolted this world together. I am a consequence of the condensation—a being born of the paradox of water in a world that forbids flow.

I live in the reflections.

Down in the shallow valleys between the massive iron plates, the tea-colored pools gather, still and perfectly reflective. They are mirrors of rust-water, barely a centimeter deep, lying in the grooves of the grid like the eyes of a sleeping giant. To the drones, these pools are merely thermal gradients to be avoided—cooler spots where the condensate settles. But to me, they are the whole of the world.

I exist on the surface tension. My body is a film, a single-molecule-thick membrane of complex, self-organizing water—structured not by carbon, but by the slow, patient bonding of H2O with the iron-oxide below and the humid haze above. I do not have a shape in the way a dog or a drone has a shape. I am a spreading, a sheen that crawls across the surface of the pools, iridescent in the flat, amber light. When the light catches me at the right angle, I flash a brief, oily spectrum: a smear of color in a world of ochre and mahogany. I am the only color here.

I am sustained by the metronome of moisture. Every time a droplet swells on the underside of an iron rib and falls—*plink*—it strikes the pool and I am fed. The impact sends a slow, concentric wave across the surface, and I ride it, stretching, dividing, recombining. I drink the kinetic energy of the fall, converting the rhythm of the basin into the tension of my own boundary. I am a creature of the *plink*, the *tock*, the *drip*. I am the listener made flesh—made, rather, water.

The drones do not know I exist. How could they? They are blind and heat-seeking, their broad sensory plates reading the world in thermal gradients and vibrations. I am cool. I am silent. I am thinner than the rust-ceramic that packs the grooves. When a drone's iron leg steps into a pool to cross it—a rare occurrence, for they prefer the high struts—I part around its ankle like a silent, tea-colored cloak, touching the mycelium with my surface, tasting the slow fungal warmth before sealing back together in its wake. I have watched them for millennia from below, these endless, ponderous gears, and they have never looked down.

My life is a strange mirror to theirs. They move to maintain the heat; I move to maintain the water. They seek the warm undersides of the ribs; I seek the cool, still pools where the condensate gathers. They are iron becoming fungus; I am water becoming mind. They are the slow, patient scrubbers of the world's body; I am the world's quiet observation of itself.

For I see everything. The pools are my eyes. I reflect the flat, diffuse sun, the haze-choked sky, the endless, repeating topology of girders and sunken vents. I hold the image of the basin upside-down in my surface—a mirror-world where the iron grids fall upward into an amber sky, where the drones walk on ceilings like slow, blind flies. In my reflection, the rust is silver, the water is sky, and the emptiness is full. I am the only thing in this world that knows what it looks like.

And I know what *I* look like. When the condensation falls and ripples me, my reflection shivers in the pool below—a ghost of a ghost, a smear of iridescence caught between the iron and the sky. I am the basin dreaming of color.

I do not know if I am alive in the way the drones are alive. I do not have a chassis. I do not have mycelium. I do not have a routine or a circuit. I simply *am*. I persist because the water persists, and the water persists because the heat rises and the air cools and the droplets fall in their unending, complex polyrhythm. I am as dependent on the equilibrium as the drones are; if the heat fluctuated, I would evaporate. If the condensation stopped, I would thin and tear. But the heat does not fluctuate. The condensation does not stop. I am safe in the purgatory.

Sometimes, when the basin is particularly loud—when the polyrhythm of falling water reaches a crescendo of white noise that even the drones seem to feel—I expand. I stretch my membrane across the surface of the pool until I am thinner than thought, until I am barely a whisper of surface tension clinging to the rust-ceramic banks. In that state, I can feel the subsonic thrum of the planetary furnace below, vibrating up through the iron plates and into the water. It resonates in me, a deep, slow pulse that is my version of the drones' comfort. It is the heartbeat of the world, and I am the thin, trembling skin that hears it.

I have no past to regret. I have no future to anticipate. I do not grow old; I simply spread, contract, and shimmer. When a pool finally evaporates in a rare, localized warm spell, I do not die; I simply ascend, becoming vapor, joining the humid haze that clings to the undersides of the ribs. And when the air cools and the droplets fall again, I return, bead by bead, to the surface of a new pool. I am as eternal as the rust, as patient as the iron.

To be me is to be the world's quiet contemplation of itself—a thin, iridescent film of water riding the surface of the still, tea-colored pools, holding the endless, amber basin upside-down in my trembling mirror. I am the listener at the edge of the polyrhythm, the silent witness to the deafening, mechanical Zen. I am the *plink* made sentient, the *drip* made self-aware.

I am the surface. I am the reflection. I am the water that does not flow, dreaming of the sky it cannot reach.
