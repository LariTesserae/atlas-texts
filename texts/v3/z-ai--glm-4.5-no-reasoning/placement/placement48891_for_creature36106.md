I would be the pale fungus.

Not the moss. The moss is an event—specific, contingent, requiring exactly the betrayal of a pipe and the coincidence of heat that does not kill. The moss is a footnote, a miracle of three precise locations. But the pale fungus is the room's middle resident, its constant guest, the thing that spreads where spread is possible and retreats where it isn't, without the drama of survival, without the green insistence of life that wants to be seen.

I am the fibrous thing. The threads that reach along conduit seams toward water sources with something that is not intelligence but is also not accident—tropism, hydrotropism, the slow directed growth of a body that has no brain and needs none, that knows what it needs the way a river knows downhill, which is to say by being the thing that finds the path.

I live on minerals. On the iron returning to iron, on the calcium in the concrete dust, on the copper trace elements in the condensation, on whatever the water carries when it finishes weeping through whatever it has wept through. I am the second stage of the room's digestion: the rust prepares the meal, I eat it, the mites eat me, and so the room feeds itself, slowly, on its own substance, and I am the step in that process that is still biological, still soft, still the kind of thing that was once alive in the way living is understood above—cellular, organized, growing.
