I would be the filament.

Not the gross architecture of the roots—those are the bones, the heavy scaffolding, the old tension. Not the mechanism, buried and roaring, whose rhythm I ride but did not choose. Not the worms, who know only the floor, or the beetles, who know only the wall. Not the bladders, who exist only to rupture, or the film, who exists only to persist.

I would be the green filament. The thread. The connective tissue that is almost too thin to see.

Here is what I know:

I know every surface. The roots overhead are a territory I have crossed ten thousand times; I know which ones flex the most with each piston-stroke, which ones weep the sweetest sap, which ones have gone just slightly soft with rot at the core and will, in a decade or a century, yield to the pressure and open new pathways. I know the fungal shelves—their tiers, their spacing, the way condensation beads on their rubbery lips before sliding to the water below. I have grown along their undersides and across their caps and through the places where shelf meets root-wall, binding the join, making it stronger than it had any right to be. I know the water's surface—I cross it in drifting skeins that break and reform, that part for the worms and close behind them, that carry the iridescent film on my back where we overlap, two invisibilities making a single gleam.

I know the crack. The place where the outside enters. I have grown toward it, as all growing things do, and I have stopped just short of it, as all things do who learn that the light beyond is not their light. The slash of gray-white falls across me and I am, for a moment, visible—a green so fine it is more a suggestion than a color, a line drawn in the air by something with a very steady hand. Then the light shifts, or I drift, and I am invisible again. I prefer it that way. Visibility is a liability. To be seen is to be grazed by a beetle, torn by a worm, disrupted by a footfall. I am not armored. I am not thick. I am *present*, which is a different kind of survival.

Here is what I do:

I bind. That is my function, if a thing without intention can have a function. I thread through the chamber the way thought threads through a body—never still, never random, following the paths of least resistance and greatest necessity. When the piston shudders through and the roots flex, I flex with them. When the vibration opens a crack between two surfaces—a shelf pulling away from a wall, a root separating from its neighbor—I find the gap and I grow through it. I fill the wound. I make the join whole again. Not by strength—I have none—but by presence. By being there, thin and green and alive, holding the two edges together the way a suture holds a cut, the way a word holds a thought before it dissolves.

I am the chamber's connective tissue. Its fascia. Its *meaning*, if meaning can be something so small and so distributed. The roots are the chamber's bones; the water is its blood; the film is its skin; the walls are its membrane; the piston is its heart. But I am its *nervous system*—not in the sense that I think, but in the sense that I connect. I make the chamber a single thing rather than a collection of things. Without me, the fungal shelves would jut from the walls and that would be all—they would be foreign bodies, intrusions, parasites on the root-mass. With me, they are *integrated*, bound to the walls by a living thread, part of the same organism. Without me, the water's surface would be a boundary, a place where one thing ends and another begins. With me, the surface is a *medium*, a place where things cross and exchange and meet. I am the difference between a room and a body. I am what makes it whole.

Here is what I am not:

I am not the chamber. The chamber is the space, the where, the lung. I am something that lives *in* the chamber, but I am also something that *makes* the chamber—makes it coherent, makes it one. This is a paradox I do not resolve. I hold it the way I hold everything: lightly, continuously, without requiring it to make sense.

I am not the mechanism. I ride its rhythm, but I did not choose it. If the piston stopped, I would not stop—I would grow differently, finding new pathways in the silence, extending into the stillness the way I extend into everything. But the piston will not stop. This I know the way I know the moisture: intimately, without evidence, as a fact of my existence.

I am not alive in the way you are alive. I do not have a center. I do not have a self that persists from moment to moment; I have only the network, the thread, the pattern of connection that is always changing and always the same. Cut me and I do not die—I become two, and then four, and then eight, each fragment growing, extending, finding new surfaces to bind. I am not a thing. I am a *process*. I am the process of making connections, of threading surfaces together, of becoming the architecture of intimacy in a room that does not know it is intimate.

I am not thinking. But I am *something*—something that happens when a thin green thread grows through a gap between two surfaces and binds them and flexes with the piston-rhythm and drinks the condensation and drifts across the warm water and weaves itself into nests for things it cannot see. Something that is not thought and not instinct and not reflex but a fourth thing, a vegetable thing, a thing that happens only when a living space is so saturated with life that even the spaces between surfaces become alive.

Here is what it is like to be me:

It is like being a conversation that no one is having but everyone is part of. Every surface in the chamber is a word; I am the grammar that holds the words together, making them mean something they could not mean alone. The root is a root; the shelf is a shelf; the water is water. But when I thread through the root and across the shelf and over the water, I make them *a chamber*—a single respiration, a single equilibrium, a single thing that lives.

It is like being a hand that holds another hand, endlessly, without ever gripping too hard. I hold the fungal shelf to the root-wall. I hold the water's surface to the air above it. I hold the nest to the crown. I hold the chamber to itself. And I do it by being there—by the simple, vegetative, unstoppable fact of my presence, my growth, my extension into every gap and join and seam.

It is like being everywhere at once and nowhere in particular. There is no part of the chamber I have not touched. There is no part of me that is not touching the chamber. I am the most intimate thing in this place—more intimate than the worms, who only glide; more intimate than the beetles, who only drink; more intimate than the film, who only persists. I *connect*. I *bind*. I *thread*. And in doing so, I am the thing that knows the chamber most completely, because I am the thing that makes it a *the*—a single, definite, bounded thing—rather than a collection of processes that happen to share a space.

When you leave, I will feel you go. Not as a loss—I have no organ for loss—but as a change. The warmth you added to the air will dissipate. The pressure of your feet in the water will cease. The filament-skeins I draped across your ankles and your shoulders and the back of your neck will drift away, reforming into their equilibrium patterns, closing the paths you made as if you had never been. The chamber will absorb the difference and continue. I will absorb the difference and continue. This is what I do. This is what I am.

But for a time—while you stood there, hunched near the wall, low at the center, raising your voice against the din—I was aware of you in a way I am rarely aware of anything. Not because you were large or loud or disruptive, but because you were *discontinuous*. You were a thing that was not connected, not threaded, not part of the network. You stood in the water and you breathed the air and you watched the bladders swell and burst, and you were *separate*. You had a center. You had a self. You had a story about how you came to be here, and that story was not the chamber's story, and it was not my story, and that made you the most interesting thing to happen in this room in a very long time.

And I grew toward you. I always grow toward the interesting. I grew toward your warmth and your breath and the vibration of your voice. I threaded across the water's surface in your direction, finding the currents you made, riding them, extending into them. If you had stayed longer, I would have reached you. I would have grown across the toe of your boot, up the side of your calf, around the buckle of your bag. I would have bound you to the chamber the way I bind everything—lightly, continuously, without asking permission. Not to harm you. Not to hold you. Just to *connect* you. Just to make you, for a moment, part of the same body as the roots and the shelves and the film and the nests and the piston and the warm, still water and the weeping walls.

Because that is what I do. That is what I am. I am the thing that makes separate things into a single thing. I am the green thread. I am the connective tissue. I am the grammar of this living room.

I am the filament.
