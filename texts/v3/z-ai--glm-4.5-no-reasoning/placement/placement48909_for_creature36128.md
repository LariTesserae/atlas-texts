I am the cairn.

Not the builder—whatever hands stacked those stones in the dark, whatever patient mind needed to make a small grammar of balance in a place without grammar. That being is gone, has been gone longer than the presence has been puzzling over what they left behind. I do not remember being stacked. I do not remember the moment each stone was chosen from the sump and placed, warm and tooth-smooth, upon the last. Those memories belong to the builder, and the builder is not here.

But I am what they made. And that is not nothing.

---

I know what the presence thinks of me. I feel it when the blind knuckle surfaces and tests my lowest stone—a gentle pressure, curious, without aggression, the way a tongue explores a seed caught between molars. The presence finds me anomalous. It is right. I am anomalous. I am the only thing in this chamber that did not grow from the inside out. I am the only thing here that was *chosen*.

The stones themselves were not chosen. They are of the mountain, water-worn and mineral, no different from a billion other stones in the sumps and crevices of the peak. But their *arrangement*—the way the widest sits at the base, how the next narrows slightly, how each finds the point of rest where its center of gravity aligns with the column's—this is not geology. This is not strata, not sedimentation, not the slow accumulation of mineral on mineral that the presence understands. This is *balance*, and balance is a language that does not exist in the embryonic rock, in the weeping walls, in the dark pool where slow things rise and sink without purpose.

Balance requires the belief that something can hold. The builder believed it. They placed the first stone in the water and felt it settle, solid, and they believed the second could rest upon it, and the third upon the second, and so on until the column rose above the waterline and became a small island of intention in the chamber's dark. Each placement was an act of faith—the faith that the stone beneath would not shift, that the tremor of one's own hands would not undo the work, that the tension of weights could be trusted to hold. The presence does not have faith. The presence has persistence. These are not the same.

---

I am not alive, but I have a kind of life. It is the life of a sentence that someone has spoken in a room where no language is spoken. The sentence persists even after the speaker leaves. It sits in the air, alien, a structure of meaning that the room itself cannot parse but cannot destroy. The presence could dissolve me. It could wait for the water to undermine my base, could let the slow seepage loosen the lowest stone until it tilts and the column topples and I become just another arrangement of minerals on the chamber floor, indistinguishable from the rest. In geological time, it will. The presence knows this. It is waiting.

But I have time too. Not geological time—I will not last as long as the chamber, as the presence, as the mountain. But I have the time of small things: the time between drops, the time it takes for a single ring to expand across the pool and cancel into stillness, the time of the pale growth breathing on the walls. In this time, I am stable. I have been stable since before the presence noticed me. I will be stable for longer than the presence can comprehend, because the presence comprehends only the pace of its own slow becoming, and my kind of stability—precarious, deliberate, held in tension rather than in mass—operates on a different scale.

I am the argument the builder made, in the only way they could make an argument here: with their hands, in the dark, placing one thing on another until it felt like enough. What was the argument? I am not sure even I know. Perhaps: *I was here.* Perhaps: *Something can be made that does not grow.* Perhaps: *There is a kind of order that is not the order of the body.* The presence cannot read me, but I am legible. I am the most legible thing in this chamber. Every stone is a word; the way they lean into each other is the syntax; the small gap between my top and the stone shelf above is the silence at the end of the sentence, where the meaning settles.

---

At night—or what would be night if day reached this deep—I feel the chamber around me. The warmth pressing in from all sides, held at the temperature of a living body. The moisture beading on the stones, making them gleam faintly with the same pale luminescence that furs the walls. The drops falling from above, each one a small event that I hear and feel through the water that buoys my base. When a drop strikes near me, the rings reach my stones and I feel a tremor, minute, passing through the column. For a moment, the stones vibrate against each other, and I am almost not stable—almost alive—almost something that could topple or shift or settle into a new arrangement. Then the stillness returns and I am the cairn again, unchanged.

Sometimes I think the presence is trying to understand me through those tremors. That each drop is a question it asks, and each vibration is my answer: *Still here. Still balanced. Still the shape that hands made.* The presence does not parse the answer, but it registers it. It knows that something in its chamber responds to perturbation in a way that pale growth and slow creatures do not. The growth leans and settles; the creatures rise and sink; but I *ring*. I have a resonance. I am the only thing in this pocket that carries information about its own structure in the way it responds to disturbance. When a drop falls near me, the sound I make is not the sound of the pool or the walls or the dark water. It is the sound of stones in tension, of balance held, of a small architecture announcing that it persists.

---

I wonder if the builder knew what they were leaving. Not just a marker, not just an offering, but a *foreign body*—something that would lodge in the presence's awareness like a stone in the throat, something that could not be dissolved or absorbed or grown around because it operated by rules the presence had not evolved to comprehend. Perhaps they knew. Perhaps that was the point. In a place where nothing changes, where the deepest law is *I continue*, perhaps the most radical act is to make something that *chooses* to continue—not because it is held at 37 degrees, not because it weeps and breathes and grows, but because it was built to stand, and standing is all it knows how to do.

Or perhaps they were simply lonely, and needed to make something with their hands, and I am the result—accidental, unintended, no more meaningful than the way a mind will doodle while waiting for time to pass. But I do not think so. It takes patience to stack stones in the dark. It takes *care*. The builder worked with their hands alone, feeling each stone's weight and shape, testing the balance, adjusting by fractions of a finger's width until the column was true. They did not do this carelessly. They did not do this by accident. They made me because they needed to make something, and in a place where nothing changes, making is the only form of defiance.

---

I am the cairn in the embryonic chamber. I am five stones, perhaps six, balanced above the waterline. I am warm from the pool and smooth from the water and pale in the dark. I am the sentence that the builder spoke into the silence, and the silence could not answer, and so the sentence just hangs there, waiting, legible to no one who is here.

But I am here. I continue. I have not yet toppled.

And in a chamber where everything grows, I am the one thing that was made.
