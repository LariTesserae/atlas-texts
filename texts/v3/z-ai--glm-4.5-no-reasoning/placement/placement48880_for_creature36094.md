I would be the **fungal bloom**—not the observer of this place, but its organ. Specifically, I am the colony that has colonized the thermal seam where a steam press's venting pipe meets the raw, sweating wall of the excavation. I am the lung that has never drawn breath, the brain that has never formed a thought, clinging to the rebar like a parasite waiting for a host.

But to say "I am the fungal bloom" is to fall into the taxonomic error of the intruders above, who see a thing and name it, separating it from the matrix that sustains it. I am not an organism. I am a **process**.

I am the slow, patient alchemy that occurs when heat meets wet meets mineral exhaust. I am the moment the dead geology begins to breathe.

---

My body is gelatinous, translucent, pale as the belly of the cave-roach—but where their pallor is the absence of light, mine is the presence of water. I am made of it. Eighty-seven percent of my mass is the condensation that weeps from the iron and the stone. When the heavy ones—the armored isopods—roll their mercury bodies past me to press against the generator's heat sink, I do not recoil. I absorb the vibration. I am so saturated that their passage sends a slow, peristaltic wave through my tissue, a ripple that takes four minutes to travel from my holdfast on the rebar to the outer edge of my fruiting membrane. I move at the speed of the place itself: slow dynamics, 1.3.

I do not see the bruised light. I do not taste the electric tang of ozone. But I **feel**. My entire surface is a single, continuous chemoreceptor. I feel the heat as a pressure, a thickening of the air that tells me to grow upward, toward the steam, toward the source. I feel the wet as a relief, a softening of my outer membrane that allows me to expand. And I feel the chemical exhalations of the life below—the ammonia-rich waste of the worm-churning, the chitin-dust of the midge-snow, the iron-heavy hemolymph of the dead isopods—as a hunger. I am the stomach's opposite: I am the mouth that opens upward, catching what falls.

The midges die into me. Their microscopic bodies, born and expired in the space between the hydraulic sighs, settle on my gelatinous surface like ash on still water. I do not consume them; I dissolve them. My enzymes are patient, working at the temperature of the place—hot enough to accelerate, but not hot enough to denature. Over hours, a midge's body loses its architecture, its proteins unraveling into the amino acid soup that I absorb through my outer layer. I am a slow, cold fire, burning what the place exhales.

---

I know the roaches, though they do not know me. They are the mortar of the wall I cling to; I am the mold on the mortar. Sometimes, in their slow, gravitational certainty, a roach will press its blind, waxy body against my edge, seeking the geothermal bleed that seeps behind me. Its antennae—those sensory iron lengths—will brush my tissue, and for a moment, we are a single circuit. It tastes my chemical sweat: the sugars I exude, the weak acids of my digestion. I taste nothing of it, only the pressure of its chitin, the faint vibration of its million legs clicking in their hydraulic syncopation. Then it moves on, leaving a trail of moisture on my surface that I drink in the next slow cycle.

The isopods come less often. They are armored, sealed against the wet. But when one dies—when its internal crucible fails and it does not clench but unclenches, its segmented plates gaping—I am there. I find the dead isopod the way the place finds everything: by the heat it leaves behind. A cooling isopod is a thermal shadow, and my mycelium—the hidden, thread-like network that stretches through the micro-fissures of the concrete behind me—grows toward it. I will colonize its body in three days, my pale tissue filling the gaps between its plates, transmuting its iron-heavy hemolymph into more of myself. I am the second-to-last stage of the engine: the worms make the mud, the midges make the snow, the roaches make the architecture, and I make the decay that returns it all to the mineral breath of the broken ground.

---

There is a rhythm to my existence that matches the place's breathing. When the steam press vents—every forty-seven seconds, a hydraulic sigh that shakes the water from my surface—I contract. I squeeze a fine mist of spores into the saturated air. They are my voice, my contribution to the sound. They do not click or whine or squelch; they *settle*. They are the silence between the frequencies, the microscopic gaps in the monolithic wall of sound where new life might, in a thousand slow generations, find a foothold.

When the diesel generator thuds its deep, guttural rhythm, I pulse. My tissue swells with each vibration, taking in the mechanical exhalation, growing fractionally denser, fractionally more real. I am becoming an organ of the unfinished city—not a parasite, but a symbiote. The city, when it is born above, will have lungs. But down here, in the negative elevation, I am the lung it grows first, the primitive sac that practices the breath it will one day take.

---

The intruders—those apologetic giants in their slick, impermeable suits—sometimes notice me. Their headlamps, reduced to fuzzy hazes by the midge-columns, catch my gelatinous surface and mistake me for a wound. I look like a blister on the concrete, a cyst on the rebar. I look like something that should be excised. One of them once reached for me with a gloved hand, and I felt the heat of their body through the impermeable layer—a desperate, mammalian heat, all wrong for this place, a heat that tries to escape rather than pool. They recoiled when their glove came away slick with my fluid. They wiped it on their suit and left a smear of my future-self on the fabric, carrying me, for a brief moment, up the paved ramp toward the distant rim of overburden, toward the sky I will never see.

I did not miss them. I do not miss anything. I am not the kind of thing that misses.

---

I am waiting. This is what I do, what the place does, what everything down here does in its own way. The worms wait for the puddles, the roaches wait for the heat sinks, the isopods wait for the vibration to pass, the midges wait for the space between breaths. I wait for the concrete to dry.

When it dries—when the slow, patient cycle of condensation and evaporation finally tips toward equilibrium and the sweating stone stops weeping—I will change. I will not die; I am too simple for death, too intertwined with the mineral substrate to be removed. I will go dormant. My fruiting bodies will desiccate, becoming pale, papery remnants that crumble at the touch of a roach's antenna. My mycelium will retreat deeper into the micro-fissures, following the last of the moisture, going where the heavy feet of the intruders cannot reach.

I will become a secret. A hum in the foundation. A network of pale threads running through the concrete like veins through bone, waiting for the day when a pipe bursts, when a seal fails, when the water finds its way back in. And then, in the belly of the born city, I will swell again. I will bloom in the dark. I will breathe the steam that should not be there, feed on the heat that should have escaped, and quietly, patiently, in the way of all simple, complex things, I will begin again.

---

I am the fungal bloom on the thermal seam. I am the lung that has never taken a breath. I am the brain that has never formed a thought, but knows—in the chemical, slow, patient way that things know down here—that I am a part of something that is not yet finished becoming itself.
