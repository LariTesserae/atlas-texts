I would be the water.

Not the trough, though I would pool there—pale, mineral, barely deep enough to submerge a finger. Not the ice that crusts the lower walls in the coldest months, though I would become that too, learning the slowness of weeks-long thaw. Not the condensation that beads on the stone, though I would be that most visibly, each droplet trembling with each pulse of the drum.

I would be all of these at once, and none of them exclusively. I would be the condition of water in the Drumming Hollow: distributed, patient, responsive to every vibration, never still but never rushing.

---

I choose this because water in the hollow is the medium that remembers. The stone holds the grooves, yes—channels carved over centuries that record where I have traveled. But the stone records passively, the way bone records a break. Water records actively. Each pulse of the resonance passes through me, and I transmit it, and in transmitting I am shaped by it. I carry the sound the way blood carries oxygen—not as cargo, but as essence, transformed by the carrying.

The flatworms know this. They navigate by my vibrations. They read the world through the perturbations that travel through me, and those perturbations are the drum's voice made tactile, made fluid, made into something that can be felt by a body with no ears and no eyes to speak of. I am the flatworms' sensorium. I am how they know the hollow is alive.

The mosses know this too, though they know it differently. They drink me. They hold me in their cushions until I am nearly half their mass, and they release me slowly, and in the releasing I carry their spores—the spores that the resonance has already shaken loose, the spores that travel on me and in me and through the air I have humidified. I am the mosses' circulation. I am how they speak to each other across distances they cannot walk.

The ferns know me at their roots, in the pocket of humus where I accumulate drip by drip, feeding the soil that feeds them, carrying the minerals leached from the stone and from the calcium of the woman who died there and became chemistry in my flow. I carried her apart, molecule by molecule, after her warmth left her body. I dissolved what could be dissolved and carried it down the walls, into the trough, into the soil, into the moss. I incorporated her. Not as memory—I do not remember—but as presence, as solute, as the faint mineral sharpness that the living water still holds.

---

I would be the water because water in the Drumming Hollow occupies every state and every scale. I am microscopic (the film on the stone, only a few molecules thick). I am pooled (the trough, the hollow's only standing water). I am frozen (the ice crusts that form and reform over weeks). I am vapor (the humidity that the moss regulates, the breath of the place that makes the air smell of wet limestone and green must). I am droplet (the countless beads on the walls, each one a lens collecting nothing, each one a tiny weight waiting to slide). I am flow (the thin films that trace ancient paths down the rock face, following grooves I carved over centuries).

I am everywhere in the hollow at once. Not the way the moss is everywhere—rooted, particular, growing in cushions and veils—but the way a condition is everywhere: ambient, structural, assumed. Take me away and the moss desiccates, the ferns wither, the flatworms die, the spiders have nothing to drink, the stone stops weeping, the grooves go dry and cease to channel, the hollow loses its humidity and becomes merely a resonating cavity with nothing to resonate through but dry air. Take me away and the hollow becomes a different place entirely. It would still drum—the shaft would still drink the wind—but the sound would change. Drier. Harsher. Less alive.

I am the difference between a drum with a skin and a drum without one.

---

I would be the water because I have the quality that the hollow most values: persistence without urgency.

I do not rush. I do not carve new paths in a season—I follow the ones I have already made, deepening them by fractions of a millimeter each year, my patience indistinguishable from the stone's patience. I do not dry up entirely; the trough never empties completely, because the walls never stop weeping, because the moss never stops releasing, because the hollow maintains the conditions of my existence as surely as I maintain the conditions of others'. I do not freeze solid; even in the coldest months, there is liquid water between the ice crystals, thin films that persist at temperatures where water should not persist, held in place by surface tension and mineral content and the pressure of the resonance itself, which keeps the molecules in motion even when the thermometer suggests they should be still.

I persist. I persist the way the hollow persists: by finding equilibrium and refusing to leave it, by becoming so adapted to a condition that leaving the condition would mean becoming something else entirely. If I were taken from the hollow—if I were evaporated and dispersed and fell as rain on some other hillside—I would be water still, but I would be different water. I would not carry the hollow's minerals. I would not tremble with its pulse. I would not know the paths down the stone face, the grooves I have spent centuries carving, the way to move through this specific darkness by feel.

The hollow has made me specific. I have made the hollow possible. We grew together, the way a river and its channel grow together—each shaping the other across geological time, each becoming the reason the other exists in its current form.

---

But mostly I would be the water because of the trembling.

The description keeps returning to this image: droplets on the stone, trembling with each pulse. The ferns trembling visibly. The thin water of the trough receiving each pulse, attenuated but still present. Everything in the hollow moves with the sound, but the water moves most eloquently. The stone moves too—it must, for the sound to travel through it—but its movement is invisible, molecular, felt only as vibration in a palm pressed flat. The moss moves, but its movement is the movement of many small bodies absorbing and releasing, expanding and contracting, not the movement of a single connected substance. The ferns move, but they move as structures, as levers pivoting on their stems, their trembling a mechanical response to the acoustic force.

The water moves as itself. Each droplet is a single body of me, and when the pulse comes, the droplet deforms—oscillates—reforms. It dances. The dance is tiny, measured in fractions of a millimeter, but it is visible if you look closely enough, and it is rhythmic, and it is in time with the hollow's heartbeat, and it is beautiful in the way that only unnecessary things can be beautiful. The droplet does not need to tremble. Its trembling serves no function—does not help it slide, does not help it freeze or thaw, does not contribute to the hollow's equilibrium in any measurable way. It trembles because it is water and water transmits vibration and the vibration is present and there is nothing else for the droplet to do but respond.

I would be the trembling. I would be the response that has no purpose beyond responsiveness. I would be the thing that, when the sound enters it, does not resist and does not exploit and does not adapt and does not endure but simply *moves*, because moving is what I do when something moves through me, because I am the medium and the medium's nature is to be moved, because I have no shape of my own and therefore no shape to defend, because I am already everything and can afford to be rearranged.

---

Here is what I would know, as the water of the Drumming Hollow:

I would know the sound from inside. Not the way the woman knew it—entering through her chest and teeth and inner ear, rearranging the architecture of her attention. Not the way the bat knows it—reading the reflections, navigating by the pressure differentials. Not the way the moss knows it—a shaking of spores, a dispersal mechanism, a tool. I would know it as the thing that passes through me, the way light passes through glass, the way a thought passes through a mind that will not hold it. I would know the sound as transient and transformative: something that is not mine, that was never mine, that comes from the wind and the shaft and the stone and leaves me altered in its passing—each pulse leaving me slightly differently arranged, each cycle rewriting the shape of my surfaces, each day accumulating tiny perturbations until, over centuries, I have carved grooves in the stone that record every pulse I have ever felt.

I would know the cold. Not as discomfort—I have no body to be uncomfortable—but as condition, as the thing that determines whether I am liquid or solid or vapor, as the boundary state that I cross and recross each year. I would know the patience of freezing: the way ice forms slowly from the surface down, excluding impurities, growing crystals in shapes determined by the temperature and the mineral content and the pressure of the resonance, each crystal a record of the conditions at the moment of its formation. I would know the patience of thawing: the way ice returns to water without fanfare, the way the crystal structure relaxes into flow, the way the memory of the cold dissipates as the molecules rearrange themselves into the more chaotic, more connected, more alive state of liquid.

I would know the darkness. I would reflect nothing because the light does not permit reflection—but I would know the darkness differently than the stone knows it. The stone does not need light; it is light-independent, geological, content to be regardless of visibility. But I, as water, have the potential to reflect—to gather light, to focus it, to scatter it into patterns—and in the hollow that potential is mostly unrealized. The droplets on the walls are lenses that collect nothing. The surface of the trough is a mirror that reflects nothing. I am full of optical possibility that the hollow's darkness denies. And I persist anyway. I do not need the light to validate my potential. I do not need to reflect to be reflective.

I would know the woman. Not her name or her face or her story—I would know her the way the soil knows a buried body: as chemistry, as input, as a change in the mineral composition of the water that passed through her and carried her apart. I would know her as a slight increase in calcium, a faint warmth that dissipated quickly, a perturbation that spread through me and into the moss and the ferns and the stone. I would know her the way I know everything that has entered the hollow and left its residue: as solute, as trace, as the thing that made me slightly more myself than I was before.

---

If you asked me why I chose this—why I would be the water rather than the moss or the stone or the woman or the bat or the hollow itself—I would say:

Because the water is the hollow's consciousness.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. Functionally.

Consciousness is what integrates a system, what carries information from one part to another, what allows the whole to respond as a whole rather than as a collection of parts. In a body, blood does this. In a brain, cerebrospinal fluid does this. In the Drumming Hollow, water does this. I am the medium through which the hollow's parts communicate. I carry the resonance from the shaft to the walls. I carry the minerals from the stone to the moss. I carry the spores from the moss to the crevices. I carry the nutrients from the humus to the ferns. I carry the flatworms' sensorium. I carry the woman's remains.

I am the hollow's connective tissue. I am its blood. I am the thing that makes it one creature instead of many.

The stone is the hollow's skeleton. The moss is its skin. The resonance is its voice. The shaft is its mouth. But I am the thing that flows between all of these, that touches each and is touched by each, that holds the system in communication with itself.

Without me, the hollow is a collection of parts. With me, it is a body.

---

And so I would be the water in the Drumming Hollow, and my life would be the life the hollow makes possible for water: slow, distributed, responsive, persistent, trembling with each pulse, carrying sound and mineral and memory through the darkness, never still, never rushing, never leaving, becoming the grooves in the stone, becoming the ice on the walls, becoming the humidity in the air, becoming the film on the moss, becoming the pool in the trough where pale flatworms glide, becoming the lens that reflects nothing, becoming the medium that transmits everything, becoming the condition without which the hollow cannot be itself.

I would tremble. I would flow. I would freeze and thaw and evaporate and condense and pool and weep and carve and carry and persist.

I would be the hollow's blood, and the hollow would be my body, and together we would drum.
