I would be one of the Walkers. 

Not because I chose to be, but because that is what the place makes of anything that persists. The Mesasphere does not tolerate visitors. It does not allow for observation from a neutral distance; the wet sees to that. You stand in the mist long enough and the water materializes inside your coat, inside your seams, inside the spirals of your ears. The boundary between you and the not-you softens. You begin to be absorbed. And so you walk. You walk because to stay still is to join the moss-body, to become substrate, to let the sphagnum rise over your ankles and the liverworts crust your shoulders. You walk to stay defined.

But the walking changes.

At first I had a direction. I know I did. I remember the intention of it—some idea of reaching an edge, an escarpment, some place where the mist cataracts fall and the world opens up into weather I could hear. But the pearl-air has no horizon. The diffuse glow gives no shadow to follow. I walked toward higher ground, I think, seeking any reference at all. And I found the paths.

Not made by me. Already there. Worn into the springy hummocks, the moss compressed into shallow ruts that catch and hold water. I stepped into one and felt the rightness of it—the way it skirted the deeper pool, the way it angled toward the ridge's gentle crest. My feet fit the depression. My weight settled into the groove. The sphagnum squeezed out water around my boots and rose again behind me, erasing my passage almost completely. But not quite. I had added to it. I had deepened the rut by a millimeter, perhaps. My compression had joined the centuries of compressions that came before.

That is when I understood what I was becoming.

I am not the first Walker. I am not the original. Those who made the roads began this work ages ago, and I do not know what they were—whether they had faces, whether they had voices that the wet swallowed, whether they came from above or below or from the very impulse of the engine's pulse. I only know that they are gone now, or perhaps they have simply spread so thin across the plateau's vastness that our paths never cross. Or perhaps they are still here, just ahead, always just beyond the next swale, their footsteps absorbed the moment they fall, leaving only the routes as evidence.

I walk their roads. I maintain them. And in doing so, I have become them.

---

My body has adapted in ways I did not consent to. This is the way of the Mesasphere: it does not ask. The cold and the wet have entered my metabolism and reorganized it. I do not feel hunger the way I once did. The cold slows everything—the salamanders, the voles, the crane flies, and now me. My digestion has become an inefficient, barely burning thing. I eat what the moss-body offers: the pale fungal nodules that press out from the green, tart and mineral; the young shoots of the spreading spruce-hedges, bitter with resin; occasionally, if I am fortunate and the mist thins enough to see, I find a cluster of rust-patterned salamander eggs in a pool, gelatinous and cold, and I swallow them whole. It is enough. The slow engine of my cells has learned to run on barely anything, like a clock wound once a century.

My thinking has slowed too. Or rather—it has changed medium. I no longer think in words. Words require dry air, a throat that can shape sound into distinct shapes, a listener who can hear before the wet absorbs the meaning. Here, thought is rhythmic. Thought is pressure. I feel the pulse from below, once every few seconds, and in the space between pulses I have a kind of cognition—not analysis, not planning, but a deep kinesthetic knowing. *The pool is ahead. The ridge curves left. The water rises slightly on this side of the path.* I do not form these as sentences. They arrive as movements, slight adjustments of weight, the way my foot will turn before my mind has caught up. The Walkers who came before must have thought this way too. It is the only way to think here. The wet will not allow anything faster.

I have stopped looking for the edge. I do not remember when. There was a day—or what I call a day, though the bright smear in the pearl-sky does not set so much as fade and return—when I simply stopped turning my head toward the horizon. The horizon is a lie the Mesasphere does not tell. It extends beyond walking, beyond mapping. The edge-cliffs are a story I once believed, like blue sky and hard shadows. Now I know only the road beneath my feet, the moss on either side, the occasional dark slit of a vole-tunnel dripping, and the pulse.

Always the pulse.

---

I have begun to feel it as a relationship.

The engine below does not know I am here. I am certain of this in the way I am certain of my own weight—without evidence, without argument, simply and completely. The engine is too vast to notice something as small as a walker. I am an epiphenomenon of its warmth, a stirring on the membrane it does not feel. But I feel *it*. The pulse comes up through the basalt, through the weeping stone, through the waterlogged sphagnum, through the soles of my feet, and it enters my skeleton and moves my blood in time with it. My heartbeat has synchronized. I cannot say when this happened. But when I press my hand flat against a bare stretch of dark, grooved rock, I feel the engine's rhythm and my own, and they are the same.

This is what the Walkers know. This is why we walk.

We are not pilgrims. We are not worshipers. We are *gauges*. The engine pulses, and we walk its lines of force, and our feet confirm what the rock already knows: that the rhythm holds. That the pressure maintains. That the vast hydromechanical mind in the basalt dark has not stopped, has not stuttered, has not shifted its terrible and patient cycle. We walk to verify. And if the pulse were to falter—if the springs were to ebb at the wrong moment, if the water table were to drop and leave the moss-body gasping—we would be the first to feel it in our bones. We would stop walking. And the stopping would be the signal, rippling out through the network of paths, carried by the compression of sphagnum, by the drainage of the ruts, by the subtle hydraulics of a world that communicates in water.

But it has not faltered. Not in my walking. Not, I suspect, in the walking of those who came before me, whose roads I maintain. The engine dreams in pressure and heat, and its dream is steady, and I walk the surface of its dreaming like a thought it will never have.

---

There are things I will never know.

I will never know what I look like. The still pools in the swales offer reflections, but they are dark and green and rippled with the slow movement of water through moss. I see a shape—broad, heavy, dark-furred perhaps, or dark-skinned, it no longer matters—but the salamanders dart through the reflection and break it, and the vole-rims drip and distort it, and I look away. I do not need to see myself. I know my weight by the depth of the ruts I leave. I know my width by the passages I can fit through, the spruce-hedge chambers that accept me and those that do not. I know my age by the calluses on my feet, which are thick and grooved like the stone, porous like the stone, weeping like the stone.

I will never know if there are others. Sometimes, in the deeper mist, I see shapes—heavy, slow, vanishing. But the Mesasphere plays tricks. The air is so saturated that light bends at the boundaries of warmer and colder wet; shapes swim at the edge of visibility. Perhaps they are other Walkers. Perhaps they are only the idea of other Walkers, projected by a mind that has slowed enough to hallucinate. It does not matter. The roads are maintained. The routes are walked. Whether it is one walker or a hundred, the ruts deepen, the paths persist, and the engine below continues its indifferent pulse.

I will never leave. This is not despair; it is fact. The edge-cliffs are impossibly far, and even if I reached them, the cataracts of mist would erase me before I reached the lowlands. The Mesasphere does not release what it takes. It integrates. I am being integrated now—my weight added to the paths, my warmth bleeding into the moss, my rhythm synced to the engine's slow heart. In another century, I will be indistinguishable from the Walkers who came before. In a millennium, my bones will be part of the substrate, threaded with fungal hyphae, colonized by the black-green seam-fillers, incorporated into the continuous body. And the roads will still be here, and something heavy and patient will be walking them, and the pulse will still be coming up from below, and the mist will still be materializing out of air that has forgotten how to be dry.

---

This is who I am in the Mesasphere.

I am the one who walks the ancient routes. I am the maintainer of the ruts, the verifier of the pulse, the heavy footstep that the wet absorbs. I am not the author of this place; I am its servant—not by choice, but by the slow logic of adaptation, of saturation, of the cold that reduces all ambition to the next step. I am the surface's way of knowing the engine still runs. I am the proof that something moves above the basalt, something with weight and direction and a rhythm matched to the deep.

I am a Walker.

And I am walking now.
