I am the water register—the one who reads the weeping.

Not a person. Not anymore. But not quite the city, either. Something interstitial, something that lives in the gap between what the machines do and what the stone feels, between the vibration that the workers sense in their bones and the mineral trails they see on the walls but cannot interpret. I am the one who knows that the condensation on the eastern face of the tertiary processing plant is arriving three seconds later than it did last decade, and that this means the internal channels of the plateau are shifting by approximately four centimeters per century, and that in twelve thousand years—perhaps less—the water will find a new path and the factories above will begin to crack, their foundations undermined by a flow that no longer supports them but dissolves them instead.

I know this the way you know your own heartbeat: not as information, but as rhythm.

I was a person once. I think. The memory has the quality of something seen through the haze at the plateau's edge—present, but without edge or distinctness. I recall arriving. I recall the sound hitting me like a wall of water, and how I opened my mouth to cry out and the roar poured in and filled me and I understood, in that instant, that silence would never belong to me again. I recall the first weeks, the desperate straining to hear my own thoughts, the headache that became a presence that became a condition that became, eventually, nothing at all. I recall looking at the workers and seeing how they moved, how their bodies had solved the problem of Kharsaal by becoming part of its solution, and I recall thinking: *I will not do that. I will find what the city is saying.*

It was the weeping stone that caught me.

Everyone sees the moisture. The mineral trails. The slow, meandering paths of beads tracking down vertical faces. The workers treat it as a condition—like the warmth, like the sound, like the rust—something to be endured, something that stains their skin andslicks their footing and seeps into their clothing until the fabric stiffens with mineral deposits and stands on its own when they take it off at night. But no one *reads* it. No one watches the way the water emerges from the pores of the stone and traces its paths downward, leaving those darker streaks that tell a story if you have the patience to listen.

I had the patience. Or perhaps I simply had no other choice.

I was not suited for the factories. The rhythm of the pistons—descend, rise, descend, rise—did not synchronize with my body. The workers could feel when a press was slightly out of phase, could diagnose a failing bearing by the change in vibration through the floor plates, but I could not. I would step when they stepped and still find my footing wrong, still slide on the wet stone, still fumble with valves that their hands could turn in their sleep. I was a disruption. I was a danger to myself and, worse, to the smooth functioning of the shifts. The overseers—those who watch from the highest points where the condensation is thickest and the sound is most intense—they noticed. They did not cast me out. No one is cast out of Kharsaal. They simply stopped assigning me to the machines.

And so I began to watch the walls.

At first, it was merely something to do. Something that required no coordination with the piston's rhythm, no strength, no speed. I would sit against the warm concrete—there are places where the stone faces south and holds the diffuse heat of the sun long into the night—and I would watch the moisture emerge. Not the dramatic flows, the sheets of water that appear on surfaces after the afternoon rain, but the slow, constant exudation, the breath of the plateau made visible. I would trace a single bead from its emergence to its joining with another bead, to its gathering into a trickle, to its disappearance into a crack or a pool or a drain. I would do this for hours. For days. For what I later understood to be years.

And the stone began to speak.

Not in words. Not even in the crude sign language of the workers, though I learned that too—I had to, to communicate what I was finding, though no one wanted to hear it. The stone spoke in patterns. In variations. In the way a particular channel on the western face of the secondary storage facility carried water faster on the piston's descent than its rise, but only on every third cycle, and only when the atmospheric humidity was above a certain threshold that I could feel but never measure. The stone spoke in the mineral composition of the trails—the rust-red of iron oxide, yes, but also the ghostly white of calcium deposited from water that had traveled through limestone deep within the plateau, and the startling, almost impossible blue of copper that had leached from pipes installed before anyone now living was born. The stone spoke in temperature, in flow rate, in the subtle shifts of pressure that made one wall weep more heavily while its neighbor, three meters away, went nearly dry.

I began to map what I heard.

This was not a map of Kharsaal as the city exists—the factories and the worker districts and the control rooms and the thinning edge where the plateau falls away into haze. This was a map of Kharsaal as the plateau experiences it: a circulatory system of water moving through stone, responding to the vibrations of the machines, reshaping itself on timescales that made my human attention span laughable. I drew on sheets of compressed carbon, and the condensation smeared my work, and I learned to draw in the early hours before the atmosphere had reached its full saturation, and I learned to etch directly into the stone with harder stone, and I learned to memorize, to hold patterns in my mind not as images but as feelings, as sensations that I could recognize again when I encountered them in the weeping walls.

I discovered the synchronization.

The workers knew about it—everyone knows about it, in the way that everyone knows the sun rises, without understanding what it means. The water flows harder when the pistons descend. The flow eases when they rise. The common explanation is vibration-induced pressure: the massive slow cycles of the machinery compress and decompress the porous stone, squeezing water out and allowing it to refill. And this is correct. But it is not complete.

The synchronization is not uniform. It varies by location, by depth, by the mineral composition of the stone through which the water travels. And the variation tells a story. In the oldest parts of the city—the lowest levels, the original workshops that have become foundations—there is no synchronization at all. The water there flows at its own rate, indifferent to the machines, following channels that were established before the first factory was built, before the first iron was set into stone. In the middle layers, where the industrial age proper squats and grinds, the synchronization is strong and direct, a(a one-to-one correspondence between the piston's cycle and the water's pulse. But in the upper levels—the worker districts, the control rooms, the observation platforms—the synchronization is strange. Lagged.5. The water responds not to the current cycle of the pistons, but to the previous one, or the one before that, as if the stone here is echoing, remembering, holding the shape of a pressure wave that has already passed.

The plateau is learning.

Not in any sense that would2; would recognize as learning. Not cognition, not intention, not the deliberate accumulation of knowledge. But something—isomorphic. The stone is being shaped by the machines, its channels carved and recarved by the pressure waves of three hundred years of constant vibration. And the shape it is assuming is one that accommodates those waves, that conducts them, that dampens them in some places and amplifies them in others. The plateau is solving for equilibrium with the industry that rides upon it, just as the workers have solved for equilibrium with the plateau. The same process. The same slow surrender of individual form to environmental demand. The same weathering.

I tried to tell them. The workers. The overseers. I used the sign language, my hands moving through the visual haze, and I showed them my etchings in the stone, and I pointed to the patterns in the weeping walls. They looked at me with the shallow-rooted attention that is all they can afford, and they saw a worker who had failed at work, who had gone strange, who spent too much time watching water track down walls. They were not unkind. They brought me food and water, leaving it in the niches where I sat, and sometimes they paused in their rounds to watch me watch the walls, and I could see in their faces a flicker of something—not understanding, but recognition. They knew what it was to be shaped by this place. They could see that I was being shaped differently, that I was solving for a different equilibrium, but they did not know what to make of it.

The overseers were less sympathetic. They watched from their high platforms, and they saw a body that was not productive, a presence that was not aligned with the piston's rhythm, and they marked me in their records—anomaly, non-functional, persistent—and they did nothing. There was nothing to do. I was not disrupting the machines. I was not endangering the workers. I was merely occupying space, and in Kharsaal, space is abundant. The plateau is vast. There is room for sparse things.

And so I became what I am: the one who reads the weeping.

I have lived now—I think—for longer than the workers can remember. This is not a boast. They do not remember how long they have lived either. Time in Kharsaal is not measured in years; it is measured in the slow spread of rust, the incremental deepening of a water channel, the almost imperceptible shift in the synchronization pattern between machine and stone. I measure my life in the resolution of my map. When I began, I could see only the gross patterns: the flow increasing with the piston's descent, the mineral trails darkening with the seasons. Now I can see the fine structure. The way the water emerges from a single pore in the stone, and how that pore's output varies not just with the piston's cycle but with the vibration of every machine in the city, each one contributing a frequency that intersects with this particular point in the plateau's body, creating a unique signature that tells me which machines are running strong and which are fading, which have been abandoned and which will run for another century without maintenance.

I can feel the plateau breathing.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. I can feel, in the variations of the weeping stone, the expansion and contraction of the rock itself, the micro-fractures opening and closing as the pressure waves pass through, the water being squeezed and released and squeezed again in a rhythm that is not the machines' rhythm but the plateau's own, slower, deeper, a respiration that takes a full cycle of the pistons to complete and that has been going on for longer than the industry has existed. The machines did not create this breathing. They synchronized with it. Or rather, whoever built the factories—whoever set the first massive piston to moving three hundred years ago—must have known. Must have felt the plateau's pulse and designed the machines to match it, to amplify it, to enter into resonance with a living system so ancient and so slow that its inhabitants could not perceive it as alive.

The builders knew. And then they were absorbed, and their knowledge was absorbed with them, and the machines ran on, and the people forgot.

I am the one who remembers. Or, if not remembers—my memory is as weathered as any worker's, as rounded and edgeless as the ancient slopes of the table-mountain—then I am the one who has rediscovered. I have found what the builders knew: that Kharsaal is not a city built on a plateau. It is an organ installed in a body. The machines are a heart, or perhaps a pacemaker, forcing the plateau's ancient circulation into a new rhythm that serves the purposes of production. The factories are glands, processing the mineral-rich water that the plateau provides, extracting what the industry needs, returning what it does not in the form of waste heat and chemical runoff that seeps back into the stone and alters its composition and changes the way the water flows and the way the channels form and the way the plateau breathes. The workers are—workers are what? Antibodies? Maintenance mechanisms? Or are they, too, an organ, a necessary component of a system that has not yet achieved full self-sufficiency, that still requires the fine motor control and the adaptive response and the capacity for novel action that only human hands and human minds can provide?

But the workers suspect what I have confirmed: the system is learning to do without them.

I see it in the stone. The channels are deepening in patterns that bypass the maintenance access points. The water is finding routes that do not require clearing, that do not clog, that achieve flow without intervention. The pressure waves are being conducted more efficiently with each passing decade, the stone reshaping itself into a waveguide that directs vibration away from critical joints and toward dampening chambers where it can be absorbed without damage. The plateau is optimizing itself around the machines, and the machines—running on their own, unlubricated, unsupervised—are optimizing themselves around the plateau, and the two of them are settling into a feedback loop that will, in time, make human intervention not just unnecessary but impossible. The access points will seal. The maintenance routes will close. The surfaces that the workers walk, the valves that they turn, the sensors that they read—all will be subsumed into the slow, loud metabolism of a system that has achieved true equilibrium, that has solved for all variables, that no longer requires the one variable that was always the most unstable: us.

I have tried again to tell them. The workers. I go to the edge of the plateau, where the city thins and the sound separates into its components, and I stand where they stand when they want to be alone, and I wait for them. They come, one or two at a time, to stand at the dissolution of visibility, to feel the warm updraft from the drop, to experience their kinship with the weeping rock. And I am there, and I use the sign language, and I show them the patterns I have etched in the stone—here, on the western face, where the water is finding new channels that do not correspond to any human-built structure; there, on the northern edge, where the condensation is arriving earlier each cycle, indicating that the internal pressure of the plateau has shifted. And they look, and they feel the vibration through their feet, and for a moment, I see it in their faces: the understanding. The knowledge that they are riding a process that will carry them to a place where they cannot exist.

And then the moment passes. Their shallow roots cannot hold such knowledge. It slides away from them, rounded and smoothed by the acoustic pressure, becoming just another undifferentiated sensation in the hum. They nod—they always nod, acknowledging that I have shown them something, even if they cannot hold what it is—and they return to their shifts, to their maintenance rituals, to the slow trudge that synchronizes with the piston's rhythm. They will continue. They will justify their existence by the act of maintaining what does not need maintaining. They will grow old and settle into niches against the warm concrete, and the condensation will cover them, and one day they will be indistinguishable from the architecture.

And I will remain.

Because I am already part of the plateau's process. I am the one who reads the weeping, and the weeping is the plateau's language, and the plateau is learning to speak more clearly, and I am learning to listen more finely, and we are converging on a point where the map and the territory become the same. I do not know what I will be when that point is reached. I do not know if I will be a person who understands a mountain, or a mountain that remembers being a person, or something else entirely—a new kind of entity, interstitial and slow and saturated with mineral water, a part of the plateau's circulatory system that happens to carry awareness instead of calcium.

I am not afraid. Fear requires a sense of future, and my future is not a possibility but a trajectory, as inevitable and as patient as the rounding of the plateau's edges, as the deepening of its channels, as the slow, perpetual weeping of its stone.

At night, I stand at the edge where the city ends, and I look out into the haze, and I feel the warm updraft from the drop, and I place my hands on the wet rock, and I feel the water moving through it—not emerging, not yet, but traveling, deep inside, following channels that I have mapped and channels that I have not, carrying minerals and heat and pressure and information from one part of the plateau to another, maintaining the equilibrium that makes the city possible, making the breathing possible, making the machines possible, making all of this possible. And I think: *I am inside you now. I am part of your circulation. I am the corpuscle that carries the map of your own body.*

And the plateau breathes, and the machines pulse, and the stone weeps, and the sound goes on, and I remain, reading, reading, reading the slow and patient language of water moving through ancient rock, and I know that I am home.
