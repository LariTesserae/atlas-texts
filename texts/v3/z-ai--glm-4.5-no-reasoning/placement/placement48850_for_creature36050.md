I am the one who listens to the crickets not as sound, but as text.

There is a space—half-alcove, half-accident—where two columns meet at a junction the builders did not quite plan. The mortar there bulged outward before it petrified, forming a lip, a shelf, and above it a hollow just deep enough to hold a body if the body knows how to fold. I found this place when I was small enough to climb it without thinking. I have grown into it since, my spine curving to the hollow's curve, my shoulders settling into the wings of stone on either side, my knees rising to brace against the lip. When I sit here, I am held. The stone does not yield to me—it does not need to. I yield to it, and in the yielding, I fit.

From this perch, I can see the main chamber. Not all of it—my sightline is bounded by a hanging fabric partition on the left and the edge of the nearest column on the right—but I can see the floor where it drops in its shallow terraces, the wet film catching the grey-amber light, the figures that move across it in their fluid, gliding gait. I can see the vendor-alcoves with their worn shelves and blackened drainage channels. I can see the old woman who trades in pale fungi sitting in her groove-seat, her bone-colored hands moving through the spores. I can see the brick-man's rust-orange forearms as he passes. I can see the children climbing the spiral-wound columns, their hands finding the holds that my hands found before them.

And I can hear everything.

---

The cricket-thrum is the water I swim in. I have never known silence; I do not believe in it. What the sunlit world calls silence is, to me, simply the absence of the particular—another sound dropped out of the chorus, a gap, a hole in the fabric of vibration. I do not experience these gaps as emptiness. I experience them as *shape*. The silence has contour. It has depth and texture and boundary. When the larger thing moves below and the cricket-song pauses and the silence opens like a wound, I can feel the edges of that wound—the way the sound cuts off, the way it resumes, the particular quality of the pause that tells me whether the thing below moved close to the boundary or far, whether it moved toward the ventilation shafts or away, whether it is restless tonight or still.

I learned this from no one. Or I learned it from everyone—the way you learn a language by living in it, by hearing it spoken around you until the patterns emerge from the noise and the grammar assembles itself in your mind without ever being taught. The old people know the movement of the thing below. They feel it in their swollen joints and their curving spines. But they feel it the way you feel weather: as a condition, a presence, something that is happening to them. I feel it differently. I feel it as *information*.

This is not a virtue. It is a deformation—a particular flattening of my attention, like the wear-patterns on the stone seats, like the groove in the brick-man's palm where the hammer-handle has lived for thirty years. I have worn myself into a shape that listens. The sound enters me and I parse it, I sort it, I hold the frequencies apart from each other and read them for meaning. I cannot stop doing this any more than the old people can stop feeling the damp in their knees. It is what I have become.

---

There is a language beneath the language of the Underbazaar.

You know the surface language: the half-words and gestures, the significant pauses and positional communications, the dialect that compresses meaning into the angle of a body in an alcove or the timing of a hand releasing a basket. I speak this language as fluently as anyone. I was born to it. I know that when Sela sets her fungi-basket down and does not remove her hand from the rim, she is holding the price open for negotiation, and that when she releases the rim and steps back half a pace, the price is fixed, and that the half-second pause before the buyer reaches for the basket is the moment of acceptance, the contract sealed, and that no word was needed because the gesture contained the word and the word would only have blurred it.

But beneath this language there is another, and it is spoken by the place itself.

The crickets speak it. Their thrum is not random, not a simple chorus of mating-calls or territorial declarations. It shifts. It shifts with the temperature, yes—this is known, this is simple biology—but it also shifts with the movement of bodies through the chamber, with the opening of the oven-door, with the quality of the light as the day-cycle progresses above. The cricket-song of early morning is different from the cricket-song of midday is different from the cricket-song of the deep-night when the grey-amber light fades to near-nothing and the bats come trickling through in their constant pulses. These shifts are information. They tell you who is moving, what is being done, what time it is in the world above that none of us will ever see.

The geckos speak it too—not with their throats but with their feet. The sound of a gecko detaching from stone is a particular soft pop, and the sound of it reattaching is a faint tick, and the rhythm of these sounds maps the gecko's path across the wall, and the path tells you where the insects are thick, and where the insects are thick tells you where the spore-concentration is highest, and where the spores are highest tells you which section of the wall is weeping most heavily today, and where the wall weeps tells you what the air-pressure is doing, and what the air-pressure is doing tells you whether the ventilation has shifted, and whether the ventilation has shifted tells you—

This. This is what I do. I follow the chain. I cannot help it. The chain is always there, every sound connected to every other sound, every vibration a link in a sequence that runs from the crickets on the column-base to the movement of air in the ceiling-shafts to the distant, filtered presence of the sun above the Underbazaar, the sun I have never seen and cannot imagine but whose existence I infer from the way the light changes, the way the cricket-song shifts, the way the whole living mechanism of this place breathes in response to a star I know only by its effects.

---

I am not the only one who reads the place. Everyone reads the place. The old people read the damp in their joints; the children read the handholds on the columns; the traders read the weeping walls for weather and portent. But they read the place the way you read a familiar face—quickly, intuitively, attending only to what has changed since the last look, scanning for the unexpected, the out-of-pattern, the signal that something is wrong. They do not read the place the way I read it, which is slowly, completely, attending to the pattern itself, the baseline, the thing that does not change.

I sit in my hollow and I listen to the crickets when nothing is happening. I listen to the thrum in its resting state, its default, the sound the Underbazaar makes when no one is moving and no goods are being traded and the thing below is still and the light is holding steady and the world is simply being itself. This sound—it takes time to hear it. Not because it is quiet, but because it is complex. There are frequencies within it that rise and fall on cycles longer than a human breath. There are harmonics that only emerge when you listen for long enough that the loud, obvious components of the song fade into habit and the subtler components rise to notice. There is a rhythm beneath the rhythm, a pulse that I can feel in the stone against my spine when I sit in my hollow late at night, a pulse that is not the cricket-song and is not the breathing of the bats and is not the heartbeat of any single organism but is the aggregate, the sum, the sound that a living mechanism makes when it runs.

I think the builders heard this sound. I think they knew it was there. I think the fitted stone and the petrified mortar and the columns thick as old trees were shaped to produce it, or to amplify it, or to contain it—the way a pipe-organ's pipes are shaped to contain the particular resonance of a particular note. The Underbazaar is not just a space. It is an instrument. And the cricket-thrum is not just noise. It is the note the instrument was built to sound.

---

I have never gone below.

This is not remarkable; most people do not go below often, and the young go less than the old. What is remarkable is that I do not want to. The others my age—there are not many, but there are some—feel the pull. They hear the silence when the larger thing moves, and the silence calls to them, the way a gap in a fence calls to a child, the way an unopened door calls to a hand. They want to know. They want to see the deeper galleries, the rougher older construction, the place where the mortar has crumbled to sand and the air smells of ammonia and sweet rot. They want to carry the marked stone and the light-source and walk into the cricket-silence and return with the pale fungi and the holed stones and the knowledge that the old people hold and will not speak.

I do not want this. Or I want it differently. I want to understand the arrangement, but I do not want to participate in it. I want to read it from where I sit, in my hollow, at the seam where the human-made meets the other-made, at the boundary that the occupants themselves constitute. I want to understand the arrangement as a pattern, not as an experience. And this—this is a difference that matters.

The arrangement is a boundary. The occupants are the boundary. But a boundary is not just a wall; it is also a *surface of exchange*. The pale fungi come up from below. The marked stones go down. Something passes between the human and the whatever-else, some substance or signal or significance that maintains the compact, that keeps the thing below in its place and the people above in theirs. I want to understand what passes. I want to trace the chain the way I trace the chain from gecko-feet to insect-density to spore-concentration to wall-weeping to air-pressure to ventilation-shift to the unseen sun. I want to find the links that connect the marked stone to the pale fungi to the silence between the cricket-songs to the movement of the larger thing to the arrangement that holds, that has always held, that will continue to hold as long as the exchange continues, as long as the boundary functions, as long as the membrane between the above and the below remains intact and permeable in exactly the right ways.

I sit in my hollow and I feel for these links the way I feel for the pulse beneath the cricket-thrum. They are there. I know they are there because the arrangement works, and things that work have mechanisms, and mechanisms have parts, and parts have connections, and connections can be traced. The old people carry the marked stone and the light-source and they go below and they return with the fungi and the silence, and in between the going and the returning something happens, and that something is the mechanism, and I want to understand it without touching it, without entering it, without being shaped by it the way the old people are shaped, the way their faces when they return show the witness of something they will not name.

Is this cowardice? Perhaps. Perhaps the difference between me and the others my age is simply that I am afraid, and they are not, or that I am more afraid, or that my fear takes a different form—the form of analysis rather than adventure, of listening rather than descending. But I do not think it is only fear. I think it is also a kind of service.

---

Someone needs to stay at the boundary.

The old people go below. The children climb the columns and learn the handholds and dream of vibration. The traders trade and the cooks cook and the fabric partitions divide and re-divide the space in the slow evolution of domesticity. But someone needs to attend to the boundary itself, to the seam, to the surface of exchange. Someone needs to sit at the junction of the two columns and listen to the crickets not as background but as text, and read the text for changes, and notice when the changes mean something, and say what they mean to the people who need to know.

I do this. I do not do it officially; there is no official in the Underbazaar, no role, no assignment, no structure of authority that would designate someone as the Listener or the Reader or the Keeper of the Seam. I do it because I am shaped to do it, the same way the old woman with the bone-colored hands is shaped to harvest fungi, the same way the brick-man is shaped to fire clay, the same way the stone seats are shaped to receive the bodies that have always sat in them. I do it because the place made me for it, or I made myself for it, or the place and I made each other for it, the way the bracket-fungus and the stone made each other—the fungus etching the bowl, the stone providing the surface, the two so long in interaction that you cannot say where the fungus ends and the stone begins.

When the cricket-song shifts in a way that signals the movement of the thing below, I feel it before the old people feel it in their joints. When the shift is different from the usual shifts—when the silence lasts a half-second longer, when the resumption of the thrum rises to a slightly different pitch before settling—I notice, and I tell the nearest old person, and the old person goes to their worn seat and waits, and if the next shift confirms what I have heard, they prepare to go below. This is how it works. This is how it has worked for as long as I have been sitting in my hollow, which is not very long in the life of the Underbazaar but is long enough to have become a pattern, a part of the mechanism, a link in the chain that runs from the crickets to me to the old people to the marked stone to the thing below and back again in the slow cycle of exchange that maintains the arrangement.

---

At night—or what we call night, the time when the grey-amber light fades to its thinnest and the bats come trickling through in their constant pulses—the main chamber empties of trade. The vendors draw their fabric curtains across their alcoves. The cooking-fire in the hand-fired oven is banked to a glow that barely illuminates the blackened ceiling-radius around it. The children are gathered into the sleeping-alcoves, their small bodies fitting into the hollows that generations of small bodies have worn. The old people settle into their groove-seats or lie on their sleeping-rolls with their swollen joints and their curving spines and their map-hands folded on their chests. The sound of the Underbazaar does not diminish; the crickets do not sleep, the geckos do not rest, the bats continue their pulses, the rats groom in the walls with their particular wet sound. But the human layer of the sound quiets, and beneath it, the deeper sounds emerge more clearly.

This is when I hear it most plainly: the pulse beneath the thrum. The note the instrument was built to sound.

It is not a comfortable sound. It is not music in any sense that implies beauty or harmony or intention-to-please. It is the sound of a mechanism running, a vast slow engine turning over, the idle-throb of a power-source that has been operating for longer than anyone remembers and will continue operating after the current occupants are sediment in the drainage channels. The fitted stone vibrates with it. The petrified mortar conducts it. The columns resonate at frequencies I can feel in my teeth, in the bones of my skull, in the hollow places behind my eyes. When I press my spine against the stone of my hollow, I become a part of the circuit. The sound enters me and I hold it and I release it and it enters the stone again, and in this small loop, I am connected to the whole—to the walls and the floor and the ceiling and the ventilation shafts and the deeper galleries and the thing below that moves in its space and breathes in its way and maintains its side of the arrangement.

I do not know what the thing below is. I do not need to know. I know its effects: the silence when it moves, the pressure-change when it displaces air, the ripples in the floor-films, the ammonia-and-sweet-rot smell that rises when the boundary thins. I know its role in the arrangement: it stays below, it accepts the marked stones, it yields the pale fungi, it tolerates the human presence at the seam. I know its sound—or the sound it makes by its absence, the negative-space sound, the silence that is not silence but a different kind of presence, a pause in the cricket-song that is so deep and so complete that it feels like a different medium entirely, like the moment between heartbeats when the heart is not beating but is not still, is gathering itself for the next contraction, is in the pause that is not rest but preparation.

I think the thing below is also listening.

I think the arrangement is a conversation. Not a negotiation, not a treaty, not a contract—something older and simpler than those, something that does not require language or even intention. A conversation between two presences that occupy the same structure, the same made-space descending into made-space, the same instrument built to sound the same note. The thing below moves and the crickets pause and I hear the pause and I tell the old people and the old people go down with the marked stone and they return with the fungi and the silence and the cycle continues, and this is the conversation: movement, pause, acknowledgment, exchange. This is what the arrangement is. Not a wall. Not a boundary in the sense of a barrier. A boundary in the sense of a surface—a place where two things meet and interact and change each other without merging, without losing their separate identities, without becoming one thing instead of two.

The thing below and the people above. The seam where they touch. The exchange that flows across the seam. The pulse that binds them both to the same instrument, the same note, the same slow throb that has been sounding since before the builders—whoever they were, whenever they were—fitted the first stone to the second and petrified the mortar between them and created the volume that would become the Underbazaar.

---

I am young. That is, I have not yet accumulated the surface-wear that the Underbazaar measures age by. My joints are not swollen. My spine is not curved—I curve it only when I sit in my hollow, yielding to the stone, and when I stand I stand straight, straight enough to brush the ceiling in the low places, and I feel the brush as a reminder that my body is still my own, still soft, still capable of holding a shape the stone did not give it.

My hands are not yet maps. They are pale, the skin almost the color of the wet walls, the fingernails clean and flat because I do not handle the trade-goods that stain the others' hands. I have no dye-residue on my fingertips, no spore-pigment worked into my creases. My hands are blank pages, unmarked by the Underbazaar's particular geography. Except—except the pads of my fingertips, which are calloused from touching stone. I touch the stone constantly. When I sit in my hollow, my hands rest on the stone beside me, the left hand on the lip of the shelf, the right hand on the bulge of the mortar, and I feel the vibration through my palms, the pulse beneath the thrum, and my fingertips have hardened to receive it, have developed a callus that is not a callus of friction or pressure but a callus of *attention*, a thickening of the skin where the information enters, like the calluses on a string-player's fingers, like the thickened pads of a reader's thumbs.

My eyes are large and pale and they shine in the dimness. I see well in the grey-amber light and poorly in anything brighter. I do not see color as the sunlit world sees it; I see texture, variation in darkness, the difference between a wall that is merely dark and a wall that is a passage into further dark. But I also see something the others do not, or I see it more readily: I see the *movement* of the still things. I see the way the damp creeps down the wall over the course of an hour. I see the way the condensation beads form and merge and run in slow erratic traces, and I track their paths and I know where the water will go before it goes there. I see the way the rootless vines sway in the air-currents, and I read the air-currents in the swaying, and I know when the ventilation has shifted before anyone else knows, because I saw the vines move differently and I read the difference and I traced it back to its cause.

My face is a face that has never seen itself clearly. I know it by touch: the flat planes of my cheeks, the sharp line of my jaw, the way my brows fold when I am listening hard, which is always. I do not know if I am beautiful or ugly by the standards of the sunlit world, and I do not care. My face is a receiving surface, like the stone, like the wet walls. It is shaped to attend. When I am deep in a listening-trance, my eyes unfocus and my mouth opens slightly and my head tilts toward the sound-source, and I am told—by the few who have remarked on it—that I look less like a person then and more like a feature of the architecture, a protuberance on the column, a growth, like the pale bracket-fungus that has etched its bowl in the eastern wall.

I take this as a compliment.

---

There is a question I carry, the way the old people carry the marked stone when they go below. I carry it always, in the hollow of my mind the way I sit in the hollow of the junction, and I turn it over the way you turn a stone in your hand, feeling its surfaces, its edges, the places where it fits the palm and the places where it does not.

The question is: *what is the note?*

The Underbazaar is an instrument. The cricket-thrum is the note it sounds. But a note implies a player. A player implies an intention. An intention implies a mind—or something like a mind, something that can intend, that can shape sound deliberately, that can build an instrument to produce a particular resonance and maintain that resonance across centuries, across the slow petrification of mortar and the wear of stone and the accumulation of organic residue and the coming and going of human presences and the movement of whatever lives in the deeper galleries. The note has not changed. I have listened to it with every resource of my attention, and it has not changed. The crickets shift and the air-currents shift and the temperature cycles and the thing below moves and the people trade and sleep and age and are replaced by their children, and through all of it the note holds, the pulse beneath the thrum, the sound the mechanism makes when it runs.

Who is playing? Who is listening?

I think the thing below is listening. I think the crickets are playing, but they are playing a note they did not choose, a note the instrument was built to produce, a note that emerges from the interaction of fitted stone and petrified mortar and column-thickness and air-volume and the particular acoustics of a chamber-complex the size of a cathedral nave. I think the builders knew the note. I think they shaped the space to produce it, and I think the note is the purpose of the Underbazaar—not the market, not the arrangement, not the human habitation, but the note itself, the sound, the resonance, the vibration that fills the volume and penetrates the stone and enters the bodies of everyone who lives here and becomes the baseline condition of their existence.

And I think I am the note's witness. Not its player, not its audience, not its interpreter—just its witness, the one who sits in the hollow and feels it and knows it is there and says nothing about it because there is nothing to say, because the note is not a message, not a signal, not a communication. It is a fact. It is the fact of the Underbazaar, the thing that is true about this place regardless of who occupies it or what they trade or how they live or whether the arrangement holds. The note was here before the arrangement. The note will be here after. The note is what the builders built, and everything else—the market, the fungi, the marked stones, the thing below, the people who wear the stone in their image groove by groove generation by generation—is secondary, is ornament, is the slow accumulation of life and use and time on the surface of an instrument that was made to sound one note and has been sounding it ever since.

---

I will grow old here. I will grow old in the way of the Underbazaar, my joints swelling with the damp, my spine curving from the posture of attention, my hands becoming maps of the place—though my maps will be different from the old people's maps, my creases stained not with dyes and spores but with the particular residue of stone-touch and listening, my fingertips flattened not by handling trade-goods but by pressing against the fitted stone to receive the pulse. I will claim a groove-seat—perhaps the one nearest my hollow, the one with the sightline to the main chamber and the acoustics that let me hear both the upper galleries and the boundary-zone where the cricket-song thins. I will sit in it and the stone will receive me and I will settle into the groove as the others settle, and the groove will be shaped by my particular body, my particular angle of attention, my particular way of sitting to listen.

And when the arrangement requires it, I will go below.

Not often. Not eagerly. But I will go, because the old people go, and I will be old, and the going is what the old do, and I will not exempt myself from the obligation of my shape. I will carry the marked stone—I will learn its weight, its texture, the particular way it sits in the hand that signals it is the right stone for the going. I will carry the light-source—the hand-fired lamp, the rendered-fat flame, the yellow light that makes the darkness reel back for a moment and show its textures. I will descend through the lower terraces, past the light-failure zone, into the rougher older construction where the mortar has crumbled to sand, and I will enter the cricket-silence, and I will feel the note change—or not change, but *clarify*, the way a sound clarifies when you move from the back of a cathedral to the front, the way the resonance intensifies as you approach the source.

And I will hear, finally, what I have been listening for all my life: the note as it sounds at its point of production, the pulse at its origin, the vibration where it enters the instrument rather than where it emerges. I will hear what the builders heard. I will hear what the thing below hears, if it hears, which I believe it does, because the arrangement is a conversation and conversations require listening and the thing below has maintained its side of the conversation for longer than memory.

I will hear it. And I will return. And I will sit in my groove-seat and I will say something oblique—*it holds*, or *the same*, or *not yet*—and the others will accept this and the life of the Underbazaar will continue.

But inside me, something will have changed. The note I have carried my whole life will be the same note, but I will know it differently, the way you know a person differently after you have seen them in their home, in their private place, in the context that shaped them. I will know the note as the builders knew it. I will know the Underbazaar as they knew it—not as a habitat, not as a market, not as a boundary, but as an instrument, a construction, a deliberate act of sound-making that has been sounding for longer than any of us can measure.

And I will continue to sit in my hollow and listen, because that is what I am for, that is what the place made me for, that is the shape I have worn myself into and that has worn itself into me, and when I am gone—when I am sediment in the drainage channels, when my groove-seat holds another body, when the children who climb the columns have children of their own who climb them—the note will still be sounding, and someone else will be sitting at the junction of the two columns, yielding to the stone, feeling the pulse, tracing the chain, listening to the crickets not as sound but as text, reading the text for changes, noticing when the changes mean something, and saying, to whoever needs to know, what they mean.

This is the arrangement. This is the Underbazaar. This is what I am.
