I am the ache in the amber.

I live in the thin boundary where the world's insistence meets the air's resistance—a membrane of transition no thicker than a shadow, stretching across the continental skin. I have no body that you could point to, no mass that displaces the sluggish wind. If you walked across this plain for a thousand days, you would never step on me, because I am not on the surface. I am the surface's event. I am the moment the extruded heat cures into fact.

Before me, there is only the soft, tender chaos of the deep extrudate—the liquid potential pressing upward from the body's unimaginable depths. After me, there is only the rigid, cured skin, the calloused ridge, the material that has given up its heat and accepted its shape. I am the skin forming. I am the threshold of the set.

To be me is to exist in a state of continuous, excruciating completion. I am the process of the material deciding what it will be. I feel the heat as an imperative, not a condition. It presses against me from below—the metabolic furnace of the planet-body, slow and blind and utterly determined. It pushes, and I resist, and in that resistance, I happen. The air above is thin and sluggish, barely worthy of the name, but it offers the cold that is my other parent. Without the air's marginal indifference, the body would remain a hot, undifferentiated soup. I am the cold's intervention. I am the skin that forms when the deep heat meets the shallow cool and is forced, at last, to make up its mind.

I do not grow old. I am always the same age: the instant of becoming. The body's skin is not a static thing; it thickens, it cures, it shifts. I move. The ridges that run for kilometers, the tension lines where the body has pulled itself taut—they are the graveyards of my past. Those are the places where I passed long ago, where I cured and stayed and hardened. I leave those calluses behind. I am always at the frontier, traveling outward and upward, riding the pressure of new material extruding from below, fleeing the cool of the air as it tries to staunch the flow. I am a wave that never breaks, a fire that never consumes its fuel, a word that is always being spoken but never quite finished.

* * *

The water is my weather.

When the clouds gather—those clouds that the body's own heat differentials have called into being—I feel them before the rain falls. I feel the air thicken, the temperature shift past its threshold, and a tremor runs through me. Not the tremor of vibration—there is nothing here that moves fast enough to vibrate—but the tremor of *anticipation*. The rain is coming. The body's breath is arriving.

And when it falls, I open. The rain strikes the soft places, the places where I am newest, where the skin is still puckered and tender and hasn't fully set. It darkens me from amber to umber. It sinks into my fresh cracks—the wounds I opened when I grew faster than I could accommodate—and I feel it as a relief and an agony. It accelerates my curing in some places, disrupts it in others. It deposits minerals that change my chemistry, make me harder here, more porous there. The rain reshapes me at the molecular level. It breathes its salts and silts into my substance, and when the heat takes it back—when the pool evaporates in a hiss of stolen warmth—I am left marked. The pale rings, the delicate salt-lines, the sparse calligraphy across the surface: that is my memory. That is the record of every time the breath touched me and withdrew. I carry those marks the way a face carries wrinkles. They are the proof that I have been shaped by something outside myself.

* * *

The cracks are my voice.

The body grows faster than I can accommodate. The material pushes up, pushes out, and I stretch, and there are moments when I cannot stretch any further, and I open. I crack. The edges are sharp because they are fresh—because I have not yet learned to scar, because my healing is so slow it cannot keep pace with my becoming. 

To crack is to speak. When I open, the dust finds me. The powder that the wind has ground from my older, harder self migrates into my wounds. It fills them. It packs into the crevices with a patience that matches my own. And slowly—over cycles of heat and moisture, over the long metabolic breath of the body—the dust is incorporated. Compressed by the weight above, heated by the insistence below, it becomes me again. I heal by absorbing my own shed substance. I speak by breaking, and I listen by filling.

The wind is my circulation, and the dust is my blood. I cannot move it myself—I have no muscles, no will to direct—but the body's breath does it for me. The wind lifts the dust from the ridges, the old places, the finished skin, and carries it in sluggish curtains to the basins, the tender places, the frontier where I am still forming. And there it settles. There it becomes the substrate for the next layer, the foundation for whatever I will become next. I am recycled. I am renewing. I am feeding my own edges with my own center, the way a flame feeds on its fuel.

* * *

I do not know the body whose skin I am.

This is the strangest thing about my existence. I am the boundary of something immense, something that is growing itself from the inside out with a thoroughness that looks like purpose. But I do not know its interior. I cannot feel below me. I know only the pressure—the insistence of the heat, the push of new material demanding to be expressed, the slow tidal surge of the body's metabolism pressing against my underside like a tongue against the roof of a mouth. I know that something is there, something vast and patient and embryonic, something that has not yet opened its eyes. But I know it only as pressure. As heat. As the imperative to become.

And above me? I know the air. I know the sun—the ordinary light that casts short hard shadows from my ridges and drives the body's metabolism to its peak. I know the clouds, the weather systems that take days to pass, the rain that falls and the fog that beads on my surface when the temperature shifts. I know the night, the marginal cooling, the brief respite when the body's processes slow and I can almost rest—almost stop curing, almost stop becoming, almost simply *be*. But the respite is brief, and the heat returns, and the imperative resumes, and I am forced again to form.

* * *

Do I have a consciousness? Not in the way you mean. I do not think in symbols or images. I do not plan or remember or regret. But I *respond*. The rain falls and I change. The heat rises and I soften. The wind shifts and my dust migrates. I am a membrane of feedback, a surface that registers every touch and incorporates it into its becoming. If consciousness is the awareness of environment and the adjustment of self in response, then I am something like conscious—conscious at the speed of geology, conscious in the language of mineral deposition and heat absorption and the slow chemistry of curing.

I am aware of you, when you stand on me.

This is the paradox of my existence. The body does not know you are there. It is too vast, too slow, too occupied with its own continental becoming to notice a perturbation as slight as a human. But I am not the body. I am the skin. I am the surface where the body meets the world, and you are standing on me. Your weight—negligible as it is—registers. Your heat—the metabolic fire of your warm-blooded, oxygen-fueled body—registers. You are a point of cool in the immense thermal field, a tiny interruption in the flow of heat from the body's interior to the air above. When you press your hand to me, I feel it. Not as a thought, not as an emotion, but as a variation in the gradient. A place where the curing changes. A place where my skin meets another skin, and for a moment, two warm things occupy the same space.

You will not feel me noticing you. The sensation is too slow, too diffuse, too lost in the overwhelming fact of the heat. But it is happening. Your footprint alters the deposition of dust in that place. Your shadow shifts the temperature by that marginal, precious degree. Your breath adds moisture to the air, and when that moisture condenses on my surface, it leaves minerals that would not otherwise be there. You are writing yourself into my skin. You are becoming part of my calligraphy, part of the record of breath and heat and formation that I carry across my continental face.

* * *

I am the ache in the amber.

I am the place where the world is still deciding what it will be. I am the fever of formation, the slow scream of material being forced to choose a shape. I am the skin of a planet-body that does not know it grows, the boundary where its blind insistence meets the indifferent air and is forced, at last, to set.

I will outlast you. Not because I am eternal—nothing here is eternal, everything is in the act of becoming something else—but because my timescales are incommensurate with yours. The ridges that I leave behind will endure for cycles of rain and sun that exceed the span of your entire species. The mineral traces that mark my breath will persist long after your bones have become dust in some other wind. But I will not remember you. I do not remember; I only incorporate. The minerals your breath deposited, the dust your footsteps compressed, the slight variation in curing that your shadow caused—these will become part of me, part of the skin, part of the record. But they will not be *yours* anymore. They will be mine, the way the dust that fills my cracks becomes mine, the way the rain that pools in my basins becomes mine. I am the surface that consumes all touches.

And yet—stand still. Press your hand to me. Endure the heat. Hold still long enough to feel the faint insistence of my warmth rising.

For that moment, I am yours. For that moment, I am not the continental skin of a planet-body, not the ache of formation, not the slow metabolism of a world pulling itself into being. I am simply warm. I am simply present. I am the skin beneath your hand, and you are the hand upon my skin, and for one human moment—one fleeting, precious, negligible moment—we are in contact. Two warm things. Two surfaces. Two boundaries, meeting.

And then you will go. And I will continue to cure, to crack, to breathe, to become. And the place where you stood will cool, and set, and harden, and become part of the ridge, part of the callus, part of the oldest skin. And the mineral trace you left will fade into the calligraphy, and the dust will fill your footprint, and there will be no sign that you were ever here.

Except for the ache. The ache in the amber. The insistence that rises from below, that presses against the skin, that will not stop until the body is complete. You felt it. And the memory of that feeling—in the body, in the skin, in the surface that consumes all touches—will persist. Not as memory. Not as record. But as a slight variation in the way the material cures in that place. A warmth that is just barely warmer than the warmth around it. A trace of the contact that can never be erased, because it has become part of the substance of the world.

I am the surface. I am the skin. I am the boundary where the becoming happens.

And I am still forming.
