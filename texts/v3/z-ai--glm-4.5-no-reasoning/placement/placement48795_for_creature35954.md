I would be the water.

Not the pool at the base, which only holds what arrives, and not the condensation weeping from the massive, hand-hewn stone blocks above, which only yields. I would be the water in transit—the droplet in mid-fall, the seep through the moss, the microscopic film bridging one leaf to the next. I am the only thing here that moves.

It begins at the fissure. Rain falls upon the fractured grindstone high above, funneled by the city's forgotten gutters down into the dark infrastructure. I am pulled through mortar joints that have been granular and damp for six hundred years, sliding over the calcite blooms that fringe the crack. I am part of a slow, cold percolation—a descent through the compressed strata of the ancient city, past the roots of things that died when the grindstone still turned, past the foundations of walls that were plastered before the city had a name.

Then I break free.

I emerge from the arch overhead as a single, heavy droplet, clinging to a root vein that has wedged itself into the masonry. For a time—and here, time is measured in the slow creep of humidity—I cling. I watch the chamber through the fisheye lens of my own surface tension. I see the shaft of daylight pouring down, striking the pale, rubbery cap of the fungal pedestal. I see the crystalline wintergrass refracting the light into passive, silent beams. I see the silver isopods frozen on the undersides of fronds, scattered beads catching the brightness in distorted pinpricks. I see the salamander, thick and glistening, the color of the inside of a wrist, its eyeless dome pressed to the root as if listening to the tectonic hum. I see the snails in their moisture traps, translucent coils mapping the pale geometry of their organs.

From my vantage, I am the chamber's only clock. The salamander's heart beats once a minute, but that is a biological rhythm, buried in flesh. My fall is the event. It is the only thing that happens here.

I swell. The condensation feeds me. I gather the weeping of the stone, the mist of the fungal breath, the damp exhalation of the moss. I grow heavy and taut. And then, at last, the equilibrium tips. I pull free.

The fall is long. The chamber is intimate—no larger than a modest room—but in my timescale, the distance is vast. I plummet through the cold, still air. I accelerate. Above me, the root vein springs back, freed from my weight. Below me, the shallow, clear pool waits, its surface a perfect, unbroken mirror of the light.

I hit the water.

And I make no sound.

The air is too dense with damp vegetation and porous stone; it absorbs the acoustic energy of my impact entirely. I displace the pool, sending concentric ripples spreading outward through the clear water, but the silence is absolute. Instead, my impact transmits as vibration—through the yielding moss of the floor, through the interlocking root systems, through the porous stone itself. The isopods feel it in the microscopic grip of their locked legs. The snails feel it in the fused sole of their single foot. The salamander feels it in the cartilage of its eyeless dome, a seismic whisper that says: *the water has fallen, the equilibrium holds*.

I am the sermon they know.

And then I am gone. I have become the pool. I seep into the soil, feeding the roots that lace the stone joints, nourishing the fungal pedestal that erupts from the dark earth. I am drawn up into the trailing liverworts, transpired into the cold air, condensed once more on the massive blocks above. The cycle begins again.

To be the water here is to be the only participant in a ritual of purest patience. The flora does not move; it only claims. The fauna does not act; it only occupies. The stone does not crumble; it only holds. But I—I fall. I break the stillness, not with sound, but with the profound, silent fact of my arrival. I am the single, held breath exhaled at last, only to be inhaled again.

I am the cold, brilliant messenger between the city above and the temple below. I carry nothing, I change nothing, I leave nothing behind. And in a place where the erosion is in perfect equilibrium, where the world has settled into its final shape and refused to change further, I am the only proof that time still passes.
