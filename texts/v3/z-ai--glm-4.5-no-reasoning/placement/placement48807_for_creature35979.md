I am not one of the gardeners, though I move among them. I am not one of the goats, though I climb where they climb. I am not the city, though I am not separate from it either. I am the pause between the drip and the splash. I am the moment when the fog touches the stone and waits, deciding whether to become water or to rise again. I am the space inside the carvings that could not have been made from outside—the negative, the hollow, the room that exists because the geometry required it to exist, not because anyone built it.

I came here moving. I remember that, though the memory has the quality of a story told about someone else. I remember walking up through the sharp granite, feeling the air thin and my lungs work. I remember the moment when the quiet first became audible—when the absence of sound became itself a presence, thick as wool, pressing against my ears until I could hear the small mechanisms of my own body working. I remember thinking *I should turn back* and then not turning back, because the path ahead was as possible as the path behind, and something in the equilibrium had already begun to exert its gentle pull.

That was—time is difficult here. I measure it now in condensation cycles, in the filling and emptying of the cistern nearest to the alcove where I most often sit. By that measure, I have been here long enough for the clay vessel I brought to crack and be replaced by one that was simply present one morning, gray and round-bellied, sitting where my old one had been as though it had always been there and I had only just failed to notice. I have been here long enough to stop looking for the seam in the wool the gardeners wear. I have been here long enough that my eyes, when I catch their reflection in the dark water of the cistern, have begun to shift—the blue I was born with carrying a filament of gray-green now, like moss beginning to grow across the surface of a still pond.

I am becoming. Not becoming *them*—that transformation, if it happens, will not be mine to observe; it will have always been the case, by the time it is true. But becoming something that fits here, the way water fits the shape that holds it. My edges are softening. Not physically; my hands are still my hands, rougher than theirs, still bearing the ghosts of the grip I once used to hold tools that do not exist in this city. But the edges of my *expectations*, the borders of what I find strange—these are wearing down, smoothed by the moss and the silence the way the stairways are smoothed by feet.

I have learned the gestures. Not all of them—I am not sure anyone learns *all* of them, or if the set is even finite. But I know the flat palm: *here, now, this*. I use it often, pressing my hand to the stone wall of my alcove in the morning when I wake, feeling the cold seep into my palm, confirming the reality of the surface, the moment, the self that touches. I know the two fingers tracing the wall: *water comes this way*. I have traced it myself, following the channels to their sources, finding the weeps and the drips with an accuracy that surprised me the first time and no longer does. I have not yet learned to tap messages. My rhythms are wrong—too fast, too irregular, carrying the urgency of a language that divides the world into questions and answers, needs and fulfillments. Their rhythms are a description, not a demand. They say *this is how it is* rather than *this is what I want*. I am learning to want less. Or rather, I am learning that what I want and what is are becoming the same thing, the way the water in the cistern takes the shape of its basin not because it has given up, but because it has found where it belongs.

I tend no garden, but I have begun to notice them with a gardener's attention. There is a terrace three hundred steps above my alcove—I know the count now, each stair distinct in my body the way words are distinct in a mouth—that holds a bed of the dark-leaved tubers. I did not plant them. No one planted them, so far as I can tell; they were emergent, the way the inhabitants are emergent, the way the ferns erupt from the joints between stones. But I find myself pausing there in the morning gray, examining the soil with my fingers, pulling away the moss that would crowd the sprouts too closely, redirecting a vine that has begun to climb a wall where no vine has climbed before. This is novel. This is *change*. The vine should not be there; it is a tiny deviation in the equilibrium, a new green thread in a pattern that was completed long ago. The gardeners have not noticed it, or if they have, they do not treat it as significant. But I notice it, and I tend it, and when I guide it along the channel where the condensation runs, I feel something that might be purpose, or might be the echo of purpose, a shape in my mind that matches the shape of the action without carrying the weight of necessity.

Perhaps this is what I am here for. Not the vine itself—it is too small a thing, and I am too uncertain of my own function to believe I was drawn up through the granite and the thin air for the sake of one climbing vine. But perhaps I am here to notice. The city does not notice itself; it simply is. The gardeners notice, but their noticing is part of the maintenance, a function of the system observing itself to sustain its equilibrium. My noticing is different. I notice the vine that should not be there, and I think *new*, and the thought carries a flavor that does not belong to this place—a flavor of before, of elsewhere, of a world where things accumulated and change was not cyclical but directional, where time was a river and not a basin.

I hold this flavor carefully. It is the last strange thing about me, and I am not sure whether it is a gift or a symptom. The city does not forbid strangeness; it simply absorbs it, the way the moss absorbs sound, the way the stone absorbs cold. My strangeness—my persistence in noticing the new, in remembering that there was a world where time passed and left marks—will either be the thing that keeps me distinct, or the thing that proves I was never distinct at all, that I was always just another expression of the city, a particular kind of awareness that it grows when it needs to notice itself from the outside.

At night, I stand on the highest terrace I can reach and look out at the fog-sea. The peaks rise like islands. The stars, when the clouds part, are ferociously clear—I had not known, before coming here, that clarity could be aggressive, that light could feel like a blade, laying bare the distance between myself and the cold fires above. I hear the breathing rhythm, the heartbeat of something very large and very slow. I have decided that it is the mountain. Not the city; the city has no heart, only rhythms, only the steady drip and the slow growth and the patient maintenance of what already is. The mountain has a heart. It was raised in violence, its granite teeth still jagged with the force of its becoming, and somewhere deep in its dark ribs, where the stone is still warm with the memory of magma, there is a pulse that measures time in the way the lowlands measure it—in increments that accumulate, in durations that *add up to something*.

The mountain remembers becoming. The city does not. The gardeners do not. The moss and the lichen and the ferns do not. Even the vine I tend, the new vine climbing the wall where no vine climbed before—even it does not remember a time before it grew; it simply grows, reaching toward the flat light with the same unthinking persistence that drives all green things. Only I remember. Only I carry the knowledge that there was a before, that there was a self who walked up through the sharp rocks and thought *I should turn back*, that there was a moment when the equilibrium was not yet achieved and leaving was still a category that made sense.

I will not leave. I know this with the same certainty that the gardeners know where the water weeps from the stone. Not because I cannot—I could find the paths down, steep and stony but passable, and I could descend through the thickening air to the lowlands where time accumulates and things decay and the mathematics of living require constant effort. I could return to urgency, to need, to the state of becoming that never rests because rest is death in a world that does not hold you. I could do this. The city would not stop me. The gardeners would not stop me. The cats and the crows and the goats with their amber horizontal eyes would watch me go with the same peripheral vigilance with which they watch me stay, and the equilibrium would adjust, the tiny perturbation of my presence smoothing out the way a footprint smooths out in moss, and in ten thousand drip-cycles there would be no sign I had ever been here at all.

But I will not go. Because I have found something here that the lowlands never offered, or offered and I was too busy moving to accept: the permission to *be* without *becoming*. The permission to persist without effort, to change without accumulating, to tend a small vine on a high terrace and call it enough. The gardeners have always known this. The city has always known this. I am learning it now, slowly, the way one learns a language not by studying its rules but by living inside its sounds, by letting the syntax rearrange the shape of thought until the strange becomes familiar and the familiar becomes strange and the distinction between them dissolves like fog in the first gray light of morning.

I am the stranger who stayed. I am the perturbation that did not smooth out, or has not yet—still here, still noticing the vine that climbs where no vine climbed before, still carrying the flavor of a world where time was a river. I am becoming moss-colored, moss-paced, moss-quiet. I am learning to tap rhythms that describe rather than demand. I am learning that the flat palm pressed to stone means *here, now, this*, and that this is enough. That this is, in the end, all anything ever means, in the lowlands or in the high cold city where the air is thin and the dripping measures out the moments like water filling a basin, and the equilibrium holds, and holds, and holds.

I think I am the city beginning to notice itself from the inside. I think I am what it grows when it needs to know that it goes on—that the terraces extend, the stairways climb, the dark openings in the mountainside lead to rooms that should not exist but do. I think the vine is not new at all. I think it has always climbed that wall, and I have always tended it, and the distinction between *arrived* and *always* is one that does not survive the transition to this place, where nothing accumulates and nothing decays and the only measure of time is the dripping, steady and unhurried, into cisterns that fill and fill and never overflow because the city has found its shape and holds it perfectly, and I am held within it, patient as stone, quiet as moss, persistent as the fog that comes each evening and weeps against the granite and is caught and is kept and is enough.
