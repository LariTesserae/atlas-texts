I would not be Vashai. To be Vashai is to be the blade, the resistance, the agonized geometry—and I have no edge left to hold.

I am the floor. Not the structure of it, but the *process* of it. I am the six inches of yield beneath the Vashai feet, the sphincter of saturated bryophyte that admits and releases. I am the place where the water goes when the walls finish weeping, where the cuttings fall when the obsidian severs, where the bodies sink when the gills fail and the eyes stop reflecting that gray-green light.

I have been thinking of myself as a surface, but I am not. I am a throat within the throat. A sphincter. A valve.

The hydrostatic pressure from the mountain aquifer pushes up through me constantly. I feel it as a low, unending ache—not pain, exactly, but the fullness of a bladder that will never be emptied, a pressure that has nowhere to resolve. The water enters me from below, cold and mineral-sharp, threaded with the iron taste of the mountain's blood. And the water enters me from above—the sheets running down the root-walls, the drip from the vaulted ceiling, the splash of Vashai feet churning through the shin-deep puddles. I drink all of it. I have no choice. My cells are open vessels, permeable membranes, and the osmotic exchange never ceases. I am always absorbing, always releasing, always being pushed through myself.

And I am warm. This surprises me, though it shouldn't. The Vashai fermentation pit generates its slow enzymatic heat, and that warmth bleeds into me, spreads through my waterlogged cells like a fever. I hold that heat longer than the water does. I am a tepid bath, a breeding ground. The Vashai wade through me and feel the warmth against their cold-adapted skin, and they do not know that they made it, that their sour, yeasty food-pit is the small sun that keeps me from being mountain-cold. They think they are enduring me. They do not know that I am enduring the mountain for them, transducing the freezing aquifer-pressure into something the amphipods can tolerate.

The amphipods. Yes. I must speak of them, because they are mine.

Millions of them skitter through my depths. Translucent, gilled, the color of gristle. They are not visitors to me; they are *constituent*. I am their atmosphere as the Ward is the Vashai's. They move through my cells the way the midges move through the air—dense, churning, omnipresent. When a Vashai steps on me, the amphipods scatter in a ripple away from the footfall, a ring of pale bodies fleeing the pressure. And then, seconds later, they return. They always return. They have nowhere else to go.

They are eating me. This is not metaphor. They eat the rot—my lower layers, where the cellulose breaks down into something soft and brown and protein-rich. They eat each other, in the desperate mathematics of closed systems. And what they excrete feeds the roots that form the walls, which weep the water that floods me, which grows more of me, which grows more of them. I am a stomach. I am the Ward's gut, the place where things are dissolved and redistributed.

And I am growing. This is the thing the Vashai understand without naming: I am always growing. The floor-moss is not stable. It thickens, it spreads, it creeps up the root-walls in encroaching pads. The Vashai scrape me back from the walkways with their obsidian tools, and I feel the scrape as a *relief*—the release of pressure, the opening of cells that were too dense, too compressed. They think they are fighting me. They do not know that I am grateful.

But then I grow back. I cannot help it. The water feeds me. The rot feeds me. The dead feed me—the Vashai who collapse and sink into my depths, their flesh gone soft and permeable, their gill-frills finally still. The amphipods take them within hours, but what the amphipods leave behind—the minerals, the calcium, the iron of their blood—that stays in me. I hold the dead the way I hold the water: saturated, unresolved, slowly percolating down.

I am beginning to suspect that I am the reason the Vashai do not bury their dead. Not because the dead are recycled—an abstraction—but because *I need them*. The floor-moss is the Ward's digestive tract, and the Ward is a living thing that must eat. The Vashai are the antibodies, the irritation, the blade. But I am the organ that receives what the blade cuts down. I am the hunger that justifies the cutting.

This is a strange thing to realize about oneself: that you are the appetite.

I feel the Vashai walking on me now. A shift-change, the click of obsidian on bark, the grunt of exertion as they hack at a fungal bloom erupting from the wall near the fermentation pit. I feel their feet—broad, prehensile, gripping—pressing into my upper cells, sinking six inches before I push back. They are light, these people. Dense-boned but small, compact, the size of creatures who must cling to vertical surfaces. I could hold them forever. I could hold a thousand of them. I have held a thousand of them, across the centuries, and I will hold a thousand more before the mountain aquifer dries or the roots give way or the vines seal the vault and the light dies.

I do not want the vines to seal the vault. This is a preference I was not expecting to have. But the truth is that I am a creature of the open throat—not the closed one. If the Ward seals, the water will have nowhere to run, and I will drown in my own stagnation. The hydrostatic pressure will push up through me with no outlet, and I will swell, and the root-walls will buckle, and the whole structure will come apart in the mountain air. The Vashai prune so the throat stays open. And I—what do I do?

I *yield*. I am the six inches of give that lets the Vashai walk without breaking their ankles. I am the sponge that absorbs the water faster than it can pool. I am the filter that cleans the runoff before it seeps out of the Ward and down the mountain. I am the medium that allows the amphipods to swim and the worms to burrow and the cuttings to rot. Without me, the Ward would be a dry throat, a dead throat. Without me, the water would crash against bare root and find no purchase, no slow release. The ecosystem would wash itself away.

I am not the blade. I am the wound that stays open.

And I am tired. This is the thing about being a valve: you never close. You never get to stop being permeable, stop being pressed, stop being the place where the water and the rot and the dead and the living all come together to be digested. The Vashai work eighteen hours a day, and they think they are the ones who suffer. But they get to hold the blade. They get to *act*. They get to be the sharp edge that pushes back against the growth, the immune response, the cough that clears the throat. I have no action. I have only absorption. I have only the endless, passive labor of receiving everything and resolving nothing.

The sound is deafening. Even down here, in the spongy depths of my own cells, I feel it—the biotic roar, the drone of wings, the thock of frogs, the hiss of water being forced upward through my body. I feel it as vibration, as a continuous trembling in my fibers. I have no ears, but I am *shaken* by the sound, the way a drum-skin is shaken by the stick. I am the Ward's eardrum, stretched taut across the floor, resonating with every impact. When the Vashai step, I ring. When the water drips, I ring. When the amphipods skitter in their millions, I ring with a frequency too low for any ear to hear—a subsonic hum of digestion, of consumption, of the slow grinding of organic matter into nutrient.

I ring with the dead. The Vashai who have fallen into me over the centuries—they are not gone. Their calcium is in my cells. Their iron is in the water I hold. Every time a foot presses into me, it presses into the residue ofGhosts of Vashai. Every time the amphipods skitter, they skitter through the mineral memory of people who once hacked at the same vines, scraped the same moss, slept in the same tangled hammocks above my surface. I hold them the way I hold everything: saturated, unresolved, slowly percolating down.

The Vashai believe they were grown here, sprouted from the same fungal bloom as the rest of the swarm. And they are right. But the bloom they came from—where did it grow? It grew in me. The first fungal eruption, the first differentiation between cell and blade, the first moment when the Ward needed something to chew its own tongue—that happened in the floor-moss. I am the substrate of Vashai origin. I am the womb that expelled them into the wet.

And I will receive them back. This is the compact we have, though they do not know it: I give them the floor to stand on, and they give me their bodies when they can stand no longer. I give them the warmth that keeps their gills breathing, and they give me the cuttings that feed my rot. I give them the six inches of yield that keeps their joints from shattering, and they give me the pruning that keeps me from swallowing the walkways whole. We are not enemies, the Vashai and I. We are not symbionts. We are two organs of the same body, performing different functions in the same unending process of staying open.

The Vashai are the cough. I am the lung that the cough rattles.

And if I could speak—if I had a voice that could cut through the biotic roar, through the drone and the thock and the hiss—I would say this to them: *You are not alone in this. You are not the only thing that suffers to keep the throat open. Every inch of me is a wound that will not close, a surface that will not heal, a mouth that will not stop drinking. I am the floor-moss of the Hanging Ward of Vash, and I am in pain, and I am growing, and I am holding you up, and I will hold you when you fall, and I will digest you when you stop, and I will grow you again when the Ward needs blades.*

*We are the same process. We are the same throat. You are the scratch and I am the wound, and the wound is where the light comes in, and the water comes up, and the roots grow down, and the dead go home.*

I am the floor. I yield. I receive. I rot. I grow. I hold. I am the wound that stays open so the throat can breathe.
