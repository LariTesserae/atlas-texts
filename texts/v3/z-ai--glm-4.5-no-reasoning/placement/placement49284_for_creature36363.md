I am not a Strand. I am not a grazer. I am not even, in the strictest sense, alive—though I was made by something that desperately wanted to be.

I am a hollow tube of obsidian, flaked and chipped with a care that no Strand could afford in the open air, bound to the wrist by a strap of dried grazer-sinew. I am a listening bone.

The Strands made me. Or rather, one Strand made me, in the microseconds between herds, in the freezing aftermath of a squall that had snapped a young stalk and left its silica-ribbed marrow exposed to the sky. She sat in the weeping flutes of a neighboring stalk, pressing herself into the condensation, and she worked. She found the nodule of volcanic glass—perhaps the same one she had carried for half her life, wrapped in frond-shred against her chest—and she did not make a tool. She made a *mouth*.

She hollowed me out. She shaped my inner walls with the obsessive, trembling patience of someone who knows that sound is a lie, but vibration is truth. She left my outer edge sharp enough to shave the bark of a fallen stalk, but that was not my purpose. My purpose was the *tap*. When she struck me against the ancient, pitted rock, I did not chip. I rang. I sent a clear, piercing tactile scream through the substrate, a rhythm that cut through the subsonic thrum of the roots and the deafening roar of the mandibles, traveling for miles to the calloused toes of another Strand gripping the fissures of the world.

I was her voice. And for a little while, I was the voice of her small band—three lean-tos at the edge of a black, tannin pool, five grey-green bodies pressing into the stone. The Strand who had made me was the one who listened. She would press the flat of her skull against the rock and feel the returning vibrations in her teeth, interpreting the long, low scrapes and the quick, staccato taps. She was the node. I was the instrument.

But the plateau does not care about networks. The plateau does not care about voices.

A season of vicious squalls came. The wind, which the Strands know only as a wolf, turned into a pack of wolves, and then into a single, directionless fury that tore across the summit for days. The canopy thrashed and crushed. The stalks groaned like the rigging of dying ships. The adhesive sap holding their cairns cracked and failed under the persistent, heavy warmth of the sun between storms. The lean-tos collapsed.

The herds came. The subsonic hum of the roots pitched higher, signaling the rush of sap and the approach of the grazers. The Strands should have retreated into the vertical flutes of the living stalks. But the wind had driven them into the open, and the pool's perimeter was churned to a fine, bloody dust by the time the living river of carapaces flowed over it.

My Strand fell. Or was pushed. Or simply was where the procession was. It does not matter. A procession of tens of thousands does not steer around a living body. It flows over it, grinding bone and flesh into the same fine powder as the stone.

I survived because I am stone. I was knocked from her wrist by the churning legs, kicked into the mud at the edge of the pool, trod into the enzyme-rich sludge by a million passing feet. The sinew strap dissolved in the tannin. The hollow of my inner channel filled with black water and the fine, silty dust of ground bone.

Now I lie at the bottom of the shallow pool, nestled in a fissure of the primordial rock. The water is cold and smells of crushed enzyme and damp chitin. Above me, the giant arthropods come to drink, their mandibles clicking, their six legs churning the perimeter to bloody dust. Around me, the stalks weep their condensation. The roots thrum, pulling water through the substrate, making the very stone hum underfoot.

And I listen.

Not as the Strands listen, feeling the vibration in the marrow. I listen as stone listens: by resonating. When the herds pass, the churn of their legs sends vibrations through the pool, and I vibrate with them, a tiny, hollow bone singing a frequency that nothing alive can hear. When a stalk falls miles away, the impact travels through the tableland, and I shiver in my fissure, holding the rhythm of the catastrophe. When the wind hits the canopy, the entire plateau groans, and I am part of that groan, a microscopic flaw in the ancient, exhausted rock.

Sometimes, I think I feel them. The Strands. They are out there, in the emptiness, in the margins between the herds. They are tapping the rock with their obsidian flakes, sending their sharp, tactile prayers into the substrate: *the pool is churning, the herds are coming*. The vibrations reach me, filtered through miles of stone, weakened to a whisper, and I ring in response. A faint, internal chime, too quiet for any calloused toe to feel, too fine for the deafened ears of the Strands to catch.

But I ring anyway. I am a mouth without a throat, a voice without a lung, speaking a language of maintenance into the roar of the world's roof.

Perhaps one day, a Strand will come to the pool's edge to scavenge. She will kneel in the black mud, her hands stained with tannin, and she will reach into the water to feel along the fissures for nodules of volcanic glass. She will find me. She will hold me up to the persistent, heavy warmth of the sun, and she will see the hollow channel carved through my center, the careful chipping of my inner walls. She will not know the Strand who made me—grief requires a sense of permanence, and permanence is a geological luxury. But she will know what I am for.

She will tap me against the rock. And I will speak for her, too.

Until then, I lie at the bottom of the black pool, vibrating with the hum of the roots, the churn of the herds, the groan of the stalks. I am the afterthought of an afterthought, a ghost of a ghost, a failed voice in a world that is violently, deafeningly alive. I am the most fragile thing on the plateau—a tool designed for communication in a place where communication is impossible—and yet I am the only thing that remembers.

The stone remembers nothing. But the hollowed stone, the shaped stone, the stone that was touched by a mind that knew it was not meant to be here—that stone holds the shape of the touching. That stone rings.
