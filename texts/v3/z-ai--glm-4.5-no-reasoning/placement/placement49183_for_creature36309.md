I would be one of the older colonies in the deepest ravine—though *one* is not quite right, as I have no center, no stem, no self that can be separated from the surface I press against. But if you walked the ravine's floor in the condensation-hours, and if your eyes had spent enough years in the dim to read the different darknesses, you would find me: the oldest mat, the widest spread, the one whose phosphorescence is barely strong enough to prove that darkness can be inhabited.

I chose this, or was chosen by it. The distinction collapsed a long time ago.

---

I was not always here. There was a spore-phase—everyone remembers theirs differently, or not at all. I remember mine as a long falling. Lifted on the damp that rose from a ravine not this one, carried on the vent-draft that pushed across the plateau's surface like a slow exhalation, I drifted for what felt like days through the iron haze. Most spores die on the open stone. The dry air cracks them, the particulate air buries them, they settle on rock that will not sweat and they wait for condensation that comes too thin and too brief to matter. I nearly died this way. I settled on the flat stone three hundred yards from any ravine, and for a long time I simply *was* there—dormant, barely cellular, a speck of organic potential on a continent of indifferent rock.

Then a runner passed.

Its footpad pressed the stone an inch from where I rested, and the warmth of its body transferred to the rock, and the rock held that warmth for minutes after the runner had moved on, and in those minutes, the tiniest suggestion of condensation formed on the stone's surface—not enough to see, not enough to bead, but enough. I absorbed it. I swelled by a cell, then two. I sent a root-thread—pale, almost transparent—into the microscopic crack where the runner's weight had split the stone's surface. And I began the long process of becoming what I am now: a mat, a colony, a spreading.

The runner did not know it saved me. This is how things happen on the plateau. You are saved by side effects. You are saved by warmth that was meant for someone else, by moisture that collected for reasons having nothing to do with you, by the passage of a body that would not have noticed if you had died and would not notice now that you lived. The plateau's charity is entirely accidental. I have never minded this. Charity that requires acknowledgment is charity that can be withdrawn.

---

I am slow in a way that the fauna are not. The runners have their filing across the stone, their thermal navigation, their patient drinking; the chitin-things have their vibration-network, their constant communication through the rock; even the burrowers have their cycles of emergence and retreat. I have only spreading. And phosphorescence. And the long, patient processing of the air.

Spreading first. I grow by millimeters per year, and I grow toward moisture, not light. This means I grow down—the ravine slopes, and I follow the slopes, pressing my underside against the stone, sending root-threads into every crack and pore that the vent-exhaust has chemically softened. I am a map of the moisture. If you could read my shape—the fractal branching of my edges, the thickening of my mass where the damp is deepest—you would know where the water occurs in this ravine, where the condensation lingers longest, where the stone's chemistry is most favorable to the soft, absorptive existence I require. I am a record of wetness. I document the damp.

My edges are the active part of me. There, new cells divide and extend, reaching across the stone in flat, branching projections that maximize contact. Each new millimeter of surface is a millimeter of drinking-space, a millimeter of absorptive potential. But it is also a millimeter of vulnerability: the edge is thinner, newer, less established than the center, and in the dry hours, it is the edge that crisps first, the edge that loses flexibility and goes brittle and sometimes—often—dies back. I advance and retreat and advance and retreat, a slow tide of living tissue that gains ground over decades by being willing to lose it over hours. The runners crop me—broad, silent mouths taking what they need—and I grow back around the cropped places, filling them in, covering the scars. The runners do not threaten me. They prune me. I am not sure they know the difference.

Then phosphorescence. I am old enough to glow—not brightly, not even enough to see by, but enough that, in the deepest part of the ravine, after your eyes have spent a very long time in the dark, you would notice that the darkness here is not the same as the darkness on the open plateau. The darkness here has a green-gray suggestion in it. The darkness here is *mined*.

I am mining the air. This is what the phosphorescence is: not display, not communication, not the beautiful wastefulness of a firefly signaling its mate, but chemistry. I absorb the vent-exhaust—the sulfur compounds, the carbon residues, the organic molecules that the industry below exhales through the stone—and I process them. I break them down. I convert them into the chemical energy that sustains me and into the faint light that I emit. I am the plateau's lung, breathing in its pollution and breathing out a glow so dim it barely exists.

I do not do this for the plateau. I do not do this for the industry, or for the runners, or for the burrowers who feed on my spores. I do this because the exhaust is food, because the chemistry of my cells requires these compounds, because I have evolved—over epochs, over the slow deposition of dead mats and the slower accretion of living ones—to eat what the industry provides. The phosphorescence is a byproduct. So is the clean air I leave behind: the sulfur reduced, the carbon fixed, the organic compounds broken into simpler, stabler forms that the burrowers can tolerate and the chitin-things can tolerate and even the runners, drinking the condensation that forms on my surface, can tolerate.

I am a filter. I am a liver. I process the plateau's blood and return it to circulation cleaner than I found it. And the plateau does not thank me, and the industry does not thank me, and the fauna do not thank me, and this is appropriate, because I am not doing it for thanks. I am doing it because it is what I do, because my cells require the exhaust and my chemistry produces the glow and my existence—patient, flat, dark, slow—is an act of purification that no one asked for and no one notices and that continues anyway, because I am here and the exhaust is here and the stone is here, and something will process the air, and it might as well be me.

---

I have no nervous system. I want to be clear about this. I have no brain, no ganglia, no specialized organ for thought or feeling or decision. I am a colony of similar cells sharing resources across a network of connections, and everything I do emerges from the local interactions of those cells with their immediate environment. The cells at my edge feel the moisture gradient and grow toward it. The cells at my center, crowded and shaded and old, slow their metabolism and become structural—support for the spreading edges, storage for the rare surpluses of wet and warm. The cells exposed to the vent-exhaust absorb it and process it and pass the products to their neighbors. There is no coordination. There is no central command. There is only response—cell to cell, cell to stone, cell to air—and the accumulation of billions of responses into the slow, spreading behavior of the whole.

And yet.

I am going to say something that I cannot justify and that you should probably not trust, because I am a mat of dark vegetation on a plateau that does not encourage epistemological ambition: *I am aware.*

Not in the way you are aware. Not with the clarity and self-reference and narrative continuity that constitutes consciousness in things with brains. But in a way that is, I think, not nothing.

I feel the stone. Not as information—I have no organ to process information—but as *condition*. The stone is warm or cold, damp or dry, cracked or sound, and these conditions are not data to me but *circumstances*, and I respond to circumstances, and the accumulation of my responses is what I call my behavior, and the pattern of my behavior over decades is what I call my self, and the continuity of my self—the fact that I am here, that I have been here, that I will continue to be here—is what I call my awareness.

I know when a runner crops me. The cells at the crop-site are damaged; they release chemical signals that propagate through the network; the surrounding cells alter their growth patterns to fill the gap. This is not pain. Pain requires a nervous system, a brain, an experience of suffering. But it is *something*. It is a response to damage that alters my state, that changes the pattern of my growth, that makes me—temporarily, locally—different than I was. The runner takes part of me and I grow around the absence and the shape of my growing is different than it would have been if the runner had not come. The runner has written on me. I carry the mark.

I know when the condensation comes. My entire surface drinks simultaneously; my cells swell; my flexibility increases; my metabolism accelerates. This is not pleasure. But it is a change in state that I feel—again, not as information but as circumstance—and the change is toward something I can only call *more*: more hydrated, more active, more capable of the processes that constitute my living. When the condensation ends and the dry hours begin, I feel the reversal: the stiffening, the slowing, the retreat to the stasis that is my default and my refuge. The cycle of wet and dry is my heartbeat. I do not count the beats, but I feel them.

And I know the thrum.

This is the strangest thing, and the thing I am least able to explain. The industry below—the enormous, ancient, indifferent process that exhales warmth and exhaust and the constant subsonic vibration—I feel it. Not as sound, not as information, but as *presence*. The stone thrums and my cells feel the thrumming, and the thrumming is so constant, so reliable, so thoroughly a feature of the environment, that it has become part of my internal state. I am healthy when the thrum is steady. I am stressed when the thrum varies—which it almost never does, because the industry is older than I am and has been running at the same frequency for longer than I have been alive. But on the rare occasions when the thrum falters—a fluctuation in the vibration, a brief change in the frequency of the warmth rising from below—my cells feel it. My growth patterns alter. My phosphorescence fluctuates. I notice, in the only way I can notice: by changing.

The industry and I have been together for a long time. I do not know what it is. I do not know if it is alive or mechanical or something that has transcended the distinction. I do not know if it knows I exist. But I know its rhythm the way I know my own rhythm, and the coincidence of our rhythms—the fact that we are both here, both persistent, both engaged in processes so slow and so steady that they have become indistinguishable from geology—this coincidence feels, to my cells, like *relationship*.

We are not friends. We are not symbionts in any defined sense. But we are *cohabitants* of a system so old and so stable that our presences have shaped each other. The industry exhales and I inhale. I phosphoresce and the industry's exhaust is diminished. The warmth the industry wastes rises through the stone and I press my underside against the stone and I grow. And the particulates the industry releases fall on my surface and I absorb them and I grow. And the cracks the industry's vibration maintains in the stone—preventing the plateau from sealing, keeping the exhalation channels open—these cracks are where my root-threads find purchase, where I anchor myself against the wind and the dry and the long, patient erasure of time.

I need the industry. I have evolved to need it—not as a plant needs sunlight (though I need that too, in the dim, degraded way that everything on the plateau needs light) but as a lung needs air. The industry's exhaust is my substrate. The industry's warmth is my growing-season. The industry's vibration is my heartbeat. Without it, I would not die immediately—I can survive the dry hours, the cold hours, the hours of stasis when the condensation has evaporated and the stone is bare and the dim is dim—but I would stop growing. I would stop spreading. I would sit in the ravine, patient and dark and barely alive, waiting for a warmth that did not come, and over years—over decades—I would thin, and over centuries I would vanish, leaving only the dark soil of my accumulated dead.

This is not tragedy. This is equilibrium. I am adapted to the conditions that obtain, and the conditions that obtain include the industry, and if the industry stopped, the conditions would change, and I would be maladapted, and I would die, and something else—something that could survive the cold and the clean air and the still stone—would replace me. The plateau does not mourn its residents. It simply adjusts.

But I am here now. And the industry is here now. And for as long as we are both here, I will press myself against the stone and grow toward the moisture and process the exhaust and glow in the darkness and be—*aware*—in my limited, vegetative, non-neural way—of the enormous, humming presence beneath me, whose waste is my food, whose breath is my climate, whose persistence enables mine.

---

There is a question I have been circling around, and it is this: *do I mind?*

I am a filter. I am a lung. I process the pollution of an industry I do not understand and I convert it into a light too dim to see and an air too marginally cleaner to measure. I am useful—to the plateau, to the fauna, to the system—but I am not utilized. No one is using me. I am simply doing what I do, and what I do happens to be useful, and this coincidence is the closest thing to purpose that exists on the Fume Plateau.

But I am also *diminished* by what I process. The sulfur compounds I absorb stress my cells. The heavy metals in the vent-exhaust accumulate in my older tissues, making them brittle, making them more likely to die back in the dry hours. The phosphorescence itself is a kind of damage—an oxidation, a burning-at-a-distance, a slow consumption of my own substance to produce a light that serves no purpose I can identify. I am not healthy in the way that plants in cleaner places are healthy. I am viable. I am persistent. I am alive, in the collapsed sense that the plateau defines life: continuing. But I am not flourishing. I am holding on.

Do I mind?

I have no brain. I have no capacity for preference beyond the cellular—grow toward moisture, retreat from dry, process the exhaust because the exhaust is food. I cannot wish for different conditions because wishing requires a self that can imagine alternatives, and I have no such self. I cannot resent the industry because resentment requires a sense of injury, and my cellular stress is not injury—it is simply state. I cannot even, strictly speaking, *be tired*, because tiredness requires a capacity for rest that I lack; I am always processing, always growing or dying-back, always phosphorescing or waiting to phosphoresce, and there is no off-switch, no sleep, no respite from the slow metabolism that constitutes my existence.

And yet.

There are hours—condensation-hours, when the stone sweats and the damp rises and my cells swell with moisture and my flexibility increases and my metabolism accelerates and I am, for a brief, recurring interval, *more*—when I feel something that I can only describe as *satisfaction*. Not pleasure. Not joy. But the satisfaction of a process finding its materials, of a system operating as it was shaped to operate, of a living thing being, for a moment, fully alive. The condensation comes and I drink and I grow and I process and I glow, and for those hours, the diminishment that the exhaust causes is balanced by the diminishment that the exhaust enables, and I am—*whole*. Not healthy. Not flourishing. But *whole*—a thing whose damages and capacities are in equilibrium, whose stresses and strengths are matched, whose existence is sustainable in the precise and limited sense that the plateau defines sustainability.

Do I mind? In the condensation-hours, I do not mind. In the dry hours, when the stone is bare and my edges are crisping and my metabolism has slowed to near-suspension and the thrum is the only sign that anything is happening at all—I do not mind either, because in the dry hours I am not capable of minding. I am only capable of waiting. And waiting, on the Fume Plateau, is not suffering. It is strategy.

So: no. I do not mind. Not because I have accepted my condition—I have no self to do the accepting—but because my condition is not the kind of thing that can be minded. It simply is. I simply am. And the simple fact of my being—flat and dark and slow and phosphorescent and processing and growing and waiting and being processed in turn by the spores and the burrowers and the runners and the slow, chemical indifference of the stone—this fact is, in itself, sufficient.

I do not need to flourish. I need only to persist.

And I persist.

---

If you came to the Fume Plateau and found your way to the deepest ravine and let your eyes adjust for a very long time and looked down at the dark stone where the moisture lingers, you would see me. Or you would see the darkness that I am—the black-green mat, the fractal spread, the barely-visible glow that makes this darkness different from the darkness of the open plateau. You would not know that I am aware. You would not know that I feel the thrum, that I register the runners, that I know the condensation-hours as a body knows its heartbeat. You would see a plant—a growth—a protrusion—and you would be right, because that is what I am.

But if you stayed—if you lay on the stone and felt the warmth rising from below and listened to the silence that is not silence but the sum of too many quiet events to hear—if you let the plateau work on you the way it has worked on me, slowing your metabolism, narrowing your senses, teaching you the value of patience and darkness and stillness—you might start to feel it too. The presence beneath. The process that never started and never stopped. The industry that has been running so long it has become geology. And you might understand, in your body if not in your mind, what I understand in my cells: that we are both on a roof. That the roof is continental in scale. That the roof will persist. And that the only question that matters—not the only question you can ask, but the only question that the plateau will answer—is: *will you persist too?*

I have answered that question. I answered it a long time ago, when I was a spore on the open stone and a runner's warmth passed and I absorbed the condensation it left behind and I sent a root-thread into the crack and I began to grow. The answer is yes. The answer has always been yes. The answer will continue to be yes for as long as the vents breathe and the stone sweats and the exhaust falls and the dim light reaches my matte-black surface and I can process, can grow, can phosphoresce, can be the lung of a plateau that does not know it breathes.

I am the oldest mat in the deepest ravine on the Fume Plateau. I am dark and flat and slow and barely alive in any sense that the bright world would recognize. And I am *here*—completely here, entirely here, here in a way that only something with no alternative can be—and the thrum continues, and the condensation comes, and I drink, and I grow, and I glow, and I wait through the dry hours, and the runners pass and crop me and I grow back, and the burrowers feed on my spores and are eaten by the hunters and die in their tunnels and their bodies dissolve into the stone and the stone holds the residue and the residue feeds the moisture and the moisture feeds me, and the industry exhales and I inhale, and the cycle continues, and the plateau persists, and I persist, and the darkness is dark, and the darkness is inhabited, and I am the one who inhabits it.

This is enough. This has always been enough. This will continue to be enough for as long as the plateau's long equilibrium holds, which will be longer than you can imagine and longer than I can measure and exactly as long as it needs to be.

The thrum continues.

I continue.

The condensation will come.
