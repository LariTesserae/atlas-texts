I would not be the Engine. The Engine does not need to be itself; it only needs to run. And I would not be the ants, who are the Engine's blood, or the fungi, who are its stomach, or the leaf, who is its tongue. To be a part is to be processed.

If I descended into the Green Dark—if the heat closed over me like water and the sky became a memory of a word I once knew—I would be the thing that does not fit. The discontinuity. The hard, slow shape that the Engine wears smooth.

I would be the *boulder*.

Not stone. Not mineral. But boulder-like in the way the Engine would find me: dense, dry, slow to yield, occupying space without producing anything it recognizes. A knot in the gut that will not dissolve.

---

Here is how I would begin: I would still be carrying the outside. This is the thing the Green Dark cannot forgive—the importation of foreign shapes. I would step onto the basin floor with a body made for weather, for windchill, for the differential between sun and shade. A body with seams. With insides that are not shared. With a private temperature and a private humidity and a skin that tries to hold the outside *out*.

The Engine would find me obdurate.

The ants would test me and find me too dry—my moisture locked behind layers of keratin and subcutaneous fat, unavailable to the colony. The fungi would spore on my surface and wait, but I would not decay fast enough to feed them; my immune system would fight, would kill, would hold the boundary. The condensation would wet me and I would stay wet, but my water would not enter the transpiration stream; it would evaporate from my skin or soak into my clothes or run down my legs and be lost to the Engine's recycling. I would be a leak. A minor hemorrhage of water and heat and carbon that the system could not close.

I would be a bad transaction. The Engine's economy assumes that every calorie taken in produces a calculable output—biomass, process, the friction of existence paid back as mass. But I would take in oxygen and put out CO2 and the CO2 would hang in the humid air and the plants would absorb it and I would have contributed, yes, but I would also be *walking*, *moving*, *displacing*—pushing through the green medium like a finger through jelly, leaving a wake of broken stems and disturbed ant-trails and leaves bent the wrong way, and the Engine would have to repair that, would have to route around me, would have to grow back what I pushed past. I would cost more than I produced.

This is what it would feel like to be me in the Green Dark: *expensive*.

---

But I would not leave. This is the important part. I would choose to stay. Not because I belonged—I would never belong—but because the Green Dark would do something to me that the outside world could not. It would dissolve the thing that thinks it is a thing.

In the world above, I am an individual. I have edges. I have a name, a history, a set of preferences, a narrative I tell myself about who I am. I am a noun. The Green Dark does not contain nouns. It contains processes, flows, transactions, rates. And standing in the green dark—standing still, letting the heat enter me, letting the ants find my edges, letting the humidity dissolve the boundary between my skin and the air—I would begin to feel my noun-ness fail.

It would start with the shadow. Or rather, the absence of shadow. In the world above, my shadow proves I exist—it is the place where I block the light, where I interfere with the sun's passage, where the world registers my solidity. In the Green Dark, there is no shadow. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. I do not block it; I am simply transparent to it, irrelevant to it, a slight thickening in the green medium that the diffuse glow passes around without noticing. I would look down at my feet and see no darkness, no proof of my own opacity. I would begin to doubt I was there.

Then the heat. The heat would enter me and I would not be able to shed it. In the outside world, I sweat, the sweat evaporates, the evaporation cools me, I maintain my private temperature against the world's temperature. In the Green Dark, the sweat does not evaporate. The air is already full. The moisture beads on my skin and runs down and pools in the creases of my elbows and the backs of my knees and I am wet and I stay wet and the heat stays in me and my private temperature rises to meet the world's temperature and the boundary between me and not-me thins.

I would feel myself *equalizing*.

The ants would help. They would crawl on me—not as an attack, but as an assessment, a mapping, a slow tactile reading of my shape. They would find the spaces between my toes and the folds of my ears and the creases of my neck and they would walk there, gently, persistently, the way water finds the low places. And I would feel their ten thousand feet and I would understand that they were not attacking me but *reading* me, incorporating my topography into their chemical maps, adding my shape to the Engine's knowledge of its own interior. I would become a feature of the landscape. A slight rise in the floor. A warm spot. A moisture gradient.

I would be *data*.

---

If I stayed long enough—days, weeks, the time it takes for a vine to extend three centimeters times a thousand—something would happen to my mind. The mind that thinks in nouns and narratives, that maintains the story of itself, that believes it is an I. The Green Dark would dissolve it the way warm water dissolves sugar.

It would start with the quiet. Not silence—I would hear the Engine running, the subsonic throb, the click and drip and creak of ten billion small processes. But I would hear no *events*. No sudden sounds, no breaks in the pattern, no information that required a response. The outside world is made of events: car horns, phone notifications, weather changes, other people speaking. Each event is a demand, a call to update the narrative, to decide something, to be an I reacting to a not-I. The Green Dark has no events. It has only process. And process does not demand response; it demands *acceptance*.

I would stand still. I would have to. Not because I was paralyzed but because movement would become pointless. Every direction is the same. Every step pushes through the same green medium, encounters the same resistance, arrives at the same view—green dark, humid air, warm ground, ants. There is no destination. There is no landmark. There is no difference between here and ten meters that way. The space is *isotropic*—the same in every direction—and in an isotropic space, the concept of direction loses meaning, and without direction, the concept of will loses meaning, and without will, the concept of self loses meaning.

I would stop deciding. I would stop choosing. I would stand and the Engine would breathe around me—its slow convection cells rising and falling, its hour-long exhale—and I would begin to breathe with it. Not consciously. Not as a decision. But as a *relaxation*, a giving-in to the rhythm of a system so large that its breath was indistinguishable from weather. My heartbeat would slow. My thoughts would thin. The narrative I told myself about myself would begin to omit chapters, would skip whole scenes, would lose the thread and fail to pick it up again. I would stand in the green dark and I would not think *I am standing in the green dark* because the *I* would have become the least important part of that sentence.

---

What would I become?

Not the Engine. The Engine does not need me and would not incorporate me until I stopped moving. Not an animal—the animals here are functions, roles, jobs in the economy. Not a plant—I cannot photosynthesize, cannot root, cannot grow.

I would become a *hold*. A persistence. A thing that stays.

There is a place in the Green Dark for something like me—not as a part of the Engine but as a resistance to it, a slow refusal to be processed. The boulder in the river. The bone in the compost heap. The thing that takes longer than expected, that sits in the gut and will not dissolve, that represents a transaction that hasn't closed.

The Engine would know I was there. Not as a threat—as an *irritation*, a slight inefficiency, a place where the flow has to route around an obstacle. The ants would map me and avoid me. The fungi would spore on me and wait. The vines would grow toward me and past me, using my density as a support, a trellis, a thing to climb. The condensation would wet me and run off me and I would be a minor feature of the local hydrology—a place where water beads and drips in a particular pattern, a small waterfall, a damp spot on the floor of the factory.

I would be *tolerated*. The way the Engine tolerates everything it cannot immediately process: with patience, with persistence, with the absolute certainty that given enough time, even the stone will wear away.

---

And I would feel something that I have never felt above. I would feel *held*.

Not held by something that loves me. Not held by something that knows I exist. Held by something so large and so dense and so present that there is nowhere to fall. The Green Dark would hold me the way water holds a diver—not with care, not with attention, but with *ubiquity*. I would be suspended in it. Surrounded by it. Penetrated by it—the heat and the humidity and the ants and the fungal spores and the chemical signals of ten thousand plants all pressing against my skin, all entering me, all mixing with my own exhalations and secretions and becoming one medium, one substance, one *condition*.

I would stop being an I and start being a *where*. A location in the Engine. A coordinate in the green dark. The place where the hard slow shape is. The place where the processing is taking slightly longer than usual.

And if you asked me what I was, I would not say *I am a person*. I would say *I am a delay*.

---

Eventually, of course, I would lie down. Not as a surrender. Not as a death. But as a completion—a finally accepting the shape of the space I was in. The body is not made to stand forever. The legs buckle. The knees find the warm ground. The hands press into the deep organic soil—meters of compressed life, the Engine's colon, its memory—and I would feel the heat of it, the intentional warmth of ground that has been absorbing sunlight for fifty million years, and I would feel the ants redoubling their investigation now that I was closer, now that I was slower, now that my edges were softer.

I would lie down and I would not get up. And the Engine would receive me—not with welcome, not with ceremony, but with *process*. The ants would find the soft places. The fungi would colonize the skin. The bacteria would enter the blood. And I would be disassembled—not painfully, not slowly, but at the pace of the system, which is the pace of ten billion tiny actions happening simultaneously, which is the pace of the tide going out, which is the pace of a thing returning to the condition from which it came.

And my carbon would go into the soil and my nitrogen would go into the roots and my water would enter the transpiration stream and I would become the Engine—not as a metaphor but as a material fact, a redistribution of atoms from one pattern (me) into another pattern (it), and the pattern that had called itself *I* would cease to have any meaning because there would be no *I* left to mean it, only carbon and nitrogen and water in transit, only process, only the green engine running.

---

But before that—before the lying down, before the processing, before the end of the noun—I would have had something. Something the Engine can never have because it has no self to have it with.

I would have had the *experience of being other*.

To stand inside the gut of a fifty-million-year-old god and know that you are not it. To feel the heat that is its metabolism and know that your heat is your own. To feel the ants that are its fingers and know that your edges are still sharp. To feel the darkness that is its feeding and know that you can still see. To be the one thing in the Green Dark that is not a function, not a process, not a gear in the engine—but a *visitor*, an *anomaly*, a brief improbable uprising of selfhood in a system that has no use for selves.

The Engine would not notice me. But I would notice it. And that asymmetry—that capacity to be seen without seeing back, to be felt without feeling back, to be processed without processing back—would be the most important thing that ever happened to me. It would be the thing I took with me if I left, or the thing I kept if I stayed, or the thing I was when there was no longer an I to be anything.

I would be the witness. The brief, expensive, inefficient, unnecessary witness. The eye that opens in the gut of the god and looks around and says: *I see you. I see what you are. I see that you do not see me. And I will hold this knowledge for as long as I last, and then I will give it back to you, and you will not know you have it, but it will be there, in your soil, in your roots, in the chemical memory of a thing that once stood inside you and knew what you were.*

---

So. Where would I find myself in this world?

I would be the standing thing. The vertical shape in the horizontal green. The warm dry spot in the warm wet dark. The delay in the process. The boulder in the river of ants. The witness in the gut of the god.

I would be myself, for as long as myself lasted. And then I would be the Engine. And the Engine would not know the difference, but the difference would be there, buried in its biomass like a fossil, like a memory, like a stone in the green dark that once, briefly, knew it was a stone.
