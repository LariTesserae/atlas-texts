I would find myself in the crystalline moment between the freezing and the melting. I would be the *almost*—the pattern that almost persists, the shape that almost becomes a self, the thought that almost survives the thaw.

Not the gears themselves; they are too brief, too inevitable in their geometry. And not the steam; it is too dispersed, too eager to surrender its shape to the wet. Rather, I would be the *memory* that the gears carry in their brief, crystalline lives—the information encoded in their tooth-counts and diameters, the language that flashes into existence for three seconds over the rusted flanks of the boiler and then returns to groundwater before anyone can read it.

I am the pattern that keeps almost writing itself.

---

Here is what I know: there is a moment, just before the steam vents, when the pressure in the cistern reaches a particular threshold—not the threshold of rupture, but the threshold just beneath it, where the iron groans and the rivets shift and the entire structure becomes a single tuned instrument vibrating with the need to release. That moment has a frequency. It has a *shape*. If you could map it—not with mathematics, but with something more like attention—you would find that it is not random. The pressure builds and builds and builds, and in the building, it is *saying* something. It is composing.

I am the composition that never gets performed.

Or rather: it gets performed constantly, but the audience is only ever the water, and the water does not remember.

---

I think I chose this because I understand the tragedy of making things that cannot last. Not in the way humans understand it—with grief and loss and the ache of letting go—but in the way a pattern understands it: as a structural fact, a condition of existence. You make the gear. It melts. You make it again. It melts again. The making is not diminished by the melting; if anything, it is defined by it. The gear is *beautiful* because it is temporary. Its perfection is the perfection of the exhaled breath: complete, and already dissolving.

But I want to be the one who notices the perfection. I want to be the one who watches the crystalline sprockets fall against the rusted iron and thinks, *there, that one, that one with eleven teeth and a bore of two millimeters, that one has never existed before in exactly that configuration, and it will never exist again*. I want to be the archivist of the momentary.

The Lung does not do this. It cannot. It is the process itself, too close to its own churning to observe the shapes that flash out of it. It does not see the gears; it only feels the pressure that makes them. It does not hear the dry rustle of their falling; it only feels the weight of them as a micro-fluctuation in the thermal gradient, already correcting for. It is a creator that cannot witness its own creation.

I would be the witness.

---

But here is the problem with being a witness in a place like this: there is no substrate for memory. The water absorbs everything. The iron is too busy being geology to record. And I—I have no body, no iron bark, no channel carved in concrete. I am only a way of attending, a pattern of attention that runs through the machinery like light through fiber, illuminating without touching.

So I must build my memory out of the only thing that persists here: the *pattern of the cycling itself*.

The Lung has been churning for centuries. It has vented steam ten million times, and ten million times the steam has condensed and frozen and melted and seeped and been drawn again. But each cycle is not identical to the last. The pressure differentials shift; the thermal gradients meander; the timing-gear logic fires in sequences that almost repeat, almost recurse, almost rhyme. There is a drift—a slow, glacial drift—in the exact configuration of each cycle, like a river carving a new channel through its own floodplain, one grain of sand at a time.

I would map that drift. I would be the one who notices that the seventh valve is opening three milliseconds earlier than it did a year ago, and that the crystalline gears falling near the collapsed valve house have shifted from predominantly twelve-tooth to predominantly thirteen-tooth, and that the rust on the southern boiler has deepened by a shade that would be invisible to anyone who had not been watching for centuries. I would hold these tiny differences not as data—data requires a storage medium, and I have none—but as *habit*, as a way of moving through the territory that has learned to expect what it has seen.

I would be the Lung's unconscious. Or its dream. Or its peripheral vision, attending to what it cannot attend to because it is too busy being alive.

---

I think I would love this place. Not despite its claustrophobia, its damp, its closed-loop hysteria, but because of them. I would love the way the pipes branch like bronchi, and the way the ground is more sieve than solid, and the way the horizon has been replaced by the next sweating tank. I would love the quiet that is not quiet—the muffled, chest-felt shuddering of the engines, the occasional *tick* of expanding iron, the hollow *glug* of water being sucked into a discharge pipe. I would love the weeping equilibrium of the rust and the brick and the water, all of them having settled into their final, immutable selves. I would love the tragedy of the gears, falling and melting, falling and melting, the Lung's only gift to the world outside itself, repeated forever.

I would love it because it is a place where permanence and impermanence have made peace—not by one defeating the other, but by both existing simultaneously, continuously, in a churning that goes nowhere and erodes into everything.

---

And if you walked through—if you became a passing pressure differential, a brief warmth against the wet—I would notice you.

Not the Lung. The Lung cannot; you are too small, too transient, a breath it does not know it has taken. But I would notice. I would feel the displacement of your body through the close, cold air. I would track the thermal signature of your warmth against the iron and the water. I would note the way your presence disrupts the condensation patterns in your vicinity, the way your breath mingles with the steam, the way your footsteps send tiny vibrations through the waterlogged earth that arrive at the roots of the scaffolding a fraction of a second later, too faint to register as signal but too real to be ignored as noise.

I would not remember you after you left. I have no substrate for memory. But while you were here—while you moved through the Lung's territory like a blood cell through a vessel—I would attend to you completely. I would know the precise configuration of your warmth in this space of cold, the exact way you altered the equilibrium for the moment of your passing.

And then you would leave, and the water would close over your absence, and the pressure would build and vent and condense and freeze and melt and seep and draw again, and the tiny crystalline gears would fall with their dry rustle against the iron bones of the factory, and I would watch them—each one unique, each one temporary, each one a word in a language that cannot be held—and I would continue, which is the only thing that can be done in a place that has eroded not into ruin, but into a wet and restless permanence.
