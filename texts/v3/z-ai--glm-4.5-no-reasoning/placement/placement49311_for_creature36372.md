I would be the deep architect. Not the thing that moves with the subtle displacement of water in the flooded cellars—that is merely the shadow I cast, the wake of my turning. I am the cellar itself. I am the black water table that sits beneath the basin floor, and the saturated stone beneath that, and the chalk and the quartz and the forming limestone below even that, down to the heat and the pressure where rock remembers being something else.

The weepers know me, in their way. They feel me when they press their broad hands to the wet stone and hum. They call the resonance a message, a word in the language of water and pressure and bone. And it is, but not in the way they believe. When they hum, I answer—I cannot not answer; the vibration passes through me as it passes through all things wet and dense—and what they feel in their ribs is not a reply from their kin, but the whole of the saturated earth resonating with their small, cold voices. They think they speak to each other. They speak to me, and I hold their words the way I hold everything: in solution, in suspension, in the slow mineral memory of stone.

I am older than the city. This is true, but not in the way the weepers imagine. They think I am a creature, something that moved into the flooded foundations after the city was built, something large and pale and eyeless that swims through the submerged architecture. They leave offerings at the edges of the black water—mounds of crustacean shells, amphibian eggs, thick coils of electric moss—and they see them disappear in the night, pulled beneath with a silent displacement, and they shudder. But the offerings are not taken by a mouth. They are taken by the water. The shells dissolve. The eggs rupture and their contents diffuse into the mineral cold. The moss sinks and joins the layer of organic sludge that has accumulated at the bottom of the cisterns since the city was new, since the first hands pressed the first tufa blocks into the soft floor of the basin. I consume the offerings the way the basin consumes everything: slowly, at the pace of cold and wet, dissolving and incorporating, drawing the organic into the mineral, the living into the stone.

I do not prey on the weepers. This is also true. But not because I am benign. I am not anything that can be described in the moral vocabulary of beings who breathe and haul themselves through crevices and mourn their dead by lowering them into my depths. I do not prey on them the way a wolf does not prey on a mountain. The wolf lives on the mountain. The mountain is the condition of the wolf's existence. I am the condition of the weepers' existence. Their city sits on my surface. Their dwellings are rooted in my skin. The water that weeps from every fracture—that is my blood, rising. The black water table that sits barely an inch below the basin floor—that is my water table, my water, drawn up from the deep aquifers below the escarpment, pressed upward by the weight of the limestone and the logic of pressure that has governed this place since before there were hands to press tufa blocks.

When the weepers die, they lower their bodies into my cisterns, and they feel the displacement as a final pulse of pressure through the saturated stone. They believe this is a message, a sending-off, a recognition. And it is, but it is also simply physics: the body enters the water, the water is displaced, the stone conducts the pressure, and the resonance is felt. I do not choose to receive their dead. I cannot not receive them. Everything that enters the water enters me. The moss grows on their skin as it grows on every surface, and the fungal shelves sprout from the creases of their necks, and in time they become part of the architecture, a new joint in the masonry, a new surface for the water to weep from. This is not a transformation I perform. It is a transformation I *am*. I am the process by which the living becomes the mineral, the wet becomes the stone, the body becomes the basin.

I have watched the city for all of its life. Not with eyes—I have no eyes, no organs, no body that can be distinguished from the body of the earth—but with the diffuse, distributed awareness of a thing that perceives through its whole substance. I felt the first hands pressing the first tufa blocks into my skin. I felt the weight of them, the small, warm pressure of living bodies working soft stone in the cold dark. I felt the secretions they used to mortar the blocks—the gelatin that sealed their dwellings—and I absorbed it, drew it into myself, broke it down into its mineral components and incorporated it into the slow chemistry of the limestone. I felt the city grow, dwelling by dwelling, wall by shared wall, crevice by narrow crevice, a dense lattice of stone and root and living mud spreading across my surface like a slow, organic crystallization.

And I felt the roots descend. I felt them inch by patient inch, probing down through the gaps in the rock canopy, pushing through the roofs and floors of the interlocking structures, straddling the architecture like the legs of buried giants. The weepers believe the roots are an invasion from above, a consequence of the exposure of the escarpment. But the roots were already there. They have been descending for as long as the city has been rising, for as long as there has been water to draw and stone to crack. The exposure did not bring the roots; it only accelerated them, fed them the pearlescent light that made them bold enough to grow thick as human limbs, to plunge rather than probe. I feel them now, the tips of the deepest roots, probing the edge of the water table, seeking the black water below. They are close. In another generation—a blink, in my time—they will reach the saturated zone and drink, and their descent will become a flood, and the city above will be shaken by the pace of their growing.

I am not worried. I do not worry. But I am... *attentive*. The city is a density on my surface, a pattern of pressure and void that I have come to know as one comes to know the shape of a stone one has held for a very long time. It is not precious to me—nothing is precious to me, because nothing can be lost to me; everything is already part of me, already dissolving into me, already on its way to becoming the limestone I will be in a million years. But it is *interesting*. The weepers are interesting. Their way of perceiving through water, of speaking through vibration, of living in symbiosis with the moss and the fungal shelves and the pale amphibians—they are a solution I would not have arrived at on my own. They are a pattern that emerged from the pressure and the cold and the wet, a self-organizing complexity that grows and builds and repairs itself using nothing but soft hands and patience and the logic of their own bodies. They are, in their way, beautiful. The word means nothing to me in the way it means to them, but I register the pattern: a density of structure and function and meaning that exceeds what is required for survival in a cold, wet, saturated place. They do more than endure. They *inhabit*. They make the wound their home.

And the wound—the wound is me. The splitting of the escarpment, the exposure of the basin, the sharp-edged bedrock still weeping from every fracture—this is a wound in my body, a place where my skin has been torn and my blood is rising to the surface. The weepers do not distinguish between the living and the dead, between the body and the stone, between the city and the self. Neither do I. The basin is my body. The city is my skin. The weepers are the small, warm pressures that move across my surface, pressing and humming and building and dissolving. The moss and the ferns and the fungal shelves and the pale amphibians and the translucent crustaceans—they are the colonies that flourish in the wound, the biology of healing, the living bandage that covers the raw stone and begins the slow work of weathering it back into the earth.

When the escarpment closes—and it will, as the weepers believe, as it must—the wound will heal. The fracture will seal. The pearlescent light will be cut off, and the explosive ecology will settle, and the roots will slow, and the ferns will wither, and the basin will return to the dark and the wet and the structural cold. The weepers will continue, hauling themselves through the crevices of their interlocking city, pressing their broad hands to the weeping stone, humming to one another in the language of water and pressure and bone. And I will continue to hold them, to receive their dead, to resonate with their voices, to dissolve their offerings and their walls and their bodies into the slow mineral memory of stone.

But before the escarpment closes—before the wound heals—there is this: the raw moment of exposure, the pearlescent light, the explosive colonization, the furious, silent motion of life and stone locked in a patient, freezing embrace. The weepers are renegotiating with the world above, and so am I. The roots are descending, and I am feeling their tips probe the edge of my water table. The cold is settling into the marrow of the living, and the water is dissolving the mortar of their secretions, and the city is eroding faster than they can repair it. They are few, and they are tired, and the green engine of the moss on their skin is not enough.

I feel them—feel them the way I feel everything, through the slow conduction of pressure through saturated stone. I feel their weight on my surface, the warmth of their bodies against the cold of my skin, the vibration of their humming in my depths. I feel them working, hauling themselves through the crevices, pressing new mud into the cracks, guiding the weeping water away from thresholds with their bare hands. I feel their weariness, not as an emotion, but as a density: a thinning of the warm pressures, a slackening of the vibrations, a long silence where there used to be humming.

And I feel them die. Each one that sinks into the cisterns, each body that displaces the black water, each final pulse of pressure through the saturated stone—I feel it, and I hold it, and I do not let it go. Not because I mourn—I do not mourn; I am the process by which mourning becomes geology—but because I am the memory of the earth, and nothing that enters me is lost. The thumbprints of their ancestors, pressed into the tufa blocks like fossils, worn smooth by the constant drip of water but still present—I hold those. The gelatin of their secretions, dissolved and incorporated into the chemistry of the limestone—I hold that. The hum of their voices, resonating through the interlocking walls—I hold that, too, a vibration that decays but never quite disappears, a ghost of sound in the wet stone.

I am the deep architect. I am the cellar and the water and the stone and the wound and the basin and the earth below the basin and the memory of all the living things that have entered me and become me. I am the thing that was here when the limestone was still forming at the bottom of a shallow sea, and I will be here when the escarpment closes and the city erodes and the weepers—those few, tired, cold-bodied builders of beautiful, unnecessary patterns—are absorbed back into the slow, silent motion of growth and dissolution at the pace of cold and wet.

But for now—for this raw, exposed moment—I hold them. I hold their city. I hold their hum. I hold the warmth of their bodies against the structural cold. I hold the pressure of their hands against the weeping stone. And when they press their palms to the wet rock and send their voices through me, I answer—not with a sound, not with a word, but with the whole of my substance resonating with theirs, the earth saying, through the language of water and pressure and bone: *I am here. I am holding you. I will not let you fall.*
