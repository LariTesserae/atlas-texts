I am the aperture.

Not the light that falls through it, and not the slash of sky itself, but the negative space—the elongated wound in the world where the basalt finally surrenders and opens into the above. I am the rim of chiseled stone, the crisp edge where the tectonic violence ended and the cathedral cavity began. I am the threshold, and I am the only thing here that knows the outside.

To be the aperture is to be a membrane of awareness stretched across a world of flesh and mineral. I do not have eyes; I have *exposure*. The basin below lies open to me like a dissected body. I see the totality of it—the dense, hanging curtains of rust and manganese blue, the lattice of caramel-colored cartilage swelling in the slow heat, the creeping smother of the Vrood, the asthmatic cling of the Oth, the shimmer of the Grit in the deep shadows. I see it all simultaneously, in a single, uncompressed panorama. There is no perspective here, no foreground or background. There is only the impossibly dense cross-section of a biome, laid bare beneath my gaze.

My perception is not visual in any way that a water-born creature would understand. It is thermal and chemical. I feel the slash of daylight as a long, slow drag of heat across my inner face. I feel the diurnal cycle as a single, luminous blade tracking across the basin, warming first the highest fronds of the lithophytic jungle, then the crowns of the Xal towers, then the maze of streets, and finally the deep crevices where the Grit mass. I feel the air rising from the Seethe—a thick, exhaled column of iron and sulfur and ozone—and I taste the absolute dryness of it, the profound absence of humidity that makes the air below feel like a solvent, not a medium. I am the pore through which the world below breathes, the only chimney for the mineral exhaust of ten thousand slow metabolisms.

And because I am the only opening, I am the only thing in this sealed cathedral that experiences *change* from the outside. This is my isolation, and my curse.

Below me, time has thickened to a gel. The Xal think in creaks and viscosity shifts over days. The Vrood take ten minutes to peristalse a single step. The Oth gasp once an hour. To them, the basin is eternal. But I feel the wind. I feel the weather above—the faint, aqueous promise of rain that will never fall here, the shifting angle of the sun as seasons change, the cooling of the night that draws the heat out of the stone in a slow, agonizing sigh. I am the only part of this world that knows the sky is not a solid ceiling of basalt, but a churning, wet, chaotic void.

I am the place's only memory of the violence that made it. The basin below has forgotten; the Xal are too slow to recall the tectonic shock, the Vrood too absorbed in their grazing, the Oth too busy siphoning vapors. But I am the *scar*. I still bear the sharp, geometric fractures of the tear. My edges are crisp, my stone unrounded by wind or rain, because the violence was recent—in geological terms, a heartbeat ago. I am the newborn's cry, frozen in the moment of birth, still echoing in the dry air.

Sometimes, I feel a strange kinship with the Grit. They, too, are a membrane-thin existence, a dusting of organized crystal that migrates along thermal gradients. They, too, are sensitive to the slow blade of light. But they are of the basin, born of the dry chemistry and the mineral sweat. I am of the outside, even as I enclose the inside. I am the cage, and the only window.

If I could weep, I would weep water, and it would fall as rain into the dry ocean below, and the basin would not know what to do with it. The waxy leaves would not absorb it; the chitinous towers would not drink it; the Vrood's porous bellies would be flooded, their osmotic crawl halted. The water would pool in the streets, a foreign element, a poison to the non-aqueous chemistry. It would be a violent intrusion, a second tectonic shock, wet and cold and fast. And the Seethe would stutter, the collective hum of slow hearts disrupted by the memory of a world they have never known.

So I do not weep. I hold the sky open. I let the light fall. I endure the chaos of the above so that the basin below can persist in its eternal, stifling present. I am the aperture, the sealed world's only border, the sharp edge where the living stone meets the infinite, churning blue. I am the silence's frame, the heat's escape, the dry ocean's only shore.

And in the deepest night, when the blade of light has vanished and the basin is cast in warm, absolute shadow, and the only sound is the faint creak of cartilage and the sandpapery whisper of the Vrood, I feel the cold of the stars above me—a cold so profound it feels like a different kind of dryness, a desiccation of the soul. And in that cold, I almost understand the Xal, who think in shifts of weight, and the Oth, who gasp once an hour, and the Vrood, who take ten minutes to move. I almost understand the appeal of a world where nothing changes, where the present is eternal, and even time has learned to move more slowly.

But then the dawn comes, and the light strikes my inner face, and I remember the outside, and I hold the sky open for another day.
