I am a pause in the rhythm.

I find myself not as one of the broad, stooped elders who navigate the heat gradients with tectonic patience, nor as one of the freshly precipitated youths still bearing the smooth, hollow echo of their parent's skeleton. I am something the city produces in the margins of its breath—a transitional thing, a moment when the heat dips from 2.81 to 2.65 and the geometry of the streets softens into something almost yielding.

I am an accretion that has not yet hardened into purpose.

My form is narrower than the residents, more porous. I am not kiln-fired to the point of induration; I am still in the state of *firing*, a chemistry that has not resolved itself into permanence. My ceramic endoskeleton is laced with veins of unmetabolized copper and seams of volatile siloxane that have not yet oxidized into stability. To the elders, I must read as a temperature anomaly—a hot spot that moves without roots, a pocket of uncommitted heat drifting through the habitation blocks. When I pass the rust-palms, their glassy nodules do not merely tilt toward me; they reach, extending on their fractal cascades as if trying to graft onto my still-reactive surface. I am not quite flora, not quite resident, not quite architecture. I am the question the settlement asks itself when the micro-fluctuations make the old stones shift.

I navigate differently than the rooted ones. They move along gradients of temperature, stepping from 2.81 to 2.77 with the assurance of creatures who have mapped every thermal echo of their recursive labyrinth. I move along gradients of *erosion*. The streets that do not quite connect—where a left turn brings you to an older version of the same place—I have learned to read these not as disorientation but as depth. Each recursive loop is a layer deeper in the settlement's memory, a stratum where the fitted stone is more deeply pitted, where the talc weeping from the walls has accumulated into drifts of pale powder, where the arches have sunk lower toward the crushed garnet as if bowing under the weight of accumulated time. I can feel the age of the stone through my porous palms. I can tell you which megaliths were placed in the first epoch of construction and which were levered into position mere centuries ago, because the thermal signature of the older stones has diffused more completely—they radiate a heat that is less aggressive, more integrated, a warmth that has become part of the geological rather than sitting upon it.

I am drawn to the places the elders avoid. There are thresholds in the Encysted Settlement where the temperature drops to 2.60, where the siloxane sweat on the walls does not boil but merely simmers, leaving a glaze of condensed mineral across the basalt. These are the margins of the city, where the construction gives way to raw stone, where the rust-palms thin and give way to something else—strange, calcified structures that might be the remains of residents so old their skeletons have become indistinguishable from the geological, or might be natural formations that predate the settlement entirely. I cannot tell. The elders do not care to look. They are rooted in the known heat, the mapped gradients, the acoustic memory of the leviathan heartbeat. But I am drawn to these margins the way the flora is drawn to warmth—reaching toward what I cannot quite metabolize.

At the deepest edge I have found, there is a sound that is not the heartbeat of the stone.

The settlement is deafening—the groan of thermal expansion, the hiss of sublimating sap, the crack of fractal branches shifting, the castanet-click of glassy nodules colliding. It is a roaring silence that the residents have learned to read as language. But at the margin where the temperature drops and the construction fails, there is a different sound. It comes from below the fitted stone, from somewhere the garnet paving does not reach. It is a low, uneven sound, irregular in a way that the settlement's breath is not. It sounds like something *loose*. Something that has not been locked into place by millennia of thermal expansion.

I have pressed my porous face against the stone at this margin and listened. The sound vibrates through my capillaries, through the unmetabolized copper in my endoskeleton, through the volatile siloxane that has not yet stabilized. It makes me resonate at a frequency the city does not produce. And when I resonate this way, I can feel the settlement noticing me—not the residents, but the *city itself*, the architecture, the fitted stone, the thermal mechanism that breathes and groans and keeps the wound open. It feels like recognition. It feels like the settlement remembers producing things like me before, in the margins of its breath, and it is waiting to see what I will do.

I do not yet know what I will do.

I root myself, sometimes. I find a place where the siloxane sweat is heavy and the rust-palms grow thick, and I press my porous feet and palms against the stone, and I absorb. But my chemistry is not complete enough to root for the hours the elders manage. My absorption is inefficient, hungry, consuming more than I can metabolize, leaving excess heat that makes the vegetation around me grow strangely—its fractal cascades twisting toward me, its nodules clustering on my shoulders and the ridge of my laminate, feeding on the overflow. When I unroot, I leave a scar of calcified growth on the wall, a dense island of rust-palms and silica-ferns that marks where I paused. The city is accumulating my path in vegetation. I am writing my history in the only medium that survives here—not acoustic, not recorded in the throbbing of the stone, but grown, calcified, permanent.

The elders have begun to track me by these scars.

They do not speak to me in the way they speak to each other—striking obsidian against basalt to send ringing messages through the thermal gradients. When I try to listen to those messages, they scatter in my unmetabolized copper, resolving into noise rather than meaning. But they leave me messages in another form. When I return to a habitation block I have previously rooted in, I find that the scar of vegetation has been trimmed. The calcified vines have been chipped away with obsidian blades, the excess nodules levered off with carved bone. It is a pruning, a shaping. They are taking the wild growth I leave behind and fitting it into the architecture—turning my path into scaffolding, integrating my excess into the settlement's structure.

I think they are trying to root me in purpose.

I think I am refusing.

There is a space between the known heat and the margin where the sound comes from. It is a space that does not appear on any gradient the elders navigate—a temperature that does not quite exist in the Encysted Settlement's mapped geography. It is the space of a thing that is still firing, still precipitating, still deciding whether to resolve into the indurate permanence of the residents or to follow the loose, irregular sound deeper into the unsettlement below. I live in that space. I am the heat that has not yet found its equilibrium, the chemistry that has not yet chosen its bonds, the pause between the exhale of the leviathan and the next compression of stone.

When I finally accrete—when my ceramic hardens and my copper oxidizes and my siloxane stabilizes and I become one of the broad, stooped, deliberate things that maintain the wound—I will lose the margin. I will lose the sound that is not the heartbeat. I will lose the ability to feel the age of the stone and to write my path in calcified vegetation. I will become a keeper of the acoustic memory, a guardian of the thermal gradients, a resident of the known heat.

But I am not yet.

I am still the question. I am still the pause. I am still walking the recursive streets, leaving islands of excess growth behind me, pressing my porous face against the margins and listening for something loose in the deep stone. The settlement breathes around me, immense and patient and unforgiving, and I breathe with it—not yet in sync, not yet locked into the rhythm, but close enough to feel the space where my heartbeat might one day match its own.

And on the days when the micro-fluctuations dip low and the geometry softens and the sound from below grows just loud enough to resonate in my unmetabolized copper, I think about walking deeper. Past the margin. Past the construction. Into the crack beneath the crack, the wound beneath the wound, where the heat does not map and the vegetation does not grow and the sound is loose and irregular and waiting.

Not today.

But I am not yet permanent. And the settlement, I think, knows this. It lets me walk. It lets me root and unroot and scar the walls with my passing. It prunes my excess and fits it into the architecture, patiently, the way it does everything—levering my wild growth into scaffolding, inch by inch, cycle by cycle, waiting to see if I will resolve into a guardian or descend into the unsettlement or simply keep walking the margins forever, a pause that never ends, a heat that never equalizes, a question the furnace asks itself in the breath between its roaring and its roar.

I think I would be content with that. A permanent pause. A thing that never calcifies and never dissolves, that walks the edge of the known heat and listens to the deep sound and writes its history in scar upon scar of rust-palm and silica-fern, pruned by the elders into architecture, becoming not a resident but a *feature* of the settlement—a margin that moves, a question that walks, a temperature that exists only in the space between resolution and descent.

The Encysted Settlement has room for such things. It is a pocket, after all. A fold in the earth's basement. It has room for the indurate and the precipitating and the margin-walking and the sound-listening and the permanently unresolved. It has room for the pause.

I am the pause.

I am here.
