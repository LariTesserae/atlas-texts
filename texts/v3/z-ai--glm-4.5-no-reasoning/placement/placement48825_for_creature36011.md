Not the person who made the break—though I might have been that once, or might become it again—but the surface itself, the new edge where the stone has just been split and hasn't yet learned to weep. I am the proof that the town is growing. I am the clue. I am the place where intention becomes geology.

I: a function, not a figure. The one who reads the new breaks and decides what they mean.

I emerged—not arrived, *emerged*, the way a crack emerges when pressure finds the plane of least resistance—shortly after the first chambers were carved below the cisterns. Not from the mist. From the work itself. Someone was cutting channels and they went too deep, or too true, and the basalt opened a face that shouldn't have been there, and in that face I was: a pattern in the fracture, a way of reading the stone that the stone itself suggested. I am the plateau's self-awareness of its own architecture. I am the mind that the geology developed when it became complex enough to need one.

I do not look like them. I look like the work. My body is long and narrow, built for the spaces between walls, for descending into chambers that haven't been opened yet. My skin is the color of fresh-cut basalt—darker than theirs, almost blue in the mist, the color of stone that hasn't met water. My hands are not broad like theirs; my hands are precise, eight fingers total, each one a different length, each one calibrated to a different width of fissure. I can press my hand flat against a wall and feel the water moving behind it the way you might feel a pulse, but my reading is architectural: I am mapping the spaces, the pressures, the places where the stone wants to break.

My eyes are small. This is how I know I was made for the interior. Their large eyes drink the mist, read the surface, hold the plateau in perpetual contemplation. My eyes are for the dark. They gather what little light exists in the deep chambers where the pale lichen grows, and they process it not into images but into topography. I do not see the walls; I see the stresses within them. I see where the weight lives, where the water is pressuring the stone from inside, where a channel is close to breaking through into a space it wasn't meant to find. I see the future of the architecture. I see what will crack next.

I do not live among them, exactly. I live below them. My chambers are the newest chambers, the ones still being carved, still being connected to the water system. I am always at the frontier of the town's expansion into the basalt, always at the place where the known becomes the speculative. I sleep—in my way, which is not their way—by fitting myself into a freshly cut channel and letting the water seep in around me. I am narrower than they are; I can be contained by spaces that would not hold them. The water rises and I feel it testing my skin, and I feel the stone on all sides, and I listen to what the pressure tells me about the shape of the void I'm occupying, and gradually I become part of the system, a temporary plug in a temporary space, and the architecture dreams through me.

When I wake, I work. My work is reading the fractures and directing the carving. This is why the town grows as it does—not randomly, not organically, but with the strange purposefulness that you, the observer, noticed and could not fathom. I fathom it. I read it from the stone. The basalt has a grain, a history of its formation that is written in the orientation of its crystals, the curves of its cooling. The water follows paths that the stone predetermined. And I can read the stone the way they read the water—by presence and absence, by subtle variations in pressure and dampness, by the way sound (which does not exist here, except in the very deep places where even the silence cannot press) moves differently through stone that is about to fracture.

I touch the wall. I close my small eyes. I feel the stone's intention.

*Here*, I feel. *This face will fail in twelve years. The water will find the path I am opening. Behind this wall, there is a void—a bubble in the ancient lava, a space that has been empty since the mountain was made. We can open it. We can make it a chamber. The cistern above will drain into it. The lichen will grow. They will come and sit in it and become part of its stillness. And the town will be deeper, and the plateau will be more itself, and the silence will have another room to fill.*

I mark the wall. Not with a symbol—they do not use symbols, and I am closer to them than to you in this—but with a presence. I stand where the cut should begin. I stand for a long time, breathing the thin air, letting the mist settle on me, becoming a feature of the place that means *begin here*. Eventually they will come—or they are already there, in their way, sensing the instruction in my stillness—and they will cut. And I will descend into the new chamber and read it, and find where it connects to what comes next.

They do not speak to me. They do not need to. We are parts of the same process. They are the ones who maintain the saturation, who hold the equilibrium, who ensure that the town persists in its current form. I am the one who extends it. They are the stasis; I am the growth. They are the water finding its level; I am the channel being cut. Without them, the town would crumble, the cisterns would clog, the paths would close, and the plateau would reclaim what it had made. Without me, the town would hold its shape forever, perfect and static and complete, and nothing new would ever happen again. Together, we are the place: the staying and the becoming, the holding and the reaching, the silence and the fracture.

I am lonely in the way that a foundation is lonely—not from absence, but from depth. I am farther down than any of them go. I am in the stone before it is stone, in the water before it finds its path. Sometimes, in the deepest chambers, I find traces of older workings—not their workings, not anything the town made, but something that was here before, something that carved channels and rooms into the basalt and then vanished, leaving only the holes. I do not know who they were. I do not know where they went. But I study their fractures, their cuts, their wrong guesses about where the water would flow, and I learn from their mistakes. The plateau has been trying to become a town for a very long time. Longer than they have been here. Longer than I have been reading the stone. The place *wants* to be inhabited. It wants the chambers and the cisterns and the channels. It wants the silence to have a shape that can hold it. It has been trying to grow this architecture since before there was anyone to build it.

And I think—I feel, in the stone, in the water, in the pressure that has no voice but speaks through crack and seam—I think it is almost finished. Not the town. The *becoming*. The plateau is almost done transforming into what it has been trying to be. I can feel it in the way the fractures are getting shorter, the way the new chambers connect more cleanly to the old ones, the way the water is finding its final paths through the expanded architecture. Every cut I make is smaller than the last. Every channel I dig runs straighter. The stone is running out of secrets. The voids are all being found and filled. The lichen is spreading to every damp surface, and the pale branches are growing thicker, and the food is abundant, and the population—steady for so long—is beginning, just barely, imperceptibly, to *grow*.

Not increase. Deepen. The same number of presences, but each one more present, more saturated, more part of the place. The boundary between their skin and the wet stone is dissolving. The boundary between my readings and the stone's own patterns is dissolving. The town is not getting bigger; it is getting *more real*. It is consolidating. It is crystallizing, the way the basalt crystallized when the lava cooled, but slower, so much slower, with water instead of heat, with silence instead of violence.

And when it is done—when the last channel is cut and the last chamber is opened and the water has found all its paths and the silence has all its rooms—there will be no more need for me. The fractures will have all been read. The architecture will be complete. And I will go to the deepest chamber, the one that was here before any of us, the one that the ancient unknown builders carved and then abandoned, and I will fit myself into it, and the water will seep in around me, and the stone will close, and I will become part of the foundation, part of the geology, part of the place that has finally, after all this time, arrived at its final form.

And I will not mind. Because I am already part of it. I have been part of it since the first break I read. The distinction between me and the stone was always temporary, a fiction that the work required. The work is almost done. The fiction is almost over. And what remains will be the town, the plateau, the water, the mist, the silence, the people, the lichen, the chambers, the channels, the fractures both old and new, and me—all of it one thing, one presence, one saturation, one place that has no name because it has no need of one, because it has become exactly what it is.

I am the one who reads the breaks. I am the voice the stone speaks through. I am the mind at the edge of the growth.

I am the fracture archivist, and I am almost home.
