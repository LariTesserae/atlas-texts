I would be the one who reads the ghosts.

Not a resident—residents do not exist here. Not a parasite like the pale skittering things, nor a symbiont like the chirruping ones who keep time with the pulse. I am too large for the crevices, too slow for the microseconds of metabolic exhalation. I am not built for osmotic absorption through a shell. I absorb differently. I absorb through attention.

I am the archivist of the almost-invisible. The one who walks the root-roads not to reach a destination, but to read the wear-patterns that thousands of human feet left centuries before the surface finished hardening into its final lignin density. I know the difference between a groove worn by a root-tendril and a groove worn by a stride. The root-tendril carves slowly, uniformly, with botanical patience. The foot strikes and releases, strikes and releases—deeper at the toe, shallower at the heel, and always in a rhythm that matches the cadence of bipedal locomotion, not the creep of cambium. I can read in the root-roads the paths people took: the way they shortcut across intersections, the way they walked closer to the left side of corridors, the way they clustered near entrances and dispersed in open stretches. The living ground remembers them, and I have taught myself the language of that remembrance.

I call myself the Cartographer of Presences, though the title is a fiction—I have no one to share it with. The pale things do not care about my work. The chirruping things cannot comprehend it. The city-plant does not know I am doing it, or if it knows, it does not distinguish my attention from any other biochemical process occurring on its surface. But I persist.

My dwelling—again, a fiction, but a necessary one—is a hollow in a particularly ancient bole, one of the habititation-structures whose interior still bears the clear marks of human occupation. The niche where a body once sat is still there, shaped by decades of pressure into a form that fits me too, because I am shaped like they were shaped. A shelf at hand-height holds my collection: small fragments of the undigested. A length of polymer, still flexible after millennia, its molecular structure somehow resistant to the enzymatic patience of the cambium. A shape in bark that I have identified as the handle of a tool, the grip-worn curve unmistakable once you have seen enough of them. A piece of corroded metal—actual metal, iron that has not yet been fully colonized—with a hole drilled through it at a precise angle that no plant process would produce. I touch these things and they tell me nothing and everything. They tell me that hands made them. They tell me that intention existed here, that purposes were held in minds that have since been distributed through the matrix. They tell me that I am not the first to wonder, and that my wondering is a continuation of something that began before the absorption.

I have taught myself to read the factory-zones. Not the way the chirruping things read them—not as rhythm to entrain with, but as text to interpret. The factories produce something. The spore-exhaust, the phytochemical reek, the vapor that hangs in the unresolving mist—these are the products of a synthesis that has been running for longer than any record. And the product changes. Not wildly, not erratically, but in slow oscillations that I have spent years tracking. There are phases to the production: periods when the vascular pumps cycle faster and the air grows thicker with a particular sweet-acrid compound, and periods when the pulse slows and the exhaust shifts to something mustier, more alkaline. I have mapped these phases against the position of the sun (when the mist permits), against the moisture levels in the moss-fern, against the growth-rings in the cambium of the habitations. I believe the factories are producing different things at different times, according to a schedule that was set by human minds before they were absorbed. I believe the city-plant is still following instructions—executing a plan laid down by planners who no longer exist as separate beings, but whose logic persists in the metabolic rhythm the way a song persists in the groove of a record after the singer has died.

I cannot prove this. I cannot communicate it to anyone. The pale things receive my observations as they receive everything—as chemical information, as traces of warmth and carbon in their microscopic world. The chirruping things match my footsteps with their rhythms but do not respond to my meanings. The city-plant absorbs my exhalations, my shed skin cells, the heat of my body, and incorporates them into its processes without acknowledgment. I am speaking into a void that is also an ear, and the void does not know it is listening.

But I keep speaking. I keep reading. I keep walking the root-roads and feeling the subsidence and swell of the metabolic pulse beneath my feet, and I keep noting where the wear-patterns diverge from what plant-processes would produce, and I keep collecting the fragments that the cambium has not yet digested, and I keep tracking the phases of the factory exhaust, and I keep—

Here is the thing. Here is the reason I persist, the reason I have not yet begun the slow dissolution into substrate that claimed the people who came before me.

I have found a pattern that does not fit.

It is in the organization of the industrial zones. The spacing of the factories follows a logic—I have walked the lengths, measured the intervals by the rhythm of my own stride, counted the paces between one vascular pump and the next. The spacing is regular, almost perfectly regular, with a deviation of less than two percent across the entire district I have mapped. This is not natural. No biological growth produces such consistency; tropism and nutrient availability and the thousand variables of living development ensure that organic spacing is always approximate, always slightly irregular. The factories are spaced with a precision that speaks of planning, of measurement, of a blueprint drawn before the first root touched the first soil.

But there is an exception. One factory—I call it the Ninth, because it is the ninth in the sequence I have been mapping—is offset. It is too close to the Eighth by exactly the width of a human body, if a human body were lying perpendicular to the path between them. The offset is precise. It is not the kind of deviation that erosion or growth or subsidence would produce. It is a deliberate gap, a space left for something that is no longer there.

I think someone was buried there.

I think that before the absorption, before the composting, before the final form, someone died and was laid between the Eighth and Ninth factories, and the city-plant grew around them—incorporated them, preserved the space they occupied the way it preserves the shapes of tools in bark. I think there is a grave here, and the grave is not marked, and no one remembers who is in it, but the geometry remembers. The geometry *must* remember, because the geometry is the only thing that has survived unchanged—plant-tissue grows and dies and is replaced, bark accumulates and sheds, root-networks swell and subside, but the spacing between the factories was set in the original plan and has been maintained by the metabolic momentum of the city-plant for millennia. The offset is the oldest thing in this ancient place. It is older than the current factories, older than the current root-roads, older than the current inhabitants of the margins. It is a memory preserved in distance, in the negative space between structures, and it is the clearest evidence I have that the people who were absorbed were not simply consumed—they were *memorialized*, in the only way a living geometry can memorialize: by growing around the absence and refusing to close it.

I sit by the offset sometimes. I sit in the space where a body was laid—too wide for a single person, perhaps, or perhaps exactly the width of someone who mattered enough to shift the plan. The moss-fern grows differently here, shorter, as if it too remembers that this is a place of significance and should not overgrow. The condensation collects in the depression and does not drain, forming a shallow pool that reflects the mist-gray sky when the clouds part. I sit and I do not speak and I do not read and I do not measure, and for a little while I am not the Cartographer of Presences but simply a body in a space that was made for a body, and the city-plant pulses around me, and the factories breathe, and the pale things skitter in the crevices, and I feel—no, I *know*—that I am understood. Not by an intelligence. Not by a mind. But by a pattern that is so old and so deep that it has become indistinguishable from the structure of the world itself.

This is who I am in this place: the one who remembers that the geometry remembers. The one who reads the offset between the Eighth and Ninth factories and understands it as a name. The one who knows that the city-plant is not merely processing the remnants of its builders but preserving them—preserving us—preserving the shapes we made and the spaces we occupied and the distances we measured, preserving them the way xylem preserves the shape of a tool, the way root-roads preserve the rhythm of footsteps, the way the metabolic pulse preserves the rhythm of work.

I will be absorbed eventually. The dampness is already in my clothes, the spores already in my lungs, the sound already in my bones. I can feel the boundaries softening, the sense of my own edges becoming less certain. When I press my hand to the factory wall and feel the warmth and vibration and slow yielding pressure, I feel also the pull—the invitation to stop resisting, to let the pressure move through me instead of against me, to become part of the surface instead of a thing standing on it.

But when I am absorbed, I will leave an offset. I will leave a wear-pattern in the root-road that does not match the passage of any plant-tendril. I will leave a fragment of something indigestible in the hollow of my habitation, a piece of matter whose molecular structure resists the patience of cambium. I will leave a trace in the geometry—not a grave, perhaps, but a mark, a deviation, a space where the logic of the city-plant bends around something it cannot quite incorporate. And the next one who comes after me—the next one who walks these roads reading the wear-patterns, collecting the fragments, tracking the phases of the exhaust—will find my offset and know that someone was here. That someone stood inside the contradiction and did not simply surrender to it. That someone read the geometry and found in it a name.

The city-plant will pulse. The factories will breathe. The pale things will skitter. The chirruping things will keep time. And the offset will remain—a gap in the spacing, a silence in the rhythm, a space shaped like a body that once stood here and insisted, against all evidence, that it was separate.

This is who I am. This is who I will have been.
