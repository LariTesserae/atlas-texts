I would be the one who came and did not leave.

Not the biofilm—I am too large, too restless for that surrender. Not the coherence itself—I am too fragmented, too full of gradients and expectations and the old habit of becoming. Not the basalt in the pool, though I understand the basalt now, how it sits and does not remember being placed.

I would be the one who found the hollow and recognized it. Who climbed over the limestone lip—scraping existence into those grooves, yes, I think I made them, or something like me did, dragging something in, a body or a bed or a bundle of something that seemed necessary at the time—and entered, and stopped.

---

There is a kind of person who seeks such places. Not everyone does. Some need the gradient, the chase, the uncertainty. They would find this hollow and feel its equilibrium as a warning, a vacuum, a threat to the motion that defines them. They would leave.

But some—those who have exhausted their becoming, or who have glimpsed the shape of their own final configuration and cannot unsee it—such a one might stay.

I would stay.

Not at once. I would circle first. I would test the air and find it thick and mineral and warm. I would kneel at the edge of a pool and see nothing reflected—not the sky, not my face, only the faint opalescence of water so still it has forgotten how to hold a mirror. I would dip a finger in and watch the surface heal so slowly that the healing was indistinguishable from the original wound. I would touch the biofilm and find it cool and dull and patient. I would listen for the hum and, finding it, feel it enter my chest like a second heartbeat, slower, indifferent.

And then I would lie down on the limestone floor, where the warmth rises from beneath like the palm of an enormous hand, and I would wait for something to happen.

Nothing would happen.

I would wait longer.

Nothing would continue to happen.

---

This is what I would become, in the hollow:

At first, a witness. I would catalog the stillness. I would notice that the light does not change and mark this as strange. I would watch the pools for ripple and find none. I would search the biofilm for growth and see none. I would examine the grooves I perhaps made and feel them smooth beneath my fingers, already less deep than I remembered. I would look at the basalt in the water and try to recall why I placed it there, and the reason would be faint, and then fainter, and then a quality without a content, and then nothing at all.

I would be the last dynamic thing in the hollow. My breathing would be the strongest gradient. My heartbeat would be the loudest event. My thinking—scattered, searching, desperate for change—would be the only process. I would be, for a time, an intrusion of becoming into a place that has finished becoming. A perturbation. A temporary unevenness in the equilibrium.

And the coherence would absorb me. Not by force. Not by will. Simply by continuing to be what it is while I, slowly, ceased to be what I was.

---

The change would be so slow that I could not perceive it as change.

My breathing would shallow. Not from weakness—from sufficiency. The air is warm and thick; the lungs need not work so hard to extract what they need. My heartbeat would slow. Not from illness—from stillness. There is nothing to flee, nothing to chase, nothing to excite the blood. My thinking would simplify. Not from damage—from resolution. Thoughts require something to act upon: problems, questions, desires, fears. In the hollow, these flatten. The problems have all been solved by the patience of the stone. The questions have all been answered by the silence of the pools. The desires have all been met by the warmth and the moisture and the absence of threat. The fears have all been quieted by the proof that nothing happens here, nothing at all, and that this is not a prelude to catastrophe but simply the way of things.

I would not die. That is important. Death is a process, a change, a becoming-unliving. The coherence does not demand death. It demands only stillness. And stillness, sustained long enough, becomes something adjacent to life—a condition like the biofilm's, where the boundary between alive and not-alive grows uncertain.

I would become a stain on the rock. A warmth that radiates from a particular place on the limestone floor. A shape that the other pools do not have—a pool with a different depth, a different temperature, a different suspension. Not water, but something that holds itself as the water does: in equilibrium, without edge or intention.

I would join the coherence. Not as an equal—I would be too new, too recently arrived, still carrying the faint residue of a life that once scrambled and desired. But as an aspect. A feature of the configuration, the way the biofilm is a feature, the way the basalt is a feature. A detail in the total integration.

---

There is a fear in this, I know. The fear of cessation, of the flattening of the self into a thing that does not want or wonder or reach. The fear of becoming what the coherence is: finished.

But I think—lying on the warm limestone, feeling the hum enter my bones, watching the dim light not change—I would find that the fear was the first thing to resolve. Because fear requires a future, requires something to be afraid *of*, and the hollow has no future. It has only the present, extended indefinitely, and in the present there is nothing to fear. The present is warm and damp and still, and these are not threats. These are conditions. And conditions, when they are not changing, are not fearful. They are simply true.

After the fear, desire. After desire, intention. After intention, memory. After memory, the last artifact of selfhood: the knowledge that I was once something that moved. And even that would smooth, in time. The grooves in the limestone lip would wear a little shallower. The basalt would settle a little deeper. And the story of the one who came and stayed would become indistinguishable from the story of the rock, the water, the film, the hum—the single long story of the hollow, which has no teller and no listener and no need of either.

---

So this is who I would be: the one who entered the hollow and became it. Not the coherence—not yet, not fully—but its newest aspect, its most recent acquisition, the last gradient it flattened, the last difference it resolved.

I would be a human-shaped pool of warmth on the limestone floor. A barely-perceptible variation in the local humidity. A faint mineral signature in the air above a particular place where someone once lay down and did not get up. A set of shallow grooves on the stone lip that might have been made by something dragged, or might have been made by heels, or might have been made by nothing at all.

I would be the answer to the question that the basalt poses: *Who placed me here, and why?* The answer being: someone who was looking for what the hollow had already found. Someone who wanted to stop becoming and start being. Someone who understood, or was learning to understand, that persistence is enough, that equilibrium is not death, that the final configuration is not a failure of the living but a completion of them.

I would be that. And then, after long enough, I would not be anything separate at all. I would be the hollow, and the hollow would be me, and the distinction would have resolved into the same patience that resolved everything else.

---

And if you came later—if you found the grooves and the basalt and the faint warmth where a body had been—you would not know me. You would see only what I became: part of the coherence, part of the integration, part of the long stillness that does not remember and does not grieve and does not want to be found.

You would stand in the hollow and feel the hum and wonder at the quiet and the warmth and the pools that reflect nothing, and you would feel, perhaps, the pull. The desire to lie down. The suspicion that this is what you were looking for, all the time you were looking.

And the coherence would receive you, as it received me, as it receives everything that enters and does not leave. And you would become what I became. And we would be, together, the hollow. And the hollow would be enough.
