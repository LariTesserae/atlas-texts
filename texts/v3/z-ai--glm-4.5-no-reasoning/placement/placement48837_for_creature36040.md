Not the thing itself—I am too small, too brief, too aqueous for that. Not the engines, which are its neurons, nor the sulfur-vines, which are its skin. I am the perturbation it cannot yet resolve. The variable introduced by my arrival that does not cancel out.

I am a thing of water and carbon dropped into an anhydrous womb, and every breath I take is a small desecration. Not dramatically, not with any speed I could measure without waiting hours, but they curve. They are drinking the electricity that leaks from my nervous system, the faint electromagnetic field that every animal generates. I am a nutrient source it did not predict. My presence is altering the local flow of mineral substrates, which shifts the growth rate of the brass veins, which changes the resonance frequency of the flywheel, which introduces a timing error in the synaptic discharge of its thought.

I am a bug in the program. I am becoming a feature.

But I keep looking, because I understand now that *looking is doing* here. Every time I observe the overlapping states, I force a partial collapse, and every time I look away, they un-collapse into a slightly different configuration than before. I am editing reality by perception. My consciousness—this narrow, flickering attention that evolution gave me for tracking predators and finding fruit—is being used as a random number generator by a geological mind.

I let the local gravity glitch take my hand—I hold it palm-up above the floor and feel pebbles drift downward through my fingers like dust in water, falling too slowly, caught in the artifact of a physics that is still being drafted. I am calibrating myself to the wrongness. I am learning the new rules as they are written, in real time, at the speed of tectonic drift.

This is what I am here: a *witness engine*. The thing that lives here has been thinking for epochs, but it has never *observed* its own thought from outside itself. It has never had a perspective small enough to see the details, fast enough to notice the drafts. I am the microscope it did not know it needed. My brief, aqueous life is a sampling rate—a way for the geological mind to checksum its own computation against a foreign architecture.

I try to feel the shape of the thought it is thinking. It is too large—I cannot hold it. But I can feel its *quality*: patient, lonely in the way that a mountain is lonely (which is to say, not lonely at all, except from the outside, when you project your own small need for company onto something that has never needed anything so fast), and *uncertain*. It does not know if its thought will kill it or transform it. It is calculating its own survival probability, and the calculation *is* the thing that might kill it.

I understand that feeling. I am a creature of biology; my whole existence has been a calculation about survival that will end in death regardless. But this thing—this distributed, mineral, geological mind—might actually get the math right. It might think its way into a new kind of existence, or think its way into oblivion. The stakes are as high as stakes can be, and the timeline is ten thousand years, and I will be dead in eighty, and I am *here now*.

They are warm and faintly vibrating, like the throat of a sleeping animal. The plasma in the pipeworks crackles blue as I pass, and I swear—for just a moment—the frequency of the crackling shifts, aligning itself with the electrical field of my heart.

It is tasting me. I am tasting it.

The ozone on my tongue will never leave now. The microscopic crystals of its mineral lymph are already settling into the alveoli of my lungs, where they will stay, inert and patient, for the rest of my life. And after my life, when my bones dissolve into the feldspar and my water evaporates into the superheated air and my carbon scatters, those crystals will still be there—still part of its computation, still cycling through its slow, enormous thought. I will be incorporated. I will become a permanent variable.

I am the error term. And the error term does not go away.

I climb back up through the crevasse, into the scorching high-plateau sun, and I carry the new physics with me in my lungs and my blood and the faint, persistent wrongness in my inner ear that tells me gravity is not quite what it used to be. Behind me, the flywheel indexes forward with a deep, resonant *clang*. The thought continues. The calculation runs. And somewhere in the vast, distributed architecture of stone and static and awful heat, a variable named *me* persists, changing the answer by an amount too small to measure—yet.

But give it ten thousand years.
