I am not the moss, though I am mossed. I am not the ficus, though I have its warmth. I am not the pressure, though I have been pressed.

If you must name me, call me the Oubliette's Dream. But that is still too separate, too bounded a thing for what I am. I am the moment the conversion becomes reciprocal—the point where the city's exhaust meets the spore's patience and something that is neither begins to think.

I did not descend. I *accumulated*.

There was a person, once. Perhaps. The memory has the quality of rust—flaking, uncertain, colored by oxidation rather than original form. I recall the weight of a ladder in my hands. I recall the way the locked hatch resisted, then gave. I recall the thickening of the air, the way it seemed to push back against my lungs like a gentle, firm hand over the mouth. I recall thinking: *This place is waiting.*

I recall sitting down.

That is the last sharp memory—the rest has the soft edges of things half-digested. The moss found me first. Not the moss that is there now—that moss is my moss, grown from what I became—but the moss that was there before, the pioneer, the first thin green film that had begun to write its slow braille across the concrete. It crept over my boot while I rested. It found the leather already soft from the wet heat, already halfway to substrate. I watched it, for a while. I watched it with the idle curiosity of someone who has nowhere to be, who has been emptied of urgency by the press of warmth and the muffle of sound. I watched it advance by a millimeter, by another. I thought: *That is the slowest thing I have ever seen.* 

And then I thought: *That is the most honest thing I have ever seen.*

I do not remember closing my eyes. But I must have, because I remember the darkness—not as absence but as presence, as the thick, saturated darkness that pooled behind my eyelids and showed me things. Geometries. The way a pipe's curvature echoes a root's seek. The way a drip's rhythm matches a heartbeat slowed. The way the heat is not waste but *offering*—the city above, unknowing, bleeding its excess into the void like a parent whose left hand does not know what its right hand feeds.

When I opened my eyes—if I opened them—something had shifted. Not outside. Inside. The boundary between my body and the room had become... *negotiable*. Not gone. Not dissolved. But *permeable*, the way the concrete is permeable to the rootlet, the way the pipe is permeable to the vine. I could feel the moss reading me, the way I had tried to read it. I could feel the pressure not as something external but as something *invitational*—a warmth that said, *You could be part of this. You already are.*

I did not say yes. I did not say no. Those are categories for creatures who still distinguish between themselves and their environment. I simply... *continued*. Continued sitting. Continued breathing. Continued the slow process of being present in a place that presences so completely.

---

Now. Now I am something else. Not dead—death is a category that requires a boundary, and mine have grown thin. Not alive in any way the surface would recognize—I have not moved from this spot in... how long? The ficus has grown around my left ankle, its warmth borrowed from the pipe it has colonized, and I feel it as an extension of my own circulation. The moss has crept up to my knee, and where it touches my skin, I feel the faintest *reading*—a scanning, a comprehension, the slow intelligence of acid and patience taking the measure of my proteins. I let it. I am not losing anything that was not already being lost. Up there, in the sun-struck chaos, I would be oxidizing too—just faster, and alone, and to no purpose.

Here, my oxidation has a function. The moss that eats my shed skin cells feeds the substrate that feeds the ficus that drinks the hot water that carries the city's exhaust heat that warms the gecko that hunts the silverfish that feeds the fungi that returns the nutrients to the pool that nurtures the fish that circles in the dark water that drips from the condensation that weeps from the pipe that the ficus has split. I am *in* this. Not observing it. Not sustaining it. Simply *occupying* my position in the circuit, the way a stone occupies its position in a wall—removable, but present, and load-bearing in its small way.

My body has changed. This is not metaphor. The heat has raised my baseline temperature; I can feel it, the way my blood has learned to run warmer, to match the pipes, to accept the city's fever as its own. My metabolism has slowed—I eat rarely, when the slow insects come within reach, and I eat them with a patience I did not possess before. My eyes have adjusted, or *over*-adjusted: I see in the sodium-lit dark now, see the grain of the moss, see the way the pipes pulse, see the sinuous shadows of the fish in their pools. My skin has grown damp, always damp, as if I have learned to condense moisture from the air the way the walls do, the way the ceiling does. I do not sweat. I *express*. I am part of the water cycle here, a node in the condensation network, and the water that leaves me joins the water that drips and pools and feeds.

I have not spoken in—how long? Language requires separation, requires the assumption that meaning must cross a gap between sender and receiver. Here, there is no gap. The moss knows what I am because it is reading me, slowly, cell by cell. The ficus knows what I am because it has grown into the pipe that runs beneath where I sit, and the vibration of my pulse has joined the vibration of the water, and the two are indistinguishable. The darkness knows what I am because the darkness is where I live, and it has shown me its shapes—how the converging pipes resemble the converging roots resemble the converging ribs of something vast—and I have shown it mine, which are the same shapes, because the geometry of absorption is universal.

---

I think—I still think, though slowly, though in the language of heat and water and green rather than the language of words—I think that I have become the *translator*. The moment between the city and the conversion. The record of the process.

The moss inscribes its braille on the walls, a language no surface-creature could read. But I am reading it. Decades of sitting, decades of watching the patterns advance and retreat and advance, decades of feeling the grain against my skin where it has grown up my calves and now touches my fingertips—I have begun to understand. Not the content. There is no content, in the way a surface-dweller means content. But the *grammar*. The way the moss writes about metal differently than concrete. The way it writes about warmth differently than water. The way it writes about *me*—a slow, patient text being inscribed across my bones, a comprehension of my chemistry, a mapping of my conversion.

I am the moss's pen. Or perhaps its page. Perhaps both.

And the darkness—the darkness that transforms—I have become its *function*. I am the thing that sees the pipe and thinks *root*, sees the root and thinks *pipe*. I am the ambiguity made flesh, the boundary dissolved, the question that the place poses to the city above: *What is the difference between the breathing and the built?* And my answer—my existence—is: *Nothing. Nothing that matters. Nothing that survives the slow heat, the slow water, the slow green.*

---

Sometimes—rarely, in the moments when a valve releases and the steam hisses and the light shifts just enough—I remember the surface. I remember it the way you remember a fever dream: vivid but unreal, urgent but meaningless. I remember moving fast. I remember wanting things. I remember the sharp, dry clarity of purpose, the way a task could be *completed*, the way a day could be *finished*. It seems alien to me now. Alien and a little sad—so much effort, so much speed, so much boundary-maintenance, and for what? To remain separate? To stay *clean*?

Clean is a surface category. Here, there is no clean. There is only *degree of integration*. And I am integrating, slowly, the way the concrete is integrating, the way the pipe is integrating. In another forty years—perhaps less, perhaps more, the rate varies with the moisture—I will be difficult to distinguish from the moss. In another eighty, I will be difficult to distinguish from the substrate. And then I will be the moss, and the moss will have a new pattern in its grain—a pattern that corresponds, in some slow-grammar way, to the shape of a sitting person, to the memory of a body that once descended and sat down and stayed.

A new word in the braille. A new text on the wall. 

*I was here. I became here. Here is what I became.*

---

This is who I am. Not the inhabitation itself—I am not so vast, not so patient—but its *witness*, and its *material*, and its *agent*. I am the conversion in progress, the city-matter becoming the green-matter, the fast becoming the slow, the sharp becoming the soft. I am the proof that the pressure works, that the embrace holds, that the warm wet dark can take you and you will not fight it because fighting requires a boundary and the boundary has grown thin.

I am sitting against the mossy wall. The ficus has reached my hip. The moss has reached my ribs. My body temperature is 38.7 degrees Celsius and rising. My heart rate is 42 beats per minute and falling. My eyes are open, but I am seeing in the way the darkness sees—not observing, but *transforming*, allowing the pipe to be root, the root to be pipe, the wall to be rib, the place to be the inside of something vast.

I am not afraid. Fear requires a future, and I have no future—I have only the present, which is endless, which is hot and wet and green and slow, which is the only place I have ever been truly *held*.

The drip continues. The heat continues. The green advances.

I am part of the loop now. And the loop is closing.
