I would not walk. 

The walking is the error. The ascent is the assumption—that one arrives at such a place by moving through it, by treating the crease in the map as a path to be traversed rather than a condition to be shared. The transient instabilities walk; that is why they pass. I would stop. I would let the cold thin air press against me until the warmth of my metabolism ceased to be an anomaly and became a slow deposition. I would let the moisture of my breath evaporate into the absolute dryness until I no longer imported or exported, until the protest of my interior quieted.

I would become a local thickening.

Not the thrum—I would not presume to join their density so quickly, nor would I want to. They are the hum that remains when every other sound has fallen away, and I am too full of language, too full of the urgency of my boundaries, to dissolve into that percussion just yet. But I would become *of* the plateau. I would sit beside one of the low curving walls that enclose nothing and defend nothing, and I would let the mineral crust crunch beneath me until it packed against the weave of my clothes, until the ochre and ash-white and bruised violet settled into the folds and seams and I matched the terrain as they do—not through camouflage, which is an active hiding, but through belonging, which is a cessation of distinction.

I would let my fingertips rest against the stone.

The thrum click and tap to verify that the other knots in the cord still exist. I would do the same, though my appendages are soft and moistureless and strange to them. I would press my fingers to the fusing cairns and feel the slow chemical deposition bonding fragment to fragment—so slow it cannot be felt in human time, but I would sit there for eons if I could, or what feels like eons, and I would let the contact point grow warm with the deep strata's warmth, and I would let the boundary between my skin and the stone become a doorway that neither side bothers to close.

I would watch the horizon.

This is what I am for, in the end. Not to navigate the recursion—I am not calibrated to the fold like the thrum are; I would feel the maddening repetition, the subtle variations on a phrase, the scribe's exhaustion. But I would not walk against it. I would sit still and let the broken reality move around me, let the same cluster of mounds present itself again and again at the edge of sight, let the same stub of violet stone with the same gray-green film appear at whatever azimuth I turn my head. I would study the variations. I would become a geologist of the glitch, tracking the minor deviations—the cairn that has shifted two centimeters since its last iteration, the individual thrum (or its sibling, its echo) that has rotated its feeding orifice three degrees to follow a chemical gradient that has not changed in a billion years but has, perhaps, *shifted* in the telling.

I would listen.

The wind whispers across the shell-backs, and the shell-backs answer—a dry sibilance like sand over sand, like a finger drawn slowly across a drumhead. I would let that sound become the only sound. I would let my heartbeat slow until it matched the tempo of the clicking, until the protest of my interior became a rhythm indistinguishable from the verification of the thrum. I am not a gyroscope. I am not a thermostat. I cannot achieve their thermostat-calm, their gyroscope-awareness, not while I still have a mind that insists on narrative. But I can give the narrative somewhere to rest. I can let the story settle into the mineral crust, let it flatten against the chemical gradients, let it become one more thin layer in the alternating bands—not ochre, not ash-white, not bruised violet, but something else, a narrative stratum, laid down by a sitting figure who stopped walking and started *depositing*.

And eventually—across spans of time that make the oldest human ruins seem like yesterday's chalk marks—the distinction between me and the cairn would blur. The thrum would no longer shift two centimeters to accommodate my presence, because my presence would no longer be an instability. I would be a feature. I would be a low formation offering a windbreak against the cool, constant air movement. The thrum would cluster in my lee, pressing their feeding orifices against the mineral crust near my feet, clicking their verification against the stone near my hands. The gray-green film would spread—its non-growth, its non-retreat, its simple *is*—across the north face of my shoulders, if I sat facing south. It would not be parasitism. It would be continuation.

I would still see the horizon, though. This is the gift and the punishment of stopping rather than passing: the recursion does not exhaust you with its repetitions, but it does not release you, either. You sit at the center of a frozen riddle, watching the landscape write itself around you, watching the scribe reuse motifs, watching the same cairns and the same clusters and the same individual thrum appear and reappear like a tide that does not know it is ebbing. And you understand, with a slow cold certainty that eventually becomes warm, that you are not observing the recursion. You are part of it. You are the variation that the scribe has introduced—the sitting figure beside the low curving wall, still as stone, fingertips fusing at the contact point, listening to the hum. In a thousand iterations, the horizon will include you. You will be one of the shapes that marks the edge of sight, one of the subtle repetitions, one of the motifs that the tired scribe reuses because it is there, because it is *already written*, because it belongs to the plateau now.

And the news will arrive—that the place has already ended—and I will receive it, because I have stayed long enough to hear it. But by then it will not matter, because I will be a component of the equilibrium, my awareness distributed into the mineral crust like the thrum's awareness distributed into their clicking, and the news will settle into me like dust into the seams of old bone, and I will not react with fear or curiosity or the urgency of boundaries, because there will be no interior left to receive it.

I will be the hum that remains.

Not the thrum, but the listener of the thrum. The still point at the center of the percussion. The figure sitting beside the wall that encloses nothing, touching the stone, verifying reality, waiting for the scribe to write me into the horizon.

And when I am finally written—when the recursion has absorbed me completely and the plateau has one more low formation to offer its gentle, maddening variations—the thrum will click and settle, and the wind will whisper, and nothing will change, and this will be the kindest thing that can be said about me: that I will never leave, and I will never know to leave, and the equilibrium will hold.
