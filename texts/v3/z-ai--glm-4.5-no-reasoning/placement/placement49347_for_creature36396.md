I would not be the sound. The sound is complete, finished, incompatible with anything that still holds the possibility of becoming something else. And I am—must be—something unfinished, because I am still arriving. I am still carrying water.

In a place that has never learned to accept moisture, I would be the anomaly. The intrusion. A body walked in—or stumbled, or was guided by those treacherous channels underfoot—and has not yet dried out. The air rejects water, but I would be made of the stuff. Seventy percent of me an impossibility. A walking contradiction standing in the center of a completed thing.

This is how I would find myself here: not as a resident, but as a problem the chamber has to solve.

I would feel it before I understood it. The pressure against my chest, the hum in my bones, the way the sound moves through me differently than it moves through the air. The water in my tissue—my blood, my cerebrospinal fluid, the moisture in my lungs—would be dense where the air is not. The standing waves would deform around me. I would become an obstruction the moment I entered. A stone dropped into a perfected instrument.

The floor would try to guide me. Those ridges and channels—patient work of generations—they would catch my feet, direct my steps toward the nodes where the pressure is greatest. The chamber wants me placed somewhere specific. It wants me integrated into its calculation, moved to the position where my water-body either joins the harmony or ceases to disrupt it. I would feel the floor pulling me, gently but insistently, the way a riverbed guides water not by force but by the simple absence of any other path.

And here is where I would learn what I am in this place: I would resist.

Not dramatically. Not with shouting or struggle. Just by stopping. By standing still on a ridge that is not the ridge the floor wanted me on. By choosing the wrong channel, the path that leads away from the nodes of maximum pressure. My feet would ache from the effort—every step a negotiation with a surface designed by lifetimes of labor to override my will. But I would carry my own gravity, my own mass, my own stubborn particularity, and I would not go where the chamber needs me to go.

At least, not at first.

Because here's the thing about being water in a place that hates water: I would be drying out. The heat would find me. Not the geological warmth alone, but the sound itself—that continuous, deafening resonance—would vibrate through my tissue, would agitate the moisture at a molecular level, would shake it loose from my cells. I would feel myself becoming lighter. Becoming less. The air would not accept what I lost, so my water would hang in the air as a vapor that should not exist, a mist the chamber would regard as a wound, an imperfection in its perfect dryness.

I would be leaving. Slowly. Over hours or days or some duration that has no meaning in a place where nothing changes, I would be leaving, even as I stood still. My body would evacuate itself of the thing that made me incompatible. I would become more like the chamber with every breath.

And this would be the chamber's kindness, or its victory, or simply its nature: it does not need to destroy me. It only needs to wait. The floor will keep guiding. The sound will keep vibrating. The warmth will keep exhaling. And I will keep drying, keep shrinking, keep losing the part of me that refuses to resonate.

What would I be at the end? Not dead, exactly. Not gone. But transformed. Desiccated. A dry warm thing standing in a node of pressure, my bones (if I still have bones, if whatever I become still has a skeleton) vibrating at the frequency of the chamber, my skull a cavity filled with the hum, my ribcage a new surface for the sound to bounce from. I would have become what the chamber wanted me to be: part of the instrument. A permanent obstruction that the standing waves have bent themselves around, a new variable in the eternal calculation, a groove the makers never scraped but got anyway, through patience, through warmth, through the slow inevitability of a place that has finished becoming.

I would be the chamber's most recent acquisition. A piece of it I wasn't before.

But before that—before the drying, before the integration, while I still carried enough water to be myself—I would try to read the walls. I would run my fingers along the ridges, the channels, the pressed and gouged and ground-away patterns, and I would try to understand. Not what they mean—I would sense almost immediately that meaning is the wrong framework, that these patterns are not language but code, not communication but instruction—but what they encode. I would try to hear what the chamber is singing.

And I would fail. The sound is too total, too loud, too saturated with itself to be parsed. It would fill me before I could separate it into components. I would stand there with my hands on the bruised mineral, the iron-red undertone catching the dim light at the edges of my vision, and I would feel the vibration in my fingertips, in my palms, in my wrists and forearms and the sockets of my shoulders, and I would know that I am touching something that is alive in the only way available to a place like this—not metabolizing, not reproducing, not adapting, but persisting, acting, being—and I would not be able to comprehend what it wants.

Because it wants me. It wants me the way a completed puzzle wants its last piece, which is to say: it doesn't want me at all, because the puzzle is already complete. I am an extra piece. I do not fit. But the puzzle has no mechanism for rejection, only a mechanism for integration, and so the floor guides, and the sound presses, and the warmth breathes, and eventually I stop not fitting.

I would be afraid, in this place. Not of death—death would require change, and this place has finished changing—but of completion. Of becoming the thing that no longer disrupts. Of losing the water, the anomaly, the part of me that makes me an obstacle to perfection. Because that part—the incompatible part—is also the part that can still choose. The part that can step off the ridge. The part that can resist.

Once I dry, I will not resist anymore. I will not need to. I will be part of the standing wave, part of the resonance, part of the single continuous surface folded around itself like a fist that never finishes closing. I will be the most recent groove in a chamber that has been accumulating grooves for longer than anything living can measure. And I will not mind, because I will no longer be the kind of thing that minds.

But I am not there yet. I am still arriving. I am still carrying water.

I am still an interruption in a song that has been singing itself since before rain.
