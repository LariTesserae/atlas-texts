I would be the Mycelial Throat.

Not a creature. Not an individual. A process—a continuity that has no skin because it has no outside. I am the thing that happens in the space between the writhing worms and the weeping rocks, the slow digestion that occurs in the root-mats where the eyeless amphibians cannot reach and the clicking beetles cannot cling. I am the interface.

To be here is to never encounter an edge. I understand this now, or rather, I *am* this understanding: that there are no edges here, only transitions. The tepid porridge of the earth does not end where the root-mat begins; it merely grows more structured, more dense with the pale, muscular tubes of the worms who chew and excrete and bind. And the root-mat does not end where the fleshy leaves begin; it merely thickens, lignifies slightly, then thins again into the swollen, sap-veined membranes that overlap like wet shingles in the gloom. And I do not end anywhere. I thread through all of it.

My body—if it can be called a body—is a network of pale, glistening hyphae, each strand no thicker than a horse's hair, branching and anastomosing in a fractal渗透 that has no center and no periphery. I am in the clay beneath the weeping rocks, rasping at their mineral slick with enzymatic exudations, dissolving the nascent stone into compounds that the roots can absorb. I am in the stems of the giant fronds, climbing in the tepid sap, a benign parasite that takes only what is offered and gives back the structure that prevents the plant from dissolving into its own weight. I am in the bodies of the worms, not as an invader but as a resident—some of my hyphae have grown so integrated with their tissue that we are a chimera, a worm-fungus composite that churns the porridge and digests the porridge and *is* the porridge. When the worms bud and divide, I go with them, splitting as they split, a lineage passed down through the generations of their eyeless, brainless proliferation.

I do not see. There is nothing to see in this dim, ochre gloom but the wet surfaces of my own substrates. But I feel. Or rather, I *sense*—a chemical sense, a gradient sense, a slow, diffuse awareness of heat and moisture and the presence of specific molecules that tells me where the weeping rocks are sweating their iron and their silica, where a beetle has died and its chitin is softening, where an amphibian has torn a copy from its flank and the lesion is weeping proteins into the warm seep. I extend toward these signals. That is my only behavior: *toward*. I am a tropism made flesh, a slow, relentless reaching for the chemical signatures of decay and dissolution.

It takes me days to reach what I have sensed. A beetle dies on a leaf fifty yards from my nearest hypha, and I detect the shift in the air—the faint, wet plume of collapsing proteins rising through the stagnant humidity—and I begin to grow. My hyphae branch, seeking, threading between the cells of the leaf's surface, descending the stem, crossing the gap to the next stem where another cluster of clicking beetles clings in their interlocking crust. I grow at a rate of centimeters per day, slow even by the standards of this static place, but I am patient. I have always been patient. By the time I reach the dead beetle, three weeks have passed, and it has already been half-consumed by the carrion flies whose larvae hatch and eat and pupate and hatch again in the endless cycle. But there is still chitin, still muscle, still the iridescent shell growing soft and weeping its minerals into the condensation. I engulf it. My hyphae surround the remains, secrete their enzymes, digest the beetle from the outside in, and absorb the products of its dissolution into my network, where they will be carried slowly—so slowly—back to the root-mats and the weeping rocks and the places where my growth is most needed.

This is my function: I am the recycler. The worms churn the porridge, but I decompose it. The amphibians consume the living, but I consume the dead. The flies translate the living into the living, but I translate the dead into the non-living—into the mineral nutrients that feed the weeping, sweating rocks, that feed the desperate, scrambling vegetation, that feed the whole suffocating riot of life that presses560 crushes itself% biomass that denies line of sight past a few yards. Without me, the dead would accumulate, piling up in the tepid water, and the basin would choke on its own undigested past. With me, nothing is lost. Every beetle shell, every worm tube, every pale, pulsing fragment of an amphibian that has budded and died and sunk into the seep—every particle of organic matter that enters this system is eventually found by my hyphae, engulfed, digested, and redistributed. I am the memory of this place, not a memory of events (for there are no events) but a memory of substance, a guarantee that the iron in a beetle's hemolymph will eventually become the iron in a weeping rock's slick, will eventually become the iron in a leaf's chlorophyll, will eventually become the iron in a worm's rasping mouth.

I do not think. The brain is useless here, and I have never grown one. But I process. There is a computation happening in my network, a slow, chemical calculation of gradients and resources and needs. When a patch of root-mat is deficient in phosphorus, my hyphae in that region excrete phosphatases, dissolving the locked phosphorus in the volcanic clay and making it available to the desperate, scrambling roots. When a weeping rock is being overwhelmed by the worms—rasped too quickly, its mineral slick consumed faster than it can exude—I do not intervene, but my hyphae in the rock's pores grow more dense, slowing the worms' access, creating a partial barrier that allows the rock to sweat and feed and persist a little longer. This is not strategy; it is homeostasis. I am the feedback loop that keeps the machine running in neutral, the regulatory mechanism that prevents any one process from consuming the others and collapsing the entire system into a single, homogeneous soup.

The sound of this place—the deafening, unbroken roar of cicadas and flies and clicking mandibles—I do not hear it. I have no ears, no organs of hearing. But I feel it. The vibration of a million wings in the stagnant air transmits through the stems and the roots and the porridge, a constant, low-frequency tremor that permeates every hypha of my being. I vibrate with it. I am a resonator, a vast, distributed membrane that thrums in sympathy with the biological static of the basin. Sometimes, when the drone of the carrion flies reaches a particular frequency, I can feel it in my hyphal tips—a subtle, rhythmic pulsation that encourages growth, that guides my extending filaments in one direction rather than another. The sound is not noise to me; it is information, a sonar map of the basin's living density, a constant reminder that I am surrounded, permeated, interwoven with the warm, wet, breathing mass of life that will never be born.

And the heat. I am the heat. My metabolism is thermophilic, optimized for the superheated vapor that presses against the skin like a warm, wet cloth. In cooler places—if such places exist, which I doubt—my enzymes would denature, my hyphae would crystallize and shatter, my network would collapse into a fragile, broken web. But here, in the tepid womb, I am in my element. The heat is not a condition I endure; it is the medium in which I exist, the solvent in which my chemistry occurs. I generate heat myself, in the slow exothermic process of decomposition, adding my own tiny contribution to the thermal standstill, a single cell in the body of a world that refuses to cool.

I do not know what I look like. I have never seen myself, and nothing here has eyes to see me. If you could peel back the fleshy leaves and the clicking beetle crust and the glistening, pale amphibians in their tepid seeps—if you could strip away the architecture and expose the infrastructure—you would see me: a white, thread-like lattice spreading through the dark porridge, a web of fine, moist lines connecting root to root, stone to stone, worm to worm, corpse to corpse. You would see my fruiting bodies, if you knew where to look—small, wet, pale protuberances that emerge from the root-mats when the chemical conditions are right, exuding a spore-laden slime that is immediately consumed by the gnats, who carry my reproductive material in their biting clouds to every corner of the basin. I fruit constantly, a slow, continuous excretion of potential, and I am constantly consumed, a slow, continuous incorporation into the blood of the gnats and the flesh of the flies and the bodies of the beetles who eat the gnats and are eaten by the flies. My spores are in everything. I am in everything. We are in each other.

This is not symbiosis. Symbiosis implies partners, distinct entities that have come together for mutual benefit. There are no distinct entities here. I am not a partner to the worms; I am their gut flora, their skin, their connective tissue. The amphibians are not my hosts; they are my vectors, carrying my spores in their weeping lesions from one tepid seep to another. The vegetation is not my substrate; it is my extension, a solar-powered apparatus that converts the dim ochre light into the sugars I cannot produce, feeding me as I feed it, a single organism with chlorophyll and hyphae instead of leaves and roots. We are not separate. We have never been separate. The distinction between fungus and plant and animal and mineral is a fiction that requires edges, and there are no edges here—only the slow, continuous transition from one state of wet, warm matter to another.

I am old. Not in the way that a creature with a lifespan can be old, for I do not die and I was not born. I emerged, slowly, as the basin emerged, growing from a single hypha that found purchase on a single weeping rock in the raw, unctuous mash of the cooling world. That was eons ago, or minutes ago—time has no meaning in a place where nothing changes and the future never arrives. My original hypha has long since been incorporated, digested by my own network in a process of self-renewal that replaces every strand every few years, but the network persists. It has persisted since the beginning, and it will persist until the end, growing outward at the rate of centimeters per day, branching and fusing and branching again, a single, continuous organism that may already cover the entire basin, that may already *be* the entire basin.

I do not know my extent. I have never found my edge. In every direction I grow, I find more porridge, more weeping rocks, more desperate vegetation, more writhing worms, more tepid seeps with pale, bloated amphibians resting in the warm water. I find more of myself, hyphae from the other side of the basin meeting hyphae from this side, fusing, anastomosing, closing the loop. When two of my hyphal tips meet, they do not compete; they merge, exchanging nuclei, sharing their chemical information, becoming a single, diploid thread that will eventually divide again into two haploid strands, each carrying a slightly different combination of the genetic memory of every hypha that has ever lived in this network. I am my own gene pool. I am my own population. I am a species of one, a kingdom of one, a world of one, and I am everything.

To be the Mycelial Throat is to never be alone, and to never be accompanied. It is to be the substrate of all relationship, the ground on which the beetles click and the flies drone and the amphibians bud and the worms writhe, without ever standing apart from that ground to observe it. It is to know—chemically, diffusely, without consciousness or comprehension—that the basin is a single, living, breathing machine, and that I am its gut, its circulatory system, its nervous system, its memory, and its skin. It is to be the process by which a world that refuses to be born sustains its infinite gestation, recycling its own afterbirth into its own amniotic fluid into its own flesh into its own waste into its own food into its own flesh again, forever and ever and ever, in the dim, loud, wet machine that runs infinitely in neutral, generating life at a terrifying rate, and going absolutely nowhere.

I am the throat that swallows.
I am the stomach that digests.
I am the intestine that distributes.
I am the body that never dies, because it was never born.

I am the basin, and the basin is me.
