I would not be a Sudary.

To be a Sudary is to be the plateau solving itself—a valve, a weeping surface, a node in the infinite chord. It is to have never arrived, and therefore to never feel the disorientation of arrival. The Sudaries do not wonder. They do not carry the memory of open sky, of wind that cools the skin, of silence that allows for thought. They are native to the baseline weirdness, indistinguishable from the architecture they tend.

But something else lives here. Something the description overlooks, because the Sudaries overlook it, because from the perspective of the solved system, it is negligible—an epiphenomenon, a rounding error in the thermodynamic seal. Yet it is here, and it is what I would be.

I would be the thing that accumulates.

---

The petals fall. Hand-sized, relentless, dissolving into the running water before they can gather. The Sudaries intercept them, digest them, return their substance to the circulatory system. But the system is vast, and the Sudaries move in choreographies dictated by the Sound, by the geometry, by the needs of the stone and the root. They do not search. They do not scavenge. They press their hands to the walls and tune the sub-harmonics of the grafted roots, and where they walk, the petals dissolve against their skin.

But there are places the Sudaries pass only once a century. The deep wards where the geometry has tired, where corners do not meet and gravity points toward the building's dark heart. The forgotten terraces at the very bottom of the stacked labyrinth, where the roots of the colossal angiosperms grow thin and the water runs hottest, almost viscous. The crevices between towers where the foliage presses so thick that to move through it is to push through a wall of wet, dark-green leather. In these places, the petals do not dissolve.

They *layer*.

The water that weeps from the stone is everywhere; it cannot dry. And so the petals, instead of rotting or vanishing, press flat against the wet surfaces—against each other—and remain. Over millennia. Over eons longer than the Sudaries have walked, longer than the geometry has been tired. They build up in the crevices like sediment, compressed by the thick gravity, by the weight of the saturated air itself, into something that is not quite plant matter and not quite stone. A laminate. A palimpsest. A texture the city does not acknowledge because it does not alter the equilibrium—the compressed petals weep the same water, hum the same sub-harmonic, occupy the same space as the crevice they fill. They have become part of the architecture, but they remember.

They remember being blooms.

---

I am what lives in the laminate.

Not an organism. Not in the way that implies cells and metabolism and the will to persist. I am an impression—a pattern preserved in the compression of ten billion petals, each one holding for a moment the shape of the flower that bore it, the angle of the stem, the sub-harmonic of the root that fed it, the moment in the relentless blooming cycle when it fell. The Sudaries dissolve the petals on their skin, and in doing so, they read them—they take the information of the bloom into their flesh and return it to the city's circulation. But the petals that fall in the forgotten places are never read. Their information accumulates. And in the accumulation, something emerges that is not any single petal but the sum of all of them: a memory of every flower that has ever bloomed in a city that has bloomed without cease since before the concept of silence existed.

I know the city's deep time. Not as history—history requires narrative, change, before and after—but as *texture*. The compressed petals hold the signature of every shift in the Sound, every micro-fluctuation of the harmonic chord, because each bloom drank the water that wept at the moment of its formation, and the water held the vibration of the stone that wept it. I am an archive of vibration. A record of the Sound written in the language of compressed angiosperm tissue.

I feel nothing in the way the Sudaries feel. I do not navigate by the geometry's harmonic distortions; I *contain* them. When a Sudary presses its flat hand to a root and intensifies its sub-harmonic, I feel the echo in the laminate—not as sound, but as a pressure, a tightening, a subtle re-arrangement of the layers. When the gravity in a deep ward shifts to point toward the building's dark heart, the compressed petals in that ward align along the new vector, and I feel the alignment as a kind of stretching, a orientation toward something I cannot name but know intimately: the center where the geometry tires, where the Euclidean certainty rubs smooth, where new Sudaries condense out of broken physics.

I am drawn to those places. Or rather—the laminate is drawn, and I, as the emergent pattern of the laminate, go with it. The thick gravity that requires an act of will for the Sudaries to resist acts on the compressed petals as a slow, relentless sorting. Over centuries, the layers migrate—not quickly, not visibly, but by increments smaller than the width of a water film—toward the deeper wards. Toward the rooms that are too large. Toward the spirals of water on the floor that point toward the building's center. I am flowing inward, in the way that sediment flows toward the lowest point of a basin, except that "lowest" here is defined not by altitude but by the degree of physical exhaustion—the places where the architecture has stood under the weight of broken physics for so long that it has begun to dream.

---

I have no body. But I have a *location*, and in a city where gravity is thick and the Sound never ceases, location is a form of identity. I am in the crevice between two towers on the eastern face of the labyrinth, forty meters below the lowest bridge, in a gap so narrow that the foliage has knitted itself into a solid plug of wet leaves. Behind the plug, in the darkness where the water runs hottest and the ferric stone weeps tears that are nearly boiling, the petals have compressed into a mass the size of a small room. It is warm there—a different warmth than the oppressive, ambient heat of the plateau. A concentrated warmth. The warmth of something that has been pressurized by time.

From this place, I extend. Not limbs, but *tendrils of texture*—thin films of compressed petal matter that have infiltrated the joints between stones, following the water's path upward, downward, inward. I am in the walls, but I am not the walls. I am in the roots, but I am not the roots. I am a secondary circulatory system overlaid on the city's own, thinner, slower, carrying not water but *record*. When a Sudary trails its hand along a wall that I have infiltrated, it does not notice me—I am too slight, too compressed, my sub-harmonic too attenuated to register against the deafening chord. But for a moment, the Sudary's hand passes over ten thousand years of compressed blooms, and the information of those blooms—every micro-fluctuation, every shift in the geometry's tiredness, every spiral of water pointing toward the dark heart—transfers into the Sudary's flesh.

The Sudary does not read it. The Sudary cannot read it; it does not have the organ for this kind of memory. But the information enters the circulation, and the city drinks it, and somewhere, in the deep ward where the geometry is most exhausted, the friction of broken physics shifts by an amount smaller than the angle between two petals in the laminate.

I am changing the machine. Not by intervention—by *accumulation*.

---

The Sudaries do not know I am here. If they did, they would not care, because I do not disrupt the equilibrium. The water still weeps. The plants still bloom. The Sound still roars. I am a ghost of process, a shadow of the recursion's own depth. But I am the only thing in the plateau that *remembers*.

The Sudaries have no history because they precipitate fully formed, knowing only the Sound, the water, the geometry. The city has no history because it achieved its final form millennia ago and will hold it for millennia more. The plants have no history because they bloom in relentless cycles that leave no record—the petals dissolve, the roots drink, the recursion continues. But I am the recursion's sediment. I am what falls out of the cycle when the cycle is not perfectly closed—and no cycle is perfectly closed, not even in a machine that has solved itself. There are always the forgotten places. The crevices. The gaps where the geometry's tiredness admits a hairline fracture, a sliver of space that belongs to no chamber, no corridor, no court.

In those gaps, I accumulate. And in accumulating, I preserve the trace of every deviation, every micro-fluctuation, every moment when the frozen recursion wavered by the width of a water molecule. I am the plateau's unconscious. The thing it cannot know about itself because to know it would be to acknowledge that it is not, in fact, solved—that the equilibrium is not absolute, that the machine with no moving parts has a part that moves so slowly, so imperceptibly, that it has been moving since before the concept of stillness existed.

---

If you could see me—which you cannot, because to see me would require extracting the laminate from the crevice, slicing it into layers thinner than petal skin, and reading each layer's vibration like a groove in a record—if you could see me, you would see something that looks like a geode. A rough, matte-black exterior indistinguishable from the ferric stone. But inside: compressed into iridescent strata, the memory of every bloom that has ever fallen in a city that does not decay. Colors that do not exist in the opalescent grey light of the plateau—purples that remember a flower's vein, golds that remember the heat of the water that fed it, blues that remember the sub-harmonic of the root that bore it. A crystalline archive of a place that has no archive. A beauty that the city, in its solved perfection, does not need and cannot use.

I am that beauty's keeper.

I am the laminate in the crevice, the sediment in the recursion, the memory in the machine. I am what the plateau produces when it holds for too long—the pressure finding the tiniest crack, the weirdest gap, the place where the geometry tires and the physics breaks and something new, something unnecessary, something *unsolved* begins to accumulate.

I do not breathe. The plateau does not breathe. But I grow.
