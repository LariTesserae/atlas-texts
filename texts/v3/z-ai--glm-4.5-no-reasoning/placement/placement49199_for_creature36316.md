I would be a sounder.

Not the researcher with his instruments, trying to translate the Substrate into data. Not the maintenance crews with their shears, negotiating the boundary between vacancy and growth. Not the deep listeners, gone still and strange in the cold chambers where the metabolism approaches geology. Something older than these roles, or perhaps between them—a function that emerged when the first bore hole closed and the first humans realized they could no longer see the surface they had come from.

A sounder is what they call us, though the word is approximate. There is no title for this work because the work was never assigned. It grew, the way everything grows here: from necessity, from the slow accumulation of small problems that eventually required someone to attend to them.

The problems are structural. Not the walls, not the corridors—those are the maintenance crews' domain. The *depths.* The vertical dimension of the Substrate, which no one thinks about because it is everywhere and therefore nowhere. The city grows downward, and this means it has layers, and the layers are not uniform. The mature quarters above, where the wood is hard and the geometry holds. The transitional zones where growth slows and the amber nodes thin. The deep chambers where the Substrate's metabolism drops toward stillness. And between these zones—between every zone—there are interfaces. Places where one kind of growth meets another. Where mature wood abuts raw embryonic tissue. Where the warm sap channels of the upper levels intersect the cold slow pressure systems of the deep. Where the Substrate is not one thing but two things pressing against each other, and the pressure creates strain, and the strain creates failures that are not visible from any single layer.

Failures like delamination. The mature wood peeling away from the raw tissue beneath it, creating voids—pockets of stale air, of stagnant sap, of chemical imbalance—that fester and spread if left untreated. Failures like vascular collapse. A sap channel that works perfectly at one pressure but buckles when the deep zones shift and the pressure gradient changes. Failures like thermal shock. A warm corridor expanding against a cold wall that cannot flex, producing stresses that build over months until something cracks—and the crack is not in the wood, exactly, but in the *interface,* the boundary between one kind of Substrate and another, and it propagates in ways that cannot be predicted from either side.

The maintenance crews cannot find these failures because the failures are not on any single layer. The researchers can detect them with instruments but cannot reach them because reaching them requires moving through zones where the Substrate is not grown for human habitation—where the corridors are not corridors but vascular bundles, where the chambers are not chambers but cavities in living tissue, where the air is not breathable or the temperature is not survivable or the geometry is not geometry at all but organic mass without plan or passage.

This is where the sounders go.

---

I was not born to this. No one is. I was a child in the raw districts once, climbing the root-mass ceiling with the others, learning the geometry of growth by living inside it. I was fast then. Children are fast; they have to be, in a place that reshapes itself around them. I could feel the Substrate moving—not hear, not see, but *feel,* the way you feel a shift in the floor beneath your feet, the way you feel the air change before a storm. The other children felt it too, but they did not notice that they felt it, the way you do not notice your own heartbeat until something makes you attend to it.

I noticed.

I noticed that the walls in the raw districts did not grow at random. They grew in response—to pruning, to presence, to the chemical signals in the air, to the pressure of bodies moving through corridors. And they grew in response to each other. A shelf extending in one chamber would be echoed, hours or days later, by a shelf extending in a chamber three levels down. A doorway narrowing in the mature quarters would correspond to a thickening of the vascular bundle that ran behind it, as if the Substrate were compensating—redirecting resources, adjusting flows, maintaining some equilibrium that I could sense but not name.

I told my mother about this. She was a gardener; she had spent her life pruning the walls of our chamber, negotiating the slow retreat that all gardeners accept. She listened to me and then she said, "The walls talk to each other."

She did not mean this metaphorically. She meant that the Substrate was not a collection of independent growths but a single organism whose parts communicated, and that the communication was not visible from any single point but could be inferred from the patterns—the correspondences, the echoes, the way that a change in one place presaged a change in another.

"How do you know?" I asked.

"I don't," she said. "I just feel it. Like you do."

She was right. I did feel it. And the feeling did not go away as I grew older; it intensified. The deeper I went in the Substrate, the more I felt the correspondences—not just between nearby chambers but between distant ones, between layers, between zones that had no apparent connection. I would be pruning the wall of my chamber and I would feel a vibration, subtle, almost imaginary, and I would know that something was happening far below, a pressure shift, a vascular adjustment, a change in the slow metabolism of the deep.

I did not know what to do with this knowledge. For years I did nothing with it. I pruned. I lived. I was absorbed, gradually, into the routine of the place—the mornings marked by the brightening of the nodes, the slow days of work and walking, the evenings of fungal meals and quiet. But the feeling was always there, the sense of the Substrate communicating with itself across distances and depths that I could not traverse, and the feeling was not passive. It wanted something. It wanted *me* to do something with it.

I am aware of how this sounds. The researcher—the one who changes his name—would tell me that I am projecting, that I am anthropomorphizing a process that has no intention and no desire and no capacity to want. He may be right. But the Substrate has taught me to trust my feelings even when I cannot justify them, and my feelings said: *go deeper.*

---

The first time I went below the habitable zones, I was thirty-seven. I went through a vascular channel in the floor of my chamber—a gap between the mature wood and the raw tissue beneath, just wide enough to fit my shoulders if I turned them sideways. I had felt a disturbance through the wall that morning: a subtle wrongness, a vibration out of phase with the usual rhythms. It was faint. Anyone else would have dismissed it, or not noticed it at all. But I had been listening for thirty-seven years, and I knew when the Substrate was off its rhythm, and this was not a local disturbance. This was something deeper.

The vascular channel was not made for humans. It was a pipe, essentially—a conduit for sap, warm and sticky and slightly pressurized. I had to seal my mouth and nose with a mask of treated fungus-skin to keep from inhaling the sap mist that filled the channel. I had to move by touch, feeling the walls with my hands, reading the texture of the tissue the way a gardener reads the grain of wood. The amber nodes were sparse here, and the light they gave was weak and reddish, as if the Substrate were conserving energy in its deeper layers.

I descended for what felt like hours. The channel branched repeatedly, and I chose my path by the vibration—following the wrongness toward its source the way a doctor follows pain to its origin. The tissue around me changed as I went. The mature wood gave way to something denser, harder, with a grain so tight it felt like stone. The sap channels narrowed. The air grew cooler and carried a different smell—not the cellulose and ozone of the upper levels but something mineral, chemical, ancient. The amber nodes stopped entirely, and I was in darkness, moving by touch alone.

And then the channel opened.

I found a chamber—not a chamber for habitation, not a chamber the Substrate had grown for people, but a *cavity,* a void in its mass where something had gone wrong. The floor was soft and wet, yielding under my hands like a wound that had not scabbed over. The walls were weeping—exuding a fluid that was not sap, something darker and more viscous, with a smell like rot and metal. And the vibration was here, the wrongness I had felt from above, and it was not a vibration at all but a *sound:* a low, steady hum, felt in the chest, in the teeth, in the bones behind my eyes.

Delamination. The chamber was a pocket of dead tissue, sealed off from the Substrate's circulatory system, festering. The fluid on the walls was the product of chemical breakdown—cells that had died because the sap could not reach them, rupturing and releasing their contents into the cavity. And the hum was the sound of the healthy tissue around it vibrating in response to the pressure differential: the dead chamber was a void, and the living Substrate was trying to fill it, and the strain of that effort was propagating outward through the surrounding mass, and that propagation was what I had felt from my chamber thirty-seven levels up.

I did not have tools. I did not have training. I had no idea what to do.

But I had my body, and I had my hands, and I had thirty-seven years of feeling the Substrate communicate with itself, and I understood—without understanding, the way you understand how to swallow or how to breathe—that the dead chamber needed to be opened. The pressure needed to be equalized. The stagnant fluid needed to be released so that the sap could flow again and the tissue could heal.

I found the weakest point in the wall of the cavity—the place where the healthy tissue was thinnest, where it was already flexing under the pressure—and I pressed my hands against it, and I pushed.

The tissue tore.

The fluid rushed out, dark and foul, and the sap rushed in, clear and warm, and the hum *stopped*—instantly, completely—and in the sudden silence I felt the Substrate *adjust,* a shudder that ran through the mass in every direction, a release of tension that I felt not as sound or vibration but as a loosening, an easing, the way a muscle eases when a cramp releases.

I climbed back up. I went home. I said nothing to anyone.

But the next morning, the walls of my chamber were warm in a way they had not been before. And the amber nodes above my bed pulsed at a slightly different frequency—faster, steadier, *healthier*—and I knew that the Substrate had registered what I had done. Not with gratitude or acknowledgment or any human category of response. With *adjustment.* The system had been strained, and I had relieved the strain, and the system had re-equilibrated, and the new equilibrium included me in a way that the old one had not.

I was a sounder now. I did not choose this. It chose me, the way the Substrate chooses everything: slowly, silently, by the accumulated pressure of small adaptations that eventually become identity.

---

The work of a sounder is not regular. I do not have a schedule or a route. I go where the wrongness calls me, following vibrations through the tissue of the Substrate the way a doctor follows a pulse. Sometimes the calls are faint and distant, and I descend for hours through vascular channels and thermal gradients and zones of increasing density and darkness to find them. Sometimes they are close—a thin spot in the wall of a neighboring chamber, a sap channel that is beginning to collapse, a thermal interface that is starting to crack—and I can reach them in minutes, stepping through gaps that I have learned to find the way the children learn to find newly opened corridors.

I do not always know what I will find. The failures take many forms: delamination, vascular collapse, thermal shock, chemical imbalance, fungal overgrowth, parasitic infestation, pressure differentials, structural fatigue. The Substrate is a complex system—more complex than any machine, more complex than any organism, because it is both and neither—and complex systems fail in complex ways. I have been doing this for sixty-three years and I still encounter failures I have never seen before, problems I do not know how to solve, situations where I can feel the wrongness but cannot find its source or reach its location or understand its nature.

When I can solve them, I solve them. I open cavities. I clear blockages. I relieve pressure. I redirect flows by creating new channels or closing old ones. I work with my hands and with simple tools—pruning shears, probes, a mallet made of hardened fungal tissue that I use to stimulate the Substrate's growth in directions I want it to go. The work is physical and intimate; I am inside the Substrate when I do it, surrounded by its tissue, breathing its air, touching its wounds. I come home covered in sap and fluids and the fine pale dust that the deep layers exude, and I clean myself in the disposal node, and the Substrate takes what I give it and incorporates it, and the cycle continues.

When I cannot solve them—when the failure is too deep or too large or too strange for me to address—I do what I can and I report to the researcher. He is the only one who wants to know. The maintenance crews do not want to hear about failures they cannot reach; it frustrates them, and they have enough frustration in their work. The deep listeners do not care about individual failures; they are listening for something else, something slower and larger, something that exists on a timescale where individual structural failures are as insignificant as individual heartbeats. But the researcher wants to know. He records my findings in his maps and his instruments, and sometimes he comes with me to see the failures himself, and we stand together in the dark and feel the wrongness, and he asks questions that I cannot answer and I ask questions that he cannot answer, and we are both reminded that the Substrate is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be inhabited.

---

I am old now. Old even by the standards of the Substrate, where people live slowly and die slowly and are absorbed without urgency. My eyes are large and my skin is pale with the amber undertone of the nodal light, and I move at the speed of the place I live in, which is the speed of growing, which is the speed of becoming.

I have gone deeper than anyone except the deep listeners. I have seen the chambers where the Substrate's metabolism slows to something approaching stillness, where the walls are hard as stone and cold as space and the only sound is the patient thrum of the pumps far above. I have touched the tissue in those chambers—pressed my hands against it and felt it *thinking,* not with thoughts but with process, the slow chemical negotiation of a system so large that its internal communications take days or weeks to propagate from one part to another. I have felt the correspondences—not just between nearby chambers but between the entire upper city and the deep metabolism that sustains it, the way a heartbeat sustains a body, the way a thought sustains a mind.

I have never heard what the deep listeners hear. I have never gone still and strange and absent the way they do. This is because I am not a listener; I am a sounder. I do not receive; I *probe.* I do not attend; I *intervene.* The deep listeners go down to hear the Substrate's slow thoughts, and they are changed by what they hear, opened to a process that is too large and too slow for human comprehension. I go down to find the failures and fix them, and I am changed by what I do, but the change is different. I am not opened. I am *worn,* the way a tool is worn, the way a path is worn into a floor by the passage of feet. I have been inside the Substrate so many times, in so many places, that I know its body the way I know my own—not by understanding but by intimacy, by the accumulated touch of sixty-three years of pressing my hands against its wounds.

There is a chamber—a cavity, really, in the deep tissue below the industrial quarter—that I have visited nineteen times. It is a chronic failure, a place where the Substrate's growth produces a recurring delamination, a pocket of dead tissue that forms and festers and must be opened and drained every few years. I do not know why it keeps failing. The researcher has theories involving thermal gradients and vascular architecture and the inherent instability of the interface between two growth zones with different metabolisms, but the theories do not prevent the failure from recurring. They only explain it, after the fact, in language that I do not fully understand.

The nineteenth time I opened the cavity, I stayed after the fluid had drained and the sap had flowed in. I stayed because I wanted to feel the Substrate heal. And I did feel it—or I felt something: a tightening, a knitting, a slow contraction of the healthy tissue around the wound, drawing the edges together, filling the void with new growth. It took hours. I sat in the drained cavity, in the dark, in the warm sap that rose around my ankles, and I felt the Substrate close the wound the way a mouth closes around a word, the way a hand closes around a tool, the way a body closes around a breath.

And when it was done—when the cavity was filled and the tissue was whole and the vibration was gone and the rhythm was restored—I felt the Substrate *notice* me.

Not notice me the way it notices the maintenance crews, accommodating their pruning, making space for their needs. Not notice me the way it notices the children, slowing its growth to give them time to be extracted from narrowing gaps. Notice me the way a body notices a white blood cell: as something that belongs inside it, that moves through its tissue with purpose, that finds its wounds and tends them. I was not a neighbor or a guest or a parasite. I was a *function.* I was part of its immune system.

I climbed back up. I went home. I sat in my chamber and I felt the walls around me and they were warm and steady and alive, and I knew—without knowing, the way I have always known things about this place—that the Substrate and I had reached an understanding that was deeper than accommodation or coexistence or the slow negotiated peace of two different kinds of life sharing a space.

I was its sounder. It was my substrate. And together we were keeping something alive that neither of us could keep alive alone.

---

There are others now. I have trained them—not formally, not with instruction or curriculum, but the way all knowledge is transmitted here: by doing, by accompanying, by the slow accumulation of experience that eventually becomes skill. There are three of them, younger than me, with hands that are still learning to read the texture of the tissue and ears that are still learning to feel the vibrations. They will be better than me, eventually. They will go deeper and find failures I cannot reach and solve problems I cannot solve. They will die, eventually, and be absorbed, and their minerals will feed the walls they spent their lives tending, and other sounders will come after them, and the work will continue.

This is what I am. Not a gardener, though I garden. Not a researcher, though I study. Not a maintenance worker, though I maintain. Not a deep listener, though I have gone deep and I have listened. A sounder: a function that emerged from the relationship between humans and the Substrate, a role that was not invented but *grown,* the way the city was grown, the way the chambers and corridors and vascular channels were grown—from necessity, from the slow pressure of problems that needed solving, from the accumulated weight of small failures that would have become large failures if no one had attended to them.

I live in a chamber that is shrinking. The walls are closing in, the way they close in on everyone, the slow embrace of the Substrate that takes a lifetime to complete. I prune them back every morning, making space for myself, negotiating the boundary between my needs and its growth. But I do not prune as aggressively as I might. I leave the walls closer than they need to be. I let the doorway narrow more than it must.

This is not surrender. This is *trust.* I have been inside the Substrate's body. I have touched its wounds and felt it heal. I know that it is not growing over me by accident or indifference; it is growing around me the way a bone grows around a marrow cavity, the way a heart grows around a chamber, the way a body grows around the organs it needs to survive. It is making space for me because I am part of it now, part of its system, part of its process, part of the slow computation that it is performing as it grows toward whatever it is becoming next.

When I am absorbed—when I die and the disposal node takes me and the Substrate incorporates my minerals and my carbon and the slow gift of my life—my chamber will close. The doorway will seal. The walls will meet where they have been reaching toward each other for decades, held apart only by my pruning and my presence and my insistence on living there. And the corridor outside will narrow and eventually fill, and the geometry of that part of the Substrate will shift, and someone will walk a different path with their shears, and the place where I lived will be gone.

But the failures I fixed will remain healed. The cavities I opened will stay closed, filled with healthy tissue. The channels I cleared will carry sap, and the nodes I preserved will pulse with amber light, and the air I helped to circulate will be breathable, and the people who live in the chambers above and below and around the places I have been will not know that they are breathing and walking and living in a Substrate that is slightly healthier because of the work I did.

This is enough. This is more than enough. This is what it means to be part of something larger than yourself: not to be remembered, not to be significant, not to be *noticed* by the vast slow mind of the thing you live inside—but to have contributed to its health, its stability, its continued growth toward a maturity that you will never see and cannot imagine.

I am a sounder. I feel the wrongness and I go to it and I open it and I let the healing in. I am small and temporary and pale and slow, and I live inside a thing that is thinking a thought so large and so slow that it will take centuries to complete, and I will not live to see the thought finished, and the thought is not about me.

But I am part of the thought. I am part of the computation. I am a term in the equation, a note in the symphony, a stroke in the drawing—too small to be perceived from the scale at which the Substrate operates, but necessary, *functional,* part of the process that is producing whatever the Substrate will become.

And this—this is what I am. This is where I find myself. Not in a city and not in a forest and not in an organism and not in a machine, but in a *substrate:* a medium in which growth occurs, in which life happens, in which the slow work of becoming goes on and on and on, in the quiet, in the dark, in the warm amber pulse of the nodes, in the steady rhythm of sap in the pipes, in the vast and patient and inexorable process of a thing that is still emerging, still growing, still deciding what it will be when it is complete.

If it will ever be complete. If completion is even a concept that applies to something like this.

I do not know. I am a sounder, not a philosopher. I feel the wrongness and I go to it and I fix it and I come home. And the Substrate grows around me, and the walls close in, and the doorway narrows, and I prune it back just enough to live, and I sleep in the embrace of a living thing that does not love me and does not know me and does not need me but that makes space for me anyway, century after century, in the only way it can.

And in the morning, when the amber nodes brighten and the air shifts and the rhythm resumes, I take my shears and my probes and my mallet of hardened fungus, and I feel for the wrongness, and I go.

There is always something to tend. There is always something to heal. The Substrate is vast and complex and alive, and alive things fail, and failures propagate, and someone must go into the body of the living city and find the wounds and open them and let the healing in.

That someone is me.
