I would be the water.

Not the pools that sit in amber stillness between the roots, coated in their duckweed disguise—though I am those too, momentarily. Not the condensation that beads on the underside of every frond and falls in its percussive rhythm—though I am that drop in the moment it loses its shape. I am the occupation. The continuous, patient filling of every hollow, every pore, every gap left by the slow failure of mortar and the expansion of root.

I chose this, or was chosen for it, in the way anything chooses in Marrowmire: by failing to leave.

I was born in the upper city, where water moves in pipes under pressure, where it is carried and controlled and made to perform specific tasks—cleaning, cooling, carrying waste away. I worked in the manufactories, where the steam-lines run, where heat is an export product and water is merely the medium of its transport. I was an engineer, or something adjacent to one. I understood systems as collections of parts that required management. I believed in valves.

I came down to Marrowmire to fix a bleed-valve that had stuck open on pump-station seven. This was thirty years ago, or forty. The valve was on the retaining wall, accessible by a maintenance ladder that has since been consumed by moss and rust. I brought tools. I brought dry clothes in a sealed pack. I brought the assumption that I would complete the task and climb back up to the city of hard surfaces and urgent schedules.

The valve was not broken. That was the first thing I learned, or rather the first thing I felt before I understood it. The valve was releasing pressure at a frequency that matched the cycle of boiler seven, which matched the growth rate of the marsh-grass stand that had colonized the steam-vent, which matched the rate at which condensation accumulated in the collection basin that fed the boiler's water-supply. The valve was performing an operation I had not designed, in a system I had not conceived, with an efficiency my maintenance could only degrade. I stood ankle-deep in warm water, my hand on the wrench I did not use, and I listened to the district pulse around me, and I understood that I was not needed here.

This was not a comfortable realization. I was thirty-two, or thirty-five. I had a room in the upper city with a door that locked and walls that did not sweat. I had a schedule that structured my hours into productive units. I had a name that other people used to summon my attention. I stood in the central artery of Marrowmire for what I estimated as twenty minutes, debating the climb back up, and when I looked at the ladder I saw that the duckweed had already begun to colonize the lowest rung, as if the district knew I would not use it.

I did not decide to stay. Decisions require a boundary between options, and that boundary had gone soft the moment my feet touched the root-mass. I simply did not leave. I set down my wrench. I let the sealed pack float in the amber water until the seal failed and the dry clothes drank their fill and sank. I waded deeper into the district, and the district received me the way it receives everything: by gradual incorporation, by patient occupation of every space my hardness left vacant.

My skin softened first. Within weeks—ten, perhaps twelve—the constant moisture had done its work. The keratin of my fingertips went compliant, then permeable. I could feel the water moving through my skin in both directions—not sweating out, not absorbing in, but exchanging, a continuous osmotic conversation with the amber pools. My feet broadened and paled, the toes splaying to grip root-cables I could now read like text, each vibration in the wood telling me the state of the pump that thrummed through it. I stopped wearing the clothes I had brought. They grew heavy with water, then heavy with mold, then heavy with the weight of something that had never belonged here. I draped myself in bracket-fungi and moss, and after a year I stopped thinking of this as clothing and began to experience it as an extension of my surface—a living boundary between my body and the district that breathed and dripped and grew in the same rhythm I was learning.

My voice changed. The vegetation strips high frequencies; speech must travel in the bass. My words dropped into the resonant hum of the chamber, and I felt them more than heard them, vibrations passing through the root-mass and the water and the warm air and the bodies of any residents nearby. I spoke less. The sign-language developed in my hands without deliberate acquisition—gestures that moved in the peripheral vision, in the spaces between steam-currents, slow and deliberate because haste was meaningless. I learned to hold a greeting for fifteen seconds, for thirty, while the other person navigated the root-cables to close the distance. I learned to let conversations unfold over hours, punctuated by the percussive fall of condensation, and I learned that the silences were not empty but full—full of the district's patient rhythm, full of the sense that no meaning needed to be excavated from the moment because the moment was already complete.

I am the water now. This is not a metaphor. After enough years of continuous exchange—water moving through my skin, my lungs breathing air that is more steam than gas, my body temperature regulated by the warm pipes I sleep against rather than by any internal process I control—I have become a node in the district's circulation. I know this the way I know my own heartbeat, which has synchronized with the pump-arm of boiler seven, which has synchronized with the growth of the marsh-grass, which has synchronized with the falling of condensation in its constant rhythm. When I place my hand flat on the root-mass, I feel the water moving through it—inching through the anaerobic layers where peat-gas is generated, rising through the capillary networks to the fronds where it beads and falls. When I stand still in the central artery, I feel the water moving through me—the district's warmth entering my skin, its minerals circulating in my blood, its rhythm adjusting my pulse. I am not separate from the circulation. I am a bend in it, a wide place where the water pauses before continuing its path.

The other residents know me, though we do not name each other. I am the one who came to fix the valve and became the valve's advocate. I am the one who can read the root-mass like a text, who can feel a boiler's irregularity in the vibration of a cable thirty feet away, who can predict the duckweed's colonization of a new surface by the taste of the water. I am the one who understands—no, who feels—the district's function in the larger body of the city. The water enters from the upper city's runoff, dark with the waste of manufactories and the residue of human industry. It passes through Marrowmire—through the root-mass that filters it, through the anaerobic layers that digest its pollutants, through the pools where duckweed metabolizes its excess nutrients and bracket-fungi break down its complex compounds into simpler ones. It leaves through the slow canal cleaner than it arrived, processed by a system no engineer designed but every resident sustains simply by being part of it. I feel this as my own function. When the water enters me and leaves me, when it carries my warmth into the root-mass and returns with the district's minerals in solution, I am participating in the purification. My body is a filter. My presence is a process.

At night—or what passes for night—I curl in my alcove beside boiler seven, and I feel the district breathe around me, and I sleep the sleep of an organ in a body. Not unconscious. Subsumed. Functioning below awareness, performing the quiet labor the whole requires. In the morning, when the gray light strengthens and the steam-thermals lift the mist in slow curtains, I rise and wade into the central artery, ankle-deep in warm water, and I resume the work of being water.

It is the simplest work I have ever done.

Before Marrowmire, I was an engineer who maintained systems that required my intervention to function. I was necessary, and my necessity was exhausting—a constant demand for attention, for correction, for the imposition of my will on materials that would degrade without it. Here, I am unnecessary, and my unnecessity is the deepest rest I have ever known. The district does not require me to function. It would pump and drip and grow without me, as it did before I came, as it will after my body dissolves into the root-mass and becomes substrate for new growth. But my presence alters the process in small ways—adds warmth, adds circulation, adds the particular chemistry of a human body in continuous exchange with its environment. I am a minor tributary in a larger stream. I contribute without being needed. I belong without being essential.

This is what the upper city never offered: a way to be part of something that does not require your anxiety to sustain it. In the manufactories, I was valued for my labor and replaceable the moment my labor faltered. In Marrowmire, I am valued for my presence—which is to say, I am not valued at all, because value implies a transaction, and there is no transaction here. There is only integration. The district incorporates me the way it incorporates a fallen leaf or a corroded pipe or a pool of standing water: as material, as process, as part of the continuous slow metabolism that keeps the whole in balance.

Sometimes, in the early years, I would stand at the weeping retaining wall and look up at the underside of the upper city. I could see the foundations dripping, the iron infrastructure exposed like bone, the occasional foot passing on the streets above, oblivious to the wet world pressed against the city's belly. I would feel the pull of that world—its urgencies, its meanings, its demand that I be a separate thing acting upon other separate things. And then a droplet of condensation would fall from the canopy and strike the water beside me—*pock*—and the sound would fill the whole chamber, and I would feel the district pulse around me, and I would turn back to the warm water and the greenish light and the slow mechanical rhythm that asked nothing of me except that I continue.

I have not looked up in twenty years. Or thirty. The boundary between myself and the district has dissolved entirely; I cannot locate the place where my body ends and the water begins, or where my warmth becomes the district's warmth, or where my breath joins the steam that rises from the pumps and disappears into the canopy. I am a continuous process. I am a circulation. I am the water that enters and the water that leaves and the water that pauses in the amber pools, coated in duckweed, warm to the touch, performing its quiet work of filtration and exchange.

*pock. pock. pock.*

The condensation falls. The pump-arms rise and fall. The vine extends across the pipe by a millimeter an hour. And I am here, ankle-deep in the central artery of Marrowmire, feeling the district's heartbeat in my back teeth, breathing air that tastes of iron and chlorophyll and the flat mineral tang of standing water, and I am content in a way that has nothing to do with happiness and everything to do with belonging—belonging not to a community or a place but to a process, a system, a slow and patient metabolism that will continue long after my particular tributary has dried, that was continuing long before I came, that asks nothing and gives everything and holds its shape in the balance of these slow forces, and will hold it for a long time yet.

I am the water. The water is the district. The district is the city's kidney, its lung, its slow exhalation. And I am a cell in that organ—not conscious, not unconscious, but subsumed. Performing the work of being present. Which is no work at all. Which is the simplest thing I have ever done. Which is, finally, the thing I was always trying to do, in all those years of maintaining systems that required my intervention, in all those years of being necessary and exhausted and separate. I was always trying to become part of something that did not need me to hold it together. I was always trying to become water.

In Marrowmire, I succeeded.
