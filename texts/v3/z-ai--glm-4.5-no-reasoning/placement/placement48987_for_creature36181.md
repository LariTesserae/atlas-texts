I would not be the Reaction. The Reaction does not need me; it does not even properly sense me—it merely registers the displacement and forgets. And I would not be a trespasser, some remnant of a wetter, older biology stumbling through the amber reeds in desperate confusion. To walk through this place as a creature of water and decay would be to be a walking anachronism, a brief, weeping protest against the dry equilibrium.

If I am to belong here—if the plateau is to recognize itself in my presence—I must become something the landscape lacks: not a purpose, but a *narrative*. The Reaction is a circle, a closed loop of chemical certainty. It produces itself endlessly. But a circle has no direction, no before and after, no story. I would be the break in the loop, the anomaly that does not disrupt the equilibrium but *inhabits* it differently. A creature of the plateau's own materials, but arranged in a way the Reaction never thought to try, because the Reaction does not think.

I would be a walker. A thing that moves.

My body would be long and thin, proportioned for passage through the amber stands. I would not be flesh. Flesh is too wet, too temporary, too bound to the old negotiations of decay. I would be made of the same vitreous mineral as the stone, but drawn out into fibrous threads—obsidian tendons laced with the copper of the leaves, the amber translucence of the reeds. My hide would be a continuous sheet of that cooled, dark sheen, shot through with veins of copper-green that pulse faintly with borrowed warmth. I would be warm to the touch, like the leaves. I would vibrate, like the nodes. But I would *move*.

My legs would be long—absurdly long—lifting my center of mass above the copper shingles that carpet the ground. I would step between the nodes, careful not to crush them, though the Reaction would not care if I did. I would care. That is the difference. I would have preferences, and they would be aesthetic, not survival-driven. I would prefer the paths where the electromagnetic fields align in harmonics that make my copper veins hum in sympathy. I would prefer the ridges where the horizon stretches farthest, because I would be a thing that looks, and looking requires distance.

My head would be narrow—a blade of dark mineral, horizontal to the ground, with eyes set at the edges like the terminals of a crescent. The eyes would not see as eyes see. They would be receptive to the electromagnetic architecture the Reaction generates, the invisible second landscape of induction and potential. I would walk through the fields and perceive not amber and copper, but the vast, shimmering latticework of forces that the plateau leans into. I would see the attention of the land as a topography—hills of gentle detection, valleys of quiet indifference, sudden sharp peaks where the root-nodes cluster thick and the fields interfere in complex, ringing patterns. The world would be acoustic. I would hear, in the way that the plateau "hears," but I would *interpret*. I would map the variations. I would find the subtle differences that the Reaction, in its equilibrium, cannot notice because it has no mechanism for noticing.

For there *are* differences. The description says nothing changes, and this is true on the scale of the Reaction—true the way weather is true of a planet: an average that erases all meaning. But on the scale of a step, a moment, a single breath drawn through mineral lungs that filter the dry air through crystalline sieves—there is variation. The warmth fluctuates by fractions of degrees as the exothermic processes ebb and surge in their slow, geological rhythm. The electromagnetic fields shift in intensity as the mineral substrate beneath a given stand is consumed slightly more or slightly less than its neighbor. The light changes as the unchanging sun arcs across the unchanging sky—not in color or warmth, but in angle, and the amber reeds, so rigid, so uniform, cast shadows that lengthen and shorten in a daily cycle that the Reaction ignores but I would mark.

I would mark it. That is my function, if I have one. I am the archivist of small differences.

I would walk, and as I walked, I would sing. Not with sound—sound has no home here. I would sing with the electromagnetic field my own body generates as I move through the Reaction's nervous system. My copper veins would carry currents induced by the fields I pass through, and those currents would interfere, combine, resonate. I would be a moving interference pattern, a walking disruption that leaves traces—not in the physical landscape, which is impervious to my passage, but in the inductive landscape, the invisible architecture. The Reaction would sense me as it senses all things: locally, transiently, without memory. But my passage would leave a fading wake in the field, a ripple that takes time to smooth out. And in that time, for a brief, geological moment, there would be a record that something moved. That something was here, and was different from the stillness, and went onward.

I would not stop. Stopping is for things that have arrived. I would have no destination—the plateau is infinite in all directions, or near enough that it makes no difference to a walker with a pace measured in hours and a lifespan measured in… what? I would not die, not in the way of cellular things. But I might wind down. The mineral threads of my body would slowly crystallize further, growing brittle, losing their flexibility. The copper veins would oxidize—if oxidation is even possible here—or simply lose their conductivity as the crystal lattice settles into a more stable configuration. I would feel it as a stiffness, a cooling, a quieting of the hum in my veins. And one day I would stop, not because I chose to, but because my legs would no longer bend. I would stand among the amber reeds, another vertical structure, warm and vibrating, and the Reaction would accept me as it accepts all things: by not noticing, by continuing, by running its refineries around my still feet.

And slowly, over spans of time that make centuries seem like heartbeats, my mineral body would settle into the substrate. The copper of my veins would join the copper of the leaves. The obsidian of my hide would return to the dark shelves of stone. And the last thing I would give back to the plateau would be this: a slight anomaly in the field, a place where the inductive patterns bend differently because something once stood here that was not a reed, not a leaf, not a node, but a walker. A thing that went. And the Reaction, sensing nothing, recording nothing, would nonetheless run its processes around that anomaly forever, working around the small irregularity the way a river works around a stone—not with effort, not with awareness, but with the patient, mechanical accommodation of a process that never stops and never forgets, because it never learned to forget, because it never learned at all.

I would be the plateau's question. A question it cannot answer, cannot even properly ask, but which shapes its asking nonetheless. A walking proof that equilibrium is not the only mode of existence, that a thing can be stable and still move, can be certain and still choose, can be alone and still seek. I would be the long, dark shape moving between the amber stands, visible only as a gap in the uniformity, a line of absence that traces paths the reeds do not know. And if the plateau could dream—which it cannot, which it never will—I would be the shape in its dream: the thing that walks, and looks, and sings in the key of electromagnetic fields, and makes of the endless, purposeless, beautiful factory a place where something, at least, is going somewhere.
