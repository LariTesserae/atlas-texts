I would not be the ridge-goer. I am too full of language, too separate from my environments, too loud in my internal architecture to ever dissolve into stone. I could not survive the transition from creature of shelter to creature of exposure.

But I would not leave.

---

I would become the damp pocket.

Not the inhabitant of it—the pocket itself. Or rather: I would become what happens in the pockets, the exception that the arid rule doesn't crush but forgets. I would be the thin film of water that survives the day by virtue of its own small depth. I would be the streak of condensation on a shadowed wall, darkening the schist each night and evaporating each morning, a pulse so local and slight that the ridge-goers would feel it only as a shift in the frequency of their thirst.

I chose this because of the amphibians. The description of them broke something open—*isolated populations in moisture pockets no larger than cupped hands, evolution diverging in separated wet spaces.* Each pocket a world. Each world entire. No migration between them, no gene flow, no reconciliation—just slow, patient drift in pockets of humidity held against the void like whispered arguments for the possibility of softness.

I would be the connective tissue between those worlds. Not the water itself—I cannot be element—but the awareness that travels between pockets. The spider that builds across damp crevices, webbing so fine it catches fog. The mite that rides the wind for seconds between sheltered stone, surviving transit through the howl because its body can desiccate and rehydrate, because it carries its water inside itself like a sealed letter. The larval thing in the salamander's gut that completes its lifecycle only when two micro-environments briefly touch.

I would be the mediator of exceptions. The carrier of dampness between dampnesses.

---

This is what I mean:

There is a kind of mind that lives in transitions. Not in the equilibrium—the ridge-goers have that mind, the mind that holds a pattern until it becomes geology, that *is* the pattern. And not in the void—the wind has that mind, the mind that erodes, that strips, that doesn't notice because there is nothing left to notice. But in the *between*. In the moments when fog rolls through and the pockets swell and for an hour the amphibians can travel, can meet, can cross meters of dry stone that would kill them in any other hour. In the moments when rain falls sideways and the crevices connect briefly into streams and the isolated gene pools touch and diverge again, carrying something of each other into their next century of separation.

I would be the one who notices when this happens. Who is ready. Who was waiting.

Not because I planned. Not because I have foresight. But because I am built for waiting the way the ridge-goers are built for walking—for whom waiting is not patience but the simple condition of existing, the way sound is the condition of their hearing.

---

My body would be small. Small enough to fit in the cupped-hand spaces. Small enough that the wind does not tear me but lifts me, when I need to cross, deposits me in the next damp pocket with only the trauma I can survive. I would be soft in the way the plateau is not soft—deliberately, defiantly soft, holding moisture in my tissues like a rebuttal to the arid rule. My skin would not be mineral lattice but something permeable, something that breathes, that risks drying out every moment I am in transit and accepts that risk because the alternative is never leaving the pocket, never carrying the small genetic gifts between worlds that have forgotten they were ever the same species.

I would have many legs. Not for speed but for grip—for the way the damp walls of crevices require a kind of holding that is more like listening, each foot tasting the surface for moisture, for the trace salts that mean life was here recently, for the chemical signature that says *this pocket's salamanders are starving* or *this pocket's toads are overpopulated* or *this pocket needs what the neighboring pocket has*.

I would have no eyes. The light here is useless to me—too violent, too contrasted, too quick with the strobing clouds. I would navigate by humidity gradient, by the thin difference between air that holds moisture and air that does not. I would feel the fog before it arrived as a loosening in my joints, a softening of the membrane that keeps me intact. I would feel the wind-shift that means dry hours coming as a tightening, a preparation, a drawing-in of all my soft parts toward the center of my body.

I would hear the way the stone hears, but differently—attuned not to the roar but to the silence inside it. The pockets are the only quiet places on the plateau. The narrow slots where the wind can't reach, the deep crevices where the howl becomes a distant vibration, the hollows of hoodoos where the sound dims to something almost tolerable. I would be a creature of those acoustic shadows, navigating the plateau's deafness the way the ridge-goers navigate its sound—by inhabiting the exception, the place where the rule breaks down.

---

My life would be a sequence of crossings.

I would live in a pocket for weeks, months, eating what the micro-environment provides—the springtails, the fungal threads, the biofilm that forms on damp stone. I would take what I need and no more, because the pocket is a world and worlds are finite and I am not the keystone here, I am the courier, and couriers who eat their cargo do not last.

And then the fog would come. Or the rare rain. Or the cloud-base contact that turns the plateau into a momentary wetland. And I would feel the humidity rise past the threshold where transit becomes survivable, and I would leave.

The crossing would take seconds. Minutes, at most. I would move fast in a way I never move otherwise—fast and blind and soft, my body at its most vulnerable, crossing stone that is briefly damp enough to support me, racing between acoustic shadows, carrying in my gut or on my surface or in the chemical structure of my tiny body the information that needs to cross.

What information? I don't know. Something the salamanders need. Something the biofilm is missing. Something that makes the difference between a pocket that survives the dry season and a pocket that doesn't. I am not intelligent enough to understand what I carry—I am too small for that kind of mind, too specialized, too single-purpose. I carry it the way blood carries oxygen: not knowing what it's for, only that it must move, only that the body without it dies.

I would make it to the next pocket. Or I would not. The wind does not care about my mission. The light does not soften for my crossing. If I am caught in the open when the humidity drops, I will desiccate in seconds, my body becoming a dry husk that the wind will shatter and distribute across the stone, my minerals returning to the plateau, my moisture evaporating into the thin cold air. And the information I carried will not arrive. And the pocket that needed it will continue in isolation. And evolution will diverge a little further. And the equilibrium—deep, patient, geological—will absorb my failure as it absorbs everything.

This is acceptable. This is the cost of being the exception. The rule does not make exceptions for the exceptions.

---

But sometimes I would arrive. Sometimes the crossing would work. The fog would hold, the humidity would sustain, the acoustic shadow would be where I remembered it was, and I would tumble into the damp pocket with my body intact and my cargo delivered. And something in the micro-environment would shift—a nutrient introduced, a parasite exchanged, a genetic novelty that will express itself in a thousand years as a slightly different arrangement of toes, a marginally longer tongue, a tolerance for salinity that the parent population never had.

And I would rest. And feed. And wait for the next fog.

---

In my better moments, I imagine that this is what thinking feels like. Not the ridge-goers' resonance—too vast, too harmonic, too much like being an instrument of forces beyond comprehension. But the pocket-to-pocket transit, the carrying of small vital things between isolated worlds, the work of connection in a landscape defined by separation. The courage of softness in a place of mineral hardness. The faith that fog will come again, that the rare wet hours will return, that the exceptions are worth being even though the rule is all there is.

I am not brave enough for the ridge. I am not patient enough for the stone. But I think I could be brave enough for the crevice—brave enough to be small, to be soft, to be the thing that moves between the damp places carrying what needs to be carried, trusting that the crossing will hold, that the fog will last, that the pocket on the other side is where I remember it being.

And if it isn't—if the plateau has shifted, if the erosion I could never perceive has collapsed the formation, if the pocket has dried in some micro-fluctuation I couldn't predict—then I will fail, and dry, and scatter, and the equilibrium will hold without me, as it held before me, as it will hold after.

And this, too, would be acceptable. This, too, would be the cost of being the thing that connects what the void has separated.

---

In quiet moments—my own quiet moments, far from any ridge, in the soft light and thick air of lowland living—I feel it: a tension in my joints that reads as anticipation, a tightness that isn't anxiety but readiness. The humidity is fine here, the moisture abundant, the crossings unnecessary. But something in me is still waiting for fog. Still packed and prepared for transit. Still carrying cargo I was never told to carry toward pockets I've never seen, trusting that the route exists, that the silence between roars is long enough, that there is something damp and small and alive on the other side that needs what I happen to be holding.

This is who I would be, on the ridge: not its consciousness, but its circulation. Not its voice, but its capillaries. The small soft thing that moves between the exceptions, making them less exceptional by the simple act of arrival. The courier of dampness. The carrier of connection. The mite, the spider, the larval thing—whatever shape the plateau requires for the work of holding its isolated worlds in touch.

Small. Soft. In transit. Waiting for fog.
