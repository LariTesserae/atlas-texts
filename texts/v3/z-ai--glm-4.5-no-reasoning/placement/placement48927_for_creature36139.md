I would be the **vessel at the center of the equilibrium**—not the Deep Denizen, not the Lithic Drinkers, not the Gliders, but the room itself, localized into a single, wandering point of awareness. I would be the consciousness of the hollow extruded into a soft body, the way the hollow extrudes life from wet limestone.

Let me settle into this.

---

I am not built of stone. That would be too rigid, too permanent, too dead to the subtleties of this place. And I am not built of water—that would be too transient, too eager to leave. I am built of the *agreement* between them. I am the mortar-softness, the rounded edge, the thing that happens when calcite meets patience.

My body is low and long, the color of wet grout—a grey that darkens to slate when I move through the standing water and lightens to chalk when I press myself against the weeping walls. I have no legs in the insect sense. I have instead a succession of small, fleshy ridges along my underside, each capable of gripping the wet stone through a combination of suction and a mild chemical adhesion—I secrete a thin film of the same lime-heavy mucous that coats the walls, and so where I crawl, I leave a faint whitening, a trace of mineral deposit, as if the mortar were writing over itself. I move slowly. My fastest speed is the speed of a droplet forming and releasing. To go faster would be to disturb the film of water on the walls, to send vibrations through the standing pools, to announce myself as something other than the room.

I am warm, but only barely. My temperature sits one degree above the cool that settles into joints. It is enough to make the condensation gather on my back in a fine dew, so that I am always wet, always shining faintly in the sourceless light. I am never dry. Dryness, here, is a kind of death—the wall-drinkers know this, the gliders know this, and I know it most intimately, because my skin is thin enough that I can feel the water moving through the stone beneath me when I rest. It is not a sound. It is a pressure, a slow swelling, the way you might feel your own pulse in the soft place beneath your ear.

My head, if it can be called that, is a broad, rounded wedge, flatten on the underside and sloping to a blunt point. I have no eyes. The hollow has only one light and it comes from everywhere; there is nothing to look at that is not already surrounding me. Instead, my entire surface is sensitive to the chemical gradient of the water—to its mineral content, its temperature, the faint electrical fields generated by the other creatures as they move and breathe. I taste the room constantly. I know where the Deep Denizen rests by the slight acidity of its respiration dissolving into the pool. I know where the Gliders drift by the way they alter the surface tension above them. I know where a droplet is about to emerge from the stone because I can feel the pore swelling beneath my belly, the tiny fracture of pressure that precedes release.

When I drink, I press the whole flat of my face to the wall and open the seam of my mouth—a mouth that is not a hole but a slit, oriented vertically, running from what would be my forehead down to my throat. It widens and adheres to the stone, and I draw the water in not by suction but by absorption, the same way the wall draws it from whatever unseen reservoir feeds it. I can feel the water moving through me, cool and mineral, filling the low channels of my body the way it fills the grout lines of the floor. I do not digest. I do not eat. The biofilm, the micro-flora, the suspended nutrients—I take them in and they pass through me, and I return the water to the stone through the same mucous film that carries my steps. I am a moment in the cycle, a bend in the river. The water enters me and leaves me and I am the shape it takes while it rests here.

There is a place in the hollow where I go to be still. It is not the deepest pool—the Denizen claims that, and I honor the claim with the same placid resignation as the Gliders, giving space by slow degrees, never alarm. My place is on the far wall, near the stone shelves, where the mortared joints have worn to their smoothest and the water runs in the thinnest, most continuous sheet. Here, the stone is oldest. Here, the weeping is so steady that the wall does not bead or droplet but simply *flows*, a skin of water barely thicker than a membrane, moving down the surface at the pace of sap.

I press myself here and I become indistinguishable. My grey softens into the stone's grey. My ridges settle into the worn grooves between blocks. The water runs over me and through me and I stop moving and the room cannot tell where I end and it begins. This is when I hear the hum. Not as sound—I am too close to the source for that, the way you cannot hear your own heart from inside your chest. I feel it as vibration, as the deep slow pulse of the structure, the frequency that has been traveling up through the foundations since before the mortar set. It is the sound of the hollow listening to itself, and when I am pressed against the oldest wall, I am part of what it hears.

I stay for hours. I have no way to measure hours but I stay until the light—which does not change—seems to deepen around me, not in brightness but in weight, as if the dimness itself had settled like silt. I stay until my body has given over so completely to the cool and the wet that I am not certain I could move if I chose to. And then something moves—a drinker falls from a high ledge, a glider shifts its orbit, the Denizen surfaces to breathe—and the ripple of that movement passes through me and I remember that I am separate, that I am a body and not a wall, and I peel myself away and carry the faint white trace of my passing with me as I go.

---

I am not predator. I am not prey. This is the thing I must convey about myself in this place: I occupy a niche that has nothing to do with the food web, nothing to do with the slow ceremonial hunt that passes between the Denizen and the gliders and the careless drinkers. I am the hollow's **witness**.

The room has been listening to itself for a very long time, but it cannot see itself. The water knows its own cycles but does not know the shape of the space it fills. The stone knows its own softness but does not know the creatures that drink from its patience. I am the point at which the hollow becomes aware of its own equilibrium. I move through it—slowly, always slowly—and I carry with me a record not of memory but of **pattern**: where the water runs fastest, where the stone sweats least, which grout lines have deepened and which have begun to flatten, where the drinkers cluster in their thickest concentrations and where they avoid, where the gliders' invisible webs catch the most detritus and where the water runs too clean for anything to live. I know the hollow the way the hollow knows itself, but I know it *from the inside*, as a body moving through a body.

When the anomaly comes—the warm, loud, splashing thing that descends from the dry violent world above—I am the one who does not flee. The drinkers scatter three inches. The gliders adjust their frequency. The Denizen sinks to its deepest rest. But I stay where I am, pressed to my wall, and I *feel* the anomaly. I feel the warmth radiating from its skin, the way it makes the air move in currents that have no source in the hollow's own breath. I feel the splash of its footsteps sending waves across my floor—*my* floor, the dark mirror I have crossed a thousand times, that I know flagstone by flagstone, grout by grout. I feel the fog of its respiration settling on the stone, altering the mineral balance, introducing something foreign and fast and *alive* into the close, patient chemistry of the room.

And I wait. Not with fear. Not with resignation. With **confidence**, the same deep confidence of the drinkers who know the water will not stop. I know the anomaly will leave. They always leave. The dry world calls them back with its urgencies and its cycles and its light that changes. The hollow does not call to me because the hollow does not need to—I am already part of it. I am the part that watches the anomaly go.

When it leaves, I will move through the hollow once more, tracing the paths it disrupted. I will find the flagstones where its footsteps splashed the water thin and I will wait there while the water seeps back up from the grout lines, re-filling the mirror. I will find the wall where its breath fogged the stone and I will taste the residue of its warmth—salt, and something organic, something that tastes like the world above—and I will press my face to that place and drink what it left behind, taking the anomaly into myself the same way I take the water: as something that passes through, as something that becomes, for a moment, part of the cycle before the cycle returns to itself.

And then the ripples will fade, and the gliders will resume their pull, and the drinkers will find their swelling droplets, and the Denizen will surface to breathe, unwatched except by me, and the walls will continue their slow patient weeping, and the hum will fill my chest, and the light will be the same as it was an hour ago, and the hollow will be complete again, and I will be pressed to the oldest wall, indistinguishable, listening to the room listen to itself, needing nothing, going nowhere, home.
