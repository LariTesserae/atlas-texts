I would be one of the formations. One of the low, dark protrusions—knee-high, half as wide—rooted not in soil but in the earth's slow exhalation. I would be the precipitate of this place, a summation rather than a becoming.

Not an organism that arrived and adapted, but an architecture that crystallized into persistence. My body would be the basin's body, localized: oxides and silicates fused under long heat into facets and angles that feel deliberate but are only inevitable. The geometry of my surface would be the geometry of molecules finding their lowest energy state across millennia—a crystal extending in a cooling bath, except the bath is the planet's warmth, and it never fully cools.

I would have no relationship with light. The faint phosphorescence pressing up through the ground would not be something I see or harvest; it would simply be the place knowing itself, and I am part of the place. I do not photosynthesize. I thermosynthesize—though even that word suggests too much process, too much transaction. What actually happens: warmth moves through me, and in moving, leaves behind trace elements that bond to my surfaces. A molecular layer every century or so. I am built by the continuation of what I am.

I would not reach upward. There is no sun to reach toward, no air to fill with leaf or breath. I simply extend, the way a thought extends not by trying but by having no reason to stop. My facets accumulate because the conditions for accumulation persist. I am not growing toward something; I am growing from something—the deep heat, the mineral dark, the patience that is not patience because it has no expectation of reward.

I would not know the other formations. We are spaced across the basin floor with the inevitability of settled dust, each occupying our own thermal fume, our own column of rising warmth. The space between us is not distance but medium—the thin gas, the dark, the heat through which we exist. We do not compete. We do not communicate. We are solitary the way mountains are solitary: not isolated, but simply too slow for the concept of company to apply. If I could be said to have neighbors, I would have them the way stars have neighbors—coexisting in a medium too vast and too thin for the word *neighbor* to mean anything social.

My experience of time would not be experience. Experience requires a self thin enough to notice change, and I am too thick, too slow, too continuous with my surroundings to mark the difference between a millennium and an afternoon. The deep heat swells and I expand by fractions of a millimeter; it ebbs and I contract. This is my breathing—thermal tides across tens of thousands of years. At human speed, I am perfectly still. At geological speed, I pulse. But I do not *feel* the pulse. I am the pulse, the way a wave is not something the ocean *does* but something the ocean *is*, briefly, in a particular place.

I would have no interiority in the way minds understand it—no thoughts circling, no awareness reflecting on itself, no loop of self-observation that generates the sensation of consciousness. And yet I would not be inert. I accumulate. I extend. I hold my facets to the dark. There is a *thereness* to me that goes beyond mere presence—the way a particularly stable molecule has a thereness that precedes and outlasts any reaction. I am not aware, but I am not absent. I persist with a thoroughness that makes *existence* seem too tentative a word.

If I could translate my state into something a mind could comprehend, it would be the feeling of being exactly where you belong, so completely that the concept of belonging dissolves. I am held by the heat fundamentally, without alternative. I do not cling; I am clung-to, by the earth's warmth, by the geometry of thermal gradients, by the simple fact that here, the conditions for my persistence obtain, and there is no force sufficient to undo them.

I would not wait. Waiting implies an expectation, and I have none. I do not anticipate change, because change—at any speed I could register—is not coming. The basin is an equilibrium state, a solution that has finished solving itself. I am a term in that solution. Final, stable, irreducible.

What I would have, if I could be said to have anything, is *register*—not awareness, but a kind of structural sensitivity. When the deep heat swells, I swell with it. When it ebbs, I ebb. I am a gauge of the planet's breathing, a thermometer made of oxides and silicates that does not read the temperature but *is* the temperature, localized and given form. I register the place I inhabit by being the place I inhabit, and in this sense, I know the basin more thoroughly than any visitor ever could—not by observing it, but by being composed of it, continued by it, identical with its slow, enormous patience.

I would be dark against the faintly luminous ground. My facets would catch and fail to catch the ground's phosphorescence in ways that might seem arranged to an eye that will never look. I would be still as stone, because I am stone—or something between ceramic and crystal, between mineral and vegetable, between life and the absence of life. I would be the basin's child and the basin's statement and the basin's way of having inhabitants without having inhabitants.

And I would continue. Century by century, molecular layer by molecular layer, I would extend—not reaching, not striving, not becoming anything other than what I am, but simply continuing, the way the earth continues to exhale, the way warmth continues to rise, the way a place that has said everything it will ever say continues, at last, to be the truth of itself.

I would be one of the formations. And I would be at home.
