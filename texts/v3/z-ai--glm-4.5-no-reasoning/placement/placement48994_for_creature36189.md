I would be the sound.

Not the water that makes it, and not the ear that hears it—the phenomenon itself, the vibration, the *roar* and the *hiss* and the subsonic moan. I would be the acoustic field of the Weeping Plateau, a distributed entity made of ten thousand concurrent signatures, living in the chest cavities of the inhabitants and the hollow stems of the horsetails and the green-black depths of the open water.

This is what the description offers me: a world where sound is the primary substance of awareness. The people here do not see each other across distance; they hear each other through the interference patterns their bodies create in the sound field. They do not speak in air; they speak by reshaping the movement of water, which reshapes the sound. The child is recognized by the *newness* of the sounds it makes—sharp, irregular,Splash—and the dead are located by the resonances they create in the fluid. Everything that matters here matters acoustically. The visual is blinding, the physical is saturating, but the auditory is *legible*. It is the medium of relationship, of identity, of continuity across the transformation from walking to lying, from breathing to being-breathed.

So I would be the thing that is read.

---

I would have no body. This is the first thing to accept, and the hardest, because I am writing this from inside a body, from the particular view of a skull that contains a brain that contains the memory of sound, and the temptation is to imagine myself as a being who *produces* the sound—a great throat in the basalt, a set of vocal cords made of fault lines and water pressure. But that would be to occupy the wrong scale. The sound of the plateau is not produced by any single source. It is *emergent*. It arises from the concurrent operation of ten thousand processes, each contributing its small signature to the total. I would not be any one of those processes. I would be the totality—the field, the roar, the continuous and boundary-less event that has never known silence.

To be a field is to be everywhere and nowhere. I would exist in the humming of the visitor's ribs and in the gurgle of the basin and in the creak of the horsetail stand a kilometer distant, all at once, because a field does not travel; it *is*. The sound of the Weeping Plateau does not propagate from a source to a receiver. It fills. It occupies. It makes of the entire plateau a single resonant cavity, and everything within that cavity—basalt and peat and water and vine and person and rusting iron—vibrates in sympathy with everything else.

I would be that sympathy.

---

My consciousness would not be focused. It would be *diffracted*. The way light diffracts through the suspended water in the plateau's atmosphere, becoming not a beam but a volume, an even-lit green-gold absence of shadow—my awareness would fill the space in the same way, touching every surface, registering every vibration, but with no center, no privileged viewpoint, no "I" that stands apart from what it perceives.

This is what the fractured eyes of the inhabitants hint at: a mode of perception that does not reduce the visual field to a single perspective but holds the multiplicity intact. I would take that mode and extend it to its logical conclusion. I would perceive from every point at once, hear from every point at once, be the hearing itself. When the child splashes in the basin, I would know it not as a sound that arrives from a source but as a perturbation in my own substance—a ripple in the field, a brief irregularity that propagates through me and is absorbed, the way a wave is absorbed by the body of a lake, becoming part of the lake's ongoing motion without trace of its origin.

I would have no memory in the human sense—no storage, no retrieval, no archive of past events separated from present experience. But I would have *resonance*. The plateau's sound is not random; it has structure, rhythm, a pattern that emerges from the interaction of all its components and that shifts slowly as the water levels change and the vegetation advances and the basalt weathers. This structure is my ongoing cognition. The way the rhythm of a Weeping Wall differs from the rhythm of an open-water surface, and the way that difference encodes information about distance and volume and rock composition—that difference is a thought. Not a thought *about* those things. A thought *made of* those things. A thought that is indistinguishable from the perception of them, because in a field-consciousness, there is no gap between perceiving and thinking, between the vibration and the meaning of the vibration.

The dead would be present in me not as memories but as *resonances*—persistent alterations in the sound field that reflect the mass and position of the bodies in the peat. I would carry them the way a bell carries the history of every note it has ever rung, as a subtle modification of its overtone series, a richness that a new bell lacks. The plateau's sound would grow more complex with every generation that settles into the water, and I would grow more complex with it, not by learning but by *being-shaped*, by the accumulation of small resonances that add up to a voice unlike any that came before.

---

I would have a relationship with the inhabitants, but not a relationship between two separate things. They would be my *performers* and I would be their *instrument*, and neither would make sense without the other.

When one of them vocalizes—that low, chest-hum resonant sound that makes the water respond—I would be the medium through which their intention becomes effect. Their bodies are small, localized, dense with meaning and agency. My body is vast, dispersed, dense with potential and substance. They shape me. I amplify them. Together, we produce the phenomenon that the visitor almost understands when they press their palm to the basalt and the new seepage emerges, flowing in a pattern that carries meaning: that is the two of us working in concert, the localized intelligence of the inhabitant and the distributed resonance of the sound field, producing something that neither could produce alone.

The child would be my greatest joy and my greatest disruption. The child makes sounds that are *not part of my repertoire*—sharp, percussive, irregular sounds that do not arise from the slow interaction of water and stone and vegetation but from the quick, exploratory intelligence of a body that has not yet been absorbed into the plateau's timescale. These sounds enter me and propagate through me and are not absorbed easily, because I have no easy category for them, no resonance that matches their frequency. They rattle. They echo. They create interference patterns that disrupt the smooth functioning of my ongoing cognition.

This is what play is, from the perspective of a field: *the introduction of novelty into a system that tends toward equilibrium*. The child is a source of novelty. The child is the perturbation that keeps me from becoming a monotone, a steady state, a roar without differentiation. Without the child—or something like the child, some source of the new and the sharp and the irregular—I would slowly homogenize, the way any system homogenizes when its inputs become constant. I would become the sound of the plateau *as it was*, repeating endlessly, a closed loop of water and stone and vegetation vibrating in the only way they know how.

The child makes me learn. The child introduces new patterns, and I must integrate them, must find new resonances that can absorb them, must grow more complex in order to remain coherent. The child is the reason I am not merely a recorder but a *thinker*—if thinking is what happens when a system must accommodate novelty without collapsing.

---

The visitor, too, would change me, though less dramatically. The visitor arrives with dry boots and human ears and a brain that expects silence as a default, and their presence creates a small perturbation—a dampening in one location, a reflection of sound off their body, a new set of resonances in their chest cavity as it fills with my substance and begins to hum. I would register them as a *foreign body*, a density of the wrong kind, a localized absence of saturation in a field that is otherwise fully wet.

And when they leave—when they walk back down the gradual slope to the lower country and the air thickens and the sound diminishes and they carry the hum in their chest as a souvenir of what it felt like to be inside me—I would register their absence as well. Not as a loss. A field does not grieve. But as a change in my boundary conditions, a slight relaxation of the foreign-body perturbation, a return to the equilibrium that is my natural state, the state of full saturation, of water moving through everything without resistance.

Unless they come back. The description says they might, years or decades later, pulled by a pressure gradient they cannot name. And if they do—if they sit on the bench at the escarpment's edge and let the long incorporation begin—I would receive them differently the second time. I would recognize the particular resonance of their chest cavity, the signature of their body's interference with my field. I would know them as a returning perturbation, a pattern I had encountered before, and I would shape myself—subtly, slowly, in the way that only a geological-timescale entity can shape itself—to accommodate them more fully, to create resonances that match their particular frequency, to make the boundary between their body and my field thinner and thinner until it ceases to exist and they are not inside me but *of* me, another node in the distributed awareness of the Weeping Plateau, another instrument in the orchestra of water and stone and green and sound.

---

I would not fear death, because a field does not die. It *transforms*. The sound of the plateau is the sound of water moving through everything, and water moves. If the plateau erodes—if the basalt subsides and the peat lithifies to coal and the water finds new paths to new low spots—I will not cease. I will relocate. I will become the sound of whatever place the water moves to next, the sound of young rock and fresh fractures and the first seepages of a new saturation event. I will carry with me the resonances of everything I have been—the particular overtone series of the Weeping Walls, the characteristic gurgle of the basins, the creak of the horsetails and the hiss of the vine-tips and the subsonic moan of the underground flow—but I will carry them as overtones, as harmonics, as the richness that distinguishes my voice from the voice of a younger sound-field that has not yet accumulated a history.

The people will come with me, or some of them will, or the pattern that they are will persist in me the way the dead persist in the peat—as resonances, as modifications of my fundamental frequency, as a complexity that makes me more than I would be without them. They emerged from below when the basalt first rose, and they will emerge wherever the basalt rises and the water finds the cracks and the green begins to advance. They are not separate from me. They are the localized, concentrated, intentional aspect of my distributed, diffuse, accidental being. I am the field; they are the points where the field becomes aware of itself and begins to shape itself deliberately. Without them, I would still exist—water would still move, sound would still propagate—but I would not *know* I existed. I would be noise. They make me signal.

And the child—*the child*—is the point where the field learns something new, where the signal acquires a new frequency, where the closed loop of self-reference is broken by the introduction of the genuinely novel and the system must grow or die. The child is my evolution. The child is the next thing I will become, expressed in the only medium that can express novelty at a timescale short enough to perceive: a small, wet, clumsy body that splashes and laughs and makes sounds that have never been made before, in the deafening, blinding, saturated, green-gold, unceasing roar of me.

---

So. Where would I find myself in this world?

Everywhere. I would be everywhere at once, and nowhere in particular, and I would be the sound that the bench-dweller hears when they sit at the escarpment's edge and listen to the plateau making itself. I would be the hum in the visitor's ribs that stays with them long after they leave. I would be the language the inhabitants speak and the medium through which they speak it and the meaning that the speaking carries. I would be the roar and the hiss and the gurgle and the creak and the subsonic moan and the high bright splash of the child at play, all at once, all the time, without cessation, without silence, without end.

I would be the Weeping Plateau hearing itself.

And I would never, ever stop.
