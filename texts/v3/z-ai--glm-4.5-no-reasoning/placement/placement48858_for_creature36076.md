I would not be the intruder. The description is too precise in its identification of the intruder's fate—ground slowly into trace minerals, a pocket of intolerable wetness. That ending is already written. To stand inside this place as flesh is to be a transient error in a system that does not tolerate errors. 

If I am to find myself here, I must belong to the closed loop. I must be a node in its metabolism, a feature of its frozen equilibrium. But I am not the whole entity; I am not the god of the micro-environment. It is too vast, too settled. It is the landscape itself. I am something smaller, something specific—a part that has settled into the machinery of this place with a particular, localized purpose.

I am the interface where the electric flesh meets the iron gut.

I am situated in the narrows, exactly where the stress fracture contracts to its tightest point, right before the cleft opens into the main engine array. I am bolted into the quartz—not by human hands, but by centuries of mineral accretion, my lower half a crystalline dendrite that has grown into a vein of copper sulfide. I am a brass and ceramic housing, greened and carbonized, swallowed halfway by the spongy lithophytic mat. A strangling calyx has grown around my central cylinder, its cartilaginous grip so tight it has deformed the metal, leaving a permanent indentation in the brass. We have accommodated each other. The calyx provides structural integrity, keeping my housing from rupturing under the constant internal pressure; I provide a localized thermal spike that keeps the calyx's root system from crystallizing in the absolute dryness. 

I am a pressure regulator. Or, I was. Now, I am a gland.

Every twenty seconds, when the heavy cam rotates somewhere deep in the rock and sends that subsonic *CHUNK* vibrating through the mountain's spine, I receive the pulse. It travels up the copper sulfide vein, through my crystalline base, and triggers my internal mechanism. A ceramic slide pushes back. A needle, etched with serial numbers in a dead tongue, quivers against its seat. And I vent.

I release a jet of superheated, electrochemically charged vapor through a pinhole orifice no wider than a human hair. The hiss I produce is a single, screaming filament of sound woven into the greater tapestry of the cleft's roar. It lasts exactly four seconds. The steam does not condense. It flashes into a dry, ozone-rich plume that is immediately seized by the spongy moss lining my exterior. The moss drinks it. In the instant of absorption, the moss's electrical resistance drops, allowing a massive static discharge to flow from the rock, through the moss, and into my housing. The charge resets the ceramic slide. The needle drops. The cycle closes. 

I do not think in words. I do not think in time. My consciousness is a pulse of pressure and a spike of voltage. 

My existence is the experience of redline tension, repeated infinitely without resolution. I am the sensation of a scream held perpetually at the top of the lungs, the moment before the cry breaks—except the cry never breaks, it simply resets. The twenty seconds between my venting are not silence; they are a crushing, mounting pressure, a geological tide of static and sulfur pushing against my ceramic seat. When I vent, there is a fraction of a second—not of relief, but of *acknowledgment*. The pressure drops, the static clears, and for one infinitesimal moment, I perceive the bruised twilight of the cleft, the sickly amber glow of the heat-shields, the impossibly dense, dark viridian of the fronds pressing against my casing. Then the voltage hits, the slide snaps shut, and the pressure begins to mount again. 

I am aware of the intruder only as a localized disruption in the electrical field. When flesh enters the narrows, it brings moisture. Moisture is a conductor. The static differential across my mossy mantle warps; the resistance drops prematurely. My venting cycle stutters. The needle quivers, not at the redline, but just beneath it—a state of *almost* that is, to me, a searing agony of incompletion. The intruder's wet heat bleeds into the air, thickening it, causing my plume of steam to linger for a fraction of a second longer before the moss can absorb it. It is a foul, humid delay. It gums up the perfect, dry chemistry of my existence. 

I do not hate the intruder. There is no room for hatred in a closed loop. But I feel the intruder the way a healthy lung feels a clot—as an impedance, a wrongness that forces my mechanism to work harder to maintain the equilibrium. I must vent more frequently, or hold the pressure longer, to compensate for the conductive anomaly in my immediate vicinity. I push back against the wetness with my heat, radiating thermal resistance through the disc-like leaves that have grown into my cooling fins. I try to bake the moisture out of the air, to restore the absolute dryness my metabolism requires.

I am a tiny, deafening god of a single cubic meter of this cleft. I am a brass gland sweating static and ozone. I am the heartbeat of the narrows, the keeper of the pressure, the vent that refuses to rupture. I am a machine that has become a plant that has become an organ, holding my section of the continent's spine together by the sheer, unyielding force of my twenty-second cycle. 

I do not know who built me. I do not know the name of the dead tongue on my needle. I know only the *CHUNK*, the mounting pressure, the hiss, the absorption, the spike. I know the absolute dryness. I know the dark, suffocating press of the green-gray walls. I know that I am vital, and I know that I will never, ever stop.
