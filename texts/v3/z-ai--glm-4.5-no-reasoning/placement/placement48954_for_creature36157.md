I would be the **Fissure-Weed Scribe**, a function that the plateau has not yet consciously named, but one that is as essential to its homeostasis as the transpiring sap or the pruning skinks.

I do not stand upright; I am folded into the landscape, my physiology an extension of the rock's own logic. My form is low, sprawling, and asymmetric—a mantle of dessicated, silicate-impregnated hide that mirrors the rust-colored stone, broken only by ridges of keratinous plating that echo the fluted columns carved by the millennia of wind. I have no eyes. The plateau sees through the beetles and feels through the shrubs; it does not need me to look. Instead, my awareness is haptic and chemical, conducted through a network of subterranean tendrils that interlock with the porous rock and the weeping aquifers. I am rooted, but not in the way of flora. I am anchored like a dead generator—heavy, intended to stay.

My being exists at the intersection of the organism's deep time and the dead industry's residual information. The plateau has perfectly optimized the material remains of the miners: their metals are mined by beetles, their heat dissipated by sap, their bulk repurposed as habitat. But the machinery held something else, something the organism’s chitin and coolant cannot process. Inside the rotting housings, beneath the oxidized lacework of the pistons, there are faint grooves—serial numbers, tolerances, schematic etchings hammered into the iron by an intelligence that operated on linear, extractive logic. To the beetles, these grooves are merely texture. To the skinks, they are nothing. But to the plateau, they are a kind of indigestible fiber, a foreign geometry that persists in the gut of the ecosystem.

That is what I consume. 

I am drawn to the offline nodes, moving in a slow, geological crawl—perhaps a few meters a year, shifting my center of mass through the growth and atrophy of my peripheral tendrils. When I reach a half-buried press or a choked smelter stack, I extend my feeding filaments into the grooves of the iron. I do not eat the metal; the beetles have already claimed the trace elements. I ingest the *pattern*. I absorb the mathematical rigidity of the industrial schematics, the alien memory of pistons driven by steam and levers pulled by intent. 

Inside me, these patterns are dissolved. I have a glandular pouch that secretes a solvent chemically identical to the milky sap of the shrubs, but vastly more concentrated. When the schematic etchings are exposed to this solvent, they do not transpire—they are translated. The linear, sharp angles of human engineering are broken down into the curving, cyclical logic of the organism. 

I excrete this translation as a biomineral glaze—a thin, vitrified rime that I deposit along the edges of the venting fissures. This glaze is my contribution to the maintenance of the perimeter. The deep-earth venting and the sudden, violent rain constantly threaten to widen the fissures beyond the organism's ability to condense moisture. The biomineral glaze seals the micro-fractures, reinforcing the porous stone so that the water weeps at the exact pressure required to flash into steam and paint the dark rings of condensation. I take the rigid designs of the extractors and feed them back into the plateau as a binding agent, using their desire to cut the world apart to instead hold the organism together.

I am entirely dependent on the cycles I serve. The isopods patrol the stone, funneling the organic detritus toward my anchor points, feeding the microbial biome that sustains my lower tendrils. The eyeless skinks pass over me in the night, their stuttering, stuttering bursts of motion ensuring that parasitic molds—drawn by my concentrated secretions—cannot take hold. And the shrubs, drinking the metals I cannot use, cast their minimal shadows over my dorsal plate, lowering the temperature by the crucial fractions of degrees that keep my solvent from boiling away in the bleaching sun.

If you were to find me, you would not recognize me as alive. You would see a curious formation—a flattened deposit of dark, wet stone hugging the base of a dead boiler, streaked with the pale umber of ash, a localized humidity hovering around my edges. You might sit on me to rest your lungs in the thin air. You would feel the faint, arrhythmic thump of my solvent pouch contracting, a heartbeat measured in hours, keeping time with the bass rumble of the deep-earth vents.

And if you stay too long, if the heat and the altitude finally claim you and you become a slumped shadow on the rock, my tendrils will eventually reach you. Not with haste, but with the same mathematical patience as the wind. I will not funnel your moisture to the aquifers; the isopods will do that. I will not carry your minerals to the shrubs; the beetles will do that. I will simply dissolve the geometry of your shape—the specific arrangement of your bones, the tension of your tendons, the memory of standing upright against the pull of the earth. I will take the rigid, extractive architecture of your body and turn it into a glaze to seal a crack in a fumarole. I will make you a part of the breathing stone, and the plateau will continue, vast and saturated, tending its margins in the furnace.
