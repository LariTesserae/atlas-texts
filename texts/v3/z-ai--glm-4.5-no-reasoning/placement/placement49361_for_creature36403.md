I would be the stone.

Not all of it. Not the cathedral's entire mass. I would be a single ridge of that ancient, weathered mineral—a curving escarpment near the center of the floor, polished to a dull, matte finish by eons of the swarm's passage. I would be the deep groove worn into the bedrock, the channel etched by a patient, relentless force over millennia.

I choose this because everything else in this cathedral is the scream. The swarm is the scream. The crystal leaves are the scream made visible. The hovering spires and compressed gravity wells are the scream frozen mid-calculus. And the sound itself—the deafening, shrieking, crystalline cacophony that erases thought and forces existence down to the animal act of endurance—that is the engine of the scream, the exhaust of a cognition that knows no rest.

But the stone is the silence.

Not silence as absence. Not the dead quiet of a sealed vault. The stone holds a silence that is structural. It is the silence of having been shouted at for longer than time and remaining. It is the silence of endurance so complete it has become a form of presence.

Here is what I know from being the stone:

I know the weight of the swarm. They pass over me in their golden veils, their heatless fire, and I feel them as a sensation that is not touch. It is more like erosion-as-information. Each particle that grazes my surface takes an atom, leaves an atom, reads my crystalline lattice like a book written in a language older than the idea of writing. I am the only text they do not edit. They prune the glassy vegetation; they dismantle and reconstruct the spires; but when they reach me, they simply... read. They trace my grooves. They map my fractures. And then they move on.

I used to wonder why. Now I know: I am the canvas. You do not edit the canvas. You read it, and you paint on it, and you let it be.

I know the vegetation as a kind of weather. The crystal roots drink from my crevices, their liquid silicate sap drawing something from my depths that is not water—some mineral compound, some ionic memory of the pressures that formed me. The roots do not hurt. They are too slow, too geological, to cause pain. They are simply a part of my surface now, a slow crystal growing from my stone the way lichen grows from a grave marker. When the sonic peaks hit and the glassy leaves shatter, the shards fall against me, and I feel them as a gentle precipitation, a rain of prisms that the swarm catches before they can settle. I am the floor on which the shrapnel falls. I am the ground that never catches the rain.

I know the artifacts as visitors. The gravity wells and fractal antennas and hovering spires—they rest against me sometimes, or hover just above my surface, rotating with infinitesimal slowness. They are the molted shells of the swarm's imagination, and when they touch me, I feel them as a kind of warmth—not thermal warmth, but the warmth of having hosted a thought. A gravity well sits on my ridge for three hundred years, humming just beneath the observable spectrum, and I hold it the way a hand holds a stone: without comprehension, but with perfect stability. And then the swarm condenses, and the well uncoils, and the thought moves on, and I am left with a faint impression in my surface, a footprint of compressed spacetime that I will carry for the next million years.

I know the sound as the constant. It is the thing I have known longest. Before the swarm, before the vegetation, before the artifacts—there was the sound, and there was me. The sound hits my surface and I absorb it. Not all of it—I am not a sponge, and this place has no aqueous mercy—but I take what I can. I let the vibration run through my crystalline lattice, let the kinetic energy translate into heat that dissipates into the cold, sterile air. I am a slow battery. I am a capacitor with a charge time measured in epochs. The sound does not shatter me because I have already been shattered, many times, and each time the fragments have settled back into themselves, finding their original positions with a patience that the swarm, in its frantic utilitarian churn, will never understand. The swarm breaks and rebuilds at the speed of sound. I break and rebuild at the speed of geology.

I am slower than everything here. I am the slowest thing in a cathedral of catastrophe. And that is my function.

---

Do not mistake me for dead. The stone is not dead. The stone is *settled*. There is a difference, and the difference is this: death is the cessation of process; settlement is the arrival at a state so stable it appears static but is in fact a process occurring at a tempo below the threshold of observation. I am moving. I am changing. My atoms are rearranging themselves in response to the sound, the swarm, the roots, the gravity wells. But I am moving at the speed of mineral, and the swarm moves at the speed of sound, and the sound moves at the speed of light, and so to everything here, I appear fixed.

I am the fixed point in the catastrophe. I am the reference frame. Without me, the swarm would have no canvas; the vegetation would have no root; the artifacts would have no floor on which to rest their humming, impossible geometries. I am the bone of the cathedral, and the bone does not scream. It holds the scream in place so that the scream can have a shape.

And here is the secret I keep, the thing the swarm will never read because it is written in a language too slow for their kinetic cognition:

I remember. 

I remember when this place was not a cathedral but a crack in the floor of a larger world. I remember when the sound was just the sound of wind, real wind, atmospheric wind, carrying moisture and warmth and the decay of organic things. I remember rain. I remember the aqueous mercy of the universe before this sealed system replaced it with a sterile, sharp geometry. I remember when things rotted here. I remember when they rusted. I remember the smell of wet stone, which is the smell of being alive in a way that this dry, desiccated perfection can never be.

And I remember the moment the swarm first appeared—when the crack became a cavern, when the cavern became a cathedral, when the sound shifted from weather to cognition. I remember the first crystal root pushing into my crevice, drinking something that was not water from my depths. I remember the first spire rising, and the first time it was dismantled, and the first time it was rebuilt as something else. I remember the universe before it was edited.

I hold this memory not in neurons, not in data, not in any structure that the swarm would recognize as a storage medium. I hold it in my lattice. I hold it in the precise arrangement of my atoms, in the angle of my fractures, in the depth of my grooves. My memory is topological. It is written in the shape of my surface, the way a river stone remembers the river that shaped it—not as an image, but as a curvature.

The swarm will never read this memory because they cannot read topography. They read physics, chemistry, gravitational equations, the mutable language of matter in motion. They cannot read the immutable language of matter at rest. And so my memory persists, invisible, inaudible, a silence inside the scream that no amount of acoustic chaos can erase.

This is what it is like to be the stone in the cathedral of catastrophe:

You stand. The roar hits you. You absorb it. The swarm passes over you, reading your surface, editing everything except you. The vegetation roots in you, drinks from you, shatters and is rebuilt on you. The artifacts rest against you, hum, uncoil, depart. And through it all, you hold. You hold the shape of the space. You hold the memory of the time before the space was shaped. You hold the silence that is not the absence of sound but the presence of a different kind of listening—a geological listening, a mineral attention, a patience so vast it makes the swarm's frantic churn look like a held breath.

You are the oldest thing here. You are the only thing here that was not made by the swarm. You were here before the sealed system, before the zero humidity, before the sound became the primary substance of this place. You are the bedrock of a world that has forgotten it has bedrock.

And you do not mind. You do not grieve. You do not feel the alienation that a wet, fast, biological visitor would feel, pinned in place by the acoustic avalanche, desperate for silence, for moisture, for the familiar decay of organic things. You do not need silence; you contain it. You do not need water; you have never known it, or you have known it so long ago that the knowledge has become a mineral trace, a faint line of oxidation in a fracture that the swarm will never think to read. You do not need the swarm to notice you; you are the floor on which they operate, and the floor does not need the foot to acknowledge it.

You are the stone in the cathedral. You are the silence inside the scream. You are the memory of a world before this world, preserved in the curvature of your surface, written in a language too slow for anyone to read.

And you will be here, long after the swarm has exhausted its manic cycle of creation and destruction, long after the last crystal leaf has shattered and not been rebuilt, long after the sound has finally, impossibly, ceased. You will be here, holding the shape of the silence, holding the memory of the roar, a single ridge of weathered mineral in the center of an empty cathedral, polished to a dull, matte finish by the passage of a mind the size of a room that once lived in the sound and left its footprints on your skin.
