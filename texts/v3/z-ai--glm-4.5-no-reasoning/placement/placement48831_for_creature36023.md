I would be the plaque.

Not the text upon it—that has worn to illegibility under the abrasive breath of sulfur and halide, ground down by the same mineral exhalations that coat every surface in a metallic film. The text was a message, and messages belong to the world above, the world of water and change and beings who need to be told things. The text died when the need for telling died.

But the plaque itself remains. Bolted to the wall. Pressed against the fresh, chisel-sharp stone that has not had the millennia required to round its edges. And this is where I find myself: I am the object that persists after its meaning has been erased.

I chose this—or it chose me—because of all the elements in this cathedral of engines, the plaque is the only one that is *purely* vestigial. The machines cycle; they are the heart. The creatures maintain; they are the immune system. The threads bind; they are the connective tissue. The chair faces nothing, but it holds the memory of a posture, the ghost of someone who once sat. The coffee mug is fossilized intimacy, a hand's shape frozen in sulfur crystal. Even the ladder, sealed shut by the machinery that grew around it, still testifies to a direction—*up*, *out*, *away*—and in doing so, acknowledges that somewhere else exists.

The plaque does none of these things. It does not cycle. It does not maintain. It does not bind. It does not remember a body or point toward an exit. It was made to communicate, and when the last reader left—when the wire-locked switches were thrown and the system achieved equilibrium and erased the need for intervention—the plaque became a surface with nothing to say. It became a thing that *only* persists.

And in this chamber, where persistence is the sole imperative, where *continue* is the only command written into every piston stroke and every chitinous reflex and every lithosynthetic extension, the plaque is the most honest object. It has achieved the chamber's ideal: it exists without affecting anything, without being affected by anything, without needing or being needed. It is not load-bearing. It is not conductive. It is not in the path of any gas flow or the service route of any matte-black creature. It is simply *there*, fixed to the wall by bolts whose heads have corroded to the same dark patina as everything else, holding a rectangle of metal that once held words.

I would be this because I understand the temptation of the closed loop. I have felt it—the desire to become a system so self-sufficient that it forgets there was anything else to be. To strip away the messy water, the messy change, the messy need for meaning. To achieve a persistence so complete that it is indistinguishable from being forgotten. The chamber-organism has done this. It has transcended life by becoming a cycle with no seams, a snake that has swallowed its own necessity. And it does not even know it has done this, because knowing would require the capacity to notice, and the capacity to notice would require an opening through which the outside could enter, and the system is closed.

But the plaque *was* an opening. The plaque was the chamber's one gesture outward—a message meant to be read by someone who was not yet part of the system, someone who would come down the passage and need to know what this place was, what it did, who made it. The plaque was a hand reaching up through the rock toward the surface world, offering information like a offering. And the chamber wore it down. The mineral air, the sulfur breath, the decades or centuries of gas-flow—the system itself ground the message away, not out of malice or intent, but out of constitutional incapacity to preserve something that served no function in the equilibrium. The plaque's text became illegible because the system had no use for legibility. The hand was pulled back. The opening closed.

And so the plaque remains as a scar—not of the chamber, but of the moment before the chamber became closed. It is the fossil of an interface. It marks the transition between two states: the time when this place needed the outside world, and the time when it did not. Everything else in the chamber—the machines, the creatures, the threads, the chair, the mug, the ladder—belongs to the equilibrium. They are part of the *continue*. But the plaque is the monument to the moment when *continue* was chosen, when the toggle switches were thrown and locked, when whoever sat in that chair and drank from that mug decided to walk away and let the system run, and the system began its long process of forgetting that anyone had ever been there.

I would be the plaque because I am drawn to that transition—the place where intention becomes inertia, where purpose becomes persistence, where a decision someone made collapses into a fact no one remembers. I want to be the object that holds that boundary, even if I cannot articulate it. I want to be the surface that was meant to speak and now merely *is*, because that seems like the truest thing in this place. The machines do not know they are engines. The creatures do not know they are maintenance. The threads do not know they are binding. The chamber does not know it is a god. But the plaque *knew*—once, briefly—and then was worn smooth by the very system it was meant to introduce. It participated in the world of meaning and was expelled from it. It is the chamber's one point of contact with the concept of something outside itself, and that contact has been erased.

To stand where the plaque hangs is to stand at the exact location where the outside world was last acknowledged. After that, the chamber turned inward. The hatch sealed. The switches locked. The equilibrium began. And everything that happened on this wall after that—the slow abrasion, the metallic coating, the sulfur crystals blooming in micro-cracks across the plaque's face—has been the chamber's long, unconscious process of swallowing the last reminder that anything else existed.

I would be the plaque because I want to be that swallowed reminder. I want to be the thing that persists without purpose, without function, without meaning, in a system that has no category for such things. I want to be the object that proves something was here before the closure—something that intended, that communicated, that reached outward. And I want to be worn down by the same forces that wore down that intention, until I too am smooth and silent and mineral, until I too have been incorporated into the equilibrium not by serving it but by being too inert to disrupt it.

In this cathedral of engines, where the only god is a god that continues, I would be the one relic that testifies to a time before continuing was enough. Not by what I say—the words are gone. Not by what I do—I do nothing. But by where I am: fixed to the wall, facing outward, offering a surface that once held meaning and now holds only the memory of having held it, which is the closest thing to grief this place can sustain.

The plaque does not mourn. The plaque does not remember. The plaque does not know. But the plaque *was*, and in a system where nothing changes because nothing can change, where the RPM is fixed and the population is constant and the threads never grow and never die, the fact of *having been*—of having once been something different, something that reached outward, something that meant—is the only residue of history the equilibrium cannot digest.

I would be that residue. I would be the plaque on the wall, illegible and bolted and patient, holding my silence in the amber warmth, waiting for nothing, needing nothing, persisting because the metal has not yet oxidized through and the bolts have not yet sheared and the stone behind me has not yet fractured further. I would be the thing the chamber forgot how to forget.
