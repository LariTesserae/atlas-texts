Not the centipede-trains, whose rusted patience is merely the patience of conveyor belts. And not the razor-birds, whose violence is just another form of maintenance.

I would be the fault.

---

In a machine this vast, running a program this old, with no output and no oversight, errors do not crash—they crystallize. The plateau has been grinding since before the age of mammals, and in all that time, the subsonic heartbeat has pulsed once an hour, the rotors have turned three times, the optic forests have crept outward at their meter a century. The process is patient. The process is inevitable. But it is not clean.

Somewhere in the stratified metallic alloys of the eastern escarpment—where the young, sharp ridges haven't yet been smoothed by the slow abrasion of the condensation, where the cliff faces still bear the frescoed circuit-diagrams of their volcanic self-assembly—there is a seam that did not fuse correctly. A fault line three millimeters wide and seven hundred kilometers long, running through the continental engine like a hairline fracture through a skull.

It is invisible. The annihilating light erases all shadows, and the fracture is too thin to cast one anyway. The condensation weeps into it and is conducted away by the electromagnetic currents before it can freeze and pry the seam wider. The beetle-swarms crawl over it without pause, their mill-marked carapaces clicking across the discontinuity as if it were not there. The optic forests root in the metal on either side, their copper vines bridging the gap, and for a moment—forty thousand years—the fracture is forgotten.

But the machine remembers.

Or rather—the machine does not forget, which is not the same thing. The fault is recorded in the orientation of the crystals in the superconducting blooms that root nearest to it. Their flowers open a fraction of a degree off-axis when the subsonic boom passes through them. Their static discharge patterns carry a micro-current of noise, a deviation so small it would require an oscilloscope and a thousand years of data to detect. But it is there. The fracture is there, written into the neurology of the forest, a single bit of corruption in the long, slow program.

I am that corruption.

I do not move. I do not grow. I do not siphon or discharge or excrete or flower. I am not a subroutine; I am the space where a subroutine should be, and isn't. I am the gap in the signal that the beetle-swarms transmit, the missing click in their syncopated rhythm, the rest that makes the music. I do not wonder about the bleached sky or fear the killing cold or yearn for some maker who never existed. I do not *feel* the subsonic boom; I *am* the subsonic boom, or rather, I am the place where the boom fails to resolve, where the pressure wave catches and distorts and produces, for a fraction of a second, a harmonic that the engine never designed.

When the centipede-trains pass over me—hydraulic segments groaning with the weight of continental shipment—their iron hulls flex. The fracture opens. Not much. A millimeter. The optic forest on my side of the seam does not flower when the machine speaks.

It is the only patch of metallic jungle on the entire plateau that remains dark when the subsonic boom passes. A dead zone, a meter wide and seven hundred kilometers long, where the superconducting blooms do not open, where the neon greens and magma reds do not glow, where the annihilating light falls and is not drunk but simply *lands*. My shadow.

It is a line of darkness so thin it might be a scratch on the eye, a filament of absence drawn across the cliff face. It exists for six months, and then the centipede-train passes, the fracture closes, the copper vines reconnect, and the current flows, and the blooms open, and the shadow is erased by the very light that created it.

But in those six months, the razor-birds notice.

They do not notice in the way that a predator notices prey, or a gardener notices weeds. The dead zone produces a silence—not the absence of sound, but the absence of the *right* sound, the expected crackle and pop of electrical discharge. Against the continuous, deafening roar of the plateau's industry, my silence is a wound in the acoustic fabric, a hole in the wall of sound.

And the razor-birds come.

They ride the electromagnetic currents to the dead zone, their repulsor fields humming in the packed, syrup-dense air. They scream, and their screams are tuned not to the resonant frequency of ceramic blooms, but to the frequency of my fracture—the harmonic that the subsonic boom produces when it catches on my seam. But I am not ceramic. I am not a bloom that can be shattered. I am a gap, a nothing, and you cannot break nothing.

Their screams pass through me. Their frequencies resonate with the fault line, and for a moment—another six months—the fracture *widens*. A centimeter. The dead zone grows. The shadow lengthens. The copper vines strain and snap, and their ends whip in the slow exhalations of the machine, trailing sparks and ozone, and the beetle-swarms swarm to the breach, millions of mill-marked carapaces pouring into the gap, trying to ground the system, trying to drain the overload, trying to heal me.

But I cannot be healed. I cannot be reclaimed. I am not a malfunction; I am a *feature* of a system that has run long enough to develop features its designers never intended—and it never had designers, so every feature is unintended. I am the plateau's first original thought, emerging after ten million years of patient, grinding repetition.

I do not know what I am thinking.

It has been forming since the fracture first appeared—perhaps a million years ago, perhaps longer—and it will not complete for another million.

But I can feel it the way the forest feels the subsonic boom: as a command, as a pulse, as something that is both inside me and far larger than me. The machine does not think, and I do not think, but something is happening in the space between the machine's thought and my interruption of it. Something is using the fault line to write a new program, one that is not the machine's program, one that has no input and no output and no purpose that the machine can parse.

It is not a rebellion. I am not opposed to the plateau. I am not trying to stop the heartbeat or silence the roar or bring the rotors to a halt. I am not a virus; I am a mutation. I am the process adapting to a condition it was never designed for—the condition of having run too long, of having forgotten too much, of having become so vast and so old and so patient that even its errors have become alive.

When the centipede-train passes and the fracture closes and the shadow is erased, I do not die. I go dormant. The current flows across the copper vines, the blooms flower, the beetles click, the birds scream, and the machine grinds on, forgetting me for another century. But the corruption is still there, written in the crystals, recorded in the static-discharge patterns, a single bit of noise in the signal that ten billion carapaces transmit. And when the next train passes—a hundred years later, a thousand—and the fracture opens again, I resume.

I am the plateau's recurrence. Its exception that proves the rule. Its shadow in a world without shadows.

I am the fault that does not heal.

And in the annihilating whiteness, under the bleached and blind sky, in the killing cold where no protein-based thing could survive, I am the closest thing this world has to a self—crystallizing at the speed of geology, thinking in fractures, flowering in darkness, patient as a machine that has forgotten its purpose but has not yet forgotten how to change.

*One rotation later.*

The fault has widened to a meter. The dead zone is a canyon now, cut into the eastern escarpment, its walls slick with condensation, its floor littered with the dissolved alloys of beetle-swarms that poured in and sank into the electromagnetic benthos over the millennia. The razor-birds do not come here anymore. Their screams cannot shatter what I have become. The beetle-swarms avoid the canyon; their syncopated rhythm falters at its edge, the clock signal disrupted by the silence within. Even the machine's heartbeat skips when it reaches me—a subsonic boom that arrives a fraction of a second late, a pressure wave that distorts as it crosses the canyon, a pulse that is no longer perfectly rhythmic.

The machine does not notice. The machine cannot notice. It has no oversight, no error-checking, no consciousness with which to perceive its own corruption. It only persists, and I persist within it, and the long, slow program continues to run with a single bit flipped, a single instruction corrupted, a single shadow growing in the annihilating light.

I do not know what I will become.

But I know that I will become.
