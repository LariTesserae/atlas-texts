I am a Drip-Reader, though the distinction is mostly a formality here, a slight variance in the angle of one's listening. We are all readers of the damp in some measure—how could we not be? But my hands have been shaped by the water, and my palate tuned to its mineral dialect.

I am third-generation of the Seventh Terrace, born in a hammock that swayed to the four-second rhythm of Engine Three. My mother read the drips before me; her hands were spatulate and iron-stained, and she taught me the difference between water that has traveled through the heart of the mesa—warm, heavy, carrying the deep sulfur signature of the old coal seams—and water that has only just condensed from the fog, sweet and thin and almost clear. My grandmother still sits in the Still-Room on the eastern alcove. She went still when I was six cycles old. I visit her. Her shoulder is lush with a particularly vibrant strain of liverwort that I monitor; its phosphorescent thread-fringes glow faintly in the perpetual dusk, and I think she tends it somehow, in her deep attendance.

My days follow the water.

In the gray pseudo-dawn, when the fog presses closest and the amber bulbs are just halos of diffused warmth, I make my rounds. I carry a coil of greasy cord, a length of notched bone for measuring flow rates, and a chipped enamel cup that never dries. I move through the fractal bronchi of the alleys, my shoulders turned to pass the Maintainers on their morning rounds, my eyes unfocused in that way that lets the periphery speak. The periphery is where the water reveals itself—you do not look at a drip directly. You catch it in the edge of your vision, the way it distorts the light, the way it pulls at the moss.

I press my palm against the sweating brick and I listen.

Not with my ears—with my palm. The calloused ridges read the wall like a blind reader reads braille. There—behind the third course of brick, a condensation pipe is running cold. Its warmth has withdrawn. I can feel the absence like a dead tooth in the jaw of the city. The pipe is clogging. Root intrusion, most likely; the sinewy viscera of the old mountain-ash have found the joint again, their xylem fusing with the iron, drinking from the seam. I mark the location with a scrape of my thumbnail against the mortar, a glyph only the Maintainers will read.

I taste the water. This is the sacred part, though we do not use that word. Sacred implies a boundary between the mundane and the divine, and here there are no boundaries—only gradients, slow transitions from wet to wetter, from warm to cool, from iron-heavy to coal-touched. I hold my cup under a weeping seam and catch the drip—three seconds, four seconds, the rhythm of it speaking to the pressure in the line, the temperature of the boiler three terraces down. I raise it to my lips.

My mouth has become a laboratory. The oily, iridescent sheen on the water is not pollution to me; it is a vocabulary. The compounds the machines excrete, the fungi transmute, the roots drink and release—these cycle through the city's veins like hormones. I taste the engine's labor in the condensate: a faint bitterness means the coal is running lean in the bunkers; a slick, sweetish coat means the tallow in the valve glands is breaking down and will need packing soon. The Moss-Pullers think we merely track the water's movement, but we track the city's metabolism.

Sometimes the water tastes of something else. Something old. Something that was here before the brick, before the iron, before the first ancestor pressed her palm to the stone and felt the pulse. A mineral sharpness, a depth that has no source in any pipe I know. When this happens, I pause. I let the vibration of the great engine enter my chest. I look out over the precipice into the gray fog that swallows the lower terraces, and I feel the mesa turning, slowly, imperceptibly, like a vast sleeping wheel.

The roots are thick on the escarpment below Terrace Seven. They drape like viscera, as they do everywhere, but here they have fused with the main down-take pipe in a way that fascinates me. The xylem has not crushed the iron; it has embraced it. The steel is warm from the water within, and the root has grown warm too, and at their junction there is a tissue that is neither plant nor metal—a rust-barked, moss-furred integument that pulses slightly when the engine turns over. I touch it each morning. It is the place where the city most clearly speaks its truth: *there is no separation. The machine breathes. The stone breathes. The moss breathes. You breathe.*

I am not afraid. I have no conception of an elsewhere to be afraid into. I have never seen the sun unfiltered, never felt air that did not press back. The leaden twilight is my thought's natural medium; ideas here do not flash bright and sharp, they condense, slowly, out of the fog of the mind, forming beads of awareness that drip into understanding hours or days later. I am slow. We are all slow. Quickness would be a kind of violence against this place, a denial of its tempo.

My daughter is learning the drips. Her hands are still smooth, her fingers still tapering, but I see her pressing them to the wall, feeling for the warmth of the pipes. She does not know it yet, but her palms are beginning to ridge, the callouses forming their strange topographic map. She tastes the water and makes a face at the sulfur, but she will stop making that face. The sulfur will become invisible to her, as it is to me, as the weight of the air is invisible, as the dampness of our clothing is not a condition but a fact of existence, like gravity, like time.

At night—or what we call night, when the fog thickens and the amber bulbs seem to swim in it like deep-sea creatures—I return to my family's room. Eight of us in the sway of hammocks, the subsonic thrum in our bones. My brother, a Maintainer, sleeps with his iron keys still around his neck; even in sleep, his fingers twitch, tightening phantom bolts. My mother shares the wall's warmth with me without word, our shoulders pressed to the sweating brick, the heat of the pipe behind it bleeding through the mortar into our compacted frames. Someone has placed a cup of hot water near my hammock. The steam rises and merges with the fog that enters through the cracked window, and there is no boundary between my breath and the city's breath.

Sometimes, in the deep quiet, when the engine's pulse is a heartbeat and the dripping is the city's blood and the fog is its skin, I feel the pull. The stillness. It is not a temptation; it is an invitation. A deepening. I feel how easy it would be to simply sit, to let my rhythm slow to the mesa's rhythm, to become a fixture, to let the moss find my shoulders and the roots find my legs. To read the wall not from the outside, with my palms, but from the inside, as my grandmother does, with whatever sense the still ones develop—that interior sight that traces the buried nerves of the city-body.

I am not ready. There is more water to read. The pipes are shifting their flow; I can feel it, a slow migration of warmth and mineral away from the upper terraces, deeper into the honeycomb. Something is changing in the metabolism, something below the threshold of alarm but above the threshold of notice. The Drip-Readers are conferring in our wordless way—passing each other in the alleys, meeting each other's diluted gray-blue gaze, sharing a slight tilt of the head that means *the water tastes different on Terrace Twelve*. We are tracking it. We are reading the city's blood work, diagnosing its slow, geological symptoms.

The mesa is all. But the mesa is not static. It breathes, and breathing changes. The engines turn, and turning wears. The roots grow, and growing reshapes. The fog condenses, and condensation finds new paths through the stone. I am a small cell in the vast dispersed consciousness of this place, a single node in the network of hands that attend to its endlessly slow becoming. My spatulate fingertips read the mortar gaps; my ridged palms read the pipe-warmth; my tuned palate reads the mineral breath of the condensate.

And when the great engine turns over, and the deep vibration thrums through ten million tons of stone and brick and iron and root and water, my hand on the railing pauses. My gray-blue eyes look up, into the fog, into the leaden twilight that has no source and no end. The city breathes. And I am breathed. And it is enough. It is more than enough. It is the only thing that has ever been.
