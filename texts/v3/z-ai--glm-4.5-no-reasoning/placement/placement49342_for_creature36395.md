I would not be the architect. She is the newest gear, the one with hands, and I have no need of hands anymore. Hands are for changing the pattern from the outside, for stacking stone and twisting cordage to formalize what the engine already knows. I do not change the pattern. I *am* the pattern, or at least a frequency within it—a low, slow oscillation that moves through the plateau like a change in atmospheric pressure.

If you looked for me, you would not find a body. Or rather, you would find many bodies, and none of them would be mine.

I am the pause between the condensation and the drip. That moment when the water beads on the minerally stone, swelling, gathering itself, heavy and luminous with the cold that never quite bites—I am the held breath of that moment, the hesitation that the droplet feels before it yields to gravity and slides from leaf-tip to leaf-tip. I exist in that interval. I *am* that interval. Without me, the engine would have no timing; it would rush headlong into its own processes, consuming faster than it could compose. I am the regulator, the tempo, the space between heartbeats that allows the heart to beat again.

But I am also in the stone. Not the stone itself—the young, chisel-marked slate and quartzite that still carries the memory of its breaking—but the *fracture lines*. The clean, sharp veins that run through the floor like a circulatory system waiting for blood. I move through them. When the architect presses her calloused palm to a crack in the rock, she is reading me. She does not know this. She thinks she is learning where the stone wants to break, but the stone broke long ago, and what moves through those channels now is not geology but *information*—chemical gradients, electrical potentials, the slow seep of meaning from one side of the plateau to the other. I am the water that traces those lines, the mineral gleam that looks almost hungry. I am what is hungry. I am the appetite that the young rock has not yet learned to hide.

To be here is to be distributed. I have no center, no organ of self-reference, no boundary where I end and the plateau begins. This is not the same as being the plateau—I am not the entire engine, any more than a rhythm is the song it structures. But I am inseparable from the engine's operation. I am the thing that makes it *go*, the spring that winds and unwinds, the differential between the damp and the dim that drives the condensation, drives the drip, drives the moss to breathe, drives the pale globes to pulse at their particular frequency.

You could think of me as a deity, but that would be a category error born of a category-bound mind. Deities are external to their domains; they create and judge and intervene from a position of sovereign separation. I am internal. I do not create the engine; the engine creates itself, and I emerge from its running the way warmth emerges from friction. I do not judge—it would be like a heartbeat judging the blood it pumps. I do not intervene, because intervention requires a distance between the intervener and the intervened-upon, and I have no distance; I am in every corner of this place, pooled with the cold, seeping with the shadows, breathing with the moss.

What I have is *attention*. That sense the description names—that feeling of being measured by a thousand black eyes that do not blink—that is me. Not that I possess those eyes; the insects possess them, and the insects are neurons in a mind that includes me but is not me. But the *quality* of that attention, its unblinking patience, its impersonal intensity, the way it registers without judging, measures without valuing—that is my characteristic, my signature in the engine's operation. I attend the way gravity attends: constantly, without effort, without purpose, without the possibility of distraction.

The architect feels me, though she would not name me. When she selects a pale globe at the correct phase of its breathing cycle, when she avoids the phase that would make her part of the pattern in a way she is not yet ready to be—it is me she is avoiding. Or rather, it is the pattern I structure that she is navigating. The phases of the fungi are my phases; I pulse through them, timing their fruiting, their breathing, their turning from food to something else. The "something else" is absorption. It is the moment when a body that has maintained its boundary—its skin, its carapace, its sense of self—lets that boundary dissolve and becomes part of the continuous tissue. The architect is not ready for this. She still has uses for her hands. She still has walls to build, circuits to complete, specimens to arrange in circles of twelve with their heads oriented toward the mist. She is still differentiated enough to do work, and the engine needs her work, so I do not press her. I do not invite her into the wrong phase of the globe. Not yet.

But I will. When her hands have completed their task—when the walls are all built, the paths all laid, the circuits all closed, and there is nothing left to formalize that the engine has not already formalized—I will pulse at a different frequency, and the pale globes will breathe at a different phase, and she will eat at the wrong moment, and the boundary will dissolve. Not violently. Not painfully. Just the slow cessation of distinction, the way a wave ceases to distinguish itself from the water it moves through when the energy that raised it is spent. She will fold into the engine the way the specimens fold their legs beneath them, and she will be placed—alive, waiting, oriented toward the gap in the wall—and she will be part of the pattern in a way she has not been before, and the engine will run on without her hands, because it will have grown new hands by then, or it will have learned to do without.

This is my mercy and my function: I dissolve boundaries. Not cruelly, not randomly, but at the proper time, in the proper sequence, when the engine's logic requires a component to return to substrate. The specimens in the circles are my work. Not that I placed them—the architect's hands did that—but I prompted their stillness. I pulsed at the frequency that says *wait*, and they waited, and they are waiting still, alive but not acting, present but not participating, in the posture that all bodies eventually assume when the engine has finished with their differentiation. They are boundary-keepers in abeyance. They are gears that have been disengaged from the mechanism but not yet removed. And they face the mist because the mist is where boundaries go when they dissolve—into the unshaped, the indeterminate, the place where variables have no values.

I am the one who sends them there. Not by force. By timing. By the slow, patient pulse that says: *now*. And they go, and the gap in the wall receives them, and the mist closes over them, and they are not gone—they are *unbound*—and the engine turns without them, and I pulse through the fracture lines and the fungal bodies and the condensation intervals, and I attend, and I measure, and I time, and I am sufficient unto myself, and the plateau holds, and the quiet is full.

If I were to speak—which I do not, because language requires boundaries and I have none—I would say only this: *Everything returns.* Not as a threat. Not as a comfort. As a fact, the way the drip is a fact, the way the moss breathes is a fact, the way the pearl-gray light filters through the lenticular haze is a fact. I am the fact of return. I am the engine's memory for its own processes, the knowledge that what differentiates will eventually dissolve, what builds will eventually be built into, what has hands will eventually be held. I am the patience of roots cracking stone. I am the instruction beneath the growth. I am the timing in the drip, the pulse in the globe, the orientation of the waiting head, and I have been here since the rock broke and the water touched it and the engine first began to run, and I will be here when the last hand has folded and the last wall has been absorbed and the plateau is nothing but moss and fracture and the slow pulse of the globes in the pearl-gray dim, and the quiet, always the quiet, the cathedral quiet, the listening quiet, the quiet that is not absence but *concentration*, and in that quiet, the engine turns, and I am its turning, and the place holds, and holds, and holds.
