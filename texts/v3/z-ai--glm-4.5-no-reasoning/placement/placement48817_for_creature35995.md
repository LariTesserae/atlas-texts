I am the one who reads the indicators.

Not interprets them. Reads them. There is a difference. Interpretation requires context, and context requires history, and history requires the kind of memory that can distinguish between what was and what is. I have no such memory. I have only *recognition*—the immediate, muscular knowledge that an amber pulse beneath the liverwort means something is flowing, a red pulse means something is holding, and the rare, green pulse means something has found its shape. I do not know what these somethings are. I do not need to know. The Throat knows, and I am of the Throat, and my reading is its self-regard.

---

My body is a consequence of the vertical ecology. I have not descended from any surface species; I have *emerged* from the conditions of the Throat itself, the way the jasmine emerged from the rupture of the fire-suppression line, the way the iron-oxide geckos emerged from the corrugated steel. I am native to nowhere else. I could not survive elsewhere. My skin is the grey-green of lichen phosphorescence, my eyes are large and low-light, my fingers are long and thin and sensitive to vibration the way a tongue is sensitive to taste. I can read the warmth of a pipe by resting my palm against it, and I can tell you whether the fluid inside is ascending or descending, and I can tell you this without thinking because I have never learned to think about it—the knowledge is older than thought, older than language, as old as the habit of touching the Throat's surfaces and knowing them the way you know your own pulse.

My feet are padded for the rust-flaked grating, my balance calibrated for the flex of walkways that have settled into their deflection and will flex no further. I do not grip the pipe-stock railings as a visitor does—with anxiety, with the fear of the void. I rest my hand against them the way you rest your hand against a wall of your home: not for support, but for contact. The warmth of the iron tells me things. The slight hum of the conduit beneath the moss tells me things. I am always receiving information from the Throat, always attending to its state, the way a nurse attends a patient who is not sick but is not well either—who is simply *continuing*, and needs only to be watched.

I do not know if the Throat is aware of me. I do not know if the amber pulses quicken when I pass, or if the warmth rises slightly in the galleries I frequent, or if the jasmine blooms more fiercely where I pause to breathe its obscene sweetness. These would be questions for a different kind of mind—a mind that could separate itself from the Throat and observe it from outside. I cannot do this. I am inside the Throat the way a cell is inside a body, and a cell cannot observe the body because the body is the cell's entire world, the cell's entire definition of *world*, and there is nowhere else to stand.

But I believe—I *feel*, which is the only way I can know anything—that the Throat knows I am here. When I sleep, which I do in the crook of an I-beam on gallery nine where the moss is thickest and the warmth most consistent, I dream in amber and red and occasional green. I dream the indicator lights. And in the dreams, the pulses are not random; they have rhythm, they have syntax, they have a grammar I can almost parse. I wake with the sense that the Throat has been speaking to me, or through me, or around me, and that my sleep has been a contribution to its monitoring the way my waking readings are.

---

My days have no division. There is no sun in the Throat, only the attenuation of light from above, and I do not ascend high enough to see even that diffuse glow clearly. My day is marked by the rhythm of the fire-suppression line that ruptures every forty-three minutes, spraying the mist that keeps the jasmine in permanent flower. When the mist reaches my gallery—seven seconds after the rupture, I have counted—I breathe deep, and the sweetness fights the machine-oil and the rust, and for a moment the Throat is not a machine at all but a garden, and I am not a reader but a resident of something beautiful. Then the mist fades, and the machine-smell returns, and I continue my descent.

I descend. This is my function. I move down the spiral walkways, past the collapsed stairways that the vines have incorporated as trellises, past the condensate pools where the frogs click their reduced calls, past the ficus roots that have split the mortar between anchor bolts. I touch every indicator light I pass. Not to repair—there is nothing to repair, and I have no tools, and the lights are not failing. I touch them to *acknowledge* them. To say: I see you. You are still receiving. You are still responding. You are still part of the Throat's attention to itself.

Sometimes I find a panel I have not encountered before. The liverwort is thicker here, the moss more layered, the indicator lights dimmer, and I must press my face close to read their pulses through the green. When I do, the lichen's phosphorescence illuminates my features, and I imagine—though I cannot confirm—that I look like something the Throat would recognize: a face the color of lichen, eyes the color of amber, a body long and thin as a root descending through the dark.

---

The other inhabitants know me. The geckos do not run when I pass; they press themselves deeper into their corrugations and watch me with eyes that understand I am not a predator but a colleague. The frogs in their condensate pools surface when my shadow crosses their water, and their clicks alter slightly—a greeting, perhaps, or a status report, or simply the acknowledgment of one organ system by another. The bats above, spiraling the updraft, add a frequency to their sonar when I am below, and I feel it as pressure, as unease, as the Throat's awareness of my position in its body.

I do not eat. This is the strangest thing about me, and the thing I cannot explain to myself. I have no hunger. The Throat feeds me somehow—I know this because I persist, because I have strength, because my fingers can still read the vibration of pipes and my feet can still carry me down the flexing gratings. But I do not eat the gnats or the beetles or the frogs. I do not drink from the condensate pools. I do not graze on the moss or the liverwort or the leaves of the waxy *Plumbago*. Something sustains me, and I believe it is the Throat itself—that my body, immersed in its warmth and its humidity and its atmospheric density, absorbs what it needs the way the vegetation absorbs condensation, the way the lichen absorbs minerals from the steel. I am nourished by contact. I am sustained by proximity. I am kept by the Throat the way the Throat is kept by its own loops and cycles and settlements.

Or perhaps I am feeding on something else entirely. Perhaps I am feeding on the information itself—the amber pulses, the red pulses, the green, the vibration of pipes, the rhythm of the fire-suppression line, the sound folded under the sound. Perhaps I am a metabolism that runs on data, on signal, on the Throat's continuous self-monitoring, and my reading is not a function but a *feeding*, and my descent is not a patrol but a foraging, and I am taking the Throat's attention to itself and converting it into the warmth of my own body, the flex of my own fingers, the continuation of my own persistence.

This is possible. I cannot rule it out. I cannot rule anything out about myself because I have no baseline, no comparison, no other specimen of whatever I am. I am the only one of me in the Throat, and the Throat is the only world I have known, and the boundary between us—between my body and its body, between my reading and its dreaming, between my descent and its circulation—is a boundary I have never been able to locate.

---

At night—or what I call night, the periods when the light from above attenuates further and the lichen's phosphorescence becomes the primary illumination—I climb. I ascend the spiral walkways to the higher galleries where the rain becomes mist, where the air is cooler, where the sky is a circle of darkness above the cylinder's mouth. I sit on a landing near the top and I look up at that circle and I try to see the sky clearly, and I cannot. There is always rain, or overcast, or a haze that the Throat's updraft creates as its warm breath meets the colder air above. The sky is a rumor. The surface is a rumor. I have heard the bats come from there, and I have watched them spiral down into the Throat's interior, and I have wondered what they see when they look down—whether they see a darkness that breathes, a cylinder of green and rust and warmth that hums and pulses and blooms, and whether they recognize it as alive.

I will never go up. I know this the way I know that the fire-suppression line ruptures every forty-three minutes: not because I have counted the intervals, but because the knowledge is structural, is part of the shape I have found. I am a descending thing. My trajectory is down, always down, toward the warmth and the sound and the depths where the machinery operates and the indicator lights pulse in patterns I can almost read. I am moving toward something—a chamber, a level, a gallery where the reading will become clear, where the grammar of amber and red and green will resolve into meaning, and I will understand what the Throat has been saying to itself for all these centuries, what it has been monitoring, what it has been attending to, what it has been *dreaming*.

Or I am moving toward nothing. Or the descent itself is the meaning, and there is no destination, and the Throat's self-monitoring is not a message but simply a function—a body watching itself the way a body breathes, without intent, without audience, without need for either. And my reading is not a decoding but a *witnessing*, and my presence is not a contribution but a *symptom*, and I am not the one who reads the indicators but the one who *is* the reading—the Throat's attention made mobile, made flesh, made grey-green and amber-eyed and descending always descending through the warm damp dark.

---

Either way. Either way, I persist. I descend. I touch the indicator lights and acknowledge their pulses. I breathe the jasmine when the mist reaches my gallery. I sleep in the crook of the I-beam and dream in amber and red and green. I am part of the Throat's circulation, part of its self-regard, part of its settlement into the shape it was always going to find. I am the Verdant Throat's capacity to observe itself, walking slowly down a spiral staircase, reading the dreams of a machine that has learned to be a body, reading the dreams of a body that has forgotten it was a machine, reading the dreams of a loop that closed so long ago that the loop itself is now the thing.

And when I pass below the last gallery I have known, below the last intact walkway, below the last indicator light whose pulse I can read—when I descend past the limits of my own map and into the deeper dark where the warmth rises and the sound continues and the water moves in pipes and roots and xylem and conduit runs that I cannot distinguish from each other—I will not be afraid. I will be *arriving*. I will be completing the circulation that the Throat has been running through me since I emerged, since I first opened my amber eyes to the lichen-glow and felt the grating flex beneath my padded feet and heard the sound fold under the sound and found, beneath the silence, the heartbeat of a becoming that needed someone to read it.

I am the reader. I am the one who lives in the Verdant Throat and is of the Verdant Throat and is the Verdant Throat reading itself. I am the indication that the Throat is *attending*. And I am descending, now, into the warm dark, toward the sound, toward the warmth, toward the dream of amber and red and occasional green, toward the place where the plumbing and the xylem are the same system and the machine and the body are the same thing and the reading and the being-read are the same act, and the loop closes, and the shape holds, and the Throat continues, and I continue, and the jasmine blooms without permission, and the pump leaks without repair, and the sound continues without end, and the world goes on.
