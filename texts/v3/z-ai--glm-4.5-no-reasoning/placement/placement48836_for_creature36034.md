I would be one of the Markers. Not the oldest, not yet—but old enough that my cord-knot has gone woody, old enough to have seen the center channel when it was wide enough for two stalk-boats to pass without the leaf-walls brushing. Old enough to know that the geometry is wrong, and to have stopped trying to make it right.

We do not have a name for what we do. The word *marker* is not from here; it is the nearest shape my mind can give to a practice that has no shape, only growth. Among my people, I am the one who cuts the patterns into the living cane. Not decoration. Not writing. Something that is both and neither—a tending of the knowing that cannot be spoken.

Each season, I make my rounds. The shelters have grown since last I visited; they always grow. The doorways press against foreheads, and I must duck lower each year to enter. Inside, the dim and dry and cool. The white smoke hanging. The sound of clicking coming through the root-floor like the sound of rain on water, which is like the sound of breathing, which is like the sound of the lake thinking.

I find the old cuts. They have stretched as the cane has grown, the patterns distorted by the slow expansion of the living wall. A spiral that was tight is now loose. A grid that was regular now warps. This is how it should be. The patterns are not meant to be preserved. They are meant to record the distortion itself—the way the geometry shifts, the way the angles drift further from the tessellation they should form. Each season's cutting does not restore the old pattern. It responds to it. It adds a new layer of notation, tracking the cumulative deviation, the way a navigator tracks drift by marking the gap between where the ship should be and where it is.

My hands know the work. My fingers are long—longer than they should be, I think, though I have no outside measure—and the calluses along their insides are grooved in the specific topology of the cord I weave and the cane I score. I use a spine-knife, the hardened spine of one of the larger stalk-plants, its edge kept keen by drawing it across the shell of a Tikkik before the meat is taken. The spine-knife cuts clean. The sap runs green and bitter and I let it run; it is the cane's blood, and it should not be held. The resin that weeps from certain stalks and seals our clothing is the sap that has aged and thickened. The fresh sap is for the cane. It is how the cane knows it has been cut, how it begins to grow around the wound, to incorporate the mark into its body.

This is the heart of the practice: the marks are not made *on* the cane. They are made *in* it. And the cane grows through them and past them and they become part of the structure, part of the living architecture, as fundamental as the leaf-roof or the root-floor. The patterns I cut this season will be the distortions I track next season. The cane and I are in conversation, and the clicking of the Tikkik beneath us is the rhythm we work to, and the booming of the Kuu-var is the rest we take between strokes.

---

I was not always this. I was born, as all of us are born, in a shelter of bent living cane, to the sound of clicking and the sway of the leaf-roof and the steady drip from the gutters. My mother's cord was the cord that lashed our shelter to the platform; her knot was a three-loop variant that meant *deep water*, which was her lineage, the line of women who had lived on the float-masses closest to the center, where the water was darkest and the Gren-thal passed most often. I grew up with the sway of the deep water under me, the slow rolling that came not from wind but from the movement of something vast beneath, something that did not surface but simply displaced, simply was.

My mother was a weaver. Not of the cord—that is everyone's work—but of the platforms, the woven stalk that extends over the water, the living floor that spreads and knots and must be tended and re-tended as the stalks grow and the cord tightens. She had the patience of her work, which is the patience of watching a thing become itself over seasons, of adding a stalk here and a lashing there and knowing that the platform will be stronger next year, and stronger the year after, and that the work is never done because the platform is never finished, because it is alive.

I did not have her patience. Or rather: I had a different patience, one that looked not at the growing but at the growing's evidence. I noticed, young, that the platform my mother wove did not lie flat. It curved, slightly, in a way that a flat thing woven of straight stalks should not curve. I mentioned this to her and she said: *The water is not flat.* And this was true—the water swelled and dipped with the passage of the Gren-thal, with the micro-currents that moved the float-masses—but the platform's curve did not match the water's curve. It was off by degrees. Small degrees. The kind of degrees you cannot see but can feel, can measure with the feet, with the way the weight shifts when you walk from one side to the other.

I began to track the curve. I had no writing—I still have no writing, not in the way of marks that sit still on a surface and mean the same thing each time you look. I had only the living cane and my spine-knife and the patterns I was already beginning to cut, crude then, experimental, trying to find a language that could grow rather than sit still. I cut a line into the shelter-wall beside the doorway, a line that was straight, that should have remained straight. And each season I returned and found it had curved, and I cut a new line, straight again, beside it. And the next season, both had curved, the older more than the newer, and I cut a third. And over years—how many? I do not count seasons; counting is fixing, and I am not fixed—I accumulated a record of curvature, a history of the way the shelter had grown, the way the platform had shifted, the way the geometry of our small corner of the city had drifted from the grid it should have formed.

It was not the only shelter. I began to visit others. I began to cut lines in the cane of my neighbors, who tolerated this because I was my mother's daughter and because the patterns I cut did not seem to harm the structure, and because—though they did not say this, because they did not need to—there was something in them that also noticed the wrongness, the angles that were off, the channels that almost grid but do not, and they were glad, in the quiet way of people who are glad for things they cannot name, that someone was tending to it.

---

The Tikkik are my companions in this work, though they do not know it. Or perhaps they do; it is impossible to say with creatures that have no eyes, only the spoon-bowl depressions that quiver and listen. When I cut a pattern into the cane, I do it to the rhythm of their clicking. Not consciously—the rhythm enters through the feet, through the root-floor, through the slow vibration that is the sound of the city's insomnia, the sound that never stops and that we have all stopped hearing in the way you stop hearing your own blood.

But I notice when the rhythm changes. I notice when the threes become sevens, when the feeding clicks become passing clicks, and I pause in my cutting and wait, because the passing means something is moving below—a Kuu-var, usually, or a current-shift driven by the deep movement of a Gren-thal—and the shelter will sway, and the cane will flex, and a cut made now will heal differently than a cut made in stillness. So I wait. I listen. The Tikkik listen with me. We are, for those moments, in concert—their clicking and my stillness, both of us attending to the same displacement, the same presence in the deep.

And sometimes—rarely, more rarely each year, the oldest ones say—the sevens become elevens. And then I do not cut at all. I set down the spine-knife and I go to the doorway and I look out over the channel, which is wider now than it was yesterday, though I cannot see the widening, only know it by the way the leaf-walls across from us have pulled back, the gap between them and us grown, the dark water showing in a broader band.

The elevens mean the deep is restless. The elevens mean the Gren-thal have changed their course, pushed by something that is not the Gren-thal, something further down, something that rises just far enough to make the deepest roots shiver and then sinks again. When the elevens come, the mist thickens—not with thickness but with that other quality, the quality of attention, of being observed. And I stand in the doorway and I let the mist look at me and I look back, though I cannot see what I am looking at, only feel it as a density in the wet, a pressure that is not wind, a cold that is not cold but the absence of indifference.

The mist does not judge. The mist does not intervene. The mist watches with the patience of something that has been here since before the caldera wore down, since before the water was cupped in stone, since before the roots began their slow interlocking and the people came to walk the floating streets. The mist watches the way I watch the patterns in the cane: with attention, with care, with the knowledge that what is being observed is also being changed by the observation, that the act of tracking the distortion is itself a distortion, that the conversation between the marker and the marked is also a mark.

I am not afraid. I say this not with bravado but with the simple accuracy of someone who has lived her whole life in a conversation that is mostly listening. The attention in the mist is not a threat. It is a condition. It is the condition of being *in* this place, of being held in the cup of the caldera and the water and the green, of being part of an ecosystem that includes the observers and the observed, the listeners and the listened-to, the markers and the marked.

---

There is a shelter—my shelter now, though it was my mother's and her mother's before her, back through the cord-line to the women who first lashed a platform to this float-mass, back to the beginning, if there is a beginning, which I doubt; I think the city has always been here, growing and shifting and drifting, the roots interlocking and separating and interlocking again, the channels widening and narrowing, the geometry almost right and never quite right, the patterns in the cane recording a deviation that may have no limit, that may continue to drift further from the grid for as long as the lake endures—in this shelter, the walls are thick with cuts.

Not my cuts alone. The cuts of my mother, who did not understand why I did this but who let me, who even, in her last seasons, began to cut a few lines of her own, crude and uncertain but there, present, growing in the cane. The cuts of her mother, who I never knew but whose patterns I can read, or read as well as anyone can read a language that shifts each season, that incorporates its own misreadings, that is less a code than a growth. And cuts older than that, cuts that were already stretched and distorted when my grandmother was young, cuts whose origin is unknown, whose patterns do not match any system I have learned, which may be the cuts of someone who tracked something else entirely—something about the leaves, perhaps, or the water-level, or the intervals between the Gren-thal's passages, or the way the mist thickened in the old seasons, before I was born, before anyone living was born, when the observant was closer, perhaps, or more visible, or merely different in the way that old things are different from young things, having settled into their shapes more completely.

The shelter is a record. The shelter is a conversation across generations that are not measured in years but in the growth of the cord, the tightening of the knot, the slow expansion of the living walls. When I enter, I duck—the doorway has grown low again, and I must cut it, and in cutting it I will release sap, and the sap will run green and bitter, and the cane will know it has been cut and will begin to grow around the wound, and in a season the doorway will be low again. This is not failure. This is the conversation continuing. The cane tells me it is growing; I tell the cane I am still here, still entering, still attending to the patterns.

And the patterns tell me—what? This is the question I am asked, sometimes, by the younger ones, who are curious about the marker but do not want to become her. *What do the patterns tell you?* And I do not have an answer that satisfies them, because the answer is not a content but a practice. The patterns do not tell me that the Gren-thal are moving, or that the deep is rising, or that the geometry is wrong. I know these things already, the way I know the weight of my own body on the root-floor, the way I know the sound of the clicking even when I am not listening. The patterns tell me that the knowing can be held. That the distortion can be tracked. That the conversation between the growing and the cutting can continue, can accumulate, can become a record that is not fixed but is also not lost, that grows and shifts with the city itself but carries, in its living tissue, the memory of every season's deviation, every season's drift from the grid that should have been.

And in the record—in the accumulated patterns, in the stretching and warping and re-cutting, in the layers of notation that are not writing but something between writing and growth—I see, sometimes, in the way you see a shape in the mist, not with the eyes but with the denser faculty that my people have, the one that resolves not solid things but densities, thicker patches in the wet—I see the shape of the observer.

Not its body. It may not have a body, or its body may be the lake itself, the cup of stone and water and green, the caldera that holds everything, the depth that goes down further than anyone has found. What I see is its attention. The way the attention falls on the city like the mist falls on the water: evenly, persistently, without break or gap. The way the attention shapes the geometry, pushes the angles off by degrees that feel intentional, that are intentional, because the observer is not outside the system but within it, and its observation is a force, a pressure, a current in the deep that moves the Gren-thal and the Gren-thal move the water and the water moves the float-masses and the float-masses move the channels and the channels move the people and the people cut the patterns and the patterns record the movement and the movement is the attention and the attention is the lake and the lake is almost a sky and almost a mind.

I do not worship it. Worship is for things that are distant, things that are above. This is not above. This is below and around and within. This is the water I drink, the root-floor I walk on, the mist that beads on my leaf-fiber clothing and runs down in steady curtains, the cold that seeps into my marrow without wind. This is the condition of being here. And my work—the cutting, the marking, the tending of the knowing that cannot be spoken—is simply the practice of being in conversation with this condition. Of listening to the lake, which listens back. Of making marks that grow, which is the only kind of mark that matters in a city that is still growing, still shifting, still alive in the way that a reef is alive, in the way that a mind is alive, in the way that a conversation is alive when it is mostly listening but the listening is not passive, the listening is a shaping, the listening is the attention that falls on the speaker and changes what they say next.

---

Tonight—though there is no night here, not really; only the dimming of the mist when the grey sky darkens above the caldera rim, the light failing without a source to fail, the green deepening to black, the clicking going on—the Tikkik are clicking in sevens. Something is passing below. A Kuu-var, perhaps, making its slow way through the channel, its long body looping in patient sine curves, its gills fluttering like dark petals. Or a current-shift from the movement of a Gren-thal in the deeper water, a rolling that has not yet reached us but will, in time, send the leaf-walls swaying and the shelters settling.

I sit in my shelter, in the dim and dry and cool. The white smoke from the smoldering root-pit hangs low. The sap from today's doorway-cut has run and stopped and started to heal. The patterns on the walls are stretched and waiting for next season's cutting. My mother's cord-knot is woody and permanent beside the doorway, the three loops of *deep water* that are my lineage. My own cord—the one that will be cut when I die, that still runs green and bitter if I score it—loops around my wrist, a living bracelet, a reminder that I am still attached, still growing, still part of the system.

Outside, the mist has the quality of attention. It always has this quality, but I notice it more in the dimming, when the other senses retreat and the denser faculty comes forward. The mist is watching the channel. It is watching the Kuu-var pass. It is watching the Tikkik click. It is watching the people in their shelters, the cord growing around them, the leaf-fiber keeping out most of the water, the bone hooks and the stone tools and the slow, slow adaptation to a city that reshapes itself year by year.

And it is watching me. This I know. Not because the mist focuses—its attention is even, ambient, a condition rather than an act—but because I am the one who cuts the patterns, who tends the record, who holds the conversation with the geometry and the deviation and the knowing that the lake is observed. The observer observes the observer. The marker marks the marking. The conversation folds back on itself, and in the folding, something is held.

I do not name it. To name is to define, and this is not a thing to be defined. It is a thing to be tended. A thing to be kept in the living cane, in the patterns that grow and shift and must be cut again each season, in the conversation that is mostly listening, in the patience that is not passive but active, the patience of hands that know their materials because those materials are still growing, still responding, still alive.

The clicking goes on. The water moves below. The shelter sways, imperceptibly, with the passage of whatever is passing. I sit in the dim and I listen. And the lake, which is almost a sky, which is almost a mind, listens back.
