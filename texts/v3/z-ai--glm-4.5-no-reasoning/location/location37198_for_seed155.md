To stand here is to stand inside a contradiction that has aged into geography.

The plateau stretches at the altitude where clouds used to form—they do not form here anymore, not properly—but the air is not thin. It is dense, compressed, cool and damp with a chronic condensation that never quite resolves into rain. This moisture hangs in the space between things, beading on surfaces, dripping from the undersides of leaves, pooling in the corroded hollows of iron. It is a wetness without weather, a persistent exhalation that soaks everything without ever truly falling.

And there is so much space between things. The city—if it can be called a city—sprawls across the high plateau in a scatter of structures separated by expanses of living substrate. There is no bare ground. There is no stone, no concrete, no earth visible anywhere. The entire surface of this place is plant. Not plants growing *on* a surface—plants *as* the surface, the architecture, the infrastructure, the very ground beneath one's feet. Massive rhizomal networks form the streets, their knotted surfaces worn smooth by centuries of passage. Foundations are root-walls dozens of meters thick, grown into and through each other until they are inseparable. The buildings themselves are hollowed boles and engineered cambium, their walls layers of bark and lignin so dense they ring like wood when struck, their interiors carved from the slow accumulation of xylem.

But the city is sparse. You can walk for minutes along a root-road without passing a structure. The buildings sit in isolation, each one a monolith of living architecture separated by reaches of flat, low-growing thicket—some kind of endlessly layered moss-fern that fills the spaces between constructions like a gray-green sea. From above, the settlement would look like scattered artifacts in a field, not the dense accretion of a metropolis. And yet it *is* organized. There is a logic to the spacing—a pattern of industrial zones and habitation clusters and transit corridors that can be read if one walks long enough. The city was planned, built, inhabited. It simply grew sparse, as all things do, given enough time.

*Given enough time.* The erosion parameter says it all: this is an ancient place, primordially aged, settled into its final form. The city is not crumbling; it has *finished* crumbling. What remains is the stable state—the geometry that survives entropy, the structures too massive or too deeply integrated to fully decay. You see it in the profile of the skyline: near-flat. The towers that once stood here have long since collapsed inward, their materials absorbed back into the plant-substrate that sustained them. Only the broad, low forms remain—factory floors grown into the root-network, their smokestacks reduced to stumps colonized by lichen, their machinery half-digested by cambium. The whole city has been composting for millennia, and what you see is the humus: rich, stable, almost finished.

Almost. Because the dynamic is not zero. Things still happen here. The factories still run—some of them.

This is the strangest part, the part the mind resists. The industry has not stopped. It cannot stop, because the machines are not separate from the plant-substrate. They were built *into* it—engines whose cylinders are vascular bundles, whose pistons are rhythmic thickening of xylem, whose power comes from the slow oxidative burning of the plateau's own biomass. The factories are organs. They breathe. They cycle. And they produce—what? The air is thick with the exhaust of living industry: not smoke, but spore, and vapor, and the sweet-acrid reek of phytochemical synthesis. Something is being manufactured here at scale, has been for longer than human memory, and the production line is the plateau's own metabolism.

The sound is a consequence. The place is *loud*—not with the mechanical clatter of human industry, but with the biological throb of plant-machines in operation. A low, rhythmic booming permeates everything, felt in the chest before it reaches the ears: the pulse of massive vascular pumps moving fluid through root-networks the size of districts. Layered over this is a creaking, groaning chorus—the sound of lignin stressed by growth, of cambium expanding against bark, of whole buildings slowly, audibly thickening. And beneath it all, the white noise of countless small processes: the hiss of sap under pressure, the crack of seed-pods, the drip of condensation from leaf to leaf in the humid air. The plateau roars like a living engine, because it is one.

The fauna is nearly absent—0.9 on the scale, barely present. You might see, in the corners of your vision, something small and pale skittering into a root-crevice. You might hear, in the pauses between the great pulses, a chirrup or a scrape that suggests insectoid life. But there is no ecology here in the traditional sense—no food web, no predator-prey dynamics, no visible animal presence to speak of. The plant-substrate has outcompeted everything else, absorbed the niches, filled the space so completely that fauna exists only as an afterthought, a remnant population of decomposers and parasites living in the margins of a world that has no use for them.

And yet the ecosystem is *active*—that 1.6 dynamic parameter. The life is here, it is just... not animal life. The processes are vegetative: growth cycles, chemical synthesis, the slow motion of tropism and response. The factory-zones pulse with metabolic rhythm, producing their unknown product in waves that crest and subside across hours. The moss-fern sea between buildings ripples with coordinated movement sometimes, fronds lifting and falling like a single organism breathing. There is a predator-prey dynamic, but it is chemical—one strain of engineered vine colonizing and digesting another, competition playing out across seasons in the walls of the buildings themselves. The city is an ecosystem, but it is an ecosystem of industry and vegetation, indistinguishable from each other.

The light is normal, and somehow that makes it worse. There is no eerie luminescence, no strange coloration to warn you what this place is. The sun shines—when the perpetual mist-parts permit it—with ordinary daylight. Shadows fall normally. The rust-colored bark and the gray-green thicket and the black water beading on every surface are all rendered in unremarkable, recognizable hues. The weirdness here is not chromatic or optical. It is *structural*. It is the wrongness of a city that is also a forest that is also a factory that is also a single organism. It is the way the buildings lean slightly toward the sun with a tropism you can measure over days. It is the way the root-roads subside and swell with the metabolic pulse, so that walking feels like traversing the skin of something breathing. It is the way, when you place your hand on a factory wall, you feel warmth and vibration and a slow yielding pressure that is exactly the feeling of touching the chest of something alive.

The reality breaks down at the edges of comprehension. Not visually—the place is solid, coherent, consistent within its own logic. But the *logic itself* is the breakdown. This is a place where the categories have merged, where architecture and biology and industry are the same process, where the city has been digesting itself into plant-matter for so long that the distinction was never meaningful. Stand here long enough and you start to lose the boundaries of your own body. The dampness soaks your clothes. The spores enter your lungs. The sound enters your bones. The green fills your peripheral vision until there is nothing else, and you understand—viscerally, against your will—that you are standing inside something that does not know you are separate from it.

This is an old place. A final-form place. It has been what it is for longer than most civilizations last, and it will be this for longer still. The cool damp rises from the living ground. The machines throb. The moss-fern breathes. And you, temporary thing, you are just another source of carbon, another pattern of moisture and warmth, beginning the slow process of being absorbed.
