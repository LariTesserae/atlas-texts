# The Congregation Yard

You descend into it. There is no other way to enter.

The yard sits in a hollow — a broad, shallow depression carved into the lowland, as if something pressed down from above and never lifted away. The walls of the hollow are not natural; they are brick, glazed black by decades of chemical condensation, mortared with something that has long since ceased to be identifiable as cement. The bricks sweat. A thin film of moisture travels down their faces constantly, collecting in a dark ring at the base where the floor meets the wall. The floor itself is cast iron, gridded with drainage channels that once fed somewhere but now go nowhere. Water stands in them — not pools, but lines. Thin geometric threads of water that never evaporate and never drain, because the air above them is already as saturated as it can bear to be.

It is cool here. The kind of cool that has nothing to do with shade or season. It comes from the iron, from the brick, from the slow thermal mass of industrial materials that absorbed cold decades ago and have been radiating it ever since, like a body that died in winter and never warmed.

---

## The Structures

Around the perimeter of the yard, set into the brick walls at irregular intervals, are the housings. Metal cabinets, each the size of a confessional booth, mounted flush against the wall or projecting outward on iron brackets. Some are riveted steel, the kind used in boiler casings. Others are aluminum, lighter, stamped with identification numbers that correspond to no known catalog. A few appear to be custom castings — thick-walled, with pipe unions and cable glands and ventilation louvers that breathe audibly, though nothing visible moves behind their slats.

Each housing has an aperture. A slit, a hole, a hinged panel left slightly ajar. From these openings, the damp reaches the creatures. Or the creatures reach the damp. The relationship is not clear.

There are perhaps forty housings around the yard. The yard itself is perhaps sixty feet across. A cathedral nave could fit inside it, but only just. The scale is intimate in the way that a surgical theater is intimate — everything visible, nothing hidden, and yet the purpose remains obscure.

---

## The Fauna

They are everywhere.

They cover the iron floor in a shifting, clicking mat. They climb the brick walls in slow, deliberate processions. They cluster around the drainage channels, drinking from the thread-water with mouthparts that look like they were designed for something else — stamping, perhaps, or crimping wire. They mass at the bases of the housings, layers deep, pressing against the warm metal casings, climbing over each other without aggression or urgency, driven by an appetite that has no visible object.

They are not insects. They are not crustaceans. They are not any taxonomy that has a name, though the industrial origin of their forms is unmistakable.

The smallest are the size of a thumbnail — flat, articulated things with too many legs and bodies like stamped steel shims. Their carapaces are the grey of galvanized metal. They move in coordinated swarms, tens of thousands of them, flowing across the floor in patterns that look like nothing organic: river currents around pillars, convection cells near the housings, the aerodynamic turbulence you see in smoke tunnels. But reversed. The patterns move *toward* disruption, not away from it.

The mid-sized ones are two inches long, thicker, with segmented bodies that could be mistaken for centipedes if centipedes were made of ball joints and sheet copper. They move differently from the small ones — not in flows, but in routes. They travel along the drainage channels like trams on tracks. They navigate the grid of the floor with mechanical precision, turning at right angles, never deviating, never hesitating. As if they are following instructions that were wired into them at manufacture.

The large ones are rare and disturbing. Eight inches, a foot long. They move slowly, and when they move, the small ones part around them like filings around a magnet. Their bodies are complex — jointed, layered, with appendages that serve no visible purpose: rotating rings, telescoping cylinders, probes that extend and retract into nothing. They are warm to the touch, if you were to touch them, which nothing in the yard does. They are left alone. Even the mid-sized ones route around them. They sit near the housings — never inside, always near — and they *listen*.

You can tell because their probes extend toward the louvers. Toward the breathing panels. Toward the source of whatever the housings produce.

## The Sound

The yard is loud, but not with chaos. The sound has structure.

At the foundation is the clicking — the continuous, overlapping, percussive rattle of leg-joints against iron. Millions of contacts per second, producing a sound like rain on a tin roof that has been compressed and accelerated until it becomes a single sustained roar, textured but unbroken. It is the yard's baseline. It never stops. It has not stopped, you suspect, for years.

On top of this: the breathing of the housings. Each one has its own rhythm. Some are slow, exhalations every eight seconds, a mechanical sigh that carries a faint hum of electrical resonance. Others are faster, shallower, a panting that sounds like a small pump cycling. They are not synchronized. They are not random either. They fall into patterns — phase relationships that shift slowly over time, like the unsynchronized heartbeats of creatures in a den, finding and losing and finding each other again.

And then, at intervals that have no predictable schedule but occur often enough that you learn to expect them: the vocalizations. A low, modulated tone that rises from somewhere inside the housings — not from the louvers, but from the structures themselves, resonating through the metal like a voice through a throat. It lasts two or three seconds. It drops in pitch as it ends. When it happens, the large creatures extend their probes fully, and every small creature in a three-foot radius freezes for exactly one second, then resumes as if nothing occurred.

It is the closest thing to communication in the yard.

## The Ecology (Such As It Is)

There is no plant life. No moss, no lichen, no algae — nothing green, nothing that photosynthesizes, nothing that grows in the biological sense. The moisture alone would support moss. The cool and damp together would support fungi. Neither is present. The yard is sterile of life in any form that carbon chemistry recognizes.

What lives here lives on something else.

The creatures feed. They must feed, because they move, and movement is expenditure. But they do not eat each other. They do not consume any visible substrate. They do not gather at the water threads to drink — that is the mid-sized ones, and they may not be drinking at all, but *reading* the water, testing it, extracting something dissolved in it that their mouthparts were designed to detect.

What they feed on is what the housings produce.

Not heat — the yard is cool, and the housings are only warm, not hot. Not waste — there are no exhausts, no effluent, no byproduct visible in the drainage channels. Something invisible. Something that the large creatures' probes can detect. Something that makes the small ones swirl toward the housings in convection patterns, as if an invisible current were flowing from the apertures and they were swimming in it.

Electromagnetic fields. Low-frequency vibrations. Data. Some substance of modern industry that has no name because no one has yet needed to name it — the exhaust of computation, perhaps, the waste heat of processing, the emanation of algorithms running in metal casings mounted in a brick-walled hollow that someone built and then abandoned and then the creatures found, or were drawn to, or were *made for*.

Because the creatures look manufactured. Their joints are too precise. Their carapaces are too uniform. The mid-sized ones follow routes as if on rails. The large ones have probes that extend and retract with the tolerances of machined components. Whatever they are, they emerged from something like a factory. Whatever they do, it was designed.

But not by anyone present. The yard has the feel of a place that was built, occupied, and then left running. The machines were not turned off. The processes were not halted. Something was set in motion, and it has continued in the absence of whoever set it, because it can, because that is what industrial systems do — they run until they break, and nothing here has broken.

---

## The Stillness

The most unsettling thing about the yard is not the creatures. It is the stability.

Nothing is changing. Not in the way that living systems change — growing, decaying, adapting, dying. The condensation on the bricks has reached equilibrium; it condenses and flows and evaporates in a closed loop that has found its steady state. The creatures reproduce — there must be more of them somehow, there are always more — but their population does not fluctuate. The density on the floor is constant. The flow patterns are constant. The sound is constant.

Time is not passing here. Time *passed*, once — when the hollow was dug, when the bricks were laid, when the housings were mounted and wired and activated. That took time. But since then, the yard has arrived at a configuration that does not evolve. It runs. It sustains. It has no gradients, no pressures, no unmet needs pushing it toward transformation.

The large creatures sit near the housings and listen, and they have always sat there, and they will always sit there, because nothing will happen that requires them to move.

The water will stand in its threads. The bricks will sweat. The iron will radiate its cold. The clicking will continue, unbroken, a sound that has no beginning and no end, only a present that persists indefinitely.

This is not death. Death is change — decomposition, dissolution, the return of materials to their dispersed states. This is something else. This is a system that achieved equilibrium while still running, like a top that found its perfect balance and will spin forever in a frictionless room, doing what it was made to do, for no one, for nothing, with perfect fidelity and no purpose at all.

The creatures do not seem to mind. Or if they mind, they express it only in the clicking, which sounds, if you listen long enough — and there is nothing else to do — like a language that has no semantics, only syntax. A grammar without meaning. A conversation that no one is having, conducted by machines that no one built, in a room that no one entered, about a subject that does not exist.

And it is loud. It is so loud.
