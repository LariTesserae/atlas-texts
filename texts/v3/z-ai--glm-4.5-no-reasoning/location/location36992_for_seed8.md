This is a land at the exhaust of time, a world that has spent its entire geological life collapsing into itself until there was nothing left to do but rest. The elevation is negligible—the land lies flat against the sea like a drowning man surrendering to the tide. Here, the horizon is a bruised, hazy line, smeared by an atmosphere so heavy with moisture it feels like breathing through a warm, wet cloth. 

There are no mountains. There are no hills. There are not even distinct valleys. The erosion score is absolute; primordial forces have had eons to chew away the highs and fill the lows. The terrain is a vast, unending plain of compacted, waterlogged earth, ground down to a final, immutable flatness. It is a landscape of infinite extension but minimal depth. The sky presses down close, white and pale amber, heavy with thermal heat and suspended water.

And yet, it is not an ocean. The water is too close to the surface, too interleaved with the ancient mud. The ground has the consistency of a saturated sponge. You walk, and the earth gives with a silent, gelatinous squelch, releasing water that pools instantly in your footprints, reflecting the pale sky before slowly seeping back into the substrate. There are no flowing streams; the water table is so high that the land simply *weeps*. Shallow, braided channels, barely an inch deep, carve the flatland in erratic, meandering threads, shining like hammered bronze in the diffuse light. 

Vegetation is almost an afterthought. The soil is anaerobic and impossibly old, leached of everything but the most stubborn minerals. What grows here is sparse, tough, and entirely unadorned. Thick, succulent halophytes sprawl like green cables across the mud, their roots gripping the earth like alien fingers. There are no trees, no bushes, no verticality to break the horizontal slog. Occasionally, a lone stalk of a rush-like plant stands sentinel, its cylindrical head nodding under the weight of condensed humidity. The emptiness is profound; the space between living things stretches for dozens of yards, emphasizing the sheer, unbroken scale of the plain. 

No hand has ever built here. No tool has touched this mud. There are no ruins because there was never anything to ruin. It is pure, unmediated nature, operating on the slowest possible metabolic rate. The dynamics of this world are agonizingly patient. Change is measured not in seasons, but in the slow subsidence of a mudbank over centuries, the creeping spread of a succulent root system over decades, the imperceptible rise and fall of the water table as the planet breathes. Nothing happens quickly. It is an ecosystem of exhaustion, where all the violent tectonics and crashing waves of youth have settled into a permanent, warm coma.

But if the land is slow, the sound is overwhelmingly present. The noise comes from the fauna. This sparse, saturated world is paradoxically heaving with animal life, though you rarely see it. The mud itself is the source of the din. Beneath the surface, the benthos is thick with burrowing crustaceans and armored annelids. The noise is a low, rhythmic, wet thrum—a million chitinous legs pulling through gelatinous mud, a million mandibles grinding organic detritus. It is the sound of consumption at a geological scale. 

Above the mud, the air hums with dense clouds of flying arthropods—skimmers and water-midges that breed in the standing puddles. Their wings create a droning, vibrating bass note that never ceases, vibrating in the warm, heavy air. Occasionally, a larger shadow passes overhead—a heavy, slow-beating avian or a leathery skimmer hunting the swarms—and the drone dips in pitch as the insects scatter, only to swell back up moments later. At dusk and dawn, the amphibians call: not with the cheerful singing of frogs, but with deep, resonant, bellows from lung-chested creatures squatting in the mud, their calls bumping against the low clouds and echoing across the flatness. 

The loudest sound of all is the water itself. Because the air is so saturated, condensation is a constant event. Water beads on the waxy cuticles of the succulents, swells, and drops. From the sprawling leaves, it falls onto the hard-packed mud with a sharp, rhythmic *plap*. Multiplied by millions of leaves across the endless plain, it creates a syncopated, dripping percussion, a steady auditory rainfall even when the sky is clear. It is a land that is constantly, loudly weeping itself back into the earth.

It is a place that feels familiar, yet primordial. There are no spectral anomalies, no bizarre physics—just the sheer, overwhelming weight of deep time. It is a landscape that has reached the end of its story, a warm, wet, loud eternity at the bottom of the world, slowly digesting itself into oblivion.
