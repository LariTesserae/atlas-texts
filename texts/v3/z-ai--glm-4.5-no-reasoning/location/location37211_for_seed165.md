The air does not behave as air should. At this altitude—where the atmosphere thins into a bruised, violet perjury against the void—sound does not dissipate; it crystallizes. The landscape is screaming, perpetually, in a frequency that bypasses the ears and settles directly into the marrow. It is a tectonic, sub-harmonic roar, the resonance of a mountain range that has been suspended in a state of frozen agony since before the invention of the spine. The volume is catastrophic, yet it does not deafen; it drowns out the internal monologue, scrubbing the mind blank until the only thought left is the raw, unfiltered perception of the roar.

You stand on the spine of the world. The elevation is extreme, the kind of height where the curvature of the planet becomes a visible sag in the horizon, and the stars do not wait for night—they simply burn through the bruised, dim twilight like pale lesions in the sky. Light here is a miser, arriving only in a grey, diffuse wash that casts no shadows, only deepening the sense that the world has been drained of its primary colors. It is cold—not the sharp, biting cold of a winter wind, but a profound, glacial cold that feels like a physical property of the emptiness, a temperature that exists because heat has simply forgotten this place exists.

Despite the overwhelming volume, the place never changes. The dynamic is absolute zero. The roaring sound does not fluctuate; it is a steady, eternal chord. The wind does not gust; it pushes with a constant, unyielding force that has carved the terrain into its final, immutable shapes over eons, and now holds those shapes in a rigid stasis. This is established erosion—the mountains are not wearing away; they arrived at their current geometry millennia ago and simply stopped, locked in a perfect, frozen equilibrium. Time does not flow here; it pools.

The emptiness is near-void. Looking out across the open, regional scale of the plateau, the distance between objects becomes a palpable weight. There is no flora to speak of—no trees, no shrubs, no moss. The sparse vegetation is relegated to the cracks in the stone: pale, crystalline lichens that do not grow but simply exist, mimicking the frost. They are biological, yet they feel mineral. Water is present, but only just. It does not flow. It seeps and hangs. In the deepest ravines—gashes in the obsidian-like rock that drop away into nothing—pockets of hyper-cooled dampness linger. Occasionally, a bead of condensation will swell on a sheer cliff face and fall, not as a droplet, but as a microscopic shard of ice that shatters on the rocks below, contributing a microscopic, high-pitched ping to the omnipresent roar.

The fauna is rare, and what little exists has been broken by the reality breakdown. Occasionally, a silhouette moves against the dim sky—a creature, perhaps, though its geometry is wrong. It might have too many wings, or its joints might bend in directions that suggest a tesseract rather than a skeleton. They do not eat or sleep; they simply drift on the frozen winds, riding the constant pressure gradients, their bodies partially phased out of sync with the physical world. They make no sound of their own; they are born from the landscape's noise and dissolve back into it when they collide with the static.

Weirdness here is not an overlay; it is the substrate. The extreme altitude and the near-void density have stretched the fabric of reality too thin. The mountain ranges in the distance do not stay parallel; occasionally, a peak will flicker, doubling its image for a fraction of a second before snapping back. The horizon occasionally pinches, drawing the edges of the world together like a tightening drawstring bag, only to relax again into its sagging curve. The space between the scattered remnants of the built environment is non-Euclidean; walking toward a structure seems to take longer than walking away from it, as if distance itself is repelled by the presence of construction.

And the construction—what is left of it—is pitiful and strange. There are no devices, no tech, no tools. Only scattered cairns and partial walls, built from the native black stone without mortar. They are positioned at precise, inexplicable intervals across the vast emptiness, single structures standing alone in hundreds of miles of nothing. They do not provide shelter. Their doors face downward, into the rock, or sideways into the wind. They seem to be anchors—meager, desperate attempts by some long-vanished species to pin reality down, to give the roaring void a point of reference. But the anchors are failing. The stone at the corners of these structures does not erode; it blurs. The edges lose their definition, smearing out into the atmosphere like charcoal on wet paper, the matter slowly giving up on being matter.

This is a landscape of maintenance and failure. It is a place where the universe's background radiation has been turned up to a deafening crescendo, where the cold and the damp and the silence of life have conspired to stretch existence to its breaking point, and then, inexplicably, frozen it there—forever roaring, forever dim, forever hanging a fraction of a second away from total collapse.
