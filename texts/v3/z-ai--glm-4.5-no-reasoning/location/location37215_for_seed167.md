The water does not fall here; it simply is. This is a place of perpetual submersion, a warm and lightless ocean that presses against everything with the weight of a world that drowned long ago, yet continues to live. The temperature is a constant, bathwater warmth, rising from geothermal vents in the seafloor and trapped beneath the crushing volume of the deep. Light cannot penetrate this far. The sun is a myth, a forgotten rumor; the sky is just an infinite, unseen ceiling of black water hundreds of fathoms overhead.

Down here, the elevation is virtually nonexistent. The terrain is a sprawling, low-lying plain of silt and mud, but it is not empty. This is a buried city. There is no technology left—no humming machines, no electricity, no artificial light—but the bones of an organized construction remain. Dense infrastructure spreads across the seafloor, a labyrinth of weathered avenues, stacked tenements, and sprawling plazas. The architecture is layered, built over itself in generations, now completely saturated. Stone and concrete have been worn smooth by the long, patient erosion of the deep sea. The edges have dissolved; the corners are rounded. The buildings have become topography. They stand in settled balance, neither crumbling further nor resisting the water, existing as patient, monolithic reefs. 

Despite the overwhelming presence of the sea, the space feels densely packed. The presences here are near-touching, layered one atop another within the volumetric squeeze of the water. Yet, it is profoundly quiet. Sound is swallowed by the pressure. There is no roar of currents, no crashing of waves. The acoustic space is dominated by a thick, tinnitus-like hum—the aggregated resonance of a million small, biological processes happening in the dark. Occasionally, the low, subsonic thrum of a distant seismic shift shudders through the water, vibrating the hollow doorways and collapsing stairwells, but it fades into the silence almost immediately.

Life is abundant, but it is a life stripped of color and foliage. Vegetation is sparse—only pale, rootless tangles of kelp drift like cobwebs across the intersections of the drowned avenues, catching on the eroded lintels of doorways. The true density comes from the fauna. The city is a metropolis of the blind. Shoals of silver, eyeless fish—sensing the world only through pressure and the warm chemical gradients of the water—pulse through the wide boulevards like liquid muscle. They move in tight, synchronized clouds, navigating the invisible architecture by the micro-eddies it creates in the current. 

In the lower tiers, where the mud is stirred by the warm breath of hydrothermal vents, larger things move. Pale, bloated arthropods with translucent carapaces and legs like architectural scaffolding haul themselves across the seafloor, grazing on the bacterial mats that cling to the submerged walls. They are slow, methodical, and enormous, filling the alleyways. Above them, smooth, eel-like predators glide with undulating patience, their bodies generating faint, bioluminescent pulses—strobe-like flashes of blue and green that illuminate a wet brick wall or a submerged street sign for a fraction of a second before plunging it back into absolute darkness.

There is a quiet, weird wrongness to the place, a subtle dissonance that takes time to notice. The ecosystem is entirely untethered from the solar cycle, yet it operates with a rigid, mechanical synchronicity. The sparse kelp doesn't drift randomly; it retracts and extends in slow, deliberate rhythms, as if breathing to the heartbeat of the city itself. The blind fish school not just for survival, but with an unnatural precision, their formations mapping the invisible grid of the streets below them, tracing the ghost of the urban plan. The hydrothermal vents exhale in steady, metronomic intervals, keeping time for a world that has no day or night. The predator-prey dynamic is a patient, formalized dance, happening in the dark with a synchronized grace that feels less like chaotic nature and more like a ritual enacted in the flooded nave of a dead cathedral.

This is the landscape: a warm, dark, and quiet metropolis where the grid of human intent has been entirely subsumed by the slow, blind geometry of the deep. It is a place in motion, yet perfectly still; entirely natural, yet haunted by the dense, organized ghosts of what it used to be.
