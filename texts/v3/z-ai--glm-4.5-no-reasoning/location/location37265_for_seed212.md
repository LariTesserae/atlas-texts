There is no sky. Or rather, there is a ceiling of living mass that has so thoroughly consumed the heavens that it might as well be the earth turned inside out. This is a place where the distinction between substrate and organism has collapsed—not a forest, but a pure-plant hyperbody, a single continuous tissue of green that has swallowed every cubic meter of open air and pressed it into damp, respiring channels.

You stand in a corridor. The walls are not built; they are grown, though "grown" implies a intention that isn't here. This is *eruption*. The geometry is urban—alcoves and archways, multi-story cavities stacked atop each other, streets that branch and reconnect in patterns that echo the logic of a dense, layered city—but it is all vegetable. Buttresses of compressed lignin rise hundreds of feet, their surfaces peeled back in fresh, wet curls. The formation is young. The features are sharp. Nothing has had time to weather into softness; the edges of new petioles slice like unglazed ceramics, and the tips of emerging roots are crystalline with turgor pressure, hard enough to score. This place was *recently* not here. It is still arriving.

The density is suffocating. Every surface is occupied. Shelf fungi jut from every node in overlapping armored plates. Epiphytic film—something between moss and membrane—coats the verticals in such thickness that the walls seem to breathe, contracting and dilating in slow, peristaltic waves. Above, below, around: root-tangles interlock so tightly they form load-bearing masses. Suspended vines hang in curtains so dense they read as walls until you push through them and find another corridor, another chamber, another impossible recursion of green architecture. There is no open space, only *negotiated* space—volumes that the plant-mass has, for reasons of its own chaotic metabolism, chosen to hollow out. The moisture here is intermittent but inescapable. Condensation weeps from every overhang. Pools of warm, tannin-stained water collect in the crotches where massive stems diverge, and the air carries the humidity of a closed throat. You are always damp. Your skin prunes. You are being *marinated*.

And the sound. The sound is a catastrophe.

It begins as a roar, but that word implies a single source. This is a *symphony* of overlapping biologic catastrophes. The xylem is screaming. You can hear the hydraulic pressure moving through the great columns—millions of liters of warm sap being forced upward through vascular bundles that groan and creak, their fibers splitting microscopically, each fracture a sharp report that echoes through the chambers. The sound of water under pressure, in pipes that are alive, in quantities that should require pumps and engines but here are moved by raw osmotic force alone. It is a bass thrum that you feel in your sternum before you hear it, a sub-audible vibration that makes your teeth ache.

Layered over this: the clicking. Thousands—no, *millions*—of stomata opening and closing across every leaf surface in the visible world, a collective hiss like static or like the inside of an anthill magnified beyond comprehension. And beneath it, the wet sounds. Dripping. The percussion of heavy condensation falling onto taut leaf-canvas from great height. The snap of vines contracting. The groan of lignin under torsion. The place is *moving*. It is reshaping itself even as you stand in it. Corridors close. New hollows tear open. A wall of layered thorn-plates that was three feet to your left is suddenly two feet away, the tissue expanding, the whole district of this green city *slumping* as some massive reorganization of the hyperbody's mass takes place in a section you cannot see but can hear—a distant, catastrophic groaning, like a building demolition heard through water.

There are no animals. No insects. Nothing with a skeleton or a nervous system moves in this place. And yet, it feels *inhabited*. The plant is performing every function of a city. It has infrastructure—transport corridors for sap, reservoirs for water, waste channels that drip tannin-black fluid into lower catchments. It has architecture—load-bearing columns, cantilevered balconies of shelf-fungi, tension cables of aerial root. It has *industry*—the furious, silent industry of photosynthesis, of growth, of expansion, running at a rate that defies what you know of vegetable patience. Things are being made and unmade. You watch a new chamber tear itself open: a mass of tightly packed rootlets simply *unweaving*, separating, pulling back like a fist opening, revealing a fresh room whose walls are still wet, still pale, still soft as fruit. And across the corridor, another chamber is *closing*, its walls inflating, their cells swelling with stored water, pressing inward, reducing a space the size of a room to a space the size of a closet in minutes. The turbulence is architectural. The city is building and demolishing itself continuously, without craft, without plan, driven by some Solaris logic—metabolic imperatives that reference no blueprint, no need, no purpose you can identify.

And here is where the breakdown starts to show. Because this should not be possible. The scale is too large for the speed. The structures are too organized for the chaos. You walk through a district that is laid out in a grid—four corridors meeting at a node, each lined with alcoves of identical proportion—and then you turn a corner and the geometry *fractures*. The corridors narrow to slots. The floor tilts—not because of terrain, but because the tissue has *twisted*, a whole section of the hyperbody undergoing torsion, so that vertical and horizontal lose their meanings. You find stairs—actual *stairs*, grown from the edges of overlapping shelf-fungi, descending into a pit where the light should not reach but does, because the canopy above has parted in a perfect circle, a hole that tracks no sun you can see, casting a beam of warm, white light onto a floor that is *pulsing*—the entire surface rising and falling in a uniform rhythm, like the chest of something breathing.

You try to map it. You cannot. The place is non-Euclidean in the way that a living thing is non-Euclidean—its geometry is provisional, metabolic, subject to rewrite. A landmark you passed an hour ago is now somewhere else. Not moved. *Grown* elsewhere. The same structure has been instantiated again, or has extended itself, or was always there and you misidentified its position. The spatial logic of this place is the logic of a body, not a city—districts are organs, corridors are vessels, and the whole thing is one continuous individual with no inside and no outside, only a fold where you happen to be standing, a temporary crease in a single enormous surface that has folded in on itself so many times that it has produced the illusion of rooms, of streets, of a built environment.

The warmth is biological. The air is heated by the metabolism of the mass around you—respiration, decomposition, the exothermic chemistry of a trillion cells working in parallel. It is the warmth of a compost heap, of a closed greenhouse, of a mouth. You are inside something. You have been inside it since you arrived. There is no outside to reach; the boundary between the plant and the world was erased long ago, subsumed, digested. The plant *is* the world now.

And it is aware of you. Not in a cognitive way—nothing here thinks. But the tissue near your skin reacts. Stomata dilate in your direction. Roots adjust their angle of growth by fractions of degrees, orienting toward the carbon dioxide of your breath. The humidity around your body increases as the local tissue increases its transpiration, as if tasting you, sampling you, adjusting its local conditions to accommodate or to *absorb* a new chemical signature. The walls of the corridor you stand in are slightly closer than they were when you entered. Not because the corridor is closing in any hostile way. Simply because it is *growing*, and you are in the way, and the plant does not know about obstacles. It does not know about *things*. It only knows about growth.

The roar continues. The sap screams in its columns. The stomata click. The structure reshuffles. A new chamber opens to your right, bright and wet, and another closes behind you, sealing off the way you came with a wall of inflated, water-pressured tissue that hardens even as you watch, lignin depositing in real-time, locking the exit into permanence.

You are in a city that is a creature that is a world that is becoming more of itself every second. There is no tool. There is no machine. There is only the relentless, deafening, biological engine of a plant that has eaten everything else and now, having run out of externals, is eating its own geometry—rearranging, expanding, contracting, building and unbuilding its green architecture in a storm of unreasoning, unending, vegetable fury.

The air is green. The light is green. The sound is green. You breathe it in. You are becoming part of the moisture cycle. You are being *incorporated*. 

And the plant, deaf and blind and thoughtless, grows on.
