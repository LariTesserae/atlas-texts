The darkness is not an absence. It is a substance. It presses against the skin like warm, wet velvet, and it carries the weight of a continent.

You stand on the upper escarpment of a world that has never known starlight. The elevation here is tremendous—a high plateau that sprawls across what would be continental dimensions on any smaller planet, stretching so far that the curvature of this vast place is lost beneath the sheer volume of the dark. The air is warm, almost feverish, and it moves in slow thermal bellows that you feel more than hear, enormous breaths pushed across the plateau by weather systems the size of small seas. There is no sky to watch, no horizon to measure. Only the black, and the sounds that live inside it.

And the sounds are enormous. The landscape roars. Not chaotically—there is a rhythm to it, the deep mechanical pulse of a world running hot. The sound comes from below and inside and everywhere at once: the bass thrum of pump stations buried in the escarpment, the rhythmic clanking of processing arrays that stretch for kilometers along the ridge, the high pressure hiss of steam venting from cracks in the living rock. The noise is layered, dense, a stratigraphy of sound—industrial frequencies stacking atop biological cries atop the constant, rushing voice of water. It saturates the air. You breathe it in and feel it vibrating in your marrow.

The water is everywhere. It does not fall from any sky; instead, it emerges, perpetually, from the stone itself. The high plateau is a sweating place. Water presses up through every fissure and pore of the rock, weeping from cliff faces in sheets, welling from the ground underfoot until the entire escarpment has become a kind of vertical marsh. The stone under your feet is never dry—cannot be dry—because the water table sits so close to the surface that the land floats above it, barely contained. You walk and water rises around your boots, warm and mineral, carrying the faint chemical tang of things that have been dissolved and reconstituted underground. In the deeper ravines that cut the plateau, the water does not flow so much as exude, oozing down the walls in a continuous glaze, feeding cascades that are less waterfalls than the slow spilling of a saturated world. The rock itself seems to weep, seems unable to contain what moves through it.

And yet this is not a raw or unfinished landscape. The erosion parameter tells you something crucial: this place has been in its current form for a very long time. The water has had eons to find its paths. The cliffs are not jagged but rounded, sculpted into flowing forms by millennia of solution and deposition, their surfaces etched with lapiaz—those intricate, fluted carvings that water makes in soluble stone when given enough time. The ravines have settled into their depths. The plateau has reached equilibrium with its own hydrology. This is a landscape that has finished becoming itself and now simply *is*, holding its shape in a long, slow balance between the water that would dissolve it and the geological forces that maintain its height.

But the plateau is not natural. Or rather—it was natural once, long ago, before the industry came, and now it is something else, something that holds both the ancient and the engineered in a single, uneasy grip.

The structures are scattered but unmistakable. A single processing tower rises from the mist a hundred meters to your left, its base thick with the orange and white blooms of something that has learned to metabolize the runoff. You cannot see the tower’s top—it vanishes into the dark above—but you can hear it, feel it through the soles of your feet: the deep chug of pistons, the whine of turbines, the rhythmic expulsion of heated water from vents at its midsection. The tower is old. Its metal skin has corroded to the color of the rock, has been colonized by mineral deposits and biological films until it is hard to tell where the building ends and3 and the cliff begins. It has been here long enough to become part of the landscape’s equilibrium.

And there are others. Further along the escarpment, deeper in the dark, more towers stand at intervals, each one a node in some vast extraction network that stretches across the plateau. You cannot see their connections but you can trace them by sound—the way the mechanical pulses synchronize, the way the venting rhythms align. They are pumping something from below. Water, certainly, but also something else, something that burns in the air with a faint chemical luminescence, giving the darkness a texture, a barely-perceptible glow like the inside of a closed eyelid. This is the only light the world offers: the faint, bioluminescent haze of industrial processes bleeding energy into the surrounding air, and the brighter, stranger lights that come from the vegetation itself.

Because the plateau is lush. Impossibly lush, given the darkness. The warmth and the water have driven plant life into a kind of frenzy, and every surface—the rock faces, the tower structures, the ground between your feet, the rotting carcasses of older machinery—is claimed by it. But this is not the green of a sunlit world. The flora here has never known photosynthesis. It is pale, waxen, the color of things grown in darkness: whites and creams and pale yellows, fleshy tones that make the vegetation look almost animal. Thick-stemmed succulents cluster in the wettest places, their leaves swollen with stored water, their surfaces dotted with nodules that pulse with a faint, golden light—bioluminescent centers that glow and dim in slow rhythms, as though breathing. Vines with translucent skin climb the processing towers, their circulatory systems visible within, pale sap moving in slow peristalsis. Enormous fungal growths erupt from the seeping rock, bracketing the cliff faces in tiers, their gills descending like curtains, dripping condensation constantly. The air is thick with spores and pollen, with the sweet-rot smell of biological productivity pushed to its upper limits. The density of growth is staggering; there is no gap between plants, no bare patch of stone. Every cubic centimeter is claimed. The ecosystem is layered upon itself, vines growing on fungi growing on bacteria growing on the mineral films that grow on the industrial infrastructure that grows through the rock. It is a jungle of shadow and moisture, and it is loud with its own life—the clicking of insect-analogs, the low warbling calls of something moving through the canopy, the wet rustle of growth happening in real time, cells expanding in the warmth.

The fauna is abundant, and it is strange. You hear more than you see, but what you see is enough to know that the taxonomy here has diverged from anything familiar. Things move in the dark between the bioluminescent plants, their shapes suggested by the way the faint light bends around them. There are grazers—you can hear them, the soft, continuous cropping of plant matter, the occasional wet belch of digestion—and there are predators, indicated by the sudden silences that fall when they pass, the way the insect-clicking drops to nothing, the way the bioluminescent nodules on the nearby plants flare brighter, as if in alarm. Something large moves through the vegetation to your right, displacing air, pressing the vines aside with a sound like wet leather on wet leather. You catch a glimpse—a pale mass, too many limbs, a mouth that glows from within with the same golden light as the plant nodules—and then it is gone, swallowed by the dark. It does not attack. It is not interested in you. You are not part of its ecology; you are something it has no category for, and so it ignores you, moves on, continues the ancient business of eating and being eaten that has been running here since before the towers were built.

And that is the strangest thing about this place: the industry and the biology are not in conflict. They have reached the same kind of equilibrium as the water and the stone. The towers do not poison the ecosystem; they feed it, their runoff providing mineral substrates that the flora has evolved to exploit. The fauna does not avoid the machinery; it colonizes it, nests in the warm spaces between processes, feeds on the biological films that grow on the heated metal. The landscape has absorbed the industrial, made it just another geological force, another process running in parallel with the water and the warmth and the dark. The erosion parameter confirms this—the structures are old, settled, weathered into the same rounded forms as the stone. They have been here long enough to become ruins, and the ecosystem has grown over them like a forest grows over a fallen tree, consuming them without destroying them, making them part of its cycles.

You walk along the escarpment and the world continues, vast and uncontained. The plateau does not end; it simply extends further into the dark, more towers, more vegetation, more water, more sound. The scale of the place begins to press on your sense of reality. You have been walking for what feels like hours and you have passed three more processing stations, each identical in its sound and its function, each claimed by the same dense biological overgrowth, each venting the same warm water into the same warm air. The landscape is not repeating itself—it is too organic for that, too full of variation and accident—but it is *extending*, the same themes playing out with minor variations across a distance too great to comprehend. This is the meaning of the scale parameter: the plateau is a region, not a feature. It is a landscape large enough to generate its own weather, its own ecosystems, its own history. The towers were not built by a handful of engineers; they were the project of a civilization, an industrial undertaking at continental scale. And whoever built them is gone now—absorbed into the ecosystem, perhaps, or migrated to some other dark plateau, or simply evolved into something that no longer builds towers—but the machinery keeps running. It has been running for so long that the landscape has forgotten it was ever artificial.

The water continues to weep from the stone. The plants continue to glow and breathe. The machinery continues to pulse and vent. The dark continues to press, warm and wet and total. And the sound—the sound goes on, the great layered roar of a living world, mechanical and biological and geological all at once, so loud and so constant that it has become indistinguishable from silence, just the baseline condition of existence here, the voice of the plateau itself, speaking in the only language it knows.

You are very small. The darkness is very large. And the water rises around your feet as you take the next step forward.
