It begins with sound. Not the sound of water—there is none, has never been here—but the deep, chitonous rasp of a million moving bodies, the click-clack of mandibles, the bass-drum thud of thick-stemmed things flexing and releasing in the warm, dim air. The noise is constant, layered, architectural. It fills the space like a solid, pressing against your eardrums, leaving no room for silence.

You are in a high place. The air is thin enough that breathing takes intention, but it is also warm—humid, almost, though there is no moisture to carry that humidity. The sensation of warmth comes instead from the dense biological exhalations of the place itself, the combined metabolic heat of everything living here, pressed close in this elevated bowl of ancient, dry stone. You are standing in what might once have been a crater, or a collapsed geode—some natural cathedral of layered silicate, weathered over millennia into a smooth, amphitheater shape. The walls rise on all sides, sealing the space. The scale is intimate: you could walk its circumference in ten minutes. It is a room, albeit a room open to the strange, dim sky above.

And it is lush. Shockingly, impossibly lush, given the absolute absence of water. The floor and walls of this crater are draped in vegetation, but vegetation of a kind that has never known rain or river. The things growing here are not plants, not exactly. They are dry-life forms—hyperosmotic extremophiles that draw what they need directly from the air, from the mineral substrate, from each other. There are stalks like bleached bamboo, but segmented with translucent membranes that pulse visibly with some internal transport fluid—thick, amber, viscous. There are spreading mats of fibrous, root-like material that cling to the stone without penetrating it, binding the community together in a symbiotic web. There are tall, fractal growths that unfurl in spirals, their surfaces covered in fine, crystalline hairs that chitter in the ceaseless movement of the space, producing that omnipresent hissing undertone. Everything is pale—ivory, bone-white, the pale gold of dried honey, the grey-green of lichen on a skull. Without water, there are no greens, no lush browns. The lushness is structural, textural: density of form, density of purpose, density of being.
