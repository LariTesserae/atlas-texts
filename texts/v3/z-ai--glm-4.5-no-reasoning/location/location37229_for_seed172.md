You enter through a thermal lock—though "lock" suggests a door, and this is more like a plug being pulled from a bottle. The seal breaks with a pressure equalization that you feel rather than hear, and then you are inside.

The Pocket is smaller than you expected. Smaller than anyone expects. The industrial complex that contains it sprawls across three square kilometers of high-altitude mountain ridge, but the Pocket itself—the observed space, the place that *is* this place—is a chamber carved into the facility's deepest sublevel, perhaps thirty meters across. A perfect sphere, or near enough that the manufacturing tolerances make the difference academic.

Everything here is made. This is the first truth you must accept, because there is no substrate beneath the made-ness to fall back on. The walls are not stone covered in metal; they are metal through and through, alloy extrusions running in crystalline patterns that were determined by computational fluid dynamics before the first billet was poured. The floor—you stand on a grate—is not installed over something natural. Below the grate is more structure, descending in manufactured strata all the way down. This place has no geology. It has a bill of materials.

The cold arrives not as a sensation but as a fact. The air is held at approximately -40°C, and it has been held there for eleven years, seven months, and fourteen days. You know this because the temperature is a parameter, and parameters here do not drift. The cooling system—a closed-cycle helium compressor occupying the floors above—runs at a frequency so constant that its vibration has become indistinguishable from silence. Your breath does not fog. The air is too dry for condensation. There is no water. Not low water. Not reduced humidity. *None.* The hydrogen and oxygen atoms that constitute water in most environments are present here only in bound forms—oxides on the alloy surfaces, hydrogen dissolved in the metal lattice at concentrations specified by the metallurgical requirements. Free H₂O is a contaminant this system was designed to exclude.

And the sound. God, the sound.

You expected silence in a cold, sealed place. Instead, the Pocket screams. Not with any single voice, but with a dense, structural roar that seems to emanate from the walls themselves. The alloy sphere resonates. Every surface vibrates at frequencies and amplitudes that the instruments mounted throughout the chamber maintain at precisely their set points. The sound has components in the audible range—thunderous, floor-shaking groans that you feel in your sternum—and components below, subsonic pulsations that mass against your vestibular system like a hand pressing on your balance. There are higher frequencies too, whines and shrieks from the electrical systems, the corona discharge off the bus bars, the high-voltage arcing that happens continuously inside the observation columns.

The total acoustic energy in the chamber is 147 decibels. You are wearing hearing protection rated to 31 dB reduction. It is not enough. Nothing short of total isolation would be enough. The sound is not an accident. It is a *product.* This chamber was built to produce specific acoustic conditions, and those conditions include sound pressure levels that would kill an unprotected human in minutes. The place is, in a very literal sense, a machine for being loud.

Your eyes adjust to the light. It is bright here—industrial bright, the kind of illumination designed not for comfort but for inspection. High-intensity discharge lamps in armored housings throw light at 1200 lux across every surface. There are no shadows. The chamber's geometry and the lamp placement were optimized to eliminate shadow zones. Even the grate beneath your feet is lit from below by fixtures embedded in the sub-floor structure. You can see everything, and everything you see is manufactured.

The sparse vegetation you were told about reveals itself as a scattering of engineered organisms—biomimetic growths, really, though they incorporate living tissue in their structures. They cling to the walls in isolated patches, each one a contained unit: a rectangular housing, roughly shoebox-sized, bolted to the alloy surface, with a transparent face through which you can see the culture inside. Lichens, or things like lichens—slow-growing, black-green, metabolically adapted to the cold and the dryness and the vibration. They feed on trace nutrients delivered through capillary channels in their housings. They are not decorative. Each unit is a sensor. The organisms' metabolic rates shift in response to micro-fluctuations in the chamber's parameters, and the housings' integrated optics read those shifts and report them to the monitoring systems. The scattered growths are instrumentation, dressed in the language of biology because biology, even constrained and weaponized biology, still responds to stimuli that electronic sensors miss.

There is nothing else alive here. No fauna, no vermin, not even the extremophile bacteria that colonize most industrial spaces. The Pocket is bio-sterile. The protocols for maintaining that sterility are extreme: every surface is treated with antimicrobial coatings that are reapplied on a maintenance cycle; the air passes through filtration that removes particulates down to 5 nanometers; anything organic that enters is sealed in containment or incinerated. The only living things are the sensor-lichens in their housings, and they are sealed so thoroughly that they might as well be machines.

You walk the circumference—eighteen steps along the grated walkway that rings the chamber's equator. The view does not change. The observation columns—six of them, evenly spaced—rise from the floor to the ceiling, each one a transparent cylinder a meter in diameter, inside which electrical arcing occurs continuously. Plasma filaments crawl up the columns' interiors, branching and reconnecting in patterns that the facility's operators monitor for deviations. The arcs emit light in the violet and ultraviolet, adding a sharp edge to the yellow-white of the discharge lamps. They also emit ozone, which is scrubbed from the air so fast that you smell only the faintest trace—ozone and hot metal and the cold, mineral absence of anything biological.

This is the strange thing: for all its violence, the Pocket is perfectly stable. The sound does not vary. The temperature does not fluctuate. The electrical arcing in the columns maintains patterns that were characterized years ago and have not shifted since. The sensor-lichens have not changed their readings outside of predicted ranges. The system is in equilibrium—not a passive equilibrium, not the stillness of a dead place, but an active, powered equilibrium, a balance maintained by the constant input of enormous energy. The compressors above draw four megawatts. The lighting system draws another 800 kilowatts. The observation columns and their high-voltage systems pull six megawatts more. All of this energy enters the Pocket, and the Pocket disposes of it—through cooling, through acoustic radiation, through the thermal bus that carries heat away to the radiators on the facility's roof. The place is a waterfall of energy, a constant flow-through, and yet nothing inside it changes. The flow is the same from second to second. The roar has the same texture now as it had when you entered, and as it will have when you leave.

You stand at the center of the grate—the one spot where the structural members converge, a radial pattern like a spider's web machined from titanium—and you try to feel the place as a whole. The cold is a fact. The sound is an environment. The light is a condition. Together, they constitute something like a habitat, but a habitat for nothing. No one works in the Pocket. The monitoring is done remotely. Maintenance is performed during scheduled shutdowns, which happen twice a year and require the chamber to be brought to ambient conditions—a process that takes nine days in each direction. The rest of the time, the Pocket simply *runs.*

There is a purpose. You were told it, or part of it: the Pocket is an acoustic test chamber for components that will operate in extreme conditions—deep space, polar environments, volcanic vents. The sound and cold and dryness are stresses applied to materials, and the observation columns are part of the diagnostic array. But this explanation feels insufficient. The scale of the thing—the chamber embedded in the facility embedded in the mountain, the eleven years of continuous operation, the four megawatts of cooling alone—suggests something beyond materials testing. Something that requires *this specific* combination of conditions, held at *this specific* stability, for *this specific* duration, with no variation tolerated.

The sensor-lichens shift. You notice it peripherally—a darkening in one of the housings on the far wall, a subtle change in the color density of the culture inside. By the time you focus on it, the shift has already been logged, the data already flowing to systems you cannot see. Whatever the Pocket is really for, it is watching itself with a thoroughness that borders on anxiety. It is a machine that monitors its own monitoring, a closed loop that maintains the conditions for its own continuation.

The roar continues. The cold continues. The light continues. You are a temporary presence in a place designed to exclude temporariness—to hold its state against time, against entropy, against the soft intrusions of biology and weather and change. The Pocket does not care that you are here. It will not change for your presence, and it will not miss your absence. It will simply continue, deafening and bright and cold and made, until someone decides to shut it down, and then it will take nine days to become something else, and that something else will not be this.

You make your way back to the thermal lock. The plug seals behind you. The sound drops—not to silence, but to the merely industrial clamor of the facility's upper levels. The cold eases. You can feel your face again. The water returns to the air, and your breath fogs, and you realize that in the Pocket, you had stopped noticing its absence. The dryness had become a condition of your body, not of the environment. Your skin had tightened. Your lips had cracked. You had been drying out, slowly, without water, without any solvent for the things that need solving in a living system.

The Pocket is not for you. The Pocket is not for anything alive. It is for whatever is being made there, in sound and cold and absence—a product, or a process, or a condition that exists nowhere else and can exist nowhere else, sustained by machines in a room smaller than expected, deep in a mountain, high above the world.
