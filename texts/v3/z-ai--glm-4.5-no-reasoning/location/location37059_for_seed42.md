A high lake, almost a sky. The water stands at elevation, cupped in a vast and ancient caldera whose rim has worn down into soft, weathered shoulders of stone. It is cool here, as high places kept wet are always cool—the kind of cold that seeps through fabric and into the marrow without any dramatic wind to announce it. The air smells of green and iron and the patient quiet of things that have been this way for a very long time.

But it is not quiet. That is the first surprise.

The sound comes from below and from everywhere. A low, rhythmic booming, organic and dense, overlaid with a higher clicking that patterns itself into almost-speech. The lake is loud. The surface, when you find it through the mist, does not shimmer but *pulses*, disturbed from underneath by the movements of bodies far larger than this small place should support. And the vegetation—there is no shore, no bank, no dry edge. The plants have become the land. Massive root-cities float at the waterline, interlocked rhizome masses decades thick, buoyant and slow, their upper surfaces worn into something like streets by the passage of feet and the patient grinding of water. Between these root-rafts, the dark water shows in gaps and channels, and from these gaps rise the stalks: thick, fibrous columns crowned with broad leaves that funnel the constant mist and the intermittent rain back down into the system. The leaves are the architecture. They roof over the channels, overlap into walls, curl into gutters and conduits. Everything is green and wet and alive in the sense that a reef is alive—substrate and structure and shelter all at once.

This is a settlement, though you would not know it from any distance. The built and the grown are indistinguishable. Platforms of woven stalk extend over the water, lashed to the floating root-masses with cord that is itself still growing, still tightening imperceptibly over seasons. There are shelters—hemispheres of bent living cane, their leaf-canopies so dense they shed water in steady curtains, their interiors dim and dry and cool. Fire does not live here easily. The smoke, when it comes, is wet and white and hangs low. The tools are stone and bone and the hardened spines of the larger plants. No metal. No wheel. No forge-glow. Only the patient craft of hands that know their materials because those materials are still growing around them, still needing tending, still reshaping themselves year by year so that a doorway that was tall enough last season now presses against the forehead, and must be cut again, the sap running clean and bitter.

The fauna are the sound. Below the root-streets, in the channels and the deeper pools between the float-masses, something breathes. Many things. The water is crowded with life in the way that a cathedral is crowded with air—full of it, defined by it. Pale shapes move in the dark water. Long, eel-like bodies with too many fin-ridges. Crustacean things that click and drone from the undersides of the root-platforms, their shells furred with algae, their claws bright with the orange of some deep pigment. And the boomers—whatever they are. You see them only as displacement, as the great slow rolls that send swells rocking through the channels and make the hanging leaf-walls sway. They are large and they are numerous and they are the foundation of something, though whether that thing is fear or worship or simply the background condition of existence here is impossible to say from outside.

The people—if they are people—move through this at the pace of their setting. Slow. Everything is slow. The root-rafts drift on micro-currents, their positions shifting over months, the channels between them narrowing and widening with the years. The shelters settle. The paths change. A walk between two platforms is never quite the same walk twice, though the difference is so gradual that only the oldest remember when this channel was wider, when that stalk was shorter, when you could see the open water from here. They adapt as plants adapt: by growing toward and away, by adding layer to layer, by accepting the water and the wet and the cold as conditions rather than adversaries. Their clothing is leaf-fiber, sealed with resin that weeps from certain stalks when they are scored. It keeps the water out, mostly. Nothing keeps the water fully out. The sound does not stop.

There is a strangeness here, mild but persistent. The geometry is wrong in small ways. The root-masses form patterns that almost tessellate, channels that almost grid, but the angles are off by degrees that feel intentional, that suggest a logic not built for human comfort. The clicking from below sometimes synchronizes across distant points in the water, as though the creatures are listening to each other, or to something else. The leaves, when they funnel the rain, sometimes pour their streams not down but sideways, or in spiraling arcs that catch the grey light and hold it. And the mist has a quality to it—not thickness, but *attention*, as though it were observing rather than merely obscuring.

But these are small things. The place is settled in itself. The erosion has found its balance with the growth; the water has found its level%level; the loud, crowded life beneath has made its accommodation with the slow, green architecture above. This is a lake-city at elevation, built from its own ecosystem, ancient in its adjustment, cool and wet and loud and patient, a single small vast place bounded by the caldera rim and the grey sky and the depth of the water below, which goes down further than anyone has found, into rooms of colder water where the boomers go, and the clicking continues, and the roots, even there, still grow.
