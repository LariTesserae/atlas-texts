The plateau stretches at an altitude where atmosphere should thin and quiet, but neither holds. This is the Furnace Steppe—a high, immense tableland of rusted mineral crust suspended over some vast geological heat engine. There is no water here. Not hidden, not latent, not bound in hydrate or crystal lattice. The dryness is an absolute, a physical law written into the bombardment of the surface. And there are no plants—no root, no spore, no photosynthetic ghost. This is a world prior to or long past the carbon parade, a mineral stage where something else acts out its existence.

Yet the place is overrun.

They move across the plateau in numbers that redefine the word *fauna*. They are not biological. They are industrial chimeras—vast, slow, articulated things that might have been mining engines, might have been autonomous refineries, might have been something for which no human word exists. They crawl on track-belts wide as highways, on piston-legs that take minutes to complete a single stride. Their bodies are open frameworks of girder and cable, furnace-guts burning without fuel, exhaust stacks venting shimmering heat into the already-roasted air. They number in the thousands, in the tens of thousands, stretching to every horizon, a living mechanism-ecology of grinding, rolling, processing things that were never built by any hand visible on the plateau.

Because there are no constructions here. No buildings. No roads. No ruins of a factory city, no remnants of a staging post. The built environment reads as zero—not because construction never occurred, but because whatever was made here was *absorbed* into the things that now move across it. They are the architecture. They are the city that walks.

The sound is enormous and unceasing. A low, tectonic rumble of diesel-heartbeats by the ten thousand, layered with the shriek of untreated metal on metal, the rhythmic slam of compression chambers, the vast exhalation of steam or something like steam—superheated mineral vapor screaming from relief valves. It is not a chaotic sound. It has rhythm, phrase, repetition. It is the sound of an ecosystem in mechanical metabolism, and it never, ever stops.

The heat is oppressive, radiating up from the iron-dark crust and down from a sun that hangs in a sky the color of calcined bone. But more heat rises from the things themselves—their furnace cores, their friction joints, the waste-energy of their endless, purposeless processing. The plateau bakes. Thermals rise in columns visible to the eye, distorting the horizons, making the distant crawlers shimmer and dance like things seen through deep water.

And the reality here has begun to come apart.

It starts at the edges. The far horizons don't hold—they loop, overlap, show the same ridge line at two apparent distances simultaneously. The sky directly overhead is reliable, solid, a dome of pale fire. But the periphery warps. Tracks from the crawlers don't always lead to crawlers. Some fade into the mineral crust as if the ground remembers passage but cannot retain the source. Occasionally, a crawler will be seen in two places at once—once where it should be, grinding slowly along, and once far to the side, motionless, like a reflection in a pane of reality that doesn't quite align. Whether this is distortion from the heat or something more fundamental is impossible to determine, because the instruments that might measure it are themselves unreliable here. Distance becomes negotiable. Proximity becomes uncertain.

The weirdness concentrates around the largest of the things. The Elders—there is no better word—are crawlers the size of townships, their frameworks so old that metal has transmuted into something else, a mineral-metal amalgam that rings with a tone not found on any acoustic scale. They move the slowest, some taking hours to advance their own length. Around them, the reality-warp intensifies. Light bends. Sound stutters, repeats, echoes before the event that should produce it. Smaller crawlers orbit the Elders in patterns that seem purposeful—refueling, perhaps, or something like worship, or something like grazing, if machines can graze on the waste-heat and exhaust of their larger kin.

The ecological logic, such as it is, operates without biology. The crawlers process the crust itself—scraping, crushing, heating, extruding. They leave behind tracks of altered mineral: glass-furrows where the crust has been melted and re-solidified, powder-trails of finely milled dust that the thermal columns snatch into the hot sky, deposits of strange new alloys that cool in twisted, organic-looking formations. Other crawlers follow, re-processing the deposits, feeding on the leavings of the first, in a metabolism of industry that cycles material without purpose, without product, without end. Nothing is built from the processing. Nothing is stored. The material is transformed and re-transformed in a closed loop of mechanical digestion that has no exit and no goal.

The erosion of the plateau is complete and balanced. The crust has been scraped and re-laid so many times that its current form is entirely the product of the crawlers' processing. This is no longer a natural surface. It is a machine-terrain, a topography generated by ten thousand years of mechanical passage and mineral re-compaction. Yet it looks settled, weathered, as if this state is the only state it has ever known. The crawlers have been here long enough to erase their own history, to make their intervention indistinguishable from the ground itself.

Dynamic is almost absent at human timescales. A person standing here—impossible, but imagined—would see a photograph. The crawlers move too slowly for the eye to track in real time. Only by watching for minutes, marking a distant shape against a distant peak, would the motion become apparent. The changes that reshape this place happen on scales of months, years, decades. A crawler dies—not biologically, but mechanically, its furnace-heart guttering, its joints freezing—and becomes a fixture, a new geography, a hill of inert metal that smaller machines will slowly dismantle over centuries. A colony shifts its orbit around an Elder by a fraction of a degree per season. The plateau's surface alloy-profile drifts as processing patterns migrate. Everything is happening. Nothing is fast.

The emptiness is staggering. Despite the thousands of machines, the plateau is vast beyond their ability to fill. Walk for hours and you might pass between two crawlers close enough to touch, their girder-sides hot enough to sear, their slow treads grinding the crust to powder beneath you. But walk for days and you might see nothing but the shimmering horizons and the glass-ruts of old passages. The density per volume is near-zero. The plateau is a void with machines in it, a near-empty stage where industrial monsters perform their slow metabolism under a sun that doesn't care.

And always, the sound. Even at the emptiest stretches, the sound doesn't fade. It comes from everywhere, conducted through the mineral crust itself, a bedrock-vibration of mechanical life that you feel in your chest before you hear it with your ears. The plateau hums. The plateau roars. The plateau grinds and shrieks and thuds and whistles, a symphony of un-contained industry playing without audience, without conductor, without score, beginning and ending nowhere, sustained by processes that have outlasted whatever began them.

This is the Furnace Steppe. High, hot, dry, and loud. Stripped of water and green. Occupied by a mechanism-ecology that was never built and may never die. And at its edges, where the horizons fail and the crawlers double, reality itself thins, stretches, and begins to reveal the machinery behind the machinery—gears of something that is not space and not time, turning slowly, grinding out the world one revolution at a time.
