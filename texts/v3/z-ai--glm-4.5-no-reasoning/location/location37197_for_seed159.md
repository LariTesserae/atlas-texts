You descend. The surface above—wherever that was—has become a memory of weight and silence. Here, in the subterranean expanse, the rock does not hold still. This is a place of becoming, a raw geology still writing itself, and it is anything but quiet.

The air presses against you, thick and warm, carrying the smell of heated iron, wet limestone, and something older—an organic musk that has no botanical source. The moisture here is intermittent, born not from standing water but from the clash of temperatures. Warm exhalations from the deep rock meet cooler air dragged down from above, sweating condensation onto every surface. Beads of water form, swell, and roll down the raw stone, gathering in the creases of the bedding planes before dripping into the dark. It is a place of perpetual dampness, yet you will find no rivers here—only the constant, rhythmic weeping of stone.

And what stone it is. The walls, the ceiling, the vast floor of this cavity—all of it is fresh. The rock looks carved in living memory: sharp-edged, unweathered, its mineral veins still exposed like fresh wounds. There are no rounded corners, no patient smoothing by ancient water. Instead, the geology shifts as you watch. At the regional scale, the cavern system stretches impossibly, a mid-range continent of hollow space, but the walls themselves bulge and contract. Subterranean pressure forces new rock upward in real-time; you can hear the grinding, the deep tectonic growl of formation, a bass-note that never ceases. Smaller formations crumble as the larger pressures assert themselves. It is a turbulent, embryonic world, constructing and demolishing itself with the erratic energy of an anthill disturbed.

There is no plant life. Not a filament of root, not a spore of green. The absence of vegetation is absolute, making the overwhelming presence of fauna all the more shocking. This is a mineral world, yet it teems.

They are here in the millions. The walls crawl. The air vibrates with them. They are pale, chitinous, eyeless things—creatures of the pre-metal dark that have adapted to a world without photosynthesis. Some are no larger than your thumbnail, skittering in dense, interlocking carpets across the wet rock, grazing on the bacterial and fungal slimes that bloom in the condensation. Others are longer, segmented, moving with the fluid, terrifying grace of centipedes the size of your arm, navigating the chaotic topography with twitching antennae. The density of life is staggering; the surfaces of this underground region are more organism than mineral. In the deeper crevices, thick, translucent grubs writhe in masses, generating their own faint, bioluminescent heat, casting a sickly amber glow across the damp ceilings.

The sound is inescapable. It is loud, a chaotic symphony of the raw world. Beneath the endless geological groan of shifting stone, there is the clicking. Millions of hollow legs tapping against the fresh rock, a sound like a constant, driving rain of plastic beads. When a shelf of stone fractures and falls—a frequent occurrence in this unstable place—the crash echoes through the caverns, triggering a cascade of distress signals. The skittering things shriek, a high-pitched, buzzing whine that resonates in the warm, dense air, layered over the heavy, wet splat of the fallen rock landing on the crawling carpet of life below.

Yet, within this overwhelming chaos, there are marks of something else. Scattered, inexplicable, haunting. In the hollows where the condensation pools deepest, where the rock has just settled enough to hold still for a moment, you find them: standing stones. No, not standing—*set*. They are short, roughly waist-high, shaped by blunt impact rather than careful carving. Someone—or some*thing*—has been here, working with the most primitive of hand-tools. The stones are placed in deliberate, though irregular, intervals across the wider expanses of the cavern floor. They offer no shelter, no apparent purpose. They are simply *there*, faint markers left in the rawest geology, primitive assertions of presence in a world that refuses to solidify.

Around these markers, the teeming fauna alters its behavior. The crawling masses part around the stones, leaving a precise inch of damp, empty rock at their base. The larger, predatory segments slow their twitching pace as they pass, antennae deferentially lowered. It suggests a rule to this chaotic world, an unseen order beneath the turbulence. The markers are old, older than the fresh rock pressing up around them, yet they persist. They belong to a hand that worked before metal, before refinement—just muscle, stone, and intention, carved into a place that was still being born.

You stand in the warm, deafening dark, condensation beading on your skin, and you realize this landscape is not a destination. It is a threshold. It is the under-construction basement of a world, swarming with its first, blind inheritors, marked by an intelligence that might already be gone. And it is still pushing upward, still deciding what it will be, grinding and clicking and dripping, while the pale things navigate the dark by the millions, waiting for the architecture to finally settle.
