You descend into it. A single constructed hollow, no larger than a guildhall's undercroft, sunk into the flatness of the world. There is no natural ground here—no soil, no bedrock, no sediment layer. Everything you touch is made. The walls, the floor, the ceiling: all fitted stone and mortared joint. This is a total environment, a built room the size of a small grove, and it is old.

The stone is weeping.

Water emerges from the walls themselves, not from cracks or channels but from the very pore of the material, as if the masonry were saturated through, as if it had been drinking from some shallow reservoir for centuries and now exudes what it cannot hold. The droplets form slowly, swell, and release. They do not fall so much as elongate and detach. The walls shine with it. The ceiling beads. Every vertical surface runs with a thin, continuous sheet, and where the water reaches the floor, it pools in the slight depressions between flagstones, filling the grout lines first, then spreading across the whole until the floor is a dark mirror, an inch of standing water reflecting nothing because the light is too dim.

The air is cool and close. It smells like wet chalk, like clean mortar, like the inside of a well. Condensation hangs as a fine mist that never quite precipitates or dissipates, suspended in the stillness. You can taste the mineral on your tongue. The temperature sits in that narrow band between comfortable and cold, the kind of cool that settles into your joints if you stand still too long.

The sound is immense.

Not mechanical, not chaotic—but *layered*. The weeping walls produce a soft, irregular patter, different on every surface because the stone was cut from different beds and sweats at different rates. The pooling water slaps against itself when a droplet lands. The runoff murmurs along the grout lines, finding its paths. And beneath all of this, a deep, resonant hum—not from water, not from wind, but from the structure itself, as though the hollow were a vessel for some slow vibration traveling up through its foundations. The sound fills the space completely. You feel it in your chest more than hear it with your ears. It is the sound of a room that has been listening to itself for a very long time.

The erosion is finished. This place has reached its equilibrium. The mortar joints are rounded, their original sharpness long dissolved by the slow passage of water. The stone faces are smooth in the way that only centuries of patient wetness can make them—not worn by friction or wind but *softened*, as if the material itself had relaxed into its final shape. The edges have surrendered. The corners breathe. Nothing here is crumbling; everything has simply settled into the form it was always going to become. The water and the stone have reached their agreement.

There is almost nothing here. The space is hollow in the way a cistern is hollow, in the way a bell is hollow. The room is a single volume, undivided by columns or furniture or purpose. A few stone shelves protrude from the far wall—shallow, unsupported, seeming to grow from the masonry itself—and on one of them sits a clay cup, rim-down, its surface gone soft with moisture the same as everything else. That is the only object. The emptiness is architectural. It is the point.

And yet the place is full of life.

They move along the walls: pale, legged things, the color of wet limestone. Some are as small as your thumbnail, some as long as your finger. They are not insects—they have too many joints, their bodies are too soft, and they move with a deliberation that suggests calculation rather than instinct. They drink from the weeping stone. You can watch them find a swelling droplet and wait, pressing their mouths to the surface, taking the water as it emerges. When disturbed—by a larger movement, by the displacement of air—they scatter, but slowly, and only to the next wet patch. They are not afraid. They belong here more than you do.

Others move in the water itself. Translucent, veined creatures like flattened leeches but with a symmetry too precise to be ugly, gliding across the flooded flagstones with an undulation that seems to pull the water rather than push against it. They leave no trail. And in the deepest pool, where the floor dips and the standing water thickens to two inches, something larger moves—你 can see the displacement, the slow swirl, but the dimness hides the shape. It surfaces once: a wet back, pale as bone, broad as both your hands. Then it sinks. The water settles.

The light comes from nowhere you can identify. It is dim and sourceless, a grayish luminescence that seems to emanate from the water itself, or from the stone, or from the humid air. It is enough to see by but not enough to see far. The far wall is suggestion. The ceiling is memory. The corners do not exist. The light does not flicker or change; it is the same now as it was an hour ago, as it will be an hour from now. It has no cycle. It simply *is*, the way the dampness simply is, the way the sound simply is.

The strangeness is in the completeness. This place was made—quarried, fitted, mortared, sealed—and then given over to water and time until it became something the builders did not intend. Or perhaps they did. Perhaps this was always the plan: a room that sweats, a room that fills, a room where life comes to drink. The primitive tech of its construction—hand-cut stone, lime mortar, clay vessels—suggests a purpose older than industry, older than efficiency. This is a place that does something by *being*. The water cycle within it is closed: the walls sweat, the pools fill, the creatures drink, and somewhere—perhaps through the same invisible source as the light—the water returns to the stone. The system is in balance. It has been in balance for longer than anyone remembers.

The fauna do not leave. You suspect they cannot. This hollow is their world entire—the wet walls their forest, the standing water their sea, the dim light their sun. They have adapted to its rhythms completely. When a droplet falls, the wall-drinkers do not flinch; they track it with their eyes and return to their feeding. When the larger shape moves in the deep pool, the flat gliders do not flee; they adjust their positions by small degrees, giving space but not showing alarm. The predator-prey dynamic exists—you can see the evidence in the missing legs, the scarred backs—but it proceeds at a pace matching the room itself: slow, inevitable, almost ceremonial.

You are the anomaly here. Your warmth disrupts the cool. Your footsteps send waves across the mirrored floor that the swimmers must navigate. Your breath fogs in air that had found its equilibrium without you. You are inside a system that does not need you, that was complete before you arrived and will be complete after you leave.

And still, the walls weep. And still, the water pools. And still, in the deep corner where the light barely reaches, something moves.
