The air here does not thirst; it simply has no concept of water. This is a hygroscopic void, a world where liquid is a geological memory, locked eons ago into crystalline lattices and sublimated out of existence. Yet, you are drowning in green.

This is the Canopy Substrate—the final, oldest expression of a dry-world botanical empire. At an elevation that would leave conventional atmospheres gasping and thin, the ground itself has been subsumed. There is no soil, no rock, no mineral grain exposed to the dim air. Beneath you is a continuous, ancient rhizome, a chthonic wood-mass that has spent millions of years calcifying into a substrate as hard as basalt. Erosion has done its ultimate work here. The mountains that once stood have been entirely devoured and replaced; the landscape has settled into its primordial, flattened final form—a high, endless plateau of living wood, worn smooth by time into a gentle, regional roll of undulating burls and root-ridges. 

The light is a permanent, crepuscular gloom. A distant, dying dwarf star—or perhaps a filtered orbital filter—feeds this world, casting everything in a dim, amber-bruised twilight. The sky is a dusty, anhydrous violet, utterly devoid of clouds. But the space below is filled. 

Vegetation is not a feature of this landscape; vegetation *is* the landscape. Towering xerophytic columns—fungal-baobab hybrids with bark like overlapping plates of lacquered iron—rise from the rhizome-sea, branching into a ceiling of spathes and fronds. These structures are sparse in distribution, allowing vast, echoing distances of open floor between them, yet their canopies interlock high above, weaving the sky into a grate. It is a city, but not built by hands. It is *grown* urbanism. The spaced trunks act as load-bearing pillars; the overarching arches are infrastructure; the deep, hollow knots in the rhizome are habitations. Streets wind through the roots, not paved, but worn into the living lignin by the passage of generations. It is a metropolis of botanical architecture, organized, layered, and endlessly recursive.

And it hums with deafening sound. Without water to muffle or wind to carry it away, the acoustic landscape is oppressive, a low-frequency roar that vibrates the marrow. The source is technological and alien. The tech here is post-physical, operating on principles current physics can only describe as quantum-biological interference. Embedded within the heartwood of every major column are graviton-weave maceration engines—fractal structures of carbon-lattice and photonic sap that manipulate localized gravity fields. They pull ambient trace gases from the void, compressing them into plasma-hot nodules that feed the roots. The thunderous, rhythmic boom of these engines cycling is the city's heartbeat, a subsonic pressure that fogs the mind and shakes the teeth. 

Through this loud, dim metropolis moves an abundant ecology of dry-life fauna. They are crystalline and chitinous, evolved to scrape moisture from the very air—if one could call it air—or to harvest the photonic sap weeping from the tech-implanted boles. Herds of Striders move between the pillars—hexapedal grazers with legs of segmented silicate and bodies draped in filtration-cloth, a mesh of living keratin that catches airborne nutrients. Above them, in the gloom of the structural canopy, glide Razor-Kites, predators with leading edges of volcanic glass, descending with a sound like shattering mirrors to cleave their prey. The ecosystem churns with violent pace; predator and prey clash in the open plazas of the wood-city, their shells cracking and scattering across the rhizome, where detritivorous beetles the size of fists will dismantle the corpses in hours, recycling the calcium back into the floor.

This world is deeply, structurally strange. Space behaves differently here under the influence of the maceration engines. Distances warp between the sparse trunks; a pillar that appears a mile away might take three steps to reach if the localized gravity well is folding space inward. Rain does not fall, but occasionally the air will detonate with a dry-lightning flash of sublimated gas, briefly illuminating the urban forest in stark, ultraviolet shadows—the blackened blood-vessels of an ancient, root-bound world. 

It is an active, breathing machine of a place. The flora twitches, not in any breeze, but driven by the neuro-chemical commands of the city-mind. Load-bearing pillars slowly rotate, centimeters a century, shifting the stress of the overarching streets. Root-plazas bulge upward as new growth pushes from below, swallowing the old infrastructure in slow, grinding tectonics of wood. You stand in a high, dry cathedral of vegetable iron and cosmic gravity, deafened by the thrum of a botched physics, surrounded by a thriving, violent multitude of glass and shell, walking on the skin of an organism that ate the world and built a city from its own bones.
