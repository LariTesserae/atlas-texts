To stand here is to stand inside a paradox made real—a vast, high-altitude greenhouse floating in a permanent twilight, where the boundary between the grown and the built dissolved eons ago. There is no ground beneath your feet, only a continuous architecture of living wood, and the space stretches so impossibly far that the horizon curves away into a dim, vapor-choked void.

This is the Canopy Engine, though no one built it, and no one remembers its purpose. It is a continent-spanning lattice of hyper-sequenced botanical structures. Everything is made. There is no stone, no soil, no metal—only plant matter that has been calcified, woven, braided, and hollowed into load-bearing columns, suspension cables, and vast, tiered platforms. The technology here is pre-metal, yet it achieves planetary scale: the inhabitants—or rather, the inhabitants who remain—understand only the hand-tool, the grafting knife, the spindle. They have shaped a world by bending meristems and tying lashings of living bark, pruning the geometry of reality itself until it grew into the shape of a continent.

The elevation is extreme, though you cannot see the earth below. You stand atop strata of interlocked trunk-cathedrals, miles above the surface of a planet that may no longer exist. The air is thin and warm, heated by the slow metabolic burn of the vegetative mass beneath you, carrying the heavy, fermented scent of stagnant sap and decaying cellulose. Water is everywhere, yet it never falls from the sky. It weeps. The atmosphere is super-saturated, condensing constantly against the cool undersides of the mega-flora. Water beads on the undersides of leaves the size of city blocks, accumulating until the droplets—fat, sluggish, mineral-heavy—detach and fall into the catch-basins formed by the root-crowns of the layer below. The sound of this place is the sound of that weeping: a near-silent, rhythmic dripping, so quiet it registers more as a pulse than a noise. The dominant acoustic is a thick, wet silence, broken only by the occasional groan of a petiole adjusting its angle, or the papery rustle of a million moss-spore pods desiccating in the warm, still air.

The light is perpetually dim. The sun—if there is one—is a forgotten memory, blotted out by the sheer thickness of the constructed biomass overhead. Light filters down through a hundred translucent layers of chlorophyll and lignin, arriving as a bruised, yellowish-green glow that casts no shadows, only varying densities of murk. In this penumbra, distance becomes unreliable. A mile away, a colossal flower-bell the size of a valley might seem only a stone's throw distant, its spiraled petals glowing faintly with bioluminescent foxfire.

Despite the totality of the construction, the space feels crushingly empty. The density here is near-void. Walk for days across the woven-bark causeways and you will encounter nothing but the occasional empty husk of a seed-pod large enough to house a family. The architecture is all there is. It fills the volume of the world, but the spaces between the load-bearing vines and the hollow root-tubes are immense—cavernous gaps where warm mist hangs undisturbed. You are a mote walking along the edge of a void, suspended in a lattice of living mahogany and calcified reed.

Yet life thrives here. The fauna is abundant, though it, too, has been absorbed by the architecture. There are no predators with bones; instead, there are arthropodal grazers the size of oxen, their carapaces perfectly camouflaged against the bark they scrape. Swarms of pale, eyeless homopterans drink the weeping water, their bodies like tiny, transparent vessels filling and emptying as they feed. Fungal colonies bloom and wither in the wet crevices, acting as the world's only scavengers. The ecosystem is in constant, quiet motion—a slow, biological churning of sap and spore, predator and prey, all occurring on a timescale so dilated it feels like watching erosion in real-time. 

And here, at the edge of the great empty spaces, reality begins to fray. The weirdness is not a feature of the landscape; it is the landscape's current illness. The world-engine has been running for too long without a gardener. At the periphery of your vision, the lashings that hold the world together—those ancient, hand-tied knots of living vine—occasionally slip into impossible topologies. A banyan buttress will loop seamlessly into its own shadow; a waterfall of weeping water will pause in mid-air, its droplets hovering like glass beads before continuing their fall. Sometimes, the geometry of the platforms simply stops making sense, extending into non-Euclidean angles that the eye refuses to follow. There are places where the flora has grown exhausted by the logic of physical space and has begun to grow *elsewhere*—roots reaching into flat planes of color, leaves unfurling into pure, odorless mist.

It is a place of immense age and erosion, where the forces of weather and time have long since balanced the growth and decay of the mega-structure. It is in its own state, settled and weathered, a vast machine running on chlorophyll and patience. But it is a machine whose operational parameters are slowly, quietly going mad in the dark.
