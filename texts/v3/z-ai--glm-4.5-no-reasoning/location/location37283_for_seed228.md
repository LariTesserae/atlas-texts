The world here is subterranean, yet it does not feel like a cave. You are perhaps thirty meters below what was once the surface, standing on the floor of a vast, cathedral-scale grotto. The ceiling is lost to a brilliant, diffuse glare. This is not the dim underworld of geological tradition; the space is intensely, aggressively bright. The light originates from the stone itself—a network of bioluminescent and piezoelectric veins riddling the ceiling and upper walls, pulsing with a cold, white-gold radiance that casts no shadows, only a pervasive, clinical glare that makes the air seem to vibrate.

The atmosphere is warm and sodden, pressing against the skin like a heated towel. Water is everywhere, though not in pools or streams. It hangs in the air, a supersaturated fog that condenses on every surface in fat, quivering drops. The walls—the black, slick basalt that arches overhead—are constantly weeping. Rivulets trace down the stone, not in random paths, but following etched channels: the partial remains of a built environment. There are arches carved into the rock, their edges worn round by millennia of patient water. There are faint grooves in the floor, the ghostly footprints of a civilization that left only hints—shards of a ceramic-like substance that rings like glass when struck, scattered across the ground like white seeds in the dark soil. The structures are partial, single walls disappearing into the stone, a lone column holding up nothing but the wet glare.

But the water does not only fall. It climbs. 

The sparse vegetation is a direct consequence of the violent dynamics of this place. There are no trees, no ferns, no mossy carpets. Instead, there are the Weepers: isolated, pale stalks that grip the fissures in the basalt. They are fleshy, almost translucent, standing alone with meters of empty, echoing space between them. They survive the turbulence by being entirely elastic. As the subterranean atmosphere churns—pressurized steam vents opening and closing at random, thermal shears tearing through the grotto—the Weepers are thrown into a chaotic, thrashing dance. They whip back and forth, their bulbous tips shedding sprays of condensed water.

This constant, hyperactive thrashing is the source of the sound. It is loud here, overwhelmingly so. The grotto roars with a chaotic polyrhythm: the deep, subsonic boom of shifting rock masses far below, the high-pitched hiss of superheated venting, and the staccato *crack* of the Weepers' stalks snapping like wet towels in the wind. It is an anthill of kinetic energy, a bazaar of colliding pressure fronts, a storm-state trapped in stone. You have to brace your body against the air just to stand still.

And then, there is the technology.

It does not look like machines. It looks like the weather. Woven into the water cycle, into the condensation and the steam, is something engineered—something that operates on physics that do not quite square with the thermodynamics we know. Sometimes, the falling drops of water hesitate an inch from the floor, hovering, vibrating, before snapping together into larger, unnatural geometries: perfect tetrahedrons of water that roll away across the stone, defying gravity, before shattering back into mist. Sometimes, the rising steam forms brief, fractal lattices that hold their shape for three seconds, conducting the ambient light into blazing, floating topologies before collapsing. 

These are the artifacts of a post-modern tech, operating at the nanoscale or perhaps manipulating the strong nuclear force directly. It is technology that has become so miniaturized, so integrated into the matter of the grotto, that it has become indistinguishable from the weather. The carved arches and scattered ceramics were just the housing, the crude macro-scaffolding for the real machinery, which has outlasted its creators by epochs. The machinery is the water. The machinery is the light. It is actively managing the environment, maintaining the grotto in a state of perpetual, turbulent equilibrium—creating and destroying the hovering droplets in an endless, loud, Solaris-like computation just beyond the edge of comprehension.

Fauna is rare, but not absent. There is the Glass Eel—or something that behaves like one. It is a single, solitary creature observed slipping through the chaotic air currents, its body a flexible, three-meter-long tube of transparent silicate, moving with the jerking, mechanical precision of an escaped component rather than an animal. It feeds on the hovering water tetrahedrons, intercepting them before they break, absorbing whatever data or energy the ancient systems have packed into their brief, structured lives. It vanishes into the glare, leaving only the churned wake of its passage.

You stand in the center of this room-sized cataclysm, this bright, wet engine of a world. Water streaks your face, the heat prickles your skin, and the sound rattles your chest. The sparse, pale Weepers thrash in their solitary arcs. The light pulses, steady and bright, unbothered by the chaos it illuminates. It is a landscape in the grip of its own making—ancient, weathered into its current form, yet violently alive, running a program written in steam and stone at the bottom of the world.
