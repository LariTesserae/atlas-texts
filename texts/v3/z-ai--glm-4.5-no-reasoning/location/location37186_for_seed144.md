The altitude strips the air thin. Each breath pulled at this elevation feels like drinking through cloth—present, sufficient, but never satisfying. You stand on a vast, tilted shoulder of rust-colored rock, an immense high plateau that rises far above any reasonable contour line. The scale of the place asserts itself not through dramatic peaks, but through a relentless, rolling horizontal expanse that stretches outward until the heat haze blurs the boundaries of the world. It is a country of extreme elevation and extreme heat, a combination that feels like an oversight in the design of the planet. The sun sits directly overhead, bleaching the terrain to a blinding pale umber, casting shadows so sharp they look cut from black paper.

Despite the vastness, there is no emptiness here. The landscape is packed, saturated with a density that defies the sparse green of its flora. What grows here is minimal—stunted, silver-leaved shrubs with taproots like iron, and occasional fissure-weed that bleeds a milky sap when snapped. The vegetation clings to the rock in isolated, stubborn tufts, claiming barely a fraction of the surface. Yet the space between these plants is far from vacant. It is occupied by a sprawling, interlocking ecosystem of chitin and carapace. The fauna here is abundant, overflowing, a crawling, clicking biomass that makes the stone feel like the underside of a leaf. Amber-shelled beetles the size of thumbnails swarm the cracks; leggy, dust-colored isopods roll in platoons along the thermal gradients; and hard-shelled, eyeless skinks hunt them in stuttering bursts of motion. When you look down, the ground is not still. It shifts and breathes with the microscopic chaos of a million articulated legs.

Moisture in this furnace is an act of contradiction. The place is damp, but not in the sense of rot or soft earth. Here, water is a geological anomaly, a prisoner of the heat. Deep within the endless lattice of the rock, subterranean aquifers weep. Water pushes upward under extreme pressure, only to flash into steam the moment it reaches the open air. Across the plateau, venting fissures hiss—though the sound is muffled by the thin air into a low, continuous exhalation. Around these fumaroles, the moisture condenses instantly on the surrounding stone, painting the rock faces with slick, dark rings of condensation. It is a landscape of localized humidity, where the air inches away from a vent feels like a wet sauna, while ten paces away, the air is parched and desiccating. The isopods and the silver shrubs cluster exclusively around these damp rings, fighting for the narrow margin of survival the condensation provides.

Amidst this teeming, natural exhaustion, the remnants of industry protrude like fossilized bones. The built environment is scattered—no city, no compound, just isolated nodes of obsolete machinery anchored to the plateau. A solitary smelter stack, fifty feet tall and choked with ancient, vitrified ash, stands over a dry catchment basin. Half-buried pistons jut from the rock at odd angles, their housings rotted into delicate, oxidized lacework. These are not shelters; they are the artifacts of an industrial process that was scaled to the region, engines and mechanized presses that once hammered the wealth from the deep rock. Now, they are claimed by the dense ecology. The beetles burrow into the insulation of the dead generators; the skinks bask on the sun-baked iron plates. The technology is strictly of the old industrial age—greasy, heavy, dependent on boilers and levers—but it is entirely offline, functioning now only as monumental scrap.

The erosion of the plateau tells the story of its balance. The wind has had millennia to carve the rock, and it has done so with mathematical patience. The stone is grooved into fluted columns and scalloped hollows, an established, settled form that no longer fights the elements. The occasional rain—sudden, torrential, and briefly violent—sweeps across the open expanse, but the rock is too hard and too porous to carve anew. The water vanishes into the aquifers almost instantly, leaving only the steam and the dark rings of condensation. 

Because the air is so thin, the sound of the place is largely swallowed. The acoustic environment is mostly quiet, dominated by the bass rumble of the deep-earth venting and the arrhythmic tick of carapace against stone. When a storm cell briefly blocks the sun, dropping a curtain of rain that turns to steam before it hits the ground, the silence grows even deeper, more anticipatory. The weirdness of the plateau settles into your perception slowly. It is the wrongness of the ratios: too high, too hot, too many bugs, too little green. It is the uncanny sensation of standing on a world that has been heavily engineered by an absent industry and subsequently overpopulated by a biome that should not have the carrying capacity to support it. It is a landscape of absolute saturation in the margins, where a broken engine and a weeping fissure and a million clicking beetles all occupy the exact same square inch of burning, breathless stone.
