You descend. The stairs—cast iron, flaked with rust and overgrown with a velvet of dark moss—spiral downward into a breath that is already breathing for you, humid and warm, carrying the iron-and-yeast scent of a place that has never seen the sun. You are going below the surface, into the root-civic layer, and the light fades until your eyes are just organs of pressure and guess.

This is a grove-room. A single hollow in the earth, perhaps the size of a cathedral nave, perhaps a little less—bounded by walls of packed loam and the blind, reaching fingers of taproots from some surface forest far above. But "room" is a word for dead architecture. Here, the space is a lung. The walls breathe. The floor gives slightly underfoot, not with the squelch of mud but with the turgid spring of living wood. The substrate itself is plant—choked, woven, laminated layers of root and rhizome and vining stem, so dense that the ground you walk on is merely the topmost skin of a vegetable mass that goes down and down, its own geology.

The built elements emerge from this green substrate like fossils found in reverse. Brick arches—Victorian, soot-stained—protrude from the living walls at intervals, their mortar long since digested and replaced by lignin, their cracks seeded with generations of shelf fungus. Iron pipes run in grids overhead, some still clicking with the ghost pressurizations of old steam, others burst and colonized: thick vines have made conduits of them, growing through the iron like blood through a vein, until the metal is just a flaking shell around a core of pale, wet wood. There is a sense of town here, of organized construction—streets that were once streets, a grid that once meant something—but the infrastructure has been subsumed. The civilization didn't fall. It was *gardened* into something else.

The dimness is profound but not absolute. Far above, through the lattice of roots, a bruised light filters down—stormlight from a surface you cannot see, or perhaps the bioluminescence of fungal colonies on the ceiling, a cold blue-green that pulses without rhythm. More reliable are the working lights: glass-blown industrial lamps on the pipes, wired with copper that someone maintains, burning a low amber that catches on the constant moisture. The air holds water in every possible state—vapor, droplet, film. Condensation weeps from every surface. It rains indoors, softly and locally, from the undersides of pipes. Pools collect in the hollows where roots intersect, black and still, smelling of tannin and machine oil in equal measure.

And then there is the sound. It never stops. A low, mechanical throb—there is an engine somewhere deeper, a generator or pump, churning on whatever fuel this place has found (biogas? seed oil? something grown for the burning?)—provides a cardiac rhythm beneath everything. On top of that: the creak of wood under strain, the drip and patter of internal rain, the hiss of steam escaping from fittings, the wet click of fungal bodies releasing spores. And layered over all of this, loud and constant and impossible to ignore, is the sound of life. Chittering. Buzzing. The rustle of chitin on bark. The throaty, clicking calls of things communicating in the dark.

Because this place is *theirs*.

The fauna have inherited the town. Wherever you look—once your eyes adjust to the murk—you see movement. Cockroaches the size of your thumb patrol the pipes in battalions, their glossy bodies catching the lamp-light like living jewelry. Pale millipedes, legs in the hundreds, flow over the root-walls like water finding its level. Above, in the vine-thick ceiling, something larger moves—crabs? Spiders?—their segmented legs tapping the iron as they navigate the canopy. You hear the flutter of winged insects too large to be anything but predators, and occasionally, in the pools, the dimple of something surface-feeding. The ecology here is complete and self-sustaining, a food web that doesn't need you. You are not its apex. You are not even its prey, yet. You are just *present*, and the life around you proceeds with the indifference of weather.

The strangeness is in the integration. In a forest, this density of life would be natural. In a factory, it would be infestation. Here, it is *infrastructure*. The insects pollinate the fungal crops that grow along the pipe-skins. The larger arthropods prune the vines, keeping the walkways clear—a function that was once performed by municipal workers, and is now performed by instinct. The root-walls have grown to accommodate the engine's vibration, hollowing out acoustic chambers that amplify or muffle the sound as needed, as if the plant life has *acoustically engineered* its own environment. There are moments when you cannot tell what is machine and what is biology. A valve that opens and closes on a timer—or is it the regular unfurling of a carnivorous bloom? A cable that carries current—or is it a nerve, thick and myelinated, carrying signal to some central node you haven't found?

This is a place that has settled into itself. The erosion parameter is high, but not the erosion of decay—the erosion of *agreement*. The iron rusts, and the rust feeds the fungus, and the fungus fixes nitrogen for the roots, and the roots crack the brick, and the crack draws water, and the water powers the steam, and the steam runs the engine that pumps the water. It is a system in balance, weathered into its final form. It has been here for a long time. It will be here longer.

You stand in the dimness and feel the place move around you—not threateningly, but with the busy, multiple-processor motion of an ecosystem at full operation. Something drops onto your shoulder. You brush it off without looking; it is already gone. The engine throbs. The rain falls indoors. A clicking call echoes from a brick archway that leads further down, into a room you cannot see, where the density of life is even greater and the light does not reach at all.

You are in a town that has become a gut. And it is digesting the world above it, slowly, root by root and pipe by pipe, pulling the surface down into this warm, loud, living dark.
