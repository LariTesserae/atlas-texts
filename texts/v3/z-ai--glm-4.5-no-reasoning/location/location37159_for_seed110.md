You descend into a crevice near the summit, where the mountain's hot breath meets the cold sky. Down here, in the guts of the peak, there is no daylight. The passage narrows, then opens—not into a cave, but into a pocket. A room-sized hollow in the living rock, sealed above by a shelf of stone that lets no sun in, rimmed below by a sump of dark water. You could stretch your arms and touch both walls. This is the whole world that matters here: a single contained chamber, intimate as a throat.

The stone is new. Not the familiar ancient stuff of mountains, striated and worn, but something rawer—rock that hasn't yet decided its shape. The walls bulge and retreat without logic, their surfaces unfinished, matte, mineral. You press your palm against the wall and feel warmth seep into your skin. The whole chamber is warm, held at the temperature of a living body by something deeper below. The rock has the texture of dried clay before firing, or skin that has never seen weather. It yields slightly under your thumbnail. In geological terms, this place is embryonic: a void that has only just been hollowed, or is still hollowing, into being.

And the water. It is everywhere, but it does not flow. It weeps. The walls gleam with a perpetual film of moisture that has nowhere to go. In the lower hollows, the water gathers into a black pool that fills the floor of the chamber, still as held breath. You cannot see its bottom. The surface tension is thick enough that small things could rest on it without breaking through. Overhead, condensation beads on the stone shelf and falls in single, deliberate drops—each one a quiet event, arriving with a plastic *plink* into the pool, sending out perfect rings that overlap and cancel into stillness again. The sound is almost nothing. The silence between drops is the real sound: a dense, humid quiet that sits on your ears like wool.

Despite the dark and the water, things live here. Not much, but enough to fill the space. The walls are furred with a dense, short pile of pale growth—not quite moss, not quite fungus, but something between the two, a kingdom that hasn't settled on its rules. It grows in bands according to some gradient you can't read: thicker near the waterline, patchy higher up, absent near the ceiling where the warm exhalation of the rock is strongest. The growth is almost white, luminous in a way that has nothing to do with light—it seems to hold a faint pale glow of its own, or perhaps only to be so white that even the darkness can't fully swallow it. When you reach toward it, the filaments lean away, then settle back, as if breathing.

In the pool, there are slow presences. You feel them more than see them—small displacements in the black water, gentle vortices that appear and dissolve. Once, something surfaces: a pale shape like a knuckle, smooth and blind, rolling once before sinking again. It is not alarmed. Nothing here is alarmed. There is no predator, no prey. The few creatures that exist in this pocket have no need of urgency; they move at the speed of water seeping through stone, which is to say, they barely move at all. A flat, eyeless thing clings to the wall near the waterline, its body the exact texture and color of the pale growth. You only notice it because it is slightly too regular, too symmetrical, to be vegetable. It has been there longer than you have been looking. It may have been there forever.

Against one wall, half-submerged, there is a mark of human passage. A structure is too generous a word. Someone has stacked stones—small ones, fist-sized, pale and water-worn—into a low column that rises just above the waterline. Five stones, perhaps six; it is hard to tell in the dark. They are balanced with a rudimentary care that suggests patience rather than engineering. An offering, or a marker, or simply something to do with one's hands in a place where time does not move. The stones are warm from the water, smooth as teeth. Whoever made this cairn worked with their hands alone. There is no cut, no tool-mark, no metal trace. Just the simple act of placing one stone on another, repeated until it felt like enough.

Nothing changes. This is the place's deepest property, deeper than the warmth or the water or the dark. The drops fall from the ceiling into the pool at intervals that seem random but may be cyclical on a scale too long to measure. The pale growth on the walls neither advances nor retreats. The creatures in the water rise and sink without pattern or purpose. The cairn stands. The warmth persists. There is no weather here, no season, no gradient between one moment and the next. The chamber holds itself in a steady state, a pocket of equilibrium hidden in the mountain's mass. It was like this before you entered. It will be like this after you leave. 

You crouch at the water's edge. The darkness is so complete that you can see nothing beyond the faint pale fuzz on the nearest wall, but the chamber is full—you feel it press in on you, dense with warmth and moisture and the proximity of unseen walls. The air is thick, saturated, barely breathable. You dip your fingers in the pool. The water is warm and soft, mineral, almost gelid. It clings to your skin. A drop falls from above and strikes the surface near your hand, and the quiet sound rings out and dies, and the stillness returns, and the chamber goes on being itself, unaltered, in the dark.
