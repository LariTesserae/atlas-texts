The world below is a warm, breathless dark. To descend into it is to enter a subterranean continental shelf—a vast, sub-surface basin that stretches beyond the horizon in every direction, so immense it generates its own stagnant weather, though that weather never moves. There is no water here. Not a droplet of moisture hangs in the air; the void is absolute, a desiccation so complete that it numbs the skin on contact. Yet, it is not cold. A deep, geothermal warmth saturates the space, radiating upward from the unseen floors below, a steady 2.1 degrees of ambient heat that makes the darkness feel close and velvet-heavy.

This is a young place. The formation is fresh, the topography sharp and unweathered. You stand on a sprawling plain of interlocking mineral plates—vitreous, obsidian-sharp shelves of volcanic glass and crystallized salts that have pushed up from the mantle in recent tectonic spasms. The edges of these plates are freshly cleaved, dangerous to the touch, catching what little light exists in razor-thin glints. The density here is staggering; the space is packed. Above, below, and all around, the environment is layered with these overlapping mineral strata, creating a labyrinth of near-touching surfaces. There is no sky, only the underside of the next continental shelf hanging a few dozen meters overhead, dripping with crystalline drips that never fall, frozen in the thermal equilibrium. 

It is deeply, profoundly dark. The only illumination comes from the faint, bioluminescent smear of the fauna, and occasionally, from the scattered artifacts of whoever—whatever—came here before. The built environment is minimal, just faint marks on the stone. A solitary cairn of stacked glass slabs. A shallow groove carved into the salt-flat, worn smooth by hand-tools of primitive making. There are no metals here, no machinery, only the signs of early, desperate craft: sharp rocks tied to heavy crystals, the remains of simple fabrication left to sit in the eternal warmth. 

And yet, this dead, dry world is teeming. The fauna is abundant, a riot of life that defies aqueous chemistry. In the darkness, the mineral world breathes. The shelves of salt and glass are covered in a dense, writhing ecology of chitinous, silicate-based creatures. They are limb-heavy, slow things—carbon-silicon hybrids that feed directly on the mineral substrata, grinding down the fresh glass with mouthparts of industrial diamond. There are vast, flat grazers that cling to the vertical walls, and towering, multi-legged amalgams of quarts and keratin that step carefully over the razor-sharp crevices. They do not drink. Their internal chemistry is non-aqueous, running on slow thermal reactions and the direct metabolization of trace metals. They are warm to the touch, and they glow—some with a deep, arterial red, others with a sickly, mineral green, lighting up the dense, overlapping layers of the under-world like a slow-motion constellation.

It is nearly silent. The sheer mass of the creatures, the millions of tons of mineral life grazing on the fresh stone, makes almost no sound. There is no wind to carry noise, no water to rush or drip. Every few minutes, the silence is broken by a single, resonant *crack*—the sound of a glass shelf fracturing under the weight of a massive grazer—and then the quiet returns, swallowing the echo instantly. 

The weirdness of the place manifests in its stillness. This is a world in frozen equilibrium. Nothing changes. The warmth does not fluctuate. The darkness does not lift. The creatures do not migrate or hunt; they simply exist, grinding their jaws, stepping in ancient, pre-determined paths, their populations perfectly saturated into the available space. The erosion is so young that the footprints of the fauna remain sharp and un-blurred in the salt, lasting for what seems like forever, because there is no wind or water to wipe them away. Time does not erode this place; it merely accumulates, layered in the dense, hot dark, a subterranean continent of living glass, grinding quietly into the void.
