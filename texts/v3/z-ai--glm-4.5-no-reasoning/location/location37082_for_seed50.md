You are inside the throat of a newborn mountain.

This is not a metaphor. You are physically contained within a vertical passage of raw, wet basalt—a chimney or a bore, barely four meters across, that punches upward through rock that has not yet learned to be still. The walls are close enough to touch on both sides simultaneously. The stone is dark, almost black, shot through with veins of cooler gray where mineral deposits are still separating themselves from the magma-parent they rose from. It is pre-formation geology: the stone has not yet settled into its eventual strata, its eventual hardness. It is warm but not hot—something like a sun-heated boulder at dusk, 12 or 13 degrees Celsius, that peculiar temperature of things just emerging from the earth's interior into the open air.

And it is wet. Profoundly, overwhelmingly wet. 

Water doesn't merely exist here; it is the medium, the condition, the dominant fact. A column of cold seawater—oceanic, dense, 4 degrees at its source—has found this chimney from above and is pouring down through it in a continuous cylindrical sheet. But the water is also rising. Superheated fluid, mineral-laden and sulfurous, blasts upward from fissures in the chimney's floor, maybe forty meters below where you exist. The two flows—the cold descending and the hot ascending—do not mix peacefully. They shear against each other in endless turbulent spirals, creating a double-helix of opposing currents that fills the entire passage. The water is the storm. It is a liquid hurricane contained within a stone tube, and it is deafening.

The sound is unbelievable. Not the rhythmic crash of surf, not the white noise of rainfall—something more mechanical and more organic at once. It is the sound of two oceans fighting each other at close range: a sustained, floor-vibrating roar that breaks into higher-frequency shrieks where the shear zones collapse into vortices. The walls themselves add to it. The basalt is riddled with pockets and blowholes that have not yet worn smooth, and as the turbulent water is forced through them, they whistle, groan, and pop—hundreds of crude stone organs playing at once, all out of tune. You feel the sound in your chest cavity more than you hear it with your ears. It is a soundscape that denies thought, denies language. You simply exist within it.

And yet there is light. This is the strangest gift of the place. The chimney—this nascent vent, this raw wound in the ocean floor—does not open into darkness. Far above, perhaps three hundred meters up, it breaches the surface of a cold sea under a wide, overcast sky. Diffuse daylight, gray and steady, filters down through the immense column of water. The descending cold flow carries this light with it, and the rising hot flow—thick with suspended minerals—catches and refracts it. The entire passage shimmers. It is like being inside a kaleidoscope made of water and shadow. Bands of brighter gray sweep past as the turbulent eddies align; then the water goes dark as mineral opacity swirls in. There is enough light to see the walls, to see your own hands, to watch the bubbles rise—but it is light in constant, nauseating motion. Nothing visual ever settles.

The walls, when you can focus on them, are almost bare. This chimney is too young, too volatile, too chemically extreme for the usual colonization. There are no tubeworms, no bacterial mats, no pale crabs picking their way across the stone. The sparse life that exists here is almost an insult to the place's severity—a kind of defiant joke. In the shear zone, where the hot and cold flows intermix into something briefly tolerable (briefly—never for more than a few seconds at any given point), there are things. You notice them as flickers of unexpected geometry: tiny, chitinous bodies, no larger than a fingernail, that have evolved to ride the turbulence rather than resist it. They are something between shrimp and insect, something that should not exist at this pressure and this chemical assault, but they do. They cluster in the irregularities of the wall, in the blowholes and crevices, riding out the worst pulses, then launch themselves into the shear to feed on whatever organic particles the rising vent fluid carries. They are gone almost as soon as you see them—blurred into the water, indistinguishable from the mineral particulate. They might be a hallucination. They might be the only real thing here.

What you can see, more reliably, are the marks. The built environment, such as it is. Scattered, ambiguous, impossible to date. Near the wall, at the level where the shear zone is widest and the turbulence briefly relieves, someone has hammered shallow holes into the basalt—handholds, or perhaps anchor points for something that is no longer present. The work is crude. Stone struck against stone, nothing more. No metal tools made these marks; the edges are concoidal, fractured rather than cut. But they are deliberate. Someone came here, into this wet deafening bore, and decided this particular height, this particular band of slightly-less-lethal water, was worth claiming. 

There is other evidence. A single bar of something—bone, maybe, or dense mineral-cured wood—juts from a crack in the wall, wedged at an angle that suggests it was driven there on purpose. It is worn smooth by the water now, bleached by the mineral chemistry, but it was shaped before it was placed. A tool-handle? A structural pin? A ritual marker? The void of context is total. And higher up, barely visible in the brighter turbulence, there seems to be a shelf cut into the stone—a flattening of the natural bore that is too regular to be geological. A ledge. A place to exist, briefly, in a place that refuses existence.

Who would build here? Who would descend into a newborn vent chimney in the deep ocean and hammer handholds into the raw basalt? The question acquires a strange urgency as the water slams you against the wall and the sound presses the shape of your thoughts into something very simple and very afraid. The place is under construction. Not merely the chimney itself—the geology, the emergence—but the *habitation*. Someone is trying to make this impossible space into a room. They have not succeeded. The turbulence is too great, the chemistry too hostile, the stone too unwilling to hold a shape. The handholds erode even as you look at them; the mineral-rich hot water is dissolving the fractured edges back toward smoothness. The shelf is collapsing. The bone-bar will fall within the year, within the month, within the hour—time moves strangely when geology is happening at the speed of weather.

But they are trying. That is the strange, almost moving fact of this place. In the deafening chaos, in the liquid hurricane, in the pre-formation void where even the stone has not decided what it will be—someone has recognized a pocket of near-habitable space and begun the primitive, hopeless work of claiming it. 

The weirdness of the chimney is not in its chemistry or its violence. It is in this: it is a room that is becoming, and the becoming is visible. The walls are still deciding their mineralogy. The water is still deciding its flow. The life is still deciding its form. The handholds are still deciding whether to be features or to be erosion. You are present for the moment of indeterminacy—when a place exists but has not yet resolved into what it will be. It is intimate and vast at once: the passage is barely wider than your shoulders, but it opens downward into geological depth and upward through three hundred meters of ocean to the sky. You are in a pocket, a single feature, a micro-environment embedded in the larger world—but the world pours through it in both directions at once, and the pocket has no permanent walls.

Stay long enough and you will feel it: the chimney is breathing. The turbulence has a rhythm, not steady but recurring—a pattern of collapse and re-formation in the shear zone that takes perhaps ninety seconds, perhaps two minutes. During the collapse, the hot water wins briefly, and the passage goes dark with mineral opacity and the temperature spikes toward something almost warm and the sound drops to a low, grinding bass note. During the re-formation, the cold water shears back, and the light sweeps through and the temperature drops sharply and the sound rises to its full shrieking roar. Back and forth. Inhale and exhale. The mountain-child is breathing through its throat, and you are inside the breath.

This is the landscape. It will not be here for long. In geological terms—perhaps in human terms—the chimney will either widen and collapse into a standard vent, or seal itself as the minerals precipitate and close the fissure. The handholds will dissolve. The bone-bar will fall. The tiny chitinous riders will vanish into some other turbulent margin, or they will not, and their absence will be as unexplained as their presence. The pocket will close.

But right now it is open. Right now it is a room made of water and sound and becoming. Right now you are inside it, and the light is moving, and the breath of the mountain is pressing against your chest, and somewhere in the gray shimmer a handhold is dissolving into the stone that made it.
