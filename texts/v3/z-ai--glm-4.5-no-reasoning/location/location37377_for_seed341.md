The air hangs close and wet, a dim amber atmosphere pressed down by a low ceiling of permanent cloud cover. This is the Weeping Ward—a small, bounded district, no larger than a cathedral close, where the city's old industrial bowels meet the edge of a vast, steaming fen. Heat rises from the dark water in visible shimmers, blending with the soot and condensation until the air feels like a warm, damp cloth pressed over the mouth. 

Water is everywhere, though it rarely falls from the sky. Instead, it weeps. It seeps from the sweating brickwork of the gasometers, drips from the corroded iron of the overhead gantries, and oozes from the porous black stone of the district's single, sunken plaza. The ground is a perilous margin—the kind of marsh-edge where you cannot tell where the pavement ends and the bog begins. Pools of tepid, rust-colored water stand in the hollows, their surfaces dimpled by a constant, slow condensation raining down from the pipes above.

Despite the oppressive heat and the suffocating wet, life has found a way to thrive, though it is a strange, animal-less proliferation. The sparse urban bones of the Ward—its isolated distillation columns, its lone brick pump-house, its tangles of overhead pipework—are colonized by lush, anomalous vegetation. Thick veins of moisture-loving flora crawl up the iron stanchions: heavy, wax-leafed creepers with blooms the color of bruised flesh, and towering, rootless fungal towers that latch onto the weeping masonry. These growths are spaced out, scattered widely across the shallow marsh and the ruin, leaving vast, echoing gaps of empty, steaming water between them. The emptiness is heavy; the space between the iron and the green hums with a low, wet resonance.

The light is profoundly dim. The bulk of the illumination comes from the city's industrial viscera—fist-sized glass valves on the pipes that throb with a sickly, subterranean yellow, and the occasional sparking blue hiss of an exposed transformer, isolated on a buckled concrete island. In this penumbra, the water turns to black glass, and the sprawling leaves of the marsh-plants catch the condensation like holding out tongues for rain. The darkness makes the space feel enclosed, a room without a roof, bounded by the fog and the towering silhouettes of the machinery.

There are no animals here. No birds, no insects, no rats scurrying in the masonry. The ecosystem is driven entirely by the industrial and the botanical. Yet, the Ward is deafeningly loud. The soundscape is a suffocating, mechanical roar—the rhythmic, thumping heartbeat of a subterranean pump churning somewhere deep in the brickwork, the shriek of superheated steam venting from a rusted relief valve, the deep, bass gurgle of water forcing its way through ceramic pipes. Against this brutal percussion, the plant-life plays its own breath: the heavy, wet *thwack* of sodden leaves snapping under their own weight, the groaning creak of vines tightening as they swell with heat and moisture, and the constant, hissing drip of water striking metal and water striking water. 

The processes here are ceaseless. The heat expands the iron; the water oxidizes it; the roots split the stone; the steam clears the valves; the spores burst in the damp. It is a landscape in perfect, violent equilibrium. The erosion is ancient and established—the rust has long since become a stable crust, the bricks have melted into rounded, weathered humps, and the marsh has settled into its long-held shape. 

But there is a strange wrongness to it all, a subtle perversion of the natural order that anchors the weirdness of the place. The pipework bends at angles that seem to follow the root-systems of the flora, rather than the other way around. Some of the heavy, wax-leafed creepers do not reach for the dim light—they reach specifically for the hot pipes, wrapping tightly around the iron, pulsing in perfect synchronization with the pressure valves, drinking the heat and the rust. When the steam vents, it does not burn the leaves; it inflates them, sending a ripple through the canopy like a breath held and released. It is a symbiosis of steel and sap, a mechanical garden quietly breathing in the dark wet heat, untended and alive.
