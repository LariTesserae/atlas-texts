The throat of the world is narrow here.

You are at the apex of a place that should not yet exist—a ridge of raw, unhewn basalt that tore through the crust only moments ago in geological time, its edges still sharp, its faces unblemished by the patient grinding of wind or ice. The elevation is extreme, the air so thin it feels stolen, yet the space itself is cramped. The ridge is perhaps thirty paces long before it drops into the void, and ten paces wide—a high, tight altar suspended above a universe of cloud. There is no horizon. There is only the rock underfoot and the blackness below, and above, a sky so low it seems pressed down upon you like a hand over a mouth.

It is cool here, but not the cool of open air. It is the cool of stone that has never known sunlight, of interiors, of deep places. The darkness is total and wet. There is no light save what your own body might generate—a bioluminescent thought, a phantom glow behind the eyes—but the darkness is not empty. It is dense. It presses against your skin like cloth, like something woven from absence and moisture. For there is water here, though it does not fall. It exudes. Every surface of the young rock is filmed with condensation—cold sweat beading on cold stone, thin runnels seeping from invisible seams, the ridge perpetually damp, perpetually weeping without crying. The air is saturated but does not rain; it simply *is* wet, a state of being rather than an event.

And in this cramped cathedral of dark and damp, the fauna.

Gods, the fauna.

They are everywhere. They are the walls. They are the floor. They are the reason the darkness seems woven, the reason the stone feels soft underfoot. The ridge is carpeted—no, *infested*—with life, and none of it is familiar. There are pale things like grubs but vast, as thick as your thigh, clustered in masses that breathe in unison, their bodies slick with the same condensation that films the rock. There are creatures with too many legs—dozens, hundreds—that cling to the vertical faces of the ridge where it plunges away, their chitin clicking softly as they shift, as they wait. There are things without apparent bodies at all: sheets of translucent membrane that span the gaps between stone spurs, taut and vibrating, strung between the ridge's sharp features like the webs of spiders that learned to weave from air itself. When you brush against them—and you must, because the space is so small and the density so absolute—they stick, and then release, and the membrane throbs once, as if tasting you.

The sound is relentless. It is not the sound of wind, for there is no wind at this height, or none that moves like wind—only slow, patient drafts that take hours to shift the clouds below. The sound is *them*. The clicking of chitin, the wet compression of soft bodies pressing against stone, the subsonic thrum of respiration moving through the masses like a wave through water. And beneath it all, deeper than hearing should reach, a low and constant vibration—the ridge itself, the young rock, still cooling from its violent birth, singing a note too low for ears but felt in the marrow, in the roots of the teeth. The sound does not change. It has not changed since the ridge emerged. It will not change for ages. This is a place of slow time, where the cycles of the creatures are measured in days of stillness followed by minutes of terrible, rippling motion, where the condensation builds and builds until a film of water slides down the stone and every living thing drinks at once, in unison, a single gasp of wetness echoing through the dark.

There are no structures. No tools. No marks of making. This place is untouched by hands; it is only touched by bodies, by the blind and overwhelming insistence of life that found a toehold in the raw dark and *covered* it, saturated it, made it theirs. The sparse vegetation is here too—fibrous, pale growths like hair or root that drape from the higher rocks, drinking the condensation, offering purchase to the crawling things—but they are incidental, background, a thin beard on a face that belongs to the fauna. Nothing here builds. Nothing here shapes. They simply *are*, in numbers that make the ridge feel less like stone and more like a living floor, a platform of flesh and chitin and membrane that happens, beneath it all, to be rock.

The weirdness is in the organization. There is no visible hierarchy, no predator and prey, no struggle. The masses of different creatures press against each other without aggression, without avoidance, as if they are all parts of a single organism, or all tuned to a single frequency—the frequency of the cooling stone, the frequency of the damp. They move together. They drink together. They breathe together. And they ignore you, or they notice you in ways that do not translate into threat or curiosity, simply *acknowledgment*, a brief adjustment of bodies to accommodate your presence, a ripple in the density, and then stillness again.

You are on a high, narrow, fresh-made ridge in the absolute dark, and you are standing on a living carpet in a room with no walls and no ceiling, only the black, and the wet, and the slow, loud, patient thrum of a place that does not know you are different from the rest.
