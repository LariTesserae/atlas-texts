To enter the Hanging Ward of Vash is to step inside a living throat.

The space is small—no larger than a cathedral nave and its side chapels—and every surface, every structure, every breath of architecture is plant. This is not a place where plants grow; it is a place where plant *is*. The walls are massive columns of interlocking root-mass, swollen and knotted into load-bearing walls. The floor is a dense weave of wide, spongy bryophyte pads, springy and waterlogged, yielding six inches underfoot before pushing back. The ceiling is a lattice of thick, weeping vines that have been trained and grafted over centuries into a closed vault, letting in a diffuse, gray-green light through translucent, photosynthetic membranes. It is a single, bounded room—a grove-sized chamber of living architecture—suspended high in the mountain air.

And it is wet. Not damp, but aggressively, pervasively wet. The air is a cool mist, heavy enough that breathing feels like drinking. At an elevation where the outside wind should be thin and freezing, the Ward traps its own weather. The root-walls weep. Constantly, from a thousand fissures in the bark, water seeps—mineral-rich, cold, running down the grain in thin, converging sheets. It pools in the cups of enormous epiphytic leaves that serve as the settlement's basins and gutters. It drips from the vaulted ceiling in a syncopated rhythm. There is no dry surface. Water pushes through the very substrate of the place, hydrostatic pressure from the mountain aquifer forcing moisture up through the floor-moss until it stands in shin-deep, tepid puddles in the hollows.

Despite the deluge, the chamber teems with an overwhelming density of animal life. It is a churning, multitudinous presence that fills the space to bursting. Millions of translucent, gilled amphipods skitter through the floor-moss, eating the rot and each other. Centipedes as long as a man's forearm, glossy and black, navigate the root-walls with urgent, clicking legs. The air is thick with winged insects—clouds of mayflies and midges that swirl in the pale light, their wings humming. Thick-boded, hairless frogs the color of wet stone cling to every vertical surface, throats pulsing, eyes unblinking, gorging themselves endlessly. There are no predators large enough to cull this swarm; there is only the endless, desperate mathematics of consumption and reproduction in a space where the food never stops growing.

The sound is deafening. The acoustic properties of the wet, fibrous walls should muffle noise, but the sheer biomass of the fauna overrides it. A continuous, low roar fills the chamber—the combined drone of a billion insect wings, layered with the wet, percussive *thock* of frogs landing on saturated leaves, and the shrill, scraping stridulation of the centipedes. Over this biotic roar, the water adds its own cacophony: the endless white noise of running sheets, the heavy, hollow *plonk* of drops hitting the leaf-catchers, the hiss of water being forced up through the moss. There are no quiet moments. The sound is a physical pressure, a vibrating thrum that settles into the teeth and the breastbone.

This is a settlement. It is built, organized, ancient. The people who live here—pale, compact, amphibious humans with webbed digits and wide, dark eyes—have carved out a life in this turbulent biosphere. Their technology is of the place: they harvest the thick vines and weave them into suspended hammocks and walkways above the deepest water; they chip the obsidian-like thorns of the giant bryophytes into scrapers and cutters; they use the hollowed-out base of a massive root as a communal hearth, though there is no fire—only a slow, enzymatic fermentation pit that generates heat and a sour, yeasty smell. Walkways of lashed, living stems cross the waterways. Storage pods are grown from grafted seed-pods, sealed with resinous sap.

But it is a settlement in a state of permanent, chaotic negotiation. The Ward is not stable. The plants grow with a furious, Solaris-like energy, constantly repairing the water damage but also constantly overgrowing the paths, the bridges, the living spaces. The people are engaged in an unending war of pruning. They work in shifts, day and night, hacking back the encroaching vines, scraping the invasive moss from the walkways, cutting away the pulsing fungal blooms that erupt overnight from the sodden walls. It is an anthill of desperate, manual labor. And for every vine they sever, two weep sap; for every bloom they cut, a thousand spores release. The ecosystem pushes back instantly. The waste from their pruning is consumed by the swarm within seconds, the skittering amphipods and burrowing worms recycling the cuttings back into the substrate, feeding the roots, which weep more water, which spawns more insects, which forces more frogs to spawn, which creates more noise, more droppings, more rot, more growth.

The dynamic equilibrium is hypnotic in its intensity. Creation and destruction are the same process here. The people of Vash do not maintain their town; they merely exist as a temporary shape within the flow. They are another species in the churn, building their nests in the hollows of the greater organism, tearing at the walls with their obsidian tools, their voices lost beneath the deafening roar of water and life. The air smells of ozone, wet green cellulose, and the hot, coppery scent of invertebrate biomass. It is cool, it is loud, it is suffocatingly alive. The Ward breathes around them, and they prune, and the water falls, and the swarm devours, and the roots grow back, and the walls weep on, unceasing.
