A hollow space, buried in the gut of something older than memory.

There is no water here. Not in the air, not in the soil, not bound in any cell. The atmosphere is a slow exhalation of inert gas and fungal spore—dry as calcined bone, cool as the inside of a sealed vault. If you opened your mouth here, you would taste dust and the faint alkaline bite of salts that have outlived every ocean that ever touched this place. Moisture is a concept so foreign that the very architecture has forgotten how to rot. It only desiccates, only settles, only continues.

And yet—vegetation is everything. This is the paradox that gives the space its strangeness: a jungle without wetness, a forest that has never known rain. The living mass here is lichen. Not lichen as you know it—crustose smears on rock—but lichen as cathedral, lichen as substrate, lichen as the ground beneath your feet and the walls pressing in and the ceiling lost somewhere above in absolute dark. The entire pocket is filled with it. Great columns of consolidated thallus, meters thick, structures built over millennia by the slow accretion of hyphae and algal cells working together in a chemistry that never required a water solvent. They use something else here. Something thinner and stranger—a vapor-phase exchange, perhaps, or a crystalline fluid that moves through capillary channels in the mineralized tissue at rates barely faster than diffusion.

The space is small. Pocket-scale. You could walk its perimeter in four minutes if you could see where you were going, which you cannot. The darkness is total—not diminished, not filtered, but absolute, as if light had never been invented for this place and never would be. There is no sky. There is no horizon. There is only the intimate press of living architecture on all sides, the sensation of walls close enough to touch even when you stand in the center of the single chamber that constitutes this world.

And it is built. That is what makes it so uncanny. This is not a wild growth, not a meadow or a grove—it is a settlement. Streets, or what were once streets. The lichen has grown in rows, in walls, in rectangular chambers and arched passages. Someone shaped this. Someone with hand-tools and patience, with an understanding of how to encourage growth in one direction and suppress it in another, with pruning hooks and scaffolding poles and the kind of knowledge that passes from body to body without ever needing to be spoken. The technology is primitive—there is no metal here, no forged thing, no wheel or lever. But there is craft. There is the evidence of hands that knew their work.

Who built it? Nothing remains of them. No animal life has ever existed in this sealed pocket—no grazers, no insects, no builders with bones to leave behind. The fauna parameter reads near-zero, and it is correct: this is an ecosystem of one. Only the lichen. Only the slow vegetative mind of it, spreading and thickening and conquering every surface with the patience of something that has never known death, only dormancy. Perhaps the builders were lichen too—a strain that evolved motility, intention, architecture—and then lost it again, settled back into the ancient rhythm of pure growth. Perhaps the city grew itself, responding to some gradient of nutrients or light that existed here once but exhausted itself eons ago. Perhaps the builders were something else entirely, something that came from outside, built this pocket-garden as a shrine or a prison or a last home, and then departed or died or dissolved into the walls they made.

The erosion tells the story of deep time. This place is in its final form. The streets have softened; the sharp corners of chambers have rounded; the fine details—doorways, shelves, whatever furniture the builders shaped from living tissue—have all been absorbed back into the mass. What remains is the skeleton of a city at the end of its erosion: the broad strokes, the major walls, the fundamental geometry. Everything else has been worn away by the patient friction of growth itself, each generation of lichen smoothing the work of the last, filling in the details, rounding the edges toward that eventual perfect flatness that is the endpoint of all ancient things. But it has not reached flatness yet. Not quite. The city-shape persists, ghostly and massive, a footprint that will take another million years to erase.

Despite the emptiness—the near-void of matter per volume, the vast stretches of hollow space between the great thallus-columns—this place is not silent. Sound fills it. Sound *is* the dominant experience. The lichen grows. And as it grows, it creaks. The mineralized fibers of its structure, stretched taut across chambers and spanning archways, vibrate with the impossibly slow expansion of tissue adding layer to layer. Each creak is a note below the threshold of hearing—subsonic, felt in the chest rather than perceived by the ear. But the creaks accumulate. Thousands of them, overlapping in patterns that take decades to resolve. The sound that fills the darkness is a drone, a chord, a vast harmonic resonance built from the sum total of all growth happening everywhere in this sealed space at once. It is loud. It is *very* loud—a physical pressure, a thrumming that you feel in your teeth and in the bones behind your ears. It never stops. It has never stopped. For as long as this pocket has existed—sealed, dry, dark, patient—the lichen has been growing and the structure has been singing.

Stand here (if you could stand here) and you would understand something about duration. The growth is so slow that no human lifetime could measure its progress. The sound is so constant that it has become the silence of this place—the thing that defines quiet, by its unending presence. The cool air moves in negligible currents, stirred only by the metabolic exhalations of the vegetative mass. The darkness does not vary. There is no day, no night, no season, no weather. There is only the chamber, and the living architecture, and the ancient sound of a city that is still, after everything, still growing.

A garden at the end of time. A hymn without a singer. A place where someone once built a home from the only thing that would last, and then left, and the home kept growing anyway, and forgot it was ever anything other than wild.
