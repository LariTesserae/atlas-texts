# The Underbazaar

It exists below. Not buried—*housed*. There is no natural ground here. Every surface is made, has always been made. The stone underfoot is fitted, jointed, though the joints have worn smooth under centuries of passage until the floor flows like poured material, continuous and unbroken. The walls are the same. The ceiling too. This place was constructed—deliberately, skillfully, completely—and then given over to time, and time has taken it thoroughly.

---

## What It Is

A chamber-complex, roughly the size of a cathedral nave with side aisles that branch and reconnect without reason. The ceiling hangs low enough in places to brush a raised hand. Columns thick as old trees support it, but they are not trees—they are stacked stone, spiral-wound, their mortar long since petrified into something harder than the stone itself. The whole space feels *compressed*. Dense. Every square of wall holds a shelf, a niche, a hook, a carved seat, a blocked doorway, a vent. Nothing is empty. The surfaces are layered with the residue of use: ash lines, wax buildup, scratches where bags were dragged, darkened arcs where shoulders have worn their passing into the corners.

The floor is not level. It drops in shallow terraces, three steps down, then two, then a long slope into the lower galleries where the light fails to reach and only sound persists. Water collects in these depressions. Not pools—*films*. The floor shines wet in the low areas, and in the deepest hollows, a still half-inch reflects the ceiling back grey and distorted. The damp is constant. It does not rain here, but the air holds moisture like cloth holds dye, and when the temperature shifts—as it does, hourly, from breath and body-heat and the distant ventilation that pulls air through the complex—condensation beads on the cooler stones and runs down in slow, erratic traces. The walls weep. Not copiously. Persistently.

---

## What Grows

Vegetation is sparse but *specific*. Fungi colonize the constant-damp zones: pale shelves bracketing the column bases, delicate stalks rising from the mortar cracks where organic residue has pooled, a single massive bracket-fungus halfway up the eastern wall that has grown so long in its spot that it has shaped the stone beneath it, etching a shallow bowl with its slow chemical presence. It is the color of old bone. It has been there longer than anyone remembers.

There are no roots. No green. What grows here grows on decay, on the accumulated organic matter of centuries—spilled grain, dropped fiber, the slow compost of occupation. Moss of a color not quite green, more the grey-green of oxidized copper, patches the ceiling where condensation is heaviest. It spreads in fingerprints, never thick, never more than a fuzz, but it is alive and it is everywhere if you look up long enough.

Threadlike vines descend from the ceiling joints in places—rootless things that draw moisture from the air and trace minerals from the stone dust. They do not leaf. They exist as pale lines, faint as cobwebs but stronger, swaying in the air-currents that pulse through the chambers like slow breathing.

---

## What Moves

The fauna own this place more than whatever built it did.

**Insects first**: The sound is theirs. Crickets—or things like crickets, dark and flat and adapted to the stone—chorus from every niche and crack. Their song is not the field-song of open country. It is deeper, resonant, a thrumming vibration that makes the columns hum at certain frequencies. There are thousands. Tens of thousands. When the air shifts, their sound shifts with it, rising and falling in waves that take long seconds to crest. It is loud enough to feel in the chest. Loud enough that speech must be raised to be heard, and even then, words blur at their edges.

**Then the geckos**: Pale, almost translucent, they hunt the insects along the walls and ceiling. They are abundant—dozens visible at any moment, clinging to the fitted stone with wide splayed feet, their throats pulsing. They drop sometimes, small wet impacts on the floor, and skitter into the lower galleries. They are unafraid. They have no reason to be afraid. Nothing here hunts them that they cannot see.

**Then the bats**: A colony occupies the upper crevices where the ceiling joints open into vertical shafts—ventilation, perhaps, or simply the gaps between construction periods. They are small, leathered, clicking and rustling in their roosts. They emerge in pulses, not the massive dusk-empting of a cave colony but a constant trickle, individuals dropping out and flickering through the chambers before returning. Their guano piles in the unused niches, and the fungi grow from it, and the insects eat the fungi, and the geckos eat the insects, and the bats eat the flying insects, and the cycle turns in this closed loop of metabolism that has been turning since long before the current occupants arrived.

**And other things**: Rats, certainly—or some analog, some rodent-adapted shape that knows every passage and drinks from the floor-pools and gnaws at the stored materials with incisors that grow faster than the stone can wear them. They are heard more than seen: scrabbling in the walls, the particular wet sound of them grooming in the dark intervals between chambers. Something larger moves in the deep lower galleries occasionally—something that displaces air and makes the floor-films ripple—but it is not seen from the inhabited levels, only felt as a pressure-change, a brief suck of wind, a silence among the crickets that passes and then fills again.

---

## The Light

It comes from above, filtered. The ceiling-shafts admit daylight—diffuse, as through thick cloud or fabric—but it is enough. In the main chamber, the light is a grey amber, warm-toned but not warm itself, falling in shafts that pick out the dust and the spores and the slow descent of the rootless vines. The condensation on the walls catches this light and multiplies it into scattered points, so the surfaces shimmer faintly, as if the stone itself were breathing.

Deeper in, the light fails. The lower terraces go dim, then dark, and the sound follows—muffled at first, then resonant in a different way, the cricket-song echoing off closer walls and becoming something denser, more architectural, a sound that belongs to the space rather than to the insects.

Torches have been used here. The walls show the smoke-traces: blackened arcs above the sconces, some so old they have been half-worn by condensation, ghost-marks of fires that burned and were quenched centuries apart. The current occupants do not use fire. They use the light that comes, and when it fails, they use darkness.

---

## The Human Presence

This is a market. Or it was. Or it is again.

The niches in the walls are vendor-stalls, shallow alcoves with stone shelves worn hollow by the weight of goods. Hooks above, worn grooves below where goods were tied. The floor in the main chamber is cut with drainage channels—thin, precise, leading to the low pools—and these channels are black with the residue of things spilled: oils, dyes, the dark liquid of some trade-goods that has stained the stone permanently.

The space is occupied now, but sparsely, and with a makeshift quality that overlays the original intention. Fabric hangs between columns, dividing spaces that were not meant to be divided. Baskets line walls that were meant to be passed. Someone has built a low stone oven in one alcove, blocking a ventilation shaft, and the smoke has blackened the ceiling in a radius around it. The construction is primitive—hand-fired brick, clay mortar, a grate of hammered scrap-metal that has rusted to the texture of stone. It works. It has worked for a long time.

People move through. Not many. A handful at any time, their footsteps adding to the sound-scape in a way that feels natural, another layer of the place's constant noise. They trade in small things: dried provisions, hand tools, lengths of rough fabric, the pale fungi harvested from the walls, live insects in woven cages (a delicacy, or a medicine, or both). The transactions are quiet. The cricket-song absorbs speech.

They live here, some of them. Behind the fabric walls, in the deeper alcoves, there are sleeping-rolls and stored water and the accumulated evidence of long habitation—a worn stone used as a seat for so long it has a groove for the buttocks, a child's scratches in the wall, a rope worn to fragility and replaced and the replacement worn in turn. The place has been inhabited for generations. The inhabitants are adapted to it. They do not notice the damp. They do not notice the noise. They move through the cricket-chorus as fish move through water.

---

## The Deeper Place

Below the market level, the chambers narrow. The construction changes—rougher, older, the stone less carefully fitted, the mortar crumbled to sand in places and held only by compression and habit. Something is down there. The sounds from below are different: not the bright thrum of insects but a low organic murk, a soundscape of larger things in a more confined space. The air from below smells different too—sharper, more biological, the ammonia-tang of concentrated waste and the sweet rot of protein breaking down.

The inhabitants do not go there often. When they do, they carry something—a tool, a light-source, a marked stone whose purpose is not spoken of. They return with things: pale fungi not found on the upper walls, stones with holes worn through them, sometimes living things that they do not sell and do not explain. The lower galleries are where the larger thing moves. The occupants know it. There is an arrangement. The details of it are not shared with outsiders.

---

## How It Feels

To stand in the Underbazaar is to stand inside a living mechanism. The sound is the first thing—overwhelming, continuous, the cricket-thrum so constant it becomes a quality of the air rather than a sound, a vibration-state that the body adjusts to and then stops hearing until a silence falls and the absence is shocking. The warmth is the second thing—body-heat and decomposition-heat and the slow thermal mass of stone holding the day's warmth and releasing it across the night-cycle. The damp is the third—never enough to be wet, never absent enough to be dry, a constant subtle slickness that gets into everything, that makes the stone shine, that keeps the air heavy.

It smells of insects and stone and cooking smoke and the particular clean mustiness of places where water and calcium and organic matter have been interacting for longer than memory. It is not unpleasant. It is the smell of a functioning ecosystem, which is always, in its way, the smell of health.

The weirdness is in the completeness. There is no natural floor beneath the made floor. No raw stone, no earth, no substrate. The construction goes all the way down—or seems to. The deeper one goes, the older the construction, but it never becomes *ground*. It is made all the way through. Someone, or some-ones, built this entire volume, hollowed it out of something else or assembled it from nothing, and the scale of that act, the thoroughness, is unsettling if you think about it too long. The occupants do not think about it. They live in the made-space and trade in its alcoves and sleep to the cricket-song and do not wonder what was here before the here was built, because the question has no answer, and the place has no memory of a time when it was not this.
