It is a place that has not yet decided what it wants to be.

You feel it first in the lungs. The air is heavy, saturated, carrying a chill that sinks past the skin and settles in the bone. It is cool here—high up, near the Roof of the world, where the clouds do not pass overhead so much as push through the streets. The atmosphere condenses against every surface. Water does not fall here; it materializes. It beads on the stone, it weeps from the crude mortar, it hangs in the air as a perpetual, drenching mist. You are walking through a slow, vertical rain.

The space is enclosed—a single, vast cavity in the living rock, no larger than a cathedral nave and its cloisters. This is the boundary. Beyond the rim of the settlement, there is only the solid, freezing marrow of the mountain. Within it, a city is being born, though the birth is agonizingly slow and oddly hollow.

They call it a city, but it is mostly absence. The density is near-void. You walk down what should be a main thoroughfare, but the structures are spaced tens of meters apart, separated by yawning stretches of raw, unworked stone. There are no crowds here. There is barely a populace. The settlement is a skeletal grid of hand-hewn basalt pillars and half-constructed walls, laid out with the obsessive symmetry of a grand design, yet occupied by almost no one. The echoes have nothing to bounce off of but the bare geometry of the place.

And the geometry is raw. The erosion parameter is almost zero; this is a landscape of emergence, of embryonic geology. The stone underfoot is not smooth. It is fractured, sharp, tumescent. The paving stones feel freshly cleaved from the quarry, their edges dangerously keen, their surfaces pocked with the percussion marks of primitive stone hammers. The walls rising beside you are not yet buildings; they are the *idea* of buildings. Mortar is squeezed between the blocks like dark clay, still soft, slowly petrifying in the damp cold. There are no roofs. The structures are just vertical frames, open to the weeping sky, waiting for weights and measures that have not yet been crafted. The tech here is pre-metal, reliant on the leverage of wood, the strike of stone, the bindings of wet hide and rough-spun bast. Everything requires immense manual labor, and the pace of construction reveals this—a dynamic of near-zero flux. The city grows by a single stone a month. It is a patient, geological process, indistinguishable from the mountain slowly deciding to grow a skeleton.

Yet, amidst this harsh, freezing construction, the vegetation is a riot. It is a lushness that borders on the absurd, a rebellion of green against the gray. Moss does not simply grow here; it swallows. Thick, wet carpets of emerald and chartreuse drape over the sharp edges of the raw stone, softening the brutalist geometry. Ferns uncoil from the joints of the unfinished walls, their fronds heavy with condensation, dripping endlessly into the puddles that pool in the unfinished foundations. Lichens crust the wooden scaffolding, turning the dead wood into vibrant, sculptural topiaries. The water is so near the surface that the stone itself seems to sweat, feeding the roots that have wedged themselves into the slightest crevice. It is a grove-scale jungle trapped inside a hamlet-scale ruin.

The fauna is eerily scarce. No insects hum. No birds call from the dripping ledges. The ecology is closed to animal life, as if the raw magnetic pull of the forming rock repels them. The only exception is a single species: pale, eyeless amphibians, the size of a human hand, translucent with the cold. They cling to the underside of the broad fern leaves, breathing slowly, their heartbeats visible as faint, rhythmic pulses of dark blood. They are the only witnesses to the construction, and they make no sound.

But the place is not silent. The sound is overwhelming, a constant, deafening roar that seems to come from the very bones of the earth. It is the sound of water under immense pressure, forcing its way through micro-fissures in the deep rock. It is the sound of the mountain weeping. Every surface runs with it. The weeping rocks sing a low, bass thrum that vibrates in the teeth, overlaid with the chaotic, percussive patter of a thousand drips striking stone, wood, and water. It is loud enough to drown thought, a white noise that demands surrender.

And woven into this natural cacophony is the weirdness. The sound occasionally organizes itself. Standing in the center of this empty, unfinished square, surrounded by the bare frames of buildings and the dripping jungle, you will hear a rhythm in the drips—a syncopated beat that sounds like the strike of hundreds of unseen hammers. You look to the scaffolding, expecting to see an army of ghostly masons, but there is only the mist, the ferns, and the slow weeping of the stone. The city is building itself, singing its own construction songs in the dark, pulling itself up from the magma, one wet, agonizing drop at a time.
