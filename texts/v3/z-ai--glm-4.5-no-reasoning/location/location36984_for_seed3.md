The city is still being born.

It clings to the rim of a canyon that has no right to exist—a vertical wound in the earth's crust, where the rock is still wet and soft, like clay left out in a damp room. This is a place of raw geology, of strata that haven't yet decided whether they are sedimentary or something else entirely. The walls of the canyon shear down for almost a vertical mile, and the city—**Verashalt**—is bolted and stitched into the canyon's eastern face, tier after tier of industrial architecture descending into the dim bluish haze of the depths.

There is no sunrise here. The canyon faces north-east, and the sun only grazes the uppermost tiers for an hour each afternoon. The rest of the time, light comes from the city itself: the sodium-vapor lamps that line every catwalk, the forge-glow from the lower foundries, the strange bioluminescent moss that has learned to grow on steam pipes. It is always cool—twelve degrees, fourteen, never more—and the air carries the smell of hot metal, damp stone, and something sweeter, something that might be the sap of the vegetation that has decided to colonize this place.

---

**THE WATER** comes and goes. There are no rivers on the canyon floor—only a network of seeps and weeps, moisture exuding from the raw rock faces where the stone hasn't finished compressing. The locals call them *tears*. Some tears are reliable enough to be plumbed into reservoirs; others appear for weeks, then vanish for months, leaving only mineral stains on the cliff. When the condensation is heavy—usually in the pre-dawn hours when the temperature drops to its lowest—the entire canyon fills with a thin, cold fog that the foundries turn into a brownish smog. It clings to the catwalks. You can taste iron and wet grit on your lips.

The city's engineers have built an intricate gutter system to capture and channel every drop. Copper pipes and galvanized troughs run along every tier, feeding down into the great cisterns at the canyon's base. But the pipes leak. They leak constantly. There is more water running down the outside of the pipes than inside them, and this persistent dampness is what the vegetation loves.

---

**THE VEGETATION** is lush in a way that feels insurgent.

This is not a natural landscape, and nothing about the plant life here is natural either. The dominant species is a kind of climbing fern—*Drynaria verashaltii*—that has evolved to root in the mortar between bricks, to feed on the runoff from industrial coolant systems, to grow toward the sound of vibrating machinery. It drapes from every balcony, every railing, every abandoned scaffold. Its fronds are dark green and leathery, thick as leather, and they grow in whorls that catch the condensation and funnel it down to their roots in a way that seems almost deliberate, almost intelligent.

Where the fern grows thickest—on the old tiers, the ones that haven't been occupied in decades—it forms a kind of secondary architecture. Walkways disappear under it. Doorways are sealed. Whole floors of old tenements become green cavities, the walls soft with moss and the air inside heavy and humid and smelling of mulch, even in winter. The city's maintenance crews fight it endlessly, with scythes and herbicides and controlled burns, but the fern grows back faster than they can cut it. It grows inches in a day. It grows toward the lights.

And there are other plants: a fungal network that fruits in the steam-vents, pale mushrooms with caps like shallow bowls that collect water and breed insects; a kind of vine that has learned to tap into electrical conduit, its roots wrapped around copper wire, somehow drawing current from it, its leaves faintly glowing; mosses and lichens in a hundred varieties, painting every surface in patches of grey-green, rust-orange, and a strange, powdery violet that seems to appear only where the rock is newest, where the canyon is still forming.

---

**THE FAUNA** is abundant, and much of it is unafraid.

Insects first: the canyon is full of them. Caddis flies in the damp zones, their larvae living in the slow seeps; beetles that have adapted to live in the hot pipes, their carapaces heat-resistant to an impossible degree; a species of moth, pale and silent, that orbits the sodium lamps in slow spirals and sometimes lands on the condensation-slicked windows of apartments, its wings leaving prints like watercolor strokes.

Then the birds. Pigeons, of course, but also a kind of cliff-dwelling swift that has moved into the city's ventilation shafts and now nests by the thousands in the upper tiers. Their droppings coat the unused scaffolding in a white crust. They scream at dawn—not the ordinary sound of swifts, but something harsher, more metallic, as if the city has changed their voices.

And in the lower canyon, where the fog is thick and the foundries rumble, there are larger things. The workers call them *dwellers*. They are some kind of primate—lemur-like, with enormous flat feet that can grip wet rock, and eyes that reflect light back in a color that isn't quite right, more purple than red. They live in the canyon walls below the city, in caves and crevices that the engineers haven't mapped. They come up at night to raid the compost chutes and the condensation-troughs. They are not hostile, but they watch. Their silence is unnerving.

There are other animals too, or traces of them—sounds in the deeper fog that don't match any known species, scratches on the rock face where nothing should be able to climb, the occasional corpse found in the cisterns, unrecognizable after a week in the water.

---

**THE CITY ITSELF** is industrial and vertical and half-finished.

Verashalt was built to service the extraction of something from the canyon walls—a mineral, a compound, something the records call *cordite* but which is clearly not the explosive. The mines are in the lower tiers, where the rock is softest, and the foundries process the ore on-site. The architecture reflects this: the buildings are functional, brick and iron and concrete, with smokestacks and cable-cars and funiculars connecting the tiers. There are no ornaments, no parks, no churches. There is a workers' canteen on every third level, a foreman's office on every fifth, and everywhere the endless pipes, carrying water and steam and electrical current and things that aren't labeled.

The city extends about four thousand feet along the canyon rim and about six hundred tiers down—roughly a mile of vertical development. The population is hard to count. Perhaps thirty thousand, perhaps fewer; the outer tiers are being slowly abandoned as the fern takes them, and the lower tiers belong more to the machinery than to anyone living. The inhabited zone is a narrow band in the middle, maybe forty tiers deep, where the condensation is tolerable and the foundry-smoke hasn't rendered the air unbreathable.

But the city is not complete. This is the crucial thing. The canyon is still forming—the rock is still consolidating, still shifting—and the city must constantly adapt. New tiers are being built at the top as the rim erodes outward. Old tiers are being abandoned at the bottom as the floor drops. Buildings that were plumb a decade ago now lean. Streets that were straight now curve. The engineers are always working—shoring up foundations, relocating load-bearing walls, extending bridges that no longer quite reach. The city is a process, not a place.

---

**THE WEIRDNESS** starts subtly.

It begins with the rock. The canyon walls are not uniform. In some places, the strata are normal—sandstone, limestone, shale, the expected sequence. But in other places, the rock is wrong. It contains shapes. Not fossils, exactly—more like *imprints* of things that haven't happened yet, or that happened in a different configuration of reality. There are sections of cliff that appear to contain the profiles of buildings, negative spaces where architecture has been compressed into geology. The miners have learned to avoid these zones. The ore doesn't extract properly there. The walls... listen.

And the geometry of the canyon does not hold. Distances shift. A catwalk that took five minutes to traverse on Monday takes seven on Tuesday, and the foremen don't seem to notice. The space between two adjacent tiers is sometimes ten feet, sometimes twelve, sometimes—briefly, in the early morning fog—something that makes the measuring tapes read in fractions that don't exist. The engineers blame the condensation, the softness of the rock, the settling of foundations. But the settling doesn't explain why, when you look down from the upper tiers, the city below sometimes appears to be *folded*, different sections overlapping, the same building visible in two places at once.

The vegetation participates in this. The fern grows in patterns that aren't natural—Fibonacci spirals that don't follow the Fibonacci sequence, fractal branchings that repeat at scales that shouldn't exist in a plant. And the vine that taps the electrical conduit seems to *know* things. Its growth follows the current in ways that suggest it is reading the signal, not just drawing power from it. There are workers who swear that the glow of the vine's leaves forms letters, briefly, before the pattern shifts.

The fauna is strange too. The dwellers in the lower canyon are too large for their ecological niche, too numerous, too organized. They move in groups of six or twelve, always even numbers, and they have been seen holding objects—stones, pieces of pipe, once a wrench—that they do not use for any observable purpose. They seem to be *performing* with them, going through motions that might be ritual, or might be something else.

And the sounds. The sounds are the worst.

---

**THE SOUND** is constant and loud, and it comes from everywhere.

The foundries produce the baseline: a deep, rhythmic thudding that you feel in your chest more than hear, accompanied by the higher shrieking of metal on metal, the hiss of steam, the clatter of ore-carts on rails. This is the industrial soundscape, and you can learn to sleep through it, though your sleep will be shallow and your dreams will be metallic.

But beneath the mechanical noise, there is something else. The canyon amplifies and distorts sound in ways that shouldn't be possible. The rock itself seems to resonate at frequencies that correspond to no known source. Workers in the lower tunnels report hearing voices—not words, but the cadence of speech, the rhythm of conversation, as if the stone were recording and replaying fragments of everything that had ever been said within it. The phenomenon is reproducible: certain chambers, certain times of day, certain temperatures. The engineers call it *acoustic caching* and have no explanation for it.

The vegetation adds its own layer. The fern, when it grows thick enough, rustles constantly—even when there is no wind. The sound is like whispering, or like the shuffling of pages, or like something breathing very slowly in the next room. The vine hums faintly where it touches conduit, adding a electrical buzz to the acoustic environment that makes the hair on your arms stand up.

And in the deepest part of the night, when the foundries are on low burn and the fog is at its thickest, there is a sound that comes from the canyon floor—that place where no one goes, where the rock is newest and softest, where the water pools in formations that seem to spell things. It is a sound like singing, or like the memory of singing, or like the shape that singing would make if it were pressed into stone. It is very quiet and very distant, but it carries. It carries up through five hundred tiers of industrial city, through the fern and the fog and the iron, and it arrives in the workers' dormitories as a single clear tone that is almost beautiful, that is almost music, that almost makes sense before it fades.

No one talks about this sound. But they all hear it.

---

**THE RULES** of this place are still being written.

Verashalt exists in a margin—a gap between the established and the possible. The canyon is forming; the city is forming; the reality they share is forming. Nothing here is finished, and that is both its danger and its only promise.

The rock changes. Not quickly, but measurably. Surveyors who map the canyon walls find differences each year—new formations, new anomalies, new places where the stone has decided to be something else. The city must change with it, or be broken by it. The vegetation will not be stopped. The fauna will not be displaced. The weirdness will not be contained. The best the engineers can do is build around all of it, to make a city that is flexible enough to bend when reality bends, that can hold its shape even when the shape of the canyon cannot quite hold itself.

This is why the place feels the way it does—raw, damp, loud, green, crowded and empty at once, mechanical and alive, half-real and half-still-dreaming. It is a city being built on a geology being formed in a space that hasn't finished deciding what it is. Everything here is under construction. Everything here is emerging. The fern, the dwellers, the sounds in the stone, the shapes in the rock, the city itself—they are all the same process, the same slow becoming, the same patient emergence from something that hasn't happened yet into something that might, if you wait long enough, become a place.

But not yet. Not for a long time yet. For now, Verashalt is still being born, and birth is loud and wet and strange, and the thing being born is not what it will be.
