# The Drowned Works

A heat haze sits permanently over the flats — not the dry heat of desert, but a sodden, compressive warmth, like laundry left to steam in a closed room. The air is thick. It carries water without quite being rain. Droplets form on surfaces and slide, or hang suspended in the calm, waiting for any current to carry them sideways. Breathing feels like drinking.

---

The city — if it still qualifies — spreads across a basin barely above the water table. From any elevated point, you can see how it goes: rows of structures extending into dimness, their forms simplifying with distance until they're just vertical smudges against a paler sky. The light never opens fully here. A high cloud deck or some quality of the air itself diffuses everything into a permanent late-dusk gloom, amber-grey, without shadows. Depth becomes difficult to judge. A distant smokestack might be enormous and far, or modest and near. The eye keeps correcting, failing.

Closer, the forms resolve into industry: refineries, processing plants, storage tanks, kiln-houses, cooling galleries. The architecture is brick and iron — old brick, iron gone to oxide and then beyond, to something like mineral bark. The bricks themselves have absorbed so much moisture over so many decades that they've darkened from red to near-black, and their faces have grown a pelt of fine crystalline grit. When you touch them, your fingers come away damp and faintly gritty, like feeling a cheese rind. The mortar between them has softened. In places it has washed out entirely, leaving the bricks to lean on one another by habit and weight, their joints breathing open and closed with the slow thermal sighs of the structures.

Everything is wet, but nothing flows. The water seeps, weeps, condenses. Stand still long enough and you'll feel it gathering on your shoulders, your forearms, the backs of your hands. Stand still longer and it begins to drip from you, adding your contribution to the general saturation. The ground underfoot — where it isn't the raised iron grating of the walkways — is a clay that has forgotten how to be solid. It yields slowly, like cold honey, and never fully releases what steps into it. Old rail tracks run through it, sunk to the ties, the rails themselves only visible as two faint ridges in the gleaming muck.

---

There is no plant life. Not a stem, not a filament of algae, not a lichen scale on the slowest-dripping wall. The heat and wet should produce growth — that combination wants jungle, wants swamp, wants the furious green climb of living things toward any light. But nothing green grows here. Instead, the biological niche — and it is occupied, vigorously — belongs to something else.

They come in densities that shift the word *fauna* toward *matter*.

The dominant presence is a kind of animal for which no familiar analogy quite works. They are the size and general shape of large cats, but furless, and their skin has the translucency and faint iridescence of something that evolved in permanent darkness — pale, vaguely opalescent, showing the dark shapes of organs shifting beneath. Their heads are low and wide, eyes reduced to damp dimples, and their mouths run wide and hinge low, giving them a look of perpetual reception, as though their entire front end were a single sensory apparatus. They navigate by a means that doesn't seem to be sound or scent; they simply *know* where things are, including you, with an accuracy that feels architectural rather than perceptual.

There are thousands visible from any single point. They line the roof-ridges in static rows, basking in whatever warmth the iron holds. They cluster in the interiors of open buildings, heaped in corners like sediment, rising and settling with a group respiration so slow it matches the thermal breathing of the brickwork itself. They sit on the pipes, the tanks, the rusted catwalks — anywhere that retains even a few degrees above ambient heat. In the deeper pools of standing water, their submerged forms are visible as pale distortions, barely moving, absorbing minerals directly through their skins. When they move, they move with a fluid purpose that suggests they are not going *to* something so much as flowing *through* the space, like a current in the water they so closely resemble.

They are not aggressive. They are not tame. They simply *occupy*, with a completeness that makes you the interloper, the thin presence, the thing that doesn't quite belong in the mass.

Their sound — when there is one — is a low, continuous, wet exhalation. Thousands of them breathing. It is the closest thing to weather the place has.

---

The city functions. That is perhaps the strangest thing, given the stillness and the wet and the overwhelming non-human presence. It functions the way a body functions without conscious direction — peristaltically, habitually, because the structures were built to run and have not been told to stop.

Somewhere deep in the works, engines turn. You feel them more than hear them: a low vibration in the iron walkways, a rhythmic strain that comes through the brick walls like a pulse through bone. The factories process something — the minerals in the water, perhaps, or the mineral crust that forms on every surface, or the biological by-products of the teeming fauna. Pipes carry hot fluids. Tanks exotherm, warm to the touch, and the animals cluster on them for the heat. Electrical systems still carry current; in the deeper interiors, dim bulbs glow behind their condensation, lighting rooms where no human has stood in living memory but where machinery still cycles through its operations with mechanical patience.

Steam escapes from fittings at long, irregular intervals — not the dramatic hiss of a breach but the quiet resignation of a system that has learned to tolerate its own leaks. The steam adds to the general wet, condenses on the nearest cool surface, runs down in slow tracks to the pooling ground, and the cycle continues.

What the works produce, and who receives it, is unclear. The loading docks are empty of vehicles. The storage yards contain orderly stacks of unidentifiable material — pressed blocks of something dark and dense, wrapped in a mineralized fabric that has fused with its contents. The rail tracks lead away into the dim distance and are never used. Perhaps the product moves by water, through the saturated ground itself, by some system of pipes or channels that runs beneath the visible city. Or perhaps the production is the point — the process, not the output — and the works run because running is what works do, and have done, since before anyone left, and will continue to do after the last human memory of this place has softened like the mortar and washed away into the clay.

---

Time moves strangely here, or rather it doesn't move at all — it *accumulates*, like the water, like the mineral scale, like the slow weight of all those silent bodies on the warm iron. There is no weather in the usual sense, only the unchanging condition of wet heat. No day breaks; the light level rises slightly and falls slightly on a cycle too long and too subtle to track without instruments, and the amber-grey quality never varies. Nothing decays fast enough to notice. Nothing grows. Nothing dies, visibly. The fauna do not seem to prey on each other; they may not eat at all in any recognizable way, sustaining themselves on heat and mineral and the chemical saturation of the water that is everywhere the medium of this place.

Standing in the Drowned Works, you have the sense of being inside a process that has no observer and no endpoint — a machine that has outlasted its makers and its purpose, running now on pure inertia, in a landscape that has become the machine's ideal habitat, populated by creatures that are not its product but its *symptom*, arising from its conditions the way crystalline structures arise from supersaturated solution: slowly, inevitably, in densities that leave no void unfilled.

It is quiet. The quiet of a place where all the sounds have already been made and are being sustained indefinitely. The hum. The drip. The breath. The faint percussion of settling masonry. A place in equilibrium, and in equilibrium, there is no need for anything new to happen.

You could stay. The warmth would take you. The water would find you. The animals would absorb your heat until you were the same temperature as the pipes, and then they would settle on you too, and you would become another surface in the general saturation, another slow5 process, another element in the machine's patient, purposeless operation.

It would be easy. That is what the quiet says. That is what the heat promises.
