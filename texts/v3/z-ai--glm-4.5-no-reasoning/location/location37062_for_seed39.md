# The High Seepage Plateau

At nearly four thousand meters, the land has not yet decided what it wants to be.

---

The plateau runs flat and vast — a table of stone dropped between ranges that haven't finished rising. The rock is young. That is the first thing you notice. Not the smooth ancient granite of lower country, not the patient sandstone of desert margins, but something raw and half-formed: feldspathic crust still separating from its parent matrix, surfaces that flake under fingertip pressure, edges that haven't yet been rounded by any patient force. The geology is embryonic. Whole shelves of slate lie at angles that make no structural sense — they haven't had time to settle, to find their natural failure lines. In places the ground is more like compacted aggregate than true bedrock, as though someone laid the foundation and then abandoned the project.

Which, in a way, they did.

---

The water comes from below.

Not from springs — springs imply channels, imply geological maturity, imply water that has found its route and worn it smooth. This water seeps. It emerges through every crack and pore of the unfinished rock, rising toward the cold surface where the air can't hold it. The whole plateau weeps. You see it first as a sheen: the stone underfoot is never quite dry, even when no rain has fallen for days. Then you notice the darker patches where the seepage is thicker, where the water gathers in film-thin pools across the flat, barely deep enough to reflect the dim sky. The water is clean and very cold — three, four degrees at most — and it tastes of mineral dissolution, of stone still becoming something else.

After rain, which comes frequently as low cloud pressing down against the plateau's raised back, the seepage doubles. The flat becomes a mirror broken only by emergent rock and the sparse dark shapes of vegetation. The water doesn't run off in channels; it simply sits, permeating, until the slow pressure of more water beneath pushes it onward in a sheet-flow so thin you can't see it move, only see the surfaces it leaves glistening.

There are places where the seepage has dissolved enough of the young rock to form shallow basins — not ponds, exactly, but depressions where the water deepens to ankle-height and stays. The bottoms are pale, gritty sediment. Nothing grows in it. The cold is too constant, the mineral content too raw. But life comes to the edges.

---

The vegetation is sparse but absolute in its sparsity. What grows here grows with total commitment, because half-measures don't survive at this elevation, this cold, this wet.

Dark cushions of moss — nearly black, so saturated with pigment they look like holes in the stone — cling to every surface where seepage is steady. They form ridged, corrugated mats that absorb and hold water like sponges left out under sky. Walking across them is walking across something alive and yielding; your boots press in and water rises around the footprint, and when you lift your foot the cushion slowly re-inflates. The moss is the dominant organism here, the thing that makes the place feel occupied. Without it, the plateau would be rock and water and nothing else. With it, the rock has a skin.

Higher, where the seepage thins and the wind hits harder, the moss gives way to lichen — pale grey-green crusts spreading across rock faces in slow radial patterns. You can read time in their margins: a ring of lichen four centimeters across has been growing for a century. The rock here is too young for many of them to have gotten far. In another age this plateau will be painted in them, but for now they are scattered, tentative, first attempts.

And there is something else — a plant that doesn't have a common name in any language you know, though the field guides call it *seepgrass* for lack of better. It grows in the mineral shallows, in the ankle-deep basins where cold water stands. Its leaves are pale, almost white, translucent, lying flat against the water surface like paper left to soak. It flowers nothing, seeds rarely, propagates mostly by stolons running through the sediment. It shouldn't thrive here — the light is too dim, the water too cold, the season too short. But it does, in dense local patches, and when the wind moves across the standing water the translucent leaves shift and overlap and the pale patches look like something missing, like negative space made visible.

---

The animals are the surprise of this place.

Not because they're strange — they're not, not in form or behavior, not in any way that breaks the rules of cold high wet places — but because there are so many of them, and because their presence transforms the emptiness into something else entirely.

The plateau is a crossing. That's the secret. At this elevation, between the ranges, the flat seepage-land offers the only passage for miles in any direction, and so everything that needs to move moves through it.

You see the tracks first. In the soft sediment at the basin margins, in the moss that springs back slowly after compression, in the thin mud where seepage meets stone — prints everywhere, overlapping, a palimpsest of passage. Hoof-prints of the mountain goats that cross here in groups of ten or fifteen, moving with casual competence across the wet rock, their sure feet finding purchase where yours don't. Their hair is long and rough, grey-brown, shedding water. They stand in the shallows sometimes, the water reaching their fetlocks, and drink from the seepage with an unconcern that makes you understand they belong here in a way you don't.

Smaller prints — the hares, white-coated even in the long cold season that takes up most of the year. They move at night mostly, but you see them in the dim hours too, the hours when the cloud-cover lets just enough grey light through to define shapes. They sit at the edges of the moss patches, perfectly still, ears laid flat, and they are nearly invisible until they move. Then they are gone — a white flash across dark rock, and the sound of their feet on wet stone like fingertips drumming a table.

And the foxes. Red pelage shocking against the pale rock and dark moss, they hunt the crossings. You see one every few hours if you're watching — they have routes they follow, circuits around the plateau's rim where the ground rises slightly toward the unfinished ridgelines. They move with a purpose the goats lack, a geometric intent. They know where the hares wait. They know where the moss is thick enough to muffle approach.

But the real population is the one you hear more than see. The plateau supports an insect emergence that seems, impossibly, to persist through the cold — a midge, near-microscopic, that breeds in the mineral basins by the million. Its larvae develop in the cold water, feeding on the seepgrass and whatever organic matter the seepage carries, and the adults emerge in clouds so dense they look at first like weather, like fog forming at knee-height across the flats. The goats tolerate them. The hares ignore them. The foxes eat them when they can, snapping at the clouds with an absent patience. And the midges, for their part, go about the brief urgent work of being alive, and when they die they fall back into the water they came from, and the basins take them in, and the seepgrass grows on what they were.

It's a functioning system. Sparse in species, limited in variety, but abundant in individuals and in motion. The plateau is not dead. It is not even quiet, not really — but the sounds it makes are small sounds, distributed across such distance that they arrive at the ear as texture more than volume. The drip of seepage. The shift of moss underfoot. The click of goat hooves on wet stone a hundred meters off. The wind, which is constant but not strong, pressing across the flat with a sound like breath held too long.

---

The technology is the hardest thing to explain, because it doesn't belong here, and yet it is here, and it has been here long enough that the plateau has begun to accommodate it.

At the plateau's eastern edge, where the flat rock begins to tilt upward toward the forming ridge, someone has built a pumping station. Not recently — the metal has the particular orange-brown of oxidization that takes years in cold wet conditions, decades in dry ones. The structure is industrial, no-nonsense: a concrete pad (now cracked by frost and heave), a pump house of corrugated steel, intake pipes running down into the rock through boreholes, output pipes running east and down toward something you can't see from here. The whole installation is maybe four meters on a side. Functional. Unadorned.

It runs. That's the notable thing. You can hear it when the wind drops: a low mechanical pulse, not loud but persistent, the sound of an electric pump drawing water from the seepage layer and sending it somewhere else. The power comes from somewhere too — you find the cable when you walk far enough, a black line running across the rock, pinned at intervals with steel staples driven into drilled holes. It leads north, toward whatever generating infrastructure serves this installation.

The pump house leaks heat. Not much — the cold overwhelms it — but enough that the moss grows thicker on its western wall, enough that the ice doesn't form on the metal in a thin ring around the seams. Enough that a hare sometimes shelters there, sitting in the slight warmth with its ears laid back, waiting out the worst of the wind.

The technology doesn't dominate. It isn't the point of the place. But it's present, operational, extracting something from the plateau and sending it away. Water, probably — the seepage water, mineral-rich and clean, drawn up and piped to some settlement below that needs it. The plateau gives and the pump takes and the system runs with an indifference that feels almost biological. The pump doesn't care about the goats. The goats don't care about the pump. The midges breed in the output pool where the water returns, warm and agitated, and they seem to do better there than in the cold basins, so perhaps the pump gives something back without meaning to.

---

The light is a function of the elevation and the wet.

So high, and so often in cloud, the plateau exists in a permanent condition of dimness. Not darkness — there is no true darkness here except at night, and even then the cloud-cover holds some glow from whatever distant sources exist — but a flat, even, directionless dimness that makes everything look equally near and equally far. Shadows don't fall sharply. The wet rock reflects what light there is, doubling it, making the ground surface a source as much as a receiver. Walking across the plateau in this light is walking across a grey-white surface that seems to emit from within, pale and luminous and depthless.

Color retreats. The moss is dark. The rock is pale. The water is silver. The foxes, when they cross, are the only vivid thing, and they look painted in, false, too saturated for the context. Even the rust on the pump house reads more as a texture than a hue — a brownish roughness on the metal, almost the same value as the dark stone it sits on.

Time becomes unreliable in this light. Without shadows to mark the sun's passage, without the warmth that usually accompanies daylight, the hours feel interchangeable. You could be arriving or leaving; it could be morning or late afternoon. The plateau doesn't mark time for you. It exists in its own temporal frame — geological time, seepage time, the slow time of young rock still deciding what it will be when it grows up.

---

Stand at the center of the plateau and turn in a full circle.

To the west: the ranges, still rising, their peaks hidden in the cloud that presses down. The rock there is the same young rock, fractured, raw, climbing toward some eventual height it hasn't reached. Snow on the upper slopes — or what will be snow, when the season turns. For now it's just cold and wet and very high.

To the east: the pumping station, small and functional, its cable running north like a stitch across the stone. Beyond it, the land drops, but you can't see how far; the dimness swallows distance.

To the north and south: the plateau extends, flat and wet and moss-skinned, for kilometers, for as far as the light allows you to perceive. It is not infinite. But it is large enough that crossing it takes hours, and the sameness of the terrain — seep, moss, basin, rock, repeat — creates a sense of vastness that true measurement would only confirm.

There is almost nothing here. That is accurate. And yet what is here is so thoroughly present — so completely what it is — that the emptiness reads not as absence but as concentration. The water is completely water. The rock is completely rock. The moss is completely moss. The goat standing in the shallows, drinking, is completely a goat. Nothing is partial, nothing is tentative, nothing is on its way to being something else.

Except the rock. The rock is on its way to being something else. The plateau is under construction, geological time grinding forward, the processes of formation and erosion just beginning their long argument. In a million years this flat will be something different — a canyon, perhaps, if the water finds a channel, or a desert, if the ranges rise high enough to steal the wet. For now it is this: high, cold, wet, sparse, raw, alive with small abundant life, resonant with the quiet pulse of a pump that draws water from young stone and sends it away to wherever water is needed.

For now, it is this. And this is enough.
