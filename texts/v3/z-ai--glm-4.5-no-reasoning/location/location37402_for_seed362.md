A world without water should be dead. A world of total vegetation should be drowning in it. Here, these contradictions do not resolve—they ossify. This is the Engine-Garden, a continental expanse where the substrate of reality has been entirely replaced by manufactured plant-life, operating on a dry, saline chemistry that has never seen a drop of rain.

The terrain is exceptionally low, a flat or gently undulating plain that stretches to horizons made hazy by the sheer volume of the atmosphere. There are no mountains, no valleys, only the relentless topography of growth. But this is not a forest of wood and leaf. This is a fully constructed environment. Every spire, every cable-vine, every pneumatic trunk was engineered. The entire biosphere is a factory—a continent-scale industrial machine built from genetically locked vegetal tissue. There is no soil beneath your feet, only a dense, interlocking weave of rhizome-pipes and coolant-roots, layered hundreds of meters deep. It is a made-world, down to the last molecule.

The light is blinding. A stark, featureless whiteness pours from above, not from a sun, but from the upper canopy itself. The highest stratum of this architecture is a chitin-polymer leaf-sheet, kilometers thick, which absorbs the raw radiation of the exterior void and re-emits it as a furious, actinic glare. Beneath this sky-lens, the world is bathed in a shadowless, surgical brightness that bleaches all color into shades of pale sulfur, chalk, and bruised magenta. It is cool here—the ceaseless transpiration of gases through billions of stomata absorbs the heat of the engines, leaving the air with the sharp, air-conditioned chill of a server room.

Despite the immensity, despite the continental scale of the place, it is profoundly, chokingly dense. You cannot walk between the stalks; you can only slide through the gaps. The space is saturated. Every cubic meter is packed with structural trunks, sensor-pods, and fuel-bladders. There is no sky, only the underside of the next layer. The architecture folds in on itself, a fractal packing of vegetal mechanics. It is a world of near-touching surfaces, where the pneumatic cables bulge against the coolant-pipes, and everything is vibrating at a frequency just below human hearing. 

The silence is absolute, and yet it is not empty. This is the paradox of the place: an active ecosystem buried beneath a blanket of quiet. The sheer density of the biomass dampens all sound. The heavy, fleshy walls absorb the roars of the internal combustion nodes. The hissing of high-pressure phloem is swallowed by the thick, spongy cortex of the surrounding stalks. You feel the world working—the thrum of sap-pumps beating in your chest, the subtle flexing of tendril-gears beneath your feet—but you hear nothing. It is a deafness induced by saturation. The loudest thing is the rhythmic, subsonic pulse of the continental root-network, which you register as a pressure change in your inner ear rather than a sound.

Fauna is nearly nonexistent. There are no herbivores, no predators, no birds or insects. The ecosystem is a closed loop of pure vegetal industry. The plants eat the plants. Harvester-tendrils, armored in silica-thorns, slowly reel in the older, exhausted stalks, dragging them down into the grinding compost-pits at the base layer. The only movement comes from the slow, blind seeking of feeder-roots, the mechanical uncoiling of tension-springs, and the sporadic, violent dilations of exhaust-flowers opening to vent superheated gas. It is a predator-prey dynamic stripped of animals—vegetation hunting vegetation, assimilating tissue and wire back into the loop.

And the rules of physics here are succumbing to the rot of its own scale. The weirdness sets in gradually, then all at once. The light is too bright, too flat, and it casts shadows that point in contradictory directions. You reach out to push past a cable-vine, and your hand sinks into it—not piercing the membrane, but passing through the spatial coordinates it occupies, feeling the resistance of the vine a meter deeper than your eyes tell you it should be. The air, thick with industrial pollens and aerosolized salts, refracts distance. A node-cluster ten meters away looks as if it is pressing against your face; a yawning chasm to your left is revealed, upon throwing a marker, to be a solid wall of compressed cork-bark.

Geometry has grown exhausted by the density. The Engine-Garden has packed so much made-matter into such continuity that the substrate of space itself is fraying, stretched thin by the sheer weight of presence. Gravity occasionally lets go, allowing a shower of clipped silica-leaves to drift upward for hours before being caught in the downdraft of an exhaust vent. The horizon does not converge; it simply smears, the parallel lines of the massive trunks twisting into Moiré patterns that hurt to look at. 

This is the landscape: an endless, quiet, blindingly bright engine of dry stalks and factory-vines, cool to the touch, heavy with the pressure of its own silence, weathering itself into a state of perfect, terrifying obsolescence. It is a world that has fully replaced nature with production, and in doing so, has produced a glitch in the fabric of where it is.
