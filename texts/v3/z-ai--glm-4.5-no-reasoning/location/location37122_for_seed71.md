Stand at the edge of the world’s highest plateau, where the crust has been ground down by a billion years of rain and frost into an endless, nearly flat plain. The elevation is so extreme that the atmosphere thins into a memory of sky, yet the cold here is not the sharp, killing cold of a mountain peak. It is a primordial cold, an old frost that has settled into the bedrock like dust on a forgotten altar. This is a landscape at the end of its geological life—eroded down to the root, stripped of jagged edges, smoothed into a final, immutable senescence. The horizon stretches so far that the curvature of the planet is visible, a faint, bruised line where the pale earth meets the pale sky.

It is vast. Weather does not merely pass through; it is born here, crawling across the continental expanse like slow, living thoughts. 

Yet, impossibly, the place is lush. 

A single, contiguous carpet of deep, wet moss and cold-weather fern sprawls across the ancient stone. It is a hyperborean jungle, stunted and prostrate, hugging the ground against the thin wind. The vegetation is so green it feels like an illusion against the grey monotony of the endless plateau. This greenery feeds on the strange dampness of the place. Water does not flow here; the erosion is too complete, the land too flat for rivers. Instead, moisture manifests as a persistent, heavy condensation. Every dawn, a super-chilled fog rolls in, born from unseen thermal vents deep in the crust, and wraps the plateau in a soaking embrace. Beads of water form on the hair-like fronds of the ferns, growing heavy and falling in a constant, silent drip—pocket rains that exist in isolation, a meter-wide shower surrounded by dry, cold air. 

There are no animals. Not a single insect, bird, or beast disturbs the fronds. The ecosystem moves without fauna. It is a vegetal engine, breathing through slow, turgid cycles—fronds uncurling in the condensation, retracting as the fog burns off, roots silently straining against the frost. The silence is profound, broken only by the soft, rhythmic tap of condensation falling from fern to moss, a metronome marking the slow heartbeat of an empty world.

Scattered across this infinite green-and-grey plain are the ruins of a city. It is a primitive urbanism, built by hands that knew only stone, wood, and bone. There are no soaring spires, only the low, thick foundations of a settlement that seemed to grow organically out of the permafrost. Blocks of heavy, unmarked basalt are laid in tight, spiraling patterns, chinked with the same moss that covers everything. The city is sparse. The structures are spread over hundreds of miles, separated by vast stretches of empty plateau, as if a civilization decided to build a single, continental town with miles of nothing between each home. 

Walking through the ruins, the silence deepens. There are no voices, no footsteps but your own. The architecture is organized, deliberate—there are clear roads, graded plazas, and covered cisterns designed to catch the weeping sky—but the builders are entirely absent, leaving behind only the ghost of infrastructure. 

And then, the world begins to fail.

The weirdness is in the seams of reality. At this altitude, on this deeply aged crust, the physics that holds the world together has grown tired. The closer you walk to the center of the stone ruins, the more the laws of nature fray. 

It starts with the fog. The condensation no longer falls. Instead, the water droplets hover in the air, suspended in mid-drip, catching the normal, flat daylight and turning it into a constellation of still, glassy beads. You can walk through them; they part around your chest like cold jelly, refusing to yield to gravity.

Then, the moss. In the center of the plazas, the green carpet grows upward, defying the horizontal imperative of the plateau. It spirals into the open air, forming impossible, twisting columns of vegetation that hang suspended, rooting in the empty sky. The ferns uncurl backward, their fronds retreating into themselves, time running in reverse for their cellular growth. 

The stone blocks of the ruins breathe. You place your hand on the basalt, and it is soft, warm flesh beneath your palm. A moment later, it is hard, freezing mineral. The structures are caught in an ontological loop, uncertain if they are geology or biology. 

If you listen closely through the near-silence, beneath the drip of the pocket rains, there is a sound: a low, sub-harmonic hum, felt in the teeth. It is the sound of the plateau settling into its final state, a continental-scale process of reality giving way. The sky above the city flickers, showing a brief, terrifying glimpse of a starless void, before snapping back to the flat, grey daylight. 

This is the landscape: a primordial, eroded shelf at the top of the world, lush with a wet, animal-less green, dotted with the primitive, sprawling bones of a silent city, where the weight of deep time has finally broken the rules of the world. Gravity forgets. Stone dreams. Water floats. And the cold, damp ferns keep growing, indifferent to the unraveling of the universe around them.
