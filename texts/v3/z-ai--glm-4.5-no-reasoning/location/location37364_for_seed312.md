The world is a high-altitude plateau of fresh, sharp rock, and upon it lies a city built from that same rock—vast, dense, and endlessly patient. This is a place where the crust has only recently torn open and thrust upward, so the stone beneath everything is jagged, unweathered, and unforgiving. The scale is continental; the city stretches across the plateau for miles, a dense, layered hive of hand-stacked masonry that seems to have grown organically out of the fractures and ridges of the land itself.

It is cold here. Bitterly, absolutely cold, the kind of cold that doesn't bite but rather slowly anesthetizes, sinking into the stone and staying there. There is no relief from it. The sun hangs in a thin, hazeless sky, providing light enough to see the endless grey-brown expanse, but its warmth was long ago stripped by the altitude. The air is thin and crisp, carrying the faint, metallic scent of pulverized silica and the ozone-sharpness of high places. Snow does not settle here in deep drifts; instead, the moisture manifests as a persistent, encroaching damp. There is intermittent rain—brief, stinging showers that sweep across the plateau with little warning—and a constant, freezing condensation that beads on the sheer faces of the buildings. Inside the deeper alleys, frost crystallizes in thick, bristling pelts on the walls, only to melt into dark, damp stains when the sun angles directly into a corridor. Pockets of meltwater collect in the natural cisterns carved by the young erosion into the bedrock, providing the city with its only water—clear, achingly cold, and tasting of the stone it sits in.

The city is a marvel of primitive, obsessive labor. There is no metal here, no mortar, no wheel. Everything is built of the local rock—basalts and sharp-grained granites—broken from the plateau and dressed by hand with stone and bone tools. The construction is dense, almost paranoid in its proximity. The buildings lean against one another, their walls shared, their foundations interlocking. There are no grand avenues, only narrow, labyrinthine corridors that force the wind into whistling, high-pitched groans. Stairways are carved directly into the bedrock or stacked from unshaped blocks, winding upward into multi-story tenements that block out the sky. It is a settlement that feels less constructed than aggregated, a geological formation of human intent, layered like sediment over countless generations. 

The soundscape is remarkably quiet, dominated by the low, intermittent hum of wind moving through the city's tight passages and the occasional, startling crack of thermal fracturing as the rock freezes and thaws. It is a place of slow dynamics. Change happens on the scale of centuries. A building settles over three hundred years, its doorway slowly sagging into a rhombus. A stairway is worn down a single inch over a dozen lifetimes. The people—dim, distant figures bundled in heavy, rough-woven hides—move with the patience of glaciers. There is no hurry, because hurry accomplishes little in a place so resistant to alteration. They are stoneworkers, climbers, and keepers of the condensation-traps, their lives organized entirely around the slow maintenance of the city and the harvesting of the sparse, stubborn life that shares the plateau.

For despite the harshness, the place is abundantly alive. The fauna here has evolved to fill every thermal crack and damp crevice. The base of the ecosystem is a tenacious, sparse vegetation: lithophytic lichens that crust the rocks in pale, frost-resistant patches, and hard, needle-like succulents that root in the microscopic crevices, drawing moisture from the condensation. But where the vegetation is sparse, the animal life is unexpectedly dense. The cliffs and the sheer walls of the buildings are colonized by massive, near-silent rookeries of cliff-dwelling birds—creatures with leathery wings and thick, insulating down, riding the immense thermals that spill over the plateau's edge. Their guano streaks the city walls white and green, adding a sharp, acrid smell to the cold damp. 

In the city's depths, the alleys and cisterns host a different ecology. Hairless, cold-blooded grazers with broad, sucker-like feet cling to the vertical faces, scraping the lichen and condensation from the stones. They are slow-moving, nearly invisible against the rock, yet they exist in vast, overlapping populations, their droppings pooling in the dark corners. In the deeper cisterns, where the water is darkest and coldest, pale, blind amphibians coil and uncoil in the freezing dark, feeding on the microbial slimes that bloom when the organic wash of the city drains down. At night, small, furred predators with oversized, heat-seeking eyes descend from the high roosts, moving in absolute silence through the tight corridors, hunting the grazers. 

It is a world that makes perfect, severe sense. There is nothing weird here, only the relentless logic of altitude, cold, and stone. The laws of physics and biology are followed to the letter, pushed to their limits but not broken. The city endures because it is made of the same substance as the land, and it accepts the cold and the damp as constants, not enemies. It is a landscape of waiting: the stone waiting to be worn, the people waiting out the freeze, the clouds waiting to condense. Everything is near-touching, layered, and saturated with presence, yet it feels profoundly empty—a vast, slow machine of rock and life, grinding on in the thin, cold light.
