The plateau does not end so much as it surrenders to the sky. We are at the roof of something ancient, an elevation so extreme that the air thins into a cold breath, yet the sun holds the stone in a persistent, heavy warmth. This is a primordial tableland—a landmass that has spent geological eons having its edges chewed away by wind and ice, leaving behind a vast, flattened summit of deeply aged rock. It is the end-form of a mountain, settled into its final, exhausted shape. 

The scale is staggering. The plateau extends far past the horizon, a regional expanse of open emptiness. The volume of the space is almost void; you can walk for hours and pass nothing but the same ancient, pitted stone. Yet this near-vacuum is paradoxically full. The space between presences is vast, but the presences themselves are overwhelming.

Plant life is the substrate of reality here. This is not a landscape with vegetation; it is vegetation that happens to have geology. A single, contiguous organism—a monstrous, high-altitude analog of seagrass or bamboo—has colonized the entire plateau. Thick, segmented stalks, as wide as silos and ribbed with silica, rise in isolated groves separated by miles of bare rock. They do not grow in clusters; they are strung out across the emptiness, miles between each stand, yet their root systems are interconnected, threading through the fissures of the primordial stone. The canopy is a single, shattered ceiling of grey-green blades tens of meters up, fluttering and rasping against one another. 

Moisture here is a matter of condensation and patience. Rain falls in intermittent, vicious squalls that flash across the open plateau and are gone in minutes, but the real water is harvested from the clouds. The giant stalks are covered in deep, vertical flutes. As the warm, damp air of the lower altitudes pushes up the sheer cliff-faces of the plateau, it hits the cold stone and condenses. Water perpetually weeps down the grooves of the plants, pooling in the hollows of the rock at their bases. These scattered, shallow rock-pools—black and rich with tannin—are the only water for a hundred miles in any direction, isolated oases in the vast emptiness.

Around these pools, the fauna gathers, and they are the true rulers of this place. The animal biomass is staggering, an overwhelming density packed into transient, roving masses that drift between the water sources. They are arthropodic, scaled to the vegetation—six-legged, chitin-plated grazers the size of draft horses, with mandibles designed to shave the silica-rich bark of the stalks. They move in processions of tens of thousands, a living river of carapaces that flows across the dead stone, driven by an ecosystem in constant, desperate motion. Predator and prey are locked in an endless, visible cycle; the ground around the pools is churned to a fine, bloody dust by their constant passage, the air thick with the scent of crushed enzyme and damp chitin.

The noise is a physical weight. It is deafening, a layered roar that never ceases. The foundation of the sound is the wind, which moves unimpeded across the flat expanse, hitting the stalks and making them groan and crack like the rigging of a thousand dying ships. Above that is the ceaseless, tearing rasp of ten million mandibles chewing the plant-flesh, a sound like the landscape being slowly filed away. And beneath it all, the thrumming, subsonic vibration of the roots, pulling water through the substrate, making the very stone hum underfoot. 

Embedded in this raw, biological engine are the faintest marks of something other. The scattered remains of primitive habitation. At the base of some stalks, small cairns of flat stone have been stacked to create windbreaks, their mortar just the adhesive sap of the plant. Flaked hand-tools of obsidian—too soft to pierce the grazers' chitin, useful only for carving the green wood—litter the perimeters of the pools, trod into the mud by the constant march of the herds. The builders are gone, perhaps absorbed by the ecosystem they tried to survive, but their single-room lean-tos, woven from the shredded fronds of the canopy, still lean against the stalks like broken ribs. They are nothing against the scale of the plateau, merely artifacts left in the mud.

It is an unusual place. The weirdness lies in the sheer contradiction of its existence: the emptiness that is somehow deafening, the flattened, primordial geology playing host to a savage, hyper-dense ecology. It is a world at its end and its most violently alive simultaneously. To stand on the plateau is to stand on a plain of ancient, rain-scoured stone, staring up at a rustling, alien canopy, while a tide of clicking, breathing bodies flows past your knees, driven by the endless condensation dripping down the stalks into the dark, cold pools of the world's roof.
