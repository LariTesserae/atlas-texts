The plateau hangs.

Not floats—hangs, as a stone shelf hangs from a cliff face, anchored by laws that have less to do with gravity and more to do with intention. It is a space no larger than a cathedral nave, perhaps thirty meters at its longest axis, walled on three sides by drop-offs that terminate not in valleys but in a heat-shimmer void, a thickening of the atmosphere that refuses to resolve into distance. The fourth side abuts a rib of dark, freshly-upthrust rock—vesicular, glassy in patches, still exhaling mineral warmth from whatever event brought it to the surface. The stone has the look of something broken rather than worn: edges catch light with a sharpness that has not yet been dulled by wind or water, and the fractures run in patterns too regular to be entirely geological, too deep to be entirely artificial.

Water does not pool here. It exudes.

Every surface sweats—stone, soil, the undersides of leaves. Not from rain, though rain has passed and will pass again, but from the air itself, which sits at the wet-bulb limit of what lungs can tolerate. Moisture condenses on contact, beading on the obsidian fractures in curtains that slide and re-form faster than surface tension should allow. The rock weeps. The soil beneath the thin skin of vegetation is not mud but a supersaturated sponge; step and water rises around your boot not from below but from all directions at once, the ground drinking from the atmosphere and giving back immediately, a perpetual exchange that makes the boundary between solid and liquid a matter of opinion. The air is so full of water that breathing feels like swallowing, and yet the heat—dry-bulb heat, radiant heat from the sun-normal light that pours down unobstructed—presses against the wet like a hand pressing against a wound. The two forces do not cancel. They stack. The result is a pressure that is not meteorological but almost gravitational, a weight that has to be leaned into.

The vegetation does not grow. It occurs.

There are perhaps forty individual plants on the plateau, each separated from the others by stretches of bare, sweating stone or by expanses of that saturated soil that want to be water but have not yet committed. They are not trees, though two of them have woody trunks as thick as a human thigh, branching not into limbs but into bundles—sheaves—of flat, strap-like leaves that hang vertically and weep their own moisture in continuous threads. The others are lower, broader, structures like collapsed umbels or open hands pressed flat to the ground, their surfaces dense with small stomatal pores that gulp the wet air and exhale something that is not quite oxygen—a fraction lighter, a fraction sweeter, carrying an undertaste of copper and ozone. The roots are visible where the soil is thin: not brown but a deep translucent green, like glass packed with chlorophyll, branching in fractal iterations that extend not just downward but outward, interlinking where two plants' territories touch, forming a mat that is more network than anchor. When the light shifts—when a cloud that does not block the heat but diffuses the direct beam passes across the sun—the leaves reorient, not tracking the light but tilting toward the water, presenting their broadest faces to the wettest air current, and the whole grove seems to breathe in unison, a slow vegetative inhalation that is visible as a wave of darkening and lightening green.

The sound is the sound of water moving.

Not falling—there is no stream, no cascade. But the condensation on the stone produces a continuous whisper, a sound like a thousand small mouths drinking, and the threads falling from the strap-leaves add a lower register, a guttural plip, plip that varies in pitch with the thickness of the thread and the resonance of the surface it strikes. Between these sounds there is silence, but not the silence of absence—the silence of restraint. The place is quiet the way a held breath is quiet: there is activity, process, the continuous exchange of water and gas and light, but it happens below the threshold of noise, or at a frequency that ears do not register as sound. Occasionally—a few times per hour, perhaps—there is a click, sharp and dry, from the direction of the rock rib. It might be thermal fracture. It might be something adjusting.

The structures are the rock's fault.

Or they are built into the rock, or they are what the rock becomes when it reaches the surface. Along the base of the cliff-face, where stone meets plateau, there are formations that are too regular to be natural and too integrated to be added after: horizontal slots, each the width of a hand, spaced at intervals that correspond to no mathematical sequence recognizable to conventional architecture but that produce, when viewed in aggregate, a pattern that the eye wants to resolve into a face or a diagram. Some of the slots contain objects—nodules of the same glassy mineral, each etched with grooves at the nanoscale, visible only as a slight iridescence, a rainbow sheen that shifts when the humidity changes. There is one larger opening, man-height, not a cave but a recess, its back wall occupied by a surface that is not stone: it is dark, slightly warm to the touch, and it flexes, barely, when pressed, like a membrane over fluid. It does not reflect light but absorbs it, and in the dimness at the rear of the recess, the absorbed light seems to move, to circulate, as if the surface is not a wall but a window into a medium that carries illumination the way water carries current.

Nothing moves that is not rooted or condensed or falling.

This is the strangeness of the fauna: it is not absent but stationary. There are, on the undersides of the broader leaves, clusters of small bodies—sessile, pale, each the size of a rice-grain, arranged in rows that follow the leaf's vasculature. They are not eggs, or if they are eggs, they are eggs that have been eggs for a very long time. They do not respond to touch, to light, to the water that streams past them, but they are warm—warmer than the leaf, warmer than the air—and they vibrate, each one at a frequency too high to hear but palpable if a fingertip is held close, a minute tremor like a heart beating at the limit of smallness. Whether they are animal, machine, or some category that does not distinguish between the two is not clear. They are the only fauna visible, and they might be the only fauna that exists here, or they might be the larval or dormant form of something that emerges under conditions that have not yet obtained—a spike in humidity, a drop in temperature, a particular frequency of light.

The plateau has weather.

Not the weather of open sky but the weather of enclosed space, a microclimate that generates its own cycles. The heat builds until the saturated air can hold no more, and then—without cloud, without warning—the water precipitates out, not as rain but as a simultaneous condensation, a flash-wetting that drenches every surface in a single instant, the straps and the stone and the slots in the cliff and the pale clusters on the leaves, and then the heat reasserts and the water lifts, not by evaporation but by something faster, a phase change that bypasses gas and goes directly to the clear, the invisible, the weightless. Between these cycles, the air moves in slow rotations, a convective spiral centered on the dark recess in the cliff, and the plants track it, and the threads of water bend toward it, and the nodules in the slots shimmer in response to whatever field the spiral carries, and the plateau breathes, in its way, and the breath is the weather, and the weather is the process, and the process is the place.

It is small, this plateau. Cathedral-sized. A room with walls of void and a floor of sweating stone and a congregation of forty plants and a silent liturgy of condensation and a altar-recess that looks into something that is not a room and not a sky but a medium, a carrier, a whatever-it-is that the technology of this place interfaces with—the technology that is the rock and the slots and the nodules and the dark membrane and the pale clusters and the roots that link green to green in a web that has, at its center, not a plant but a process, an exchange, a computation that happens in the language of water and heat and light and that produces, as its output, this place, this hanging wet hot sharp-edged small sparse alive quiet active strange plateau, which is not a landscape so much as a function—a function that is currently evaluating, and that will, when it finishes, produce a result that is not yet visible, but that is, even now, taking shape in the way the nodules shimmer and the clusters vibrate and the membrane flexes and the void thickens at the edges, where the plateau ends and the rest of the world—if there is a rest of the world—has not yet been calculated.
