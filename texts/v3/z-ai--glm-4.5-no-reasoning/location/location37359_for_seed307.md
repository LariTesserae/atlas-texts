It begins with a descent into heat.

You find it by accident—a fissure in the high plateau, a slit barely wide enough to admit a body, angled down into the mountain's young flesh. The rock here was still magma within living memory. Its flanks possess that newborn sharpness: edges that haven't yet been apologized for by wind or time, silicate faces split along clean planes, surfaces that catch light like broken glass. The elevation is extreme—the plateau itself sits at a height where the air should be thin and cold. But not here. Not in this pocket.

This place is a single contained void, a bladder of space embedded within the mountain, no larger than a modest room. A micro-environment sealed on all sides by volcanic walls that weep continuously. Not from rain—there is no rain this high—but from condensation. The rock is so hot that any moisture in the trapped air transforms against its surfaces. The walls breathe out heat; the air gives back water. The stone runs with it. Thin, sheeting flows descend every face, joining at the seams into threads, pooling in the corrugations of the floor. The water does not cool the rock. It simply rides it, evaporating as quickly as it accumulates, filling the chamber with a suffocating wetness—a humidity that sits on the skin like a hot, damp hand. The atmosphere is dense, saturated, the air so thick with vapor that the far wall shimmers and doubles even though the space is barely three paces across.

There is no vegetation. Not a single cell of photosynthetic intent. No green, no root, no spore-taking patience. This is a mineral world, pre-vegetal or post-vegetal—it doesn't matter which—alive only in ways that don't require stillness or sunlight. And it is overwhelming.

Because the chamber moves.

The fauna here is not present in the sense of something you observe. It is present in the sense of something that fills a space completely, the way water fills a vessel. They are flat, rust-colored things—between a lung and a leaf and a fluke—that cling in layers to every wet surface. They overlap like shingles, like scales, pressing edge to edge so tightly that the rock beneath is completely obscured. Their surfaces glisten with the same moisture that runs down the walls; they drink it directly, they breathe it, they need nothing else. When they shift—and they shift constantly—the sound is like wet leather sliding over wet leather. But even that is quiet. The whole chamber is quiet. The waterfall-seep makes no noise; the flows are too thin, too slow. The fauna move without vocalization, without the click of shell or the scrape of claw. Just that faint, organic friction, and the low, wet sound of their feeding.

Because they are feeding.

The rock is fresh, and they eat it. Their undersides secrete something—acid or enzyme, some chemistry that belongs to a kingdom science hasn't named—that dissolves the silicate surface into a mineral paste. They draw it in through their flat bodies, processing the mountain itself, converting newborn stone into rust-colored life. The chamber is an ecosystem of consumption: the rock exudes heat, the heat generates water, the water sustains the fauna, the fauna eat the rock. The process is visible at the edges of their colonies, where the sharp young stone is being smoothed away, its planes rounded into something organic by ten thousand mouths working in silence.

The density of life here is total. They cover the walls, the ceiling, the narrowing passages that lead deeper. They cover each other. Three deep in places, four deep, the lower layers breathing slowly while the upper layers drink and feed and occasionally split—reproducing by fission, by budding, by some method that involves the parent flattening until it is two adjacent children. The chamber seethes with them, though the seething is slow and quiet and wet, more like the movement of a single organism than a crowd of individuals.

And here is where the world becomes something other than itself.

The heat does something to the space. Or the density does. Or the pressure of so many living surfaces pressed so close together in so small a chamber, all of them feeding, all of them growing, all of them exuding—something reaches a threshold. The walls begin to lose their certainty. Not visually—not at first—but spatially. The chamber, which is small, which you can touch on both sides if you extend your arms, begins to feel large. The distance to the far wall fluctuates. You reach toward it and your hand arrives too soon, or too late, or passes through a region where the air resists like water. The fauna on the walls seem at moments to be the size of your palm and at other moments the size of your thumbnail, and both perceptions are true simultaneously, the way a word repeated until it loses meaning still remains a word.

The weeping rock is weeping more than water now. The condensation carries something else—tiny mineral crystals, dissolved and re-forming, that hang in the air and catch the ordinary light that filters through the entrance slit. In this vapor, the geometry of the chamber is suggested rather than enforced. The floor, which your feet confirm is level, appears to slope. The ceiling, which your hands confirm is close, recedes in your vision like the vault of a cathedral. The fauna, who have lived here longer than you, seem to navigate by touch alone, their flat bodies flowing over surfaces that you can see but cannot quite reach, as if they occupy a chamber that is adjacent to yours, overlapping but offset by some fractional dimension.

Time participates in the breakdown. The fauna move at their slow, wet pace, but occasionally a pocket of time seems to slip—a section of the wall moves faster than its neighbors, a colony pulses and splits in a sequence that your eyes assemble wrongly, cause and effect arriving out of order, the parent budding after the child has already separated. These glitches are small. They are quiet. They do not announce themselves. But they accumulate, and after enough time in the chamber—a minute, an hour, you cannot tell which—you begin to understand that this is not a place where reality has broken. It is a place where reality is being *processed*. The fauna are eating the rock, and something in the rock's dissolution, some mineral lattice or crystalline memory, is leaking into the air, into the water, into the space between one moment and the next, and the geometry of the world is digesting it badly.

You are in a stomach. Not a biological one—the metaphor is too clean. You are in a cavity where a mountain is being eaten by a life-form that has no name, in a heat that generates its own weather, in a wetness that comes from nowhere and goes nowhere, and the byproduct is a local failure of space to hold its shape. The quiet is the quiet of digestion. The darkness at the edges of the chamber is not shadow but the places where dimension has given up and the walls have folded into themselves.

And the fauna keep feeding. Layer upon layer of rust-colored patience, clinging to faces that were sharper yesterday, drinking mineral paste, breathing hot vapor, pressing close to each other in the intimate darkness, waiting for the mountain to finish becoming something else.
