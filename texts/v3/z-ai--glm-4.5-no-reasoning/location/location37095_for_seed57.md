A pocket folded shut inside the world's water-bearing skin.

You find it by touch, not sight—a slight thickening in the cold, a place where the current forgets its direction and hangs suspended. There is no shore. There is no surface overhead, or if there is, it has receded beyond the reach of any light that might survive the descent. The water here exists in degrees of darkness: the thin black where your own hand disappears an inch from your face, and the deep black beneath that, where the pocket truly begins.

The walls—there are walls, or something like them—annunciate themselves by pressure. The space is small. Smaller than a room, smaller than a coffin, smaller than the space between two heartbeats. It might be a cavity eroded into the flank of some submerged calcified mass, or a bubble of gas that never escaped the basalt when it cooled, or a wound in the water that never healed. The dimensions shift when you don't test them. When you reach, you find the ceiling, the floor, the left wall; everything is close, everything is pressing in, and the water fills the space so completely that the word *fill* is meaningless—there is no room between presences, no gap where water is not. You are not submerged in it so much as you are *held* by it, the way a thought is held by the mind that thinks it.

And yet it is not empty. Not remotely.

The vegetation came first—or was always here, which is the same thing in a place without time's ordinary permissions. It grows from every surface in pale, cool ropes and flattened fronds, the kind of growth that has never known photons and has organized itself around other currencies: the faint electrical murmur of the enclosing rock, the chemical gradients that weep from hairline cracks, something else that has no name in surface botany. The tendrils are white—not the white of death or absence but the white of things that have never needed color, the white of deep-sea worms and cave salamanders and roots exposed by accident to light they never asked for. They layer over each other, packing the walls, the ceiling, the floor, growing inward because there is no other direction to grow. In the center of the pocket, where there should be open water, the tendrils from opposite walls have met and interlocked, weaving a dense central mass like a heart that the space grew around. They move, but slowly—the curl of a frond takes hours, the retraction of a tendril that has encountered something unfamiliar takes longer. Their patience is geological. They are not waiting for anything; they are simply continuing.

The temperature sits just above the threshold where water would slow to honey. Cool, but not cold enough to kill. The chill has a texture—a slight mineral sharpness, like holding a penny on your tongue. It comes from somewhere below, from whatever the pocket is embedded within, and it is steady, indifferent, the kind of cold that does not fluctuate because it has no reason to. Nothing here fluctuates. The pressure is constant. The darkness is constant. The only things that change are the plants, and they change at the speed of sedimentation.

And then—less often, and less reliably—there is the other movement.

It begins as a failure of geometry. You reach toward the left wall and your hand arrives at the right wall, though you did not cross the space between. The central mass of vegetation is before you and then beside you and then inside your peripheral vision where nothing should be, and when you turn it is gone and the space is larger than it was, or smaller, or the same size but oriented differently, as though the pocket has rotated around some axis that does not correspond to any you can sense. Distance misbehaves. The tendrils that were close recede without moving; the walls that were touching your shoulders are suddenly an arm's length away, though you heard nothing shift and felt no current of displaced water. 

This is the reality breakdown, and it is not dramatic—it is quiet, intimate, domestic in its horror. The pocket does not reject the rules of the world above; it simply does not know them. It is a place where the rules arrived late and took root only partially, like seeds scattered on stone. Some things hold—water is wet, mass has weight, the tendrils grow toward chemical warmth—and some things do not hold at all, and the boundary between what holds and what does not is not a line but a weather, a fog that rolls in and out according to some atmospheric logic of its own. You can never be sure which rule will fail next, or when the next pocket of wrongness will open in the corner of the space where the tendrils are thickest and the darkness has a quality of *depth* that is not merely optical.

The sounds, such as they are, are all wrong for this. There should be the groan of pressure, the hiss of water moving against itself, the low tectonic murmur of whatever formation encloses this place. Instead there is almost nothing—a faint rhythmic pulse that might be the central mass of vegetation cycling through some metabolic process, and, very occasionally, a sound like a page turning, though there is nothing here that reads. The quiet is so complete that it becomes audible itself, a high fine ringing that might be your blood or might be the sound that silence makes when it is under pressure, compressed into a small space alongside everything else.

The artifacts, when they appear, are incidental and unexplained. A single ceramic shard embedded in the tendrils on the ceiling, its glaze still intact, its edges sharp—too sharp to have spent any time in water, too sharp to have been eroded by the patience that has rounded everything else in this place. A length of something that might be cord or might be root, tied in a knot that no natural process produces, vanishing into the central mass. Once, perhaps, the suggestion of a groove worn into one wall at hand-height, as though something had been dragged repeatedly across the same path—but the groove was gone when you looked again, or it was never there, or it has migrated to a different wall. The artifacts do not accumulate. They do not suggest habitation. They suggest instead that the pocket's relationship with the rest of the world is permeable in ways that go both ways—that things fall in sometimes, from wherever, from whenever, and the pocket absorbs them the way it absorbs everything: slowly, completely, without distinction.

The fauna—there is something else here. Not an animal, exactly, but a presence that moves with more intention than the tendrils and less form than anything you could name. It is felt as a displacement, a region of water that resists slightly when you reach into it, a warmth that moves through the cold like a cloud through sky. It circles the central mass. It has been circling for longer than you have been here—perhaps longer than anything has been here, perhaps since the pocket opened, if it ever opened, if it was not simply always a closed curve in the water with something circling inside it. It does not approach. It does not retreat. It maintains its orbit the way a planet maintains its orbit: not through choice but through the simple fact that orbiting is what it does, is what it is. The tendrils do not reach toward it. The artifact, if there is one, does not reference it. It is the pocket's sole inhabitant and it is indifferent to everything, including the geometry failures, including the darkness, including you, should you be here, should you be the kind of thing that can be in a place like this.

Time does not move here in the way it moves above. Or rather—it moves, but slowly, at the pace of the vegetation's growth, at the pace of the circling presence's orbit. A hundred years might pass between the unfurling of one pale frond and the next. The ceramic shard might have fallen in yesterday or in an era before there was a word for yesterday. The pocket does not care about duration; it cares about fullness, about remaining packed, saturated, every cubic millimeter occupied by water or tendril or presence or the small localized voids where the geometry fails and the rules come undone. It is a place that has filled itself completely and then stopped, or never started, or simply continued being full in the way that a stone continues being hard: not as an achievement but as a condition, fundamental and unremarkable and very, very quiet.

In the deepest part of the darkness—which is also the center of the space, which is also where the central mass is densest, which is also where the geometry is least reliable—there is a quality that is not any of the things described above but is the thing from which they all emerge. It is the pocket's nature, its verb, the thing it *does*: it holds. It holds water, it holds vegetation, it holds cold, it holds darkness, it holds whatever falls in from the impossible outside, it holds its single circling presence, it holds its shape even when its shape fails, it holds itself closed. It is a place that has decided to be a place and continues deciding, slowly, patiently, with the persistence of something that has nowhere else to be and no other mode of existing. It is a pocket in the water. It is very small. It contains everything it needs.
