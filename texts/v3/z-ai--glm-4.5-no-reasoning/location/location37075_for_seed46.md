You descend into the crevasse not by choice, but by the slow, grinding mercy of the basalt shelf splitting apart. Down here—far below the scorching high-plateau sun, embedded in the fault line of a world still knitting itself together—lies a pocket. A room. A cavity barely ten meters across, sealed by overhanging stone and the oppressive weight of the mountain.

It is brutally hot. The temperature reads like a furnace left overnight. The stone walls radiate a deep, bone-aching thermal lag, baking the air until it shimmers and warps. Yet, there is no water here. Not a drop of condensation on the black glass walls, not a trace of moisture in the superheated air. This is an anhydrous womb. The air tastes of ozone and pulverized metal, dry as a calcified lung. 

And yet, the place is overgrown.

A dense, riotous ecology clings to every fissure and bulging vein of the raw ceiling. But this is not vegetation as the surface knows it. With no water to drink, these flora have evolved a non-aqueous chemistry. They are lithotrophic and electro-synthetic—fat, glistening cords of polymerized sulfur and crystallized copper that thrust their root-hooks directly into the stone. They bleed orange and seep white, their tissues made of hydrated minerals and heavy organic salts. They look like the exposed intestines of some buried giant: pulsing slowly, densely packed, a lush canopy of flesh-pink and verdigris that carpets the ceiling and drapes down the walls in heavy, turgid loops. There are no animals. No insects, no grazers, no skittering things. The silence is profound, broken only by the deep, sub-audible thrum of the rock and the occasional, wet *click* of a mineral-pod swelling shut.

Underneath this alien canopy, the space is terrifyingly sparse. The floor of the pocket is a vast, echoing expanse of fresh, unaltered geochemistry—jagged, obsidian shards and rough, chalky berms of newborn feldspar. The erosion parameter is nearly zero; this place is embryonic, still being born. The tectonic stresses that opened this crack are still at work, and the floor feels raw, like an open wound that has not yet scabbed over. There is so much empty space between the sparse geologic features that the tiny room feels like a cathedral. There are no ruins, no artifacts, no marks of hands or tools. 

But there is industry.

Embedded seamlessly into the raw, emerging stone are engines. They are not built; they are *grown*, or perhaps *excavated*—exposed by the splitting rock as if they were pre-existing organs of the planet itself. Thick iron cylinders the size of a human torso sit in shallow hollows, bolted into the bedrock by living veins of crystallized brass. Flywheels turn at agonizingly slow RPMs, grinding through arcs of dense, sulfurous air. Pipeworks—copper and a strange, brittle titanium-alloy—snake from engine to engine, carrying no steam, but a visible, static-laden plasma that crackles with a harsh, industrial blue light. It is mechanized, scaled production running on geological time. The machines are electrified, humming with a vast, patient potential, but they move at the speed of tectonic drift. Pistons compress by millimeters an hour. Gears index forward once every few minutes with a deep, resonant *clang* that vibrates through the soles of your feet. 

This is a factory whose production cycle is measured in epochs.

The light is normal, yet deeply wrong. A fissure high above allows a shaft of standard, midday sun to spear down into the pocket. It illuminates the space with mundane clarity, casting sharp shadows from the engines and the hanging flora. But the geometry those shadows fall upon does not hold. The reality here is breaking down. The heat, the raw geology, the impossible industrial engines grinding in the deep—they are tearing the local rules of space apart. 

If you stare at the far wall, the sparse berm of feldspar seems to loop behind itself, existing in two overlapping states of elevation at once. The angles where the iron cylinders meet the stone suddenly contain more degrees than they should, opening into non-Euclidean hyper-volumes that make your eyes water. The hanging, lush cords of sulfur-vines occasionally flicker out of existence, their silhouettes detaching from the light and hovering in mid-air before snapping back to the ceiling. The floor beneath the slow-turning flywheel is not quite solid; pebbles dropped near its base seem to hang for a fraction of a second too long, suspended in a glitch of local gravity, before drifting downward like dust in water.

You stand in a hidden, slow-motion engine room at the top of the world, buried in the planet's open wound. It is a place of terrible heat and absolute dryness, where a lush, alien flora drinks electricity from raw stone, and ancient machines mill over their invisible tasks with geological patience, quietly unraveling the fabric of space around them. The quiet is the worst part. It is the quiet of a world waiting for a process to finish—a process that will take ten thousand years, and will end in the birth of a new physics.
