You descend into it through a crack in the world—a thermally expanded seam between massive, ancient blocks of fitted stone. It is a pocket, a hidden city folded into the earth's basement, no larger than a great hall but stretching on without apparent end, compressed into a dimension that excuses its modesty. The scale is intimate, almost claustrophobic, yet the space inside feels cavernous. This is the Encysted Settlement, a place built within the geological itself.

There is no water here. Not a drop. Not in the air, not in the stone, not in the veins of anything living. The humidity reads at absolute zero. Your lips part and the air steals moisture from your breath so quickly it feels like inhalation is drying you from the inside. And yet—it is lush.

The vegetation defies all aqueous logic. This is a dry biome running on non-aqueous chemistry, a solvent of liquid siloxane and heated atmospheric gases that sweats from the porous ceramic walls. The plants are not green. They are the color of oxidized copper, of rust, of deep magmatic orange—a palette of heat absorption. They grow in fractal cascades, leafless branches that terminate in glassy, heat-reactive nodules. They do not wilt; they calcify if they die, becoming permanent mineral scaffolding for the next generation. Their roots do not drink; they sublimate. They press flat against the stone, absorbing thermal energy directly from the rock and pulling silicate compounds from the air itself, metabolizing through a chemistry of fire and mineral rather than water and carbon. The growth is thick, a strangling abundance of dry vines and barrel-shaped succulents that ring the columns of the city, but it is an emptiness that contains them. Between each cluster of rust-palms and silica-ferns, there is nothing. Vast, hollow gaps of near-vacant air, so that the vegetation stands like islands in an ocean of vacant, shimmering heat.

It is hot. Unbearably so. The temperature sits just below the threshold of combustion, a steady, oppressive 2.8 out of 3. The stone—weeping a pale, powdery talc from its erosion—radiates heat like a closed oven. The settlement sits low in the earth, built into a depression of fitted basalt and fired clay, and the heat pools here, trapped by the low ceiling of the pocket-dimension. There is no breeze to dissipate it, only the slow, churning convection of superheated gas rolling over itself.

And yet, it is established. The erosion parameter is high; this is not a new place, nor a ruined one. It is in its own state, weathered into a balance. The urban construction is primitive but organized—stacked megaliths of heat-fired adobe, arches cut from single blocks of vesicular stone, streets paved in crushed garnet. The tech level is barely above the Stone Age: hand-chipped obsidian blades left wedged in doorframes, levers of carved bone, woven baskets of mineral fiber. But the construction is dense, layered, a true settlement. Someone—or something—built this city with simple tools and immense patience, fitting blocks so tightly that the thermal expansion locks them into monolithic walls. There are no animals. Not a single beast of burden, not an insect, not a bird. The streets are empty of fauna. The only movement is the vegetation, perpetually growing, perpetually retreating in the extremity of the heat, and the slow crawl of the city’s own thermal mechanisms.

Because it is active. The ecosystem is in constant motion, driven by heat cycles. As the temperature micro-fluctuates—rising and falling by fractions of degrees as the earth breaths—the siloxane solvent in the walls boils and condenses. The sound is tremendous. A low, rhythmic throb of expanding stone, like the heartbeat of a leviathan, overlaid with the sharp, crackling hiss of mineral sap sublimating on the hot rock. The dry vines creak and pop as they shift, their glassy nodules clicking against one another like castanets. It is a loud place, a roaring silence filled with the mechanical groan of a city that is slowly, endlessly, breathing.

The light is dim. The only illumination comes from the vegetation itself—a dull, thermoluminescent glow from the rust-palms, a sickly amber bioluminescence that barely pushes back the dark. The deeper streets are choked in shadow, lit only by the faint, infrared shimmer of heat rising from the pavement. It is like walking through the guts of a living furnace, where the walls are lined with dry, thriving life and the air is a solid thing that presses against your skin.

The weirdness settles in as you walk further. The streets don't quite connect. A left turn brings you back to a place that looks similar but whose stones are older, more deeply eroded. The vegetation seems to track your heat signature, nodules tilting toward you like blind faces seeking the warmth of your body. You are in a pocket, a micro-environment embedded within the crust, and the rules here are its own. The city is a wound in the rock that refuses to heal, kept open by the slow growth of fire-adapted flora and the endless, groaning expansion of the heat. It is a lush, empty, deafening furnace of a town, hidden from the sun, thriving on nothing but the world's own deep warmth.
