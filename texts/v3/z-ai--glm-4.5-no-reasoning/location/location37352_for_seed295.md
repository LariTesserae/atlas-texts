To stand here is to stand inside the throat of something vast and living, though there is nothing natural about its origin. Every surface—the ground beneath, the walls that recede into darkness above, the braces that cross and interlock at impossible distances—is made. There is no exposed stone, no soil, no raw earth. The entire environment is a construction, a fabricated megastructure that has stood for so long that it has become a geology of its own invention. Its erosion is not collapse but settlement: the slow, patient finding of equilibrium. Beams have bowed to their final resting postures; channels have widened to the exact width of the water that has worn them; floors have acquired a topography of dips and hollows worn by the passage of ten billion feet over spans of time that make the concept of "centuries" seem breathless. The structure has finished its breaking. It is now in its own state, balanced, permanent.

And yet, it is warm. The warmth is the first thing you feel against your skin—a deep, mammalian heat that rises from somewhere within the architecture itself, as though the metal and ceramic and fibrous matrices of the construction are conducting the slow respiration of some buried engine, or perhaps simply retaining the thermal memory of a sun that has not directly touched this place for ages. The temperature is a steady, enveloping thing, the warmth of a greenhouse, of a body, of a closed system that breathes in and out across decades.

That breath is the water. The moisture here does not fall as rain in any recognizable sense. Instead, it manifests as a slow, cyclical condensation. Deep within the darkness above—where the ceiling, if there is one, vanishes into a black haze of heat and density—unseen masses of humid air meet cooler strata of the built environment. Water beads on the undersides of vast platforms, accumulates along the edges of girders, grows fat and heavy, and then releases. The drops fall not as weather but as architecture: a grid of precipitation determined entirely by the layout of the construction above. Some corridors receive a steady, rhythmic drip—slow, metronomic, each drop landing in a worn steel channel where the water has carved a path so smooth it shines. Other zones receive only a fine mist that drifts down through the lattice of walkways and terraces, settling on surfaces as a perpetual dampness that never quite pools, never quite dries. And in the deepest hollows, where the built terrain steps down into basins and cisterns that were perhaps once reservoirs, water stands in dark, still masses, their surfaces broken only by the concentric ripples of distant drips arriving at long intervals.

The light is nearly absent. What illumination exists is biological and incidental—the faint, cold phosphorescence of fungal colonies that have claimed the damp vertical surfaces, the occasional glow of lichen that has evolved to convert the structure's residual heat into a dim, greenish radiance. These light sources are scattered, uneven, providing not illumination but mere reference points in the dark. You navigate by warmth and by sound.

And the sound is deafening.

This is not the mechanical roar of machinery—there is no technology here, no devices, no engines that anyone would recognize. The sound is entirely organic, and it is the sound of overwhelming presence. The fauna have made this place their own, and their numbers exceed comprehension. In the darkness, life churns. The air itself thickens with the wingbeats of insects whose populations number in the trillions—they move in clouds so dense that they generate a low, resonant hum that vibrates in the chest cavity, that blurs the boundary between hearing and feeling. On every platform, in every channel, within every hollow and conduit, things move and call and breathe. There are amphibians by the millions, their voices rising in polyrhythmic choruses that overlap and interfere, producing beating patterns that surge and fade across minutes. There are creatures that click and chatter from the structural joints, their sounds reflecting off the metal and ceramic surfaces in cascades of echo that multiply a single call into a hundred, a thousand. The air is so thick with spores and pollen and the particulate matter of ten thousand species that it has texture; you breathe it in and it coats the throat, settles in the lungs. The atmosphere is a living medium.

And through all of this, the structure endures. The built environment does not merely shelter this explosion of life; it *is* the terrain on which the ecological drama plays out. The high platforms and the deep cisterns create the elevation, the verticality that divides this place into climatic zones—warmer below, where the heat collects and the water stands; cooler above, where the air moves and the condensation forms. The constructed channels guide the water, and the water guides the life, and the life, over eons, has worn the construction into forms that serve the life. The boundary between the made and the grown has dissolved. A beam that was once straight now bows under the weight of a century's growth, its surface so colonized by flora and fauna that the original material is visible only in cross-section where some stress has fractured it—and even there, in the crack, something small and many-legged has already made its home.

The scale of the place exceeds the individual. It is a landscape, a region, a territory that extends past any horizon you might try to locate. The darkness ensures that you can never see its limits; you can only infer them from the way the sound changes as you move—how the character of the chorus shifts as you ascend or descend, how certain frequencies dominate in certain strata, how the deep pulse of the large, unseen creatures grows fainter as you climb and the shrill, ceaseless whine of the insects softens as you descend. You understand the place through its gradients: warmth increasing as you go down, moisture clustering where the construction offers condensing surfaces, life thickening wherever water and heat and structure align to create niche.

There are no events here. The dynamic is glacial. Species succeed one another across spans that would erase civilizations. A fungal bloom that overtakes a platform does so over decades; the vines that reclaim it do so over centuries; the slow erosion that finally fractures the beam and sends a section of the platform tilting occurs over millennia. And in the interim, the sound continues, deafening and unceasing, the aggregate voice of a biosphere so abundant that it has become white noise, a roar, a single continuous sound that is the signature of this place—the sound of life at such density that it has become indistinguishable from the sound of the structure itself settling, breathing, enduring.

This is a world that was built, but it was built so long ago that the builders belong to a category of time that no longer has names. What they made has become a mountain. And the mountain has become a world.
