You ascend, and the world thins.

It has always been ascending here. The plateau stretches at an elevation that should not support breath, where the atmosphere presses thin and cool against the skin like a forgotten wool shawl. The air tastes of oxidized minerals—iron, copper, something nameless that sits bitter on the back of the tongue. There is no moisture. Not a drop. The clouds that occasionally smear the pale sky never deign to touch this place; they pass like ships hull-down on a distant sea, and the rain that falls from them evaporates long before it could reach this ancient shelf.

This is a place that has finished happening.

Erosion has worked its will here across eons so vast that the concept of "geological time" feels almost juvenile—a child's measurement against the true depth of this stillness. The mountains that once stood have been ground to their roots. The valleys have been filled and leveled. What remains is a plateau of such immense horizontal scope that the horizon pulls away in every direction, a clean line where the pale amber sky meets the pale mineral earth. The surface is a crust of fine particulate and exposed strata, laid down in alternating bands of ochre, ash-white, and a strange bruised violet. It crunches underfoot with the consistency of old bone. Nothing grows here except the occasional lichen-like film—a gray-green scum that clings to the north faces of exposed rock, so sparse it seems more like a discoloration than a living thing. It does not spread. It does not retreat. It simply is, has been, will be.

And yet—despite the vacancy, despite the mineral desiccation—this place is *full*.

They are everywhere.

The fauna. The presence.

At first glance, the plateau appears strewn with stones. But the stones have joints. They have eyes—lensed and dry, without moisture or lash, set beneath ridges of chitin. They have legs, tucked flat against their bodies in configurations that do not correspond to bilateral symmetry or radial order but to some older geometry, some pattern that existed before the current template of life settled into its familiar grooves. They are the *thrum*, though they have no name because naming requires distinction, and there is no distinction here: they are a population, a saturation, a density of living bodies so complete that to walk is to step among them, to feel the dry click of carapace against boot-sole, to watch the nearest shift two centimeters to accommodate your presence without urgency, without alarm.

They are the size of fists, of skulls, of sheltering stones. Their forms vary—not wildly, but enough to track, if you had the patience of a geologist. Some are low and ovoid, with feeding orifices that press flat against the mineral crust, drawing sustenance from chemical gradients in the rock itself. Others are taller, articulated, bearing simple manipulatory appendages that click and tap against each other in patterns that might be communication or might be mere contact—a confirmation of proximity in a world where sight is less reliable than touch. Their shells match the terrain: ochre and ash-white and bruised violet. Camouflage is too active a word for it. They do not hide. They simply *belong* to the surface, as the surface belongs to them.

There are millions. Perhaps more. Across the open scope of the plateau, they cluster in the shallow depressions where the mineral crust is thin and the chemical warmth of deeper strata leaks upward. They line the edges of every subtle contour. You notice the structures.

They are not built by human hands. They are built by *hands*—by those clicking, tapping appendages—but the tech is primitive in the deepest sense: not crude, but primordial. Cairns. Rows of stacked mineral fragments, no higher than your knee, arranged in patterns that seem to follow the chemical veins in the bedrock. Low walls, curving, that enclose nothing and defend nothing but perhaps mark the boundaries of feeding territories. Occasional pits, dug shallow, lined with shell-casings of the dead—their own molted carapaces used as architecture. There is no metal here. No fabrication beyond placement. The thrum have stacked one thing upon another and called it shelter, or altar, or boundary, and the plateau is laced with these modest constructions, scattered at intervals of twenty meters, fifty, a hundred—denser near the chemical hotspots, absent on the barren flats. They are old. They are *so* old. The mineral fragments have begun to fuse at their points of contact, bonded by slow chemical deposition over spans of time that make the oldest human ruins seem like yesterday's chalk marks.

But something is wrong with the horizon.

You have been walking for what feels like hours, and the horizon has not changed. The plateau is flat; the view should be consistent. But the consistency is *too* perfect. The distant formations—the shapes that mark the edge of sight—seem to repeat. Not identically, but like variations on a phrase, like a landscape that is being *written* rather than simply existing, and the scribe has grown tired, has begun to reuse motifs. You look to your left, and the cluster of low mounds there is the same cluster you passed an hour ago—or its sibling, its echo. The thrum underfoot shift and click, and you realize with a slow cold certainty that they are not moving *out of your way*. They are moving *into positions you have already seen*. The same configuration. The same individual—how could you tell?—in the same relation to the same cairn.

The reality here is not breaking. It has *broken*, long ago, and what remains is the settled debris of that rupture, a landscape that has achieved equilibrium not through health but through petrifaction. The plateau exists in a state of frozen recursion. Space folds here, gently, like a creased map, and you are walking along the fold. The thrum do not notice because the thrum have always lived along the fold; their biology is native to this glitch, their simple neural architectures calibrated to a world where distance is negotiable and direction is a suggestion. They navigate by chemical gradient, by the taste of iron and copper and the nameless bitter mineral, and those gradients are consistent because the *rock* is consistent—the deep strata, the ancient laid-down bands, they do not recurse, they are the baseline reality against which the surface has slipped.

You stop walking. The thrum click and settle. The wind whispers.

Nothing changes.

You could walk for days, and you would find the same cairns, the same clusters, the same stub of violet stone with the same gray-green film on its north face. You would encounter the same individual thrum—or one indistinguishable from it—pressing its feeding orifice against the same mineral crust, drawing the same chemical sustenance. The cool air would press the same thin shawl against your skin. The horizon would retreat at the same pace, offering the same views, the same subtle repetitions.

This is the end of process. The plateau has reached the state that all eroded landscapes approach but never quite achieve: true stillness, true equilibrium, where the gradients have flattened and the forces have balanced and nothing remains to drive change. But it has achieved it *wrongly*, has arrived at stillness not through the exhaustion of energy but through a structural failure in the medium that holds space. And so the stillness is haunted by recursion, the equilibrium propped up by repetition, and the thrum—millions upon millions of them, covering the plateau like a living mineral layer—are not inhabitants of the landscape but *components* of it, their biological processes as much a part of the geological equilibrium as the chemical gradients they feed upon.

They do not react to you with fear or curiosity because you are a transient instability. You will pass. They will remain. The cairns will slowly fuse, the shells will slowly stack, the film will slowly spread its gray-green non-growth, and the horizon will offer its gentle, maddening variations on a theme to whatever walking things occasionally ascend to this impossible height and find, at the roof of the world, a place that has already ended and is simply waiting for the news to travel.
