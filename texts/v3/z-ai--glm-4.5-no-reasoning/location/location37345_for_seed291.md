The cold arrives not as a wind, but as a damp exhalation. It seeps upward through the iron grates and upward through the mud, a chill that tastes of rust and wet cellulose. This is a low place, a basin city built on the unconsolidated humus of a river delta that never quite finished forming. The land beneath the streets is still becoming—embryonic clay shifting over half-formed sandbars, the delta's final geometry undecided. The streets themselves seem to sense this; they buckle and incline at strange, unplanned angles, as if the architecture is slowly slipping off the back of a half-born continent.

It is packed. There is no empty space, no gap between structures. The city is a saturated medium—brick against stone against ferroconcrete against living vine. Buildings don't stand separate from each other; they merge, their walls shared, their foundations interlocking like the chambers of a wasp's nest. And into every crevice, every available surface, the vegetation forces itself. It is lush beyond reason: great curtains of moss hang from fire escapes, kudzu-like lianas throttle the smokestacks, and pale, cold-rot fungi fruit in luminescent shelves from the underside of every overpass. The green is not gentle. It is a pressure, a biological force as relentless as the industry it surrounds.

The industry is everywhere, yet it produces mostly silence. The factories are running—you can feel them. The low, subsonic grind of ball mills and the slow churning of industrial mixers propagate through the ground more than the air, felt in the molars and the sternum. But airborne, the sound is strangely absent. The dense vegetation absorbs it; the damp, cold air deadens it. What reaches the ear is a hushed, wet complexity: the drip of condensation from steam pipes, the soft tap of falling leaves on corrugated tin, the crackle of frost forming on glass. It is the quiet of a factory floor at midnight, multiplied across a hundred square miles.
