# The Maw

It begins as a wound in the mountain.

Not a cave—those have dignity, architecture, the pretense of invitation. This is a gash, a vertical split in ancient schist that opens into something the rock cannot quite close. Twelve meters wide at its throat, narrowing to four where the light surrenders. The walls weep. They have wept for millennia, and their tears have carved channels too fine for fingers to trace—hairline rills that feed the black moss, that feed the pale fungi, that feed everything that has never known the sun.

The warmth rises from below. Not the warmth of breath or blood, but the patient exhalation of stone that has spent geological ages absorbing what the planet's heart offers. The air enters cool at the maw's mouth and thickens as it descends, taking on weight, taking on the mineral stink of iron and sulfur and something else—something that has no name because no language has needed one. It is the smell of life that forgot light existed.

---

The descent is not measured in steps. There are no steps. The passage offers itself in body-lengths: the width of a shoulder turned sideways, the span of arms reaching for purchase on opposite walls, the length of a torso slithering through a constriction that the rock spent forty thousand years perfecting. The stone is smooth where it should be rough. Erosion has not been kind here; it has been *thorough*. Every edge that could cut has been rounded. Every shelf that could support a foot has been worn to a slouch. The mountain wants you to continue downward, and it has removed every argument for stopping.

The water collects in pockets that the hands find before the eyes do. Shallow basins in the floor—no, the *approximation* of floor, since gravity becomes a suggestion more than a law in the deeper sections—where condensation gathers into something almost drinkable. It tastes of the stone it was born from. It tastes of time. There are no streams here, no flow, only the endless slow transfer of moisture from air to rock to moss to air again, a cycle so patient that no single observation could prove it happens at all.

---

The sound begins before the life reveals itself.

A low vibration first, felt in the sternum before the ears can claim it. Then texture: chitin clicking against stone, in quantities that make the individual click meaningless. It is the sound of ten thousand legs finding purchase on wet rock, of mandibles working at fungal husks, of bodies the size of thumbnails shifting in masses that have no upper limit you can determine. The sound does not echo. The walls absorb it, or the bodies absorb it, or the darkness itself has grown tired of returning what is offered.

In the deeper chambers—there are chambers, sudden widenings where the schist yields to something older and softer, a calcareous marrow the mountain secretes to fill its own fractures—the sound takes on organization. Clicking becomes rhythm. Rhythm becomes the communication of things that do not need light to see each other. You are not hearing noise. You are hearing *conversation* in a language that predates the concept of language, chemical and tactile and older than the mountains themselves.

---

They are everywhere.

The walls move when you look at them directly, which means you learn not to look at them directly. The pale cave isopods—translucent, leggy, their carapaces the color of the stone they rest on—cluster in densities that redefine the word. A square hand-span of rock may support forty bodies, layered atop each other in a living sediment, each consuming what the layer below excretes, each excreting what the layer above requires. It is not parasitism. It is not symbiosis. It is something for which biology has no prefix, a relationship so total that the individual becomes a fiction the surface world invented.

The centipedes are longer. A hand-span, two, their segments clicking as they navigate the isopod fields without apparent purpose or urgency. They are not hunting. They are not fleeing. They are *patrolling*—maintaining the chemical boundaries of territories that were established before human memory and will persist after it. Their antennae sweep the air in patterns too slow to track, reading pheromone maps that record every visitor, every temperature shift, every breath of foreign air that has entered the system in the last generation.

And below them—always below, always in the strata that the light cannot reach even when it is present—the colonies of pale, eyeless beetles whose purpose remains opaque. They do not eat the moss. They do not eat the fungi. They do not eat each other. They *move*—in coordinated masses that suggest intelligence without revealing its object—toward something, or away from something, that no surface-dwelling observer has ever identified. Their density is the density of grain in a silo, of sand in a dune, of matter that has surrendered the notion of individual form.

---

The fungi are the architecture.

Threadlike, bone-pale, they extend from every surface in patterns that mimic the fractures they inhabit. Where the rock splits, the hyphae enter; where the hyphae enter, the rock splits further—not from pressure, but from the slow chemical negotiation between living mineral and mineral life. It takes centuries. The fruiting bodies emerge at intervals the human mind registers as random but the cave itself knows as tidal, tied to pressures and temperatures that shift with the breathing of the mountain. They are small. The largest is the size of a child's fist, and it glows—not with light, exactly, but with a luminescence so faint that the darkness merely *thickens* around it rather than retreating.

This is the only light the deep chambers have ever known. It is not enough to see by. It is enough to *confirm*—to verify that the darkness is a presence and not an absence, that it has texture and weight and can be differentiated from the simple lack of photons. The fungi illuminate the nearest layer of isopods, the nearest centipede segments, and nothing more. The rest remains theory.

---

Time behaves differently in the maw.

Not faster or slower—*thicker*. The air's humidity, the warmth's constancy, the sound's unvarying texture: they conspire to erase the markers by which duration is measured. A hour feels like four. A hand-span of progress feels like a journey. The creatures around you do not change—do not sleep, do not wake, do not alter their patterns in any way that acknowledgment of time would require—and their constancy becomes infectious. You stop expecting transition. You stop looking for before and after. The cave is what the cave has always been, and the slight modifications—hyphal extension of a millimeter per year, the settling of a condensation droplet into its basin over the course of an afternoon—are so gradual that they exist only as logical inferences, never as witnessed events.

The maw does not grow. The maw does not shrink. The maw *persists*, and the persistence is the defining fact of every life within it. The isopods do not evolve; they *equilibrate*. The fungi do not spread; they *saturate*. The mountain does not erode; it *negotiates* with the life it contains, each side conceding fractions of a millimeter per millennium in a compromise that will outlast any observer foolish enough to try to measure it.

---

At the deepest point the passage allows—a widening no wider than a man's outstretched arms could span, where the condensation falls as a perpetual cold rain onto stone that has been polished to the consistency of glass—the sound organizes into something almost harmonic. The click of ten thousand carapaces finding rest. The rustle of ten thousand legs beginning motion. The subsonic thrum of the mountain exhaling through cracks it has maintained since before the sea chose its current level. And beneath it all, quieter than quiet, the sound of the pale beetles moving in their unfathomable purpose, their coordinated bodies producing a friction so slight and so constant that it registers not as sound but as *warmth*—the additional fraction of a degree that the deep chambers possess over the throat of the maw, the heat of life in quantities too vast for any individual to generate.

You could stay. The warmth permits it. The creatures permit it; you are neither predator nor prey, neither obstacle nor resource, simply another surface for condensation to collect on, another source of the carbon dioxide the fungi accept as tribute. The darkness permits it because the darkness is not hostile—only *complete*, only the logical conclusion of a space that has had no reason to invent the concept of illumination.

The maw does not want you. The maw does not want anything. The maw simply *is*, in the way that mountains are and oceans are and the blind, white, clicking life within them is—the unexamined infinity that proceeds with or without witness, that has found a shape it can hold for a million years and intends to hold it for a million more.
