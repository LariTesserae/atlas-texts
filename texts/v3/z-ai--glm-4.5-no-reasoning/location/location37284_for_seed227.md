# The Drumming Hollow

You find it by sound before sight — a low, rhythmic concussion felt in the sternum before the ears parse it as sound. The entrance is a cleft in a hillside, half-hidden by a curtain of stiff, dark moss that has learned to grow downward instead of up, seeking the faint warmth that bleeds from the stone.

---

## The Space Within

Step through and the world inverts. The hollow is roughly ovoid — perhaps thirty meters at its longest axis, fifteen at its widest — a pocket torn from the limestone some geological age ago and never repaired. The ceiling is low in places, forcing a stoop, but rises in the center where a natural shaft opens upward like a throat. This shaft is the source. This shaft is the drum.

Wind moves across the hilltop above — constant, cold, west-northwest — and the shaft drinks it. The cavity resonates. Not a whistle, not a howl, but a deep, bone-frequency thrum that pulses once every three seconds, regular as a heartbeat, loud enough that you must raise your voice to be heard even at arm's length. On stormier days they say you cannot hear yourself scream. The sound has texture: a primary boom, an undertone that rattles loose calcite from the walls, and a harmonic shimmer that lives higher in the register, thin and steady as a struck bell's decay.

Your ears adjust, but they never fully adapt. There is always the pressure, the sense that the air itself is being rearranged around you with each pulse.

---

## The Walls and Their Weeping

The stone is old limestone, grey-blue and softened by time. Erosion has been at work here for millennia — the walls are rounded, the edges abolished, everything worn to a comfort of contour. No jaggedness remains. The hollow has reached its equilibrium, its most stable self. Water manifests not as streams or pools but as a condition of the air and surface: condensation beads on the walls in countless droplets, each one trembling with each pulse of the resonance. They grow heavy, slide, collect in thin films that trace ancient paths down the rock face — paths so consistent that the stone beneath them is grooved, channelled, a record of water's patient sculpture over centuries.

In the coldest months, the condensation freezes into crusts of clear ice that coat the lower walls. They melt and reform. The process takes weeks. Nothing here is quick.

At the base of one wall, where the stone meets the floor, a thin seam of water pools in a trough carved by its own presence — no deeper than a finger, never drying entirely, fed by the weeping walls above. It is the hollow's only standing water, and it reflects nothing because the light here does not permit reflection.

---

## The Green That Persists

Lush, but a lushness of the cold and dark. This is not the exuberance of sunlit forests. This is the stubborn, slow-motion profusion of things that have found a niche and filled it without urgency.

Moss is the sovereign — *Sphagnum* and *Andreaea* and forms that may have no common name, growing in dark cushions and trailing veils, thriving in the constant damp and the resonant vibration that seems to stir their spores. They cover most surfaces in a pelt of deep green, almost black in the dimness, and where they are absent, the stone looks strange, almost wounded.

Liverworts cling in flat, overlapping plates, their bodies pressed so tight to the rock they seem part of it. Fungi thread through the moss networks — pale mycelia visible at the edges of the cushions, and occasional fruiting bodies: small, rubbery brackets of shelf fungus pushing from crevices, grey and lavender, slow-growing, long-lasting. Some are years old and no larger than a thumbnail.

In the central area where the shaft opens above, and where the faintest light descends on overcast days, a stand of stunted ferns grows from a pocket of accumulated organic soil — *Asplenium*, perhaps, or something related, adapted to the cold and the acoustic assault. Their fronds are stiff, leathery, dark-veined, and they vibrate visibly with each pulse of the drum, trembling in a way that seems voluntary, as though they have learned to move with the sound rather than resist it. Around their bases, a dense mat of leaf litter and humus builds slowly — decades to accumulate an inch.

---

## The Scattered Remains

Someone lived here. Not many, and not long, but long enough to leave marks.

Against the far wall — the wall opposite the entrance, the most sheltered, the quietest corner if any corner can be called quiet — there are the remains of a sleeping platform: flat stones arranged in a rectangle, padded now by decades of moss growth but still discernible as intention. Beside it, a fire ring, shallow and blackened, though the soot has long since been absorbed into the stone's porous face. The hearth's placement is precise — centered beneath a slight overhang where the smoke would be drawn up toward the shaft. Whoever built this understood the hollow's breath.

Tools, or fragments of them: a hand-sized cobble of chert, chipped on one edge, possibly a scraper, abandoned in a crack as though set down for a moment and forgotten. A length of antler — deer, likely, though old and porous — that may have served as a pick or a hammer. Near the water trough, a shallow depression in the stone floor shows wear patterns consistent with grinding, though whether for grain, pigment, or medicine, the residue is too old to say. Nothing metal. Nothing woven that survived. The technology here is the technology of patience: stone, bone, fire, and the long familiarity with a place that most would find unbearable.

There are marks on the wall above the sleeping platform. Not pictographs, exactly — too abstract, too sparse. Grooves cut into the limestone with a harder stone, some vertical, some diagonal, grouped in clusters. A tally? A calendar? A record of the sound's rhythm, transcribed by someone who needed to count something that could not otherwise be measured? They resist interpretation, but their placement — at eye level, where one would see them upon waking — suggests they mattered.

Whoever they were, they are long gone. The moss has reclaimed most surfaces. The hollow does not remember them, or if it does, it remembers them only as another slight perturbation in its long equilibrium.

---

## The Living Sound

The fauna is sparse, and for good reason. The drumming is not kind to ears built for subtlety.

Still — life persists. In the thin water of the trough, pale flatworms glide, their senses attuned to vibration more than light. Springtails leap between moss cushions, their populations tiny but stable. Occasional cave spiders spin sparse webs in the upper crevices where the air is stillest, though the webs must be continually repaired after strong gusts send resonant shudders through the stone.

Once, you might see a bat — a single bat, not a colony — clinging to the shaft wall near the opening, its body compressed tight, its ears folded, enduring the sound for the shelter the hollow provides. It hunts outside at dusk and returns to the resonance as to a bed it cannot leave. There is something in the sound, perhaps, that it has grown to need.

Nothing else of size lives here. The deer do not enter. The foxes investigate the entrance and retreat. The sound repels most, and what remains is what has been shaped by the repelling — the tolerant, the minute, the adapted.

---

## The Quality of Being There

It is cold. Not the sharp cold of wind, but the steady cold of stone that has absorbed winter after winter and surrendered its warmth reluctantly. Your breath is visible, briefly, before the resonance tears it apart and disperses it. The air smells of wet limestone, loam, the green-vegetal must of living moss, and underneath all, a faint mineral sharpness — ozone, perhaps, or some electrostatic effect of the vibrating air.

The darkness is not absolute. On a grey day, light from the shaft reaches the floor in a diffuse column, just enough to see by once your pupils have dilated fully. At night, there is only the sound, and the sense of the walls around you, and the trembling of the water droplets on the stone, each one a small lens catching nothing.

Time moves strangely in the Drumming Hollow. The pulse is so regular that it becomes a clock, and you find yourself measuring all events against it — the drip of water taking four beats, the settling of stone taking twelve, your own heartbeat struggling to sync with the cavity's or resisting the sync. The place changes, but slowly — on the scale of lichen growth and water wear and the subtle accumulation of humus. A human lifetime is a brief event here. The hollow was old before the sleeper came, and it has been old longer since.

You could stay. There is shelter, in a fashion. There is water, of a fashion. There is nothing here that will kill you quickly. But the sound — the sound does not stop. It enters through the chest and the teeth and the fluid in your inner ear, and it reorganizes something in the architecture of attention. After hours, you stop hearing it as sound and begin feeling it as condition, as weather, as the basic fact of the environment, the way gravity is a fact. And then, when you finally leave — stepping back through the moss curtain into the wind and the silence — the absence of the drum is its own shock. The world outside feels flimsy, unmoored, too quiet to be real.

The hollow lets you go. It does not care. It goes on resonating, patient and ancient and largely empty, filled only with its own enormous sound and the small, tenacious life that has learned to live inside it — which is, when you think about it, a kind of music.
