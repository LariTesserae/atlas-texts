# The Weeping City of Shengao

## The Terrain

The city stands on a high continental plateau, a tableland so vast that its far edges dissolve into haze and atmospheric distortion. The plateau itself is ancient—a formation of metamorphic schists and water-saturated shales that have been settling into their current geometry for geological ages. The rock does not merely hold water; it *exudes* it. At every seam, every bedding plane, every hairline fracture in the stone, moisture emerges. Not in dramatic gushes or geothermal spurts, but in a constant, patient weeping—a film of water that never ceases, that has never ceased in any living memory, that may have been flowing since before the word *living* had any application here.

The plateau tilts, almost imperceptibly, toward the south and east. Over millions of years, this gradient has organized the weeping. The water finds paths. It traces the schist's foliation, follows the cleavage planes, pools in the shallow depressions that erosion has patiently carved. The entire upper surface of the plateau is wet—never flooded, never dry, but coated in a sheen of water thin as skin, thick enough to reflect the grey-amber sky in broken, skittering mirrors.

From the plateau's edge, the land falls away in massive escarpments—not cliffs exactly, but long, degraded slopes of talus and exposed shale, themselves weeping, themselves threaded with the countless threads of water that have emerged from the rock and begun their slow descent toward the lowlands that nobody in Shengao has visited in generations. The escarpments are too vast, too broken, too wet to descend safely. The city stays on the plateau. Everything stays on the plateau.

## The Structure of the City

Shengao is built *into* the wet rock, not merely upon it.

The earliest constructions—which the current inhabitants call the Deep City, though they are not its builders—were cut directly into the shale. Vast chambers, corridors, vertical shafts, horizontal galleries. The cuts are precise in a way that suggests machine excavation, but the machines are gone, if they ever existed in a form the current inhabitants would recognize. The Deep City extends down at least forty levels—someone has counted, though the lower levels flood regularly and the counting becomes theological. The chambers are large enough to hold factories, warehouses, dormitories, assembly halls. They are too large, in fact. The ceiling heights seem designed for something taller than human. The doorways are wide enough for three to walk abreast, and the thresholds are worn smooth by—by what? By the passage of bodies, by the flow of water, by time, which in Shengao is another form of water: patient, pervasive, unstoppable.

Above the Deep City, the current city rises in a dense conurbation of stone and ferrocement and riveted iron. The buildings are thick-walled—the wet demands it. Any structure with thin walls becomes saturated within a season, the interior sweating as prodigiously as the rock itself. So the buildings are massive, brutalist in the oldest sense of the word: piles of material assembled for the primary purpose of staying dry inside. Windows are narrow, set deep in their embrasures. Roofs are pitched steeply, sheathed in corrugated iron that rings and drones under the constant drip, but the water runs off them in thin curtains, feeding the channels between buildings, feeding the gutters and the cisterns and the catchments that every structure incorporates, because the water must go *somewhere* and in Shengao it is always already going.

The city is layered. The streets at ground level—the "wet level"—are open to the sky and to the weeping. They are paved in granite sets, uneven, worn slick by the constant film. Channels run along every street, cut into the stone, carrying the water in organized flows toward the great drains that puncture the Deep City and discharge into the escarpment slopes. Above the wet level, walkways and bridges connect the upper stories of buildings, creating a second street network—the "dry level," though it is only relatively dry, only less wet. Above that, in the industrial districts, catwalks and pipe-bridges and conveyor gantries create a third layer, the "iron level," where the factories vent their steam and exhaust and the workers move in shifts between the plants.

The density is extraordinary. From any vantage point, the city fills your field of view completely—there is no horizon, only the near horizons of walls and windows and iron balconies and the further walls beyond them, receding into the wet haze. The buildings press against each other, share walls, bridge across streets, lean together like drunks in a congregation. There are no parks, no open spaces, no gaps. The city is a solid, interrupted only by the thin canyons of its streets and the wider gorges of its industrial yards.

## The Water

There is no rain in Shengao. Or rather—there is rain, sometimes, heavy and grey, but it is almost redundant. The city's wetness does not depend on rain. The rock beneath weeps constantly, and the atmosphere above condenses constantly, and between the two, the city exists in a state of permanent saturation.

The condensation is perhaps the stranger phenomenon. The plateau is high—high enough that the air above it is often cooler than the mass of wet air that moves across it from the lowlands. When this air contacts the plateau's surface, it condenses. Fog forms. Not the thin, aesthetic fog of literature, but a dense, wet, *presence*—a fog that leaves droplets on every surface, that drips from eaves and lampposts and the corners of doorframes, that soaks into clothing and stone and the mind itself until everything in Shengao carries the particular weight of being never-quite-dry.

The inhabitants have organized their entire material culture around this fact. Fabrics are tightly woven, coated in waxes and oils derived from the Deep City's stores. Footwear is wooden, elevated on platforms and cleats that keep the sole above the flowing film. Buildings incorporate drainage at every scale—from the channels that line each hallway to the great arterial sewers that run beneath the main thoroughfares. There are professions devoted entirely to the maintenance of dryness: *shuoshi*, the channel-keepers, who clear the gutters and repair the seals; *mianze*, the moisture-readers, who assess the integrity of waterproofing in the Deep City's chambers; *qiao*, the bridge-wardens, who ensure the dry-level walkways do not become wet-level walkways, as they inevitably tend to do.

The sound of the water is constant and layered. At the wet level: the rush and gurgle of the channels, the splash of footsteps on wet stone, the dripping from a thousand eaves and ledges and fire-escapes. At the dry level: a quieter but unremitting *patter*, the sound of condensation forming and falling on rooftops and ironwork. At the iron level: the hiss of steam venting from factory pipes, mixing with the wet and creating localized fogs that add their own acoustic dampening, their own muffling, until the roar of a steam-hammer becomes a thick, wet *thump* that travels through the air like a heartbeat through fluid.

The total sound is not deafening, but it is inescapable. It fills the silences between speech and machines. It is the sound the inhabitants were born into, and they do not hear it—except when they leave the city, which few do, and then the silence of dry air feels like deafness, like a subtraction so total it is itself a kind of noise.

## The Absence of Green

Nothing grows in Shengao. Not plants. Not in any recognizable sense.

The rock is sterile—or rather, it is sterile of chlorophyll, of photosynthesis, of that particular chemistry that makes green things green. The water that weeps from the stone carries mineral salts, iron compounds, sulfur traces, silicates in solution. It is not hostile to life, but it does not nourish the kind of life that puts down roots and reaches for light. There is no soil. The surfaces are bare rock, ferrocement, iron, shale. Even where dust and debris accumulate in sheltered corners, even where organic matter from the city's inhabitants settles and might, in another world, form a substrate for growth—nothing takes hold. The wet is too constant, the mineral content too high, the light too diffuse through the perpetual condensation.

What does grow—what *proliferates*, in fact, with a vigor that the inhabitants find variously ordinary, unsettling, or alarming—is fungal.

The fungal presence of Shengao is not a single species but a complex of organisms—mycelial networks, fruiting bodies, lichen-like colonizations, gelatinous films—that have adapted to the city's conditions with an exhaustiveness that borders on intention. Black shelf-fungus clings to every wooden surface, breaking down the platform-shoes and the door-frames and the interior paneling with patient, unstoppable industry. Pale threads of mycelium spread through the mortared joints between stones, loosening them, creating new passages for water, which creates new habitat for fungus, in a cycle that the masons fight and lose and fight again. In the Deep City, where the chambers flood and the ventilation fails, vast sheets of bioluminescent fungus colonize the ceilings, feeding on the mineral water, emitting a faint blue-green glow that provides the only light in those forgotten zones. The glow pulses slowly—on a cycle of minutes, not seconds—as if the organism breathes at a geological tempo.

And there are the *fangyun*—the "cork-blooms"—that grow in the wettest streets and channels. They are rubbery, orange-brown masses, like collapsed balloons or discarded lungs, that inflate slowly with the water that flows through them, filtering it, digesting something from it, and then deflating to release a clearer, slightly warmer outflow. The channel-keepers dislike them because they slow the water's passage and cause localized flooding, but the older inhabitants say the fangyun keep the channels from eroding, that their gentle pressure seals the joints, that they are a kind of maintenance the city needs. There are arguments about this. There are always arguments in Shengao about the relationship between the city and the life that lives upon it.

## The Fauna

But the fungus is only the substrate, only the foundation. What makes Shengao overwhelming—what gives it the density of presence that visitors (the few, the rare, the bewildered) cite as the city's defining characteristic—is the animals.

Shengao is full of animals. Not pets, not livestock, not vermin in the ordinary sense, but a dense, multi-layered, self-organizing ecology of creatures that have lived in and on and under the city for as long as the city has existed—perhaps longer, if the Deep City's original inhabitants included non-human forms.

The most visible are the *haifu*—large, hairless, wet-skinned creatures that move through the streets at all hours, occupying the wet level with a confidence that suggests prior claim. They are roughly the size of cattle, but low-slung and broad, with wide, flat feet that spread on the wet stone and grip it. Their skin is grey-black, always glistening, always damp—they appear to derive moisture directly from the air and from the surfaces they contact. They have small, dark eyes set high on their heads, and broad mouths that they press to the wet ground, drinking the film of water, consuming whatever organic matter has settled there. They are not aggressive. They are not tame. They simply *are*, in the way that weather is: omnipresent, undiscussed, accommodated.

The haifu crowd the streets. They lie across doorways, blocking them until someone—an inhabitant, a worker, a child—steps over or around them with the practiced ease of long habit. They occupy the squares and intersections in loose congregations, breathing slowly, their broad sides slick and reflective. They excrete a thick, grey paste that the channel-keepers sweep into the gutters and that the fungal networks consume with evident relish. The cycle is closed: the haifu eat the city's waste, and the city's waste-eaters eat the haifu's waste, and the water moves through all of it, carrying the transformed matter onward, into the drains, into the Deep City, into the rock, into the long slow processes of mineralization that will, over geological time, make new shale, new schist, new stone to weep new water.

Above the haifu, in the dry level and the iron level, the *laizao* flock. They are not birds—they have no feathers, no beaks—but they occupy the ecological niche of birds with an thoroughness that suggests either convergent evolution or a very old joke. They are small, leathery, warm to the touch, with membranous wings that fold tight against their bodies and propel them through the narrow street-canyons with erratic, darting speed. They roost in every crevice, under every eave, behind every pipe and conduit and iron bracket. Their calls are high and clicking—not songs but signals, a constant chatter that adds another layer to the city's soundscape. They eat the small things—the *xianchong*, the "thread-worms," pale and legless, that wriggle in the wet film on every surface and are themselves so numerous that a single square meter of Shengao street contains, at any moment, thousands of them, microscopic and nematodean and fecund beyond counting.

And below, in the Deep City, in the flooded chambers and the forgotten shafts, there are larger things. The inhabitants have names for them—*shenyu*, "deep-fish," though they are not fish; *kui*, "the-swallowers," though no one has seen one swallow anything in living memory—but the names are placeholders for ignorance. The lower levels flood and drain on slow cycles tied to the plateau's subtle hydrology. When the water rises, things ascend from below. When it falls, they retreat. Occasionally a haifu will be found in a lower chamber, drowned, its body bloated and colonized by the blue-green fungus. Occasionally the water that drains from the Deep City carries a taste—iron-rich, organic, strange—that suggests processes below that no one has mapped.

The fauna is not separate from the city. It is not *in* the city the way a rat is in a wall or a pigeon is on a ledge. The fauna is *of* the city, constituted by its conditions, inseparable from its materials. The haifu's skin is chemically similar to the ferrocement. The laizao's wing-membrane incorporates mineral salts from the condensing air. The thread-worms digest and re-deposit the stone-dust that the water carries. The city built itself, and then the life came, and then the life and the city became the same system, and now nothing—not the inhabitants, not the machines, not the water itself—can be understood in isolation.

## The Industry

Shengao manufactures. It has always manufactured. The factories occupy the largest structures in the city—windowless, iron-clad, venting steam and smoke from stacks that rise above the iron level and disappear into the perpetual condensation. The steam adds to the wet. The smoke is absorbed by the fog. The outputs of the factories are invisible from the street, known only by the rhythm of the shifts and the flow of materials in and out through the great loading bays at the wet level.

What is manufactured? The inhabitants would say: *everything*. But a visitor would notice that almost everything manufactured in Shengao is made to endure wet. The waterproofing compounds. The sealants. The treated fabrics. The elevated footwear. The corrosion-resistant alloys that the factories produce in massive, clattering runs, pouring molten metal into molds and quenching it not in oil but in the channel-water, which flashes to steam and adds its hiss to the city's continuous sound. The machines that make other machines, all of them sheathed in brass and bronze and the strange grey alloys that Shengao's metallurgists have developed over centuries of working with metal that must never rust, must never corrode, must hold its form in a world where water is as constant as gravity.

The factories run on steam. The boilers are fed by the Deep City's stores—vast deposits of a carbonaceous shale that burns hot and slow, that may be coal, that may be something older, that the miners bring up in carloads from the deeper levels where the fungus has not yet colonized and the flooding is manageable. The steam drives the engines, the engines drive the lathes and presses and hammers, the hammers stamp out the plates and rivets and gears that hold the city together. The sound of industry is not separate from the sound of water—it blends with it, becomes it. The rhythmic thump of a stamping press is indistinguishable, at distance, from the rhythmic drip of water from an iron beam. The hiss of a vent is the hiss of rain that does not fall but condenses. The city's soundscape is a single, complex, unbroken texture, and industry is a thread within it, not a disruption.

The workers move in shifts—three shifts, eight hours each, though the hours are measured by the factory clocks and not by the sun, which is rarely visible through the condensation and which, when it is, provides little warmth at this elevation. The workers wear the treated fabrics and the platform-shoes and the waterproofed head-wrappings that Shengao's own factories produce. They are lean, wet-skinned (everyone in Shengao is perpetually damp; it is a condition of existence, not a complaint), and quiet. The work is repetitive, the factories are vast, the products are necessary. There is no ideology of industry, no narrative of progress, no dream of automation. The factories run because the city needs what they make, and the workers work because the factories need hands, and the system perpetuates itself with the same patient, slow, unremarkable inevitability as the water that weeps from the stone.

## The Inhabitants

They number in the millions—how many millions is unclear, because counting requires a dry surface to write on and a reason to care about the number, and Shengao offers neither reliably. They live in the buildings, in the chambers, in the Deep City's upper levels where the flooding is controlled and the fungus is scraped back and the blue glow is supplemented by the electric lights that the factories' generators power.

Their lives are shaped by the wet in ways that are physiological, psychological, cultural. Their skin is soft, prone to maceration, perpetually at the edge of breakdown. They develop fungal infections of their own—not the city's fungal networks, but human-specific colonizations that the physicians treat with preparations derived, ironically, from the city's fungal networks, which produce compounds with potent antimicrobial properties. Their lungs are adapted to the humidity; a visitor from a dry climate would find the air almost unbreathable, each inhalation heavy with water, but the inhabitants have larger lung capacities and slower respiratory rates, and they do not notice.

They speak a language that has twenty-seven words for degrees of wetness and no word for *dry*—the closest approximation, *xu*, means "less wet" and carries a connotation of suspicion, of something wrong, of a surface that should be weeping and is not. Their architecture, their clothing, their tools, their customs, their songs (which are slow and resonant and performed in the deep chambers where the acoustics are favorable)—all assume the wet. To imagine Shengao without water would be to imagine a body without blood. The city is the water and the water is the city.

They do not leave. Some have tried—descending the escarpments on foot, seeking the lowlands that maps insist exist. Few return. The slopes are too wet, too broken, too colonized by forms of life that the plateau's ecology does not recognize. The city is an island—high, wet, dense, self-sufficient, enclosed. The world beyond is theoretical, a space on maps that no living person has verified.

## The Slow Dynamics

Shengao changes, but slowly.

The water finds new paths. A crack opens in a chamber ceiling; the water finds it; over months, the crack widens; over years, the chamber floods; over decades, the chamber is abandoned, colonized by fungus, occupied by the deep-things, forgotten. The city contracts where it must and expands where it can, building upward into the condensation, adding new levels of walkways and workshops and dormitories, always seeking the dry, always losing the battle by degrees.

The haifu shift their congregations. The fungal networks extend their threads. The city is a process, and the process is slow, and the slowness is its own kind of violence—the gentle, persistent, water-logic violence of erosion, of saturation, of the wet finding every weakness and exploiting it over years and decades and centuries. Shengao is always breaking. Shengao is always being repaired. The two activities are not in opposition; they are the same activity, the same metabolism, the city digesting itself and rebuilding itself from the materials of its own dissolution.

Stand in the wet level at the shift-change, when the workers emerge from the factories and move along the channels and the haifu lift their broad heads to let them pass and the laizao scatter and resettle and the thread-worms continue their invisible work in the film of water that covers every surface. Listen. The sound is a wall, a texture, a medium. The water and the machines and the voices and the feet and the breathing of the haifu and the clicking of the laizao and the slow, mineral creak of the stone—it is all one sound, the sound of Shengao existing, the sound of a city that has found its equilibrium in wetness and will hold it, slowly, patiently, for as long as the rock beneath continues to weep.
