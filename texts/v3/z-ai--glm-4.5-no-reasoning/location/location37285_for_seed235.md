The plateau does not breathe. It holds.

At an elevation where the air should thin to nothing, the atmosphere instead presses down with the weight of a submerged ocean. This is a heat that does not behave as temperature should—it does not rise, it does not create thermals or drafts. It merely exists, a suffocating, pervasive absolute, resting on the skin like a solid. There is no wind. There has never been wind. The sky above is not blue but a swollen, opalescent grey, a ceiling of trapped condensation that never falls as rain yet never clears.

And from this saturated sky, water manifests. It does not drop in lines or patters against stone. It exudes. Every surface weeps. The towering edifices of the city—constructed from a dark, ferric stone that resembles cooled magma—run with continuous sheets of moisture. The water does not flow downward according to gravity alone; it climbs, seeping upward along the edges of walls, beading on the undersides of arches, condensing out of the hot air itself to run in rivulets across the ceilings of open corridors before dripping in a steady, multitudinous stream onto the floors below. The stone is not eroding. Despite the endless water, the architecture bears the matte smoothness of something that achieved its final form millennia ago and will hold it for millennia more. The water is part of its equilibrium, a circulatory system of ambient moisture.

The city is dense, stacked, labyrinthine. Towers crowd against each other like basalt teeth in a jaw, their bases widening into sprawling terrace complexes that overlap and interlock, creating a vertical maze of plazas, bridges, and enclosed courtyards. But it is empty of life. No vermin scuttle in the crevices. No birds roost on the lipless windows. No moss or fungus claims the wet stones. The only inhabitants are the plants.

They are not parasites; they are the city's co-architects. Colossal angiosperms—things like banyans crossed with pipe organs—grow in impossible profusion, their roots not displacing the stone but perfectly conforming to it, grafted into the urban geometry. They fill every court, every walkway, pressing their glossy, dark-green leaves against the weeping walls. The foliage is so thick that from a distance, the city appears to be a mountain of vegetation, the architecture merely the skeleton beneath a hide of leaves. The roots plunge into the saturated ground, drinking the ambient water, while the canopies stretch upward into the condensing mist. They bloom in relentless cycles, dropping petals the size of hands that dissolve into the running water before they can accumulate. It is a biome of perfect, frozen recursion: the plants drink the city's weeping water, the city's weeping water feeds the plants, and nothing decays.

Then there is the Sound.

It is not a noise that starts and stops. It is the acoustic signature of the place itself, a deafening, continuous chord generated by the intersection of heat, water, and stone. Every weeping wall contributes a frequency. Every root twisted around a pillar hums a sub-harmonic. The countless waterfalls cascading off the tiered rooftops—thousands of them, threading the hot, still air—combine into a roar that should drown thought. Yet because it never fluctuates, never ceases, the sound ceases to be heard as sound. It becomes a pressure in the chest, a vibration in the teeth, the auditory equivalent of the oppressive heat. It is the sound of a landscape holding its breath forever.

The weirdness here is not an eruption; it is the baseline.

Gravity is thick, like moving through sap, though nothing slows or sticks. It simply requires an act of will to lift a foot, to turn a head, as if inertia itself has been multiplied. And the architecture exploits this. Staircases ascend at angles that should require climbing on all fours, yet one simply walks them, pulled upward by the tilted plane as if the stone insists on bearing weight. Bridges arch over chasms that drop away into mist, their balustrades leaning outward at degrees that should invite a fall, but there is no falling here. The air catches. The equilibrium is absolute.

In the deeper wards of the city, where the vegetation grows thin and the water runs hottest, the geometry of the construction begins to tire. Corners do not quite meet. Doorways open onto rooms that are larger inside than their exterior walls should allow, not by much, but enough to notice—a hallway that takes four steps too many to cross, an alcove that retreats further than the wall's thickness permits. It is not a trick of perspective. The stone has surrendered a fraction of its Euclidean certainty, rubbed smooth by eons of standing under the weight of a broken physics. The water that weeps in these rooms runs in spirals on the floor, pulled by a gravity that points toward the center of the building rather than the earth below.

And yet, nothing changes. The water weeps. The plants bloom. The heat presses. The sound roars. It has roared since before the concept of silence existed. The city is a machine with no moving parts, a thermodynamic seal, a landscape that has solved itself. To walk its drenched corridors—pushing through the dense, humid air, brushing leaves that drip immediately onto your shoulders, descending stairs that hang over vertical drops into white mist, all while the deafening harmonic rings in your bones—is to enter a system that does not need you, acknowledge you, or alter by a single drop of its weeping water for your presence.
