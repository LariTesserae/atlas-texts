The air hangs thick as wet felt, saturated with the breath of ten thousand stomata. You descend into the Hollow—not a valley, but a broad, shallow depression in the living skin of the world, perhaps a hundred meters across. It is a room without a roof, bounded by the interlocking buttress roots of the peripheral plant-mass. This is a landscape where the distinction between geography and botany collapsed long ago. 

Here, vegetation is not a resident; it is the substrate, the architecture, the very ground beneath your feet. The terrain is a dense, interlocking weave of rhizome and runner, a living peat that compresses underfoot with a sodden squelch and springs back slowly behind you. Nothing is bare. Every surface—every horizontal shelf, every vertical plane—is colonized, padded, wrapped in green. Vines thick as human thighs braid into weight-bearing columns. Shelf-fungi plate the undersides of branches in overlapping shingles, and air-roots descend in curtains, so thick you must push through them like a bead curtain, your shoulders wet with the condensation they harvest from the air. The space is packed. There is no gap, no clearing, no sky visible through the canopy—only a continuous, saturated volume of living matter, receding into a dim, bioluminescent haze in the middle distance.

And it weeps. The water parameter here does not manifest as streams or pools, but as a perpetual, slow exudation. The atmosphere is at its dew point; you feel the humidity as a physical pressure against your lungs. The vast botanical superstructure acts as a colossal condensation engine. Warm air from outside hits the cool, transpiring mass of the Hollow and precipitates. Water beads on every leaf-tip, every spore-case, every hair-like trichome, accumulating until gravity pulls it down in a slow-motion rain. The ceiling of interlocking foliage above you drips with a steady, arrhythmic patter, but beneath the surface, the water emerges from the plants themselves. Weeping scar-tissue on the boles of the great central stalks bleeds a thin, clear sap that mixes with the condensation, creating shallow, anoxic puddles in the hollows of the root-mass. The water table is the living table.

The sound is overwhelming, a low, mechanical roar that vibrates in your back teeth. Yet it is entirely biological. This is the sound of the Hollow breathing—thousands of pneumatic bladder-pods inflating and deflating in slow, eight-second cycles, pumping the stagnant air through the dense vegetative mass. It sounds like the idling of a colossal diesel engine, a *thump-whump, thump-whump* that never changes pitch. The rhythm is slow, patient; the dynamics of this place operate on a timescale of hours and days, not seconds. Light shifts as the bladder-pods inflate, briefly dilating apertures in the canopy and sending shafts of normal, yellow daylight lancing down into the green gloom, before contracting again into twilight. 

Against this overwhelming biological engine, the artifacts of industry are scattered like shed teeth. Rusted iron pipes, three meters in diameter, jut from the vegetative matrix at odd angles, half-swallowed by bark and root. They do not carry oil or steam; they are colonized. The plants have repurposed them. Root masses grow inside the iron tubes, using them as insulated, high-humidity conduits to rapidly transport water and sugars across the Hollow. The metal is flaking, bleeding orange rust into the weeping water, but the vines hold the pipes aloft, incorporating their rigidity into the living architecture. Here and there, the skeletal frame of a small, brick-built pumping station is visible, its walls long since burst outward by the expansion of a central taproot that now wears the building's roof like a tilted hat. A disconnected electrical cable, its rubber insulation gray and cracked, dangles from a fuse box, its bare copper terminus greened over and slowly being digested by a phosphorescent lichen. The machines did not die here; they were subsumed. The industrial era left its tools, and the ecosystem simply ate them and kept running.

Fauna is nearly nonexistent. In the packed, slow-moving, oxygen-rich suffocation of the Hollow, there is no niche for animals. No herbivore could chew through the lignin-dense, sap-weeping walls; no predator could find prey in the undifferentiated green. The only movement besides the slow inflation of the bladder-pods is the creeping expansion of the tendrils, growing millimeters an hour, and the occasional, startling shudder as a massive, waterlogged bough shifts its weight, sending a cascade of warm condensation down upon you. 

There is a profound wrongness to the place, a strangeness that settles into your stomach. It is the dissonance of a factory that has no workers, a machine made of meat and cellulose. The warmth of the air—tropical, sticky—should breed chaos, insects, decay, but the decomposition is internalized, autophagic, hidden within the walls. The Hollow feels like an organ in a larger, dormant body. You are inside it. You are an intruder in a sealed, functioning mechanism, standing on the living floor as it slowly, imperceptibly, contracts around you, pumping the heavy, wet air through its vegetable pipes, waiting for you to stop moving so it can grow over your boots and begin the long, patient process of making you part of the architecture.
