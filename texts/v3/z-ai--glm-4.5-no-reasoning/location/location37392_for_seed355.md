The heat comes first—not the dry, scathing heat of a desert, but a thick, aqueous warmth, like breathing inside a closed throat. The air is nearly saturated. Water does not fall from the sky so much as it exudes from the world. It beads on surfaces, weeps from the pores of dark stone, and hangs in vesicles that drift like slow, clear jellyfish through the air. This is a landscape perpetually drowning on its own condensation, yet the water never seems to pool deeply enough to swim; it just saturates, turning the ground into a fever-hot sponge.

It is a low place, a wide bowl at the terminus of a forgotten watershed. The scale is regional—entire districts of this settlement stretch into the humid darkness, their outlines dissolving into the wet haze just a few hundred meters away. Yet despite this breadth, the space feels deeply isolated. There is a profound sparseness here. The structures and the occasional protrusions of organic life are separated by vast tracts of empty, steam-breathing ground. The emptiness is not an absence; it is a heavy, tangible medium, like the dark water in a flooded basement.

The built environment is the only dense thing here, but it is a density of ruin and repetition. It is a city—or the fossil of one. Layer upon layer of organized construction rises from the muck, but there are no lights, no hum of machines, no trace of the tool-users who stacked these walls. The tech is absolute zero; nothing manufactured survives here. The city appears to have been grown from the landscape itself, cast in seamless blocks of a dark, ferrous stone that sweats rust-colored tears. The architecture is irrational. Doors open onto weeping stone. Staircases spiral down into bubbling thermals. Entire avenues are lined with tenement blocks whose windows are filled not with glass, but with a solid, translucent membrane that pulses faintly with the ambient heat. 

The vegetation is sparse, parasitic, and strange. It refuses to photosynthesize in the traditional sense; in this near-darkness, nothing is green. Instead, thick, pale rhizomes wrap around the urban bones like tendons, bearing swollen, heat-blistered pods that pop intermittently, releasing damp spores that hang in the air like fog. There are no animals. The silence is vast, broken only by the sounds of the city weathering: the steady *drip-slick* of condensation running down the walls, the deep, subsonic groan of stone settling in the heat, and the occasional, unsettling snap of a heat-blister pod bursting. It is mostly quiet, but it is the quiet of a mouth slowly opening.

Erosion has long since finished its violent work and settled into a grotesque marriage with the infrastructure. The water and heat have rounded the corners of the buildings, melted the sharp edges of the streets into viscous troughs. The city is in balance, but it is the balance of a corpse resting at the bottom of a warm sea. The dynamic processes of the world are subtle but relentless: the diurnal pulse of the heat, the slow creep of the moisture level rising and falling like breath in a lung, the way the stone seems to swell during the hottest hours and contract with a grinding sigh as the temperature peaks.

And then, the balance breaks. 

The weirdness here is not an occasional glitch; it is the substrate of the environment. The darkness is not merely an absence of light—it is an active, heavy pressure that pools in the lower streets like a liquid, occasionally rising to swallow a lower district entirely before receding. Space cannot be trusted. Walk down a straight avenue, and you will find the end of the street curling inward, the buildings leaning over to whisper to each other, blocking out the sky. Leave a stone on a windowsill, and return an hour later to find it fused seamlessly into the frame, as if the building had digested it. The geometry is morbidly fluid. Sometimes a building will slowly, silently fold its face inward, erasing its own windows and doors, leaving only a flat, sweating wall facing the empty street. 

In the deepest bowls of the city, where the heat and the water and the darkness are most concentrated, the distinction between the built and the natural dissolves entirely. The stones grow soft, taking on the texture of heated flesh. The pale vines pulse with a rhythm that matches the slow drip of the condensation. There is no one here. There is no technology to explain it. There is only the city, breathing in the wet dark, slowly digesting itself, perpetually active in its decay, a sprawling, sparse monolith of architecture that has learned to rot like a living thing.
