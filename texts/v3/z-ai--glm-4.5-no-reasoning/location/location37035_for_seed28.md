You descend into it through a crack in the world—a fracture in the bedrock of a sun-baked caldera, hidden beneath a shelf of dust-red sandstone. The passage is barely wide enough for shoulders, a rough chimney of stone that opens, suddenly and impossibly, into a vast, hollow gut of earth.

This is the Chorus Vault.

It is a pocket-city, a single contained cavity shaped like an elongated gourd, hundreds of feet high but sealed entirely from the outside. There is no sky here, only the distant, domed ceiling of pale limestone, pocked with thousands of natural boreholes. Through these shafts, the desert sun punches inward, a chaos of blinding light beams that cross and recross the hollow space. The light is so acute, so refracted by the mineral dust suspended in the air, that the vault is flooded in a searing, amber-white brilliance. It hurts to look up. The heat is immense—a still, baking warmth radiating from the rock walls, trapped and amplifying in this terrarium of stone.

And the sound.

The sound is an apocalypse. It is a deafening, physical weight that strikes the chest before the ears can even parse it. The thousands of boreholes in the vaulted ceiling act as flues. As the desert winds tear across the surface of the world above, they are forced down these shafts, transforming the entire cavity into a colossal, aeolian organ. The resonance is staggering—a subsonic thrum that vibrates the marrow in your bones, overlaid with a shrieking, harmonic white noise that sounds like the tearing of ancient cloth. It never ceases. The wind dictates the rhythm, but the song is the mountain's.

Within this roaring, blinding void, there is almost nothing. The space is overwhelmingly empty. The floor of the vault drops away into a plunging, bowl-like abyss of dry, pale chalk, unbroken for acres. The emptiness is profound, forcing a sense of agoraphobia despite being buried deep underground. But clinging to the vertical walls, elevated on natural stone ledges and jutting talons of rock, is the city.

It is not a city built for people as we know them. It is primitive, constructed from the immediate geology—hand-chiseled blocks of the same pale limestone, stacked without mortar. The architecture is dense and layered where it exists, terracing upward toward the light shafts, but it is sparse overall—a handful of clustered structures on the north wall, a solitary spire on the south, a suspended bridge of woven rock-fiber spanning the emptiness. It is urban in its logic, organized in its construction, but stretched across an immense negative space. The buildings lean into the wind, shaped by millennia of the acoustic vibration, their edges rounded and smoothed by the constant micro-erosion of the circulating air.

And yet, despite the absence of water, despite the oven-heat and the deafening roar, the place is violently green.

Lush vegetation cascades from every crevice. It is a non-aqueous ecology, a chemistry born of mineral rich vapors and the strange condensation that forms in the acoustic pressure-nodes of the vault. The plants are thick, rubbery, and dark—abyssal greens and bruised purples. They do not have leaves; they have fronds of heavy, succulent tissue that swell with harvested condensation from the roaring air. Vines thick as human thighs braid across the empty space, anchoring the city to the walls, draping from the high ledges like curtains of scaled muscle. They pulse. The acoustic energy of the vault drives the metabolic pace of this flora; the louder the wind howls, the faster the cellulose weaves. The growth is so aggressive it has swallowed half the stonework, reducing the primitive architecture to bone-like armatures protruding from living green mass.

Fauna is rare, but what exists is tied entirely to this riotous vegetation and the endless noise. You see them only as silhouettes in the crossing sunbeams: long, segmented things with carapaces of translucent chitin, clattering along the vines. They are deaf, their auditory organs long since atrophied, but they navigate by the vibration in the air, dancing along the resonant frequencies. They harvest the sap of the acoustic plants—a thick, gelatinous fluid that acts as a fluidic bearing, letting them survive the crushing vibration. They are sparse, solitary, wandering the hanging gardens in near-invisible isolation.

The world operates on a rule of acoustic tension. The wind never stops, and so the city is never silent. The erosion is a process of equilibrium; the stone weathers into dust, the dust clogs the boreholes, the wind clears them, the plants bind the dust, the insects spread the spores. The vault is ancient, settled into its own weather system—a permanent, deafening cyclone of trapped wind, blinding light, and creeping wet-green life, hidden away in the dry, silent stomach of the desert.
