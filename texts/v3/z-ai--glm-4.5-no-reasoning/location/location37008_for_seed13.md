The city stands where the sky presses close, carved from the dark ribs of a young mountain range that has not yet learned to soften. The peaks here are sharp-featured, their granite teeth still jagged with the violence of their raising. Frost clings to the high places and rarely melts. At this elevation, the air carries a thinness that makes each breath a small, conscious effort, and the cold is a constant presence—not brutal, not killing, but persistent, settling into stone and bone alike.

They built upward, not outward. The architecture is primitive but determined: great blocks of basalt and granite stacked without mortar, fitted with the patience of hands that had nothing but time, leverage, and stone. The walls lean inward as they rise, creating a stepped pyramid of structures that climbs the mountainside itself. Streets are narrow and vertical—more staircase than road, cut into the living rock and worn to a faint polish by generations of feet. There are no wagons here. No wheels. Everything that moves through the city moves on legs, carrying what it can on its back.

Water comes reluctantly to this place. There are no rivers, no springs that flow with any generosity. Instead, moisture manifests as a daily phenomenon: clouds roll in at the same hour each evening, pressing against the mountain's flank, and condensation weeps from every stone surface. The inhabitants have carved narrow channels and cisterns throughout the city, catching the tears of the fog. By morning, each basin holds a thin skin of water, and the walls glisten darkly in the first gray light. Rain visits perhaps once a quarter—enough to refill the deeper reservoirs, enough to remind the stone what wetness feels like, but not enough to carve new paths through the young terrain.

And yet, the place is lush. Life has found a way that defies the obvious mathematics of cold and altitude. Moss and lichen encrust every horizontal surface in thick, soft carpets of gray-green and pale gold. Ferns erupt from the joints between stones, their fronds uncurling in defiant spirals, reaching toward the flat light that filters through the perpetual haze. On the terraces—every structure seems to have a terrace, however small—there are gardens of a sort: hardy tubers with dark leaves, creeping vines that bind one building to the next, small pale flowers that bloom in the condensation hours and close again when the air dries. The green is not the green of lowland abundance; it is muted, grayed, as though the chlorophyll itself runs cold. But it is everywhere. Life clings to this city the way it clings to a sunken ship—opportunistically, tenaciously, filling every crevice that offers purchase.

The fauna has adapted similarly. The city is full of life that moves quietly and watches. Goats—small, sure-footed, with horizontal pupils that glow amber in the dimness—navigate the upper ruins and the rocky slopes beyond, appearing on ledges that seem impossible, cropping the moss from the crevices. They are semi-domesticated, tolerated, perhaps even honored; certain thresholds bear small carvings of their curling horns. Below, in the deeper stairways and the shadowed courtyards, there are foxes with thick silver pelage, and birds—always birds. Crows cluster on the highest points, silent sentinels. Smaller birds, dun-colored and quick, nest in the gaps between building stones, emerging in the morning mist to feed on the insects that thrive in the perpetual damp. There are cats, too, long-haired and dark, belonging to no one and everyone, moving like smoke through the lower streets.

The quiet is profound. Sound dies against the stone and the moss. Voices carry only a few meters before the thick air absorbs them; the inhabitants have learned to communicate through gesture and rhythm—tapped messages on walls, patterns of movement that mean specific things. When someone walks, their footsteps are muffled by the living carpet of growth. Even the wind is hushed here, broken by the peaks above, reduced to a faint pressure that moves the mist but makes no sound. The loudest thing in the city is the dripping, and even that is soft—condensation releasing from stone to cistern with a sound like a finger tapping a wooden bowl, steady and unhurried.

Nothing changes. This is the strangest thing, and the one that takes the longest to recognize. The city exists in a kind of temporal equilibrium. The clouds come at the same hour. The condensation forms in the same amounts. The cisterns fill to the same levels. The moss grows to the same thickness and no further. The goats walk the same paths. The people—if people they are, and not some echo of people—perform the same routines: tending the same terrace gardens, carrying water in the same clay vessels along the same staircases, pausing at the same viewpoints to watch the same fog roll in. There is no decay visible. The stone does not crumble. The carvings do not weather. Even the lichen seems frozen in its expansion, a map that was completed long ago and has not added new territories since.

It is not that time has stopped. Time passes—you can feel it in the slow rhythm of the dripping, in the way the light shifts through its gray permutations. But nothing accumulates. There is no season that differs from the last season, no year that ages what the previous year did not age. The city has found its shape and holds it perfectly, like a basin of water that has found its level and now simply is. The inhabitants do not seem to mind, or perhaps do not notice. They move through their patterns with a kind of contentment, or at least absence of restlessness. There is no urgency in anything. No one hurries. No one delays. They simply proceed, at the pace the city seems to set for them.

The weirdness of the place is not dramatic. It accrues. You notice first that the carvings on the doorways—those horned goats, those spiraling ferns, those geometric patterns that might be maps or prayers—appear on surfaces that could not have been carved after construction, some in places that would require the carver to stand inside solid stone. You notice that certain staircases seem to lead to rooms that should not exist given the geometry of the buildings above, and that retracing your steps does not always return you to where you began. You notice that some of the inhabitants have been walking the same path for what seems like hours without appearing to grow tired or change expression, and that when you look away and look back, they have not moved at all—except they are further along now, as though they moved only when unobserved. You notice that the cats watch you with an attention that seems less like animal curiosity and more like assessment.

And the city extends. That is the other thing. From any vantage point, you can see more of it—more terraces, more stairways, more dark openings in the mountainside—climbing higher and spreading further than seems possible for the population it contains. There are districts that appear unoccupied but not abandoned, maintained in the same pristine equilibrium as the rest, gardens tended by no visible hand, water channels running with condensation that no one arranged. The city goes on past the curve of the mountain, past the edges of what you can see in the mist, and you understand that you could walk for days and not find its boundary. It is not a large city in terms of population—the figures you see are sparse, scattered, leaving long stretches of empty steps between one encounter and the next. But the construction itself is vast, a slow expansion into the rock that may have no limit.

At night—when the light dims to something almost like darkness, though never true darkness at this altitude, where the sky retains a faint luminescence even at its nadir—the city becomes quieter still, and the dripping becomes the only marker of time's passage. If you stand in one of the high terraces and look out, you can see the fog settled in the valleys below like a white sea, and the peaks of other mountains rising from it like islands. The cold sharpens. The stars, when the clouds part, are ferociously clear, undimmed by any lower atmosphere. You might hear, very faintly, a sound that is not the dripping—a rhythm like breathing, or like the heartbeat of something very large and very slow. It might be the mountain. It might be the city itself. It might be nothing at all, just the blood in your own ears, finally audible in this place where silence goes deep enough to hear such things.

You do not leave. This is not because you cannot—the paths down exist, steep and stony but passable—but because leaving feels like a category error, like stepping out of a dream you did not know you were having. The city holds. Not with force, not with any visible constraint, but with the gentle inevitability of equilibrium. Objects at rest tend to remain at rest. You came here moving, and now you are here, and the city goes on around you, dripping and growing and going nowhere, and it is the easiest thing in the world to stay.
