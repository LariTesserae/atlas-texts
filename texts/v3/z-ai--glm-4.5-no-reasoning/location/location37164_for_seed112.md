You emerge onto the canopy, and the world is a single, continuous, screaming green.

There is no soil beneath. There is no bedrock. Down there—if you could fall, which you cannot—is only a recursive abyss of root-mass and lignin, a planetary loam of living wood that has compactor-pressed itself over geological ages into something harder than sandstone. Water here is a rumor, a phantom. There are no rivers, no rain, no oceans. The atmosphere hangs cool and bone-dry, a thin, bright gas that tastes of ozone and shredded cellulose. This is a world where aqueous chemistry was outcompeted millennia ago. The substrate is the plant itself; the plant is the continent; the ecosystem is a single autotrophic monolith that has swallowed every niche and now only negotiates with itself.

And it is *loud*.

The sound hits you before the light does—a physical pressure, a tectonic groan combined with a high, metallic shrieking. The canopy—this vast, continental shelf of interlocked megafolia—is in a state of permanent, mechanical seizure. The plants here are not passive photosynthesizers; they are industrial engines. The leaves—scaleless, translucent plates the size of city blocks—are angled to catch the blinding, unfiltered stellar radiation, and they *move*. Giant hydraulic pistons of pressurized sap slam the leaf-shelves into new orientations with a concussive *THWUMP* that rolls across the horizon like artillery fire. Millions of them fire at once, chasing the sun, grinding against one another. The friction generates the shrieking—wood on wood, amplified to a deafening, continuous scream that vibrates in your chest cavity and blurs the edges of your vision.

The scale defies comprehension. You are standing on a high-altitude ridge—a spine of compressed, dead trunk-matter that rises above the main canopy shelf. From here, the plant-topography stretches to the curve of the planet. There are no constructed structures, no artifacts, no marks of tool-use. And yet, the environment is fully industrialized. It is a factory without architects. The megafauna—thick-shelled, six-limbed glyptodonts the size of haul trucks—exist solely as mobile processing plants. They bore into the trunk-continents with auger-like mandibles, chewing through the wood to extract the pressurized, volatile saps. The saps are not water-based; they are a non-aqueous solvent—a hydrocarbon-alcohol cocktail that ignites at lower temperatures. 

When a bore-hole hits a high-pressure vein, the sap geysers out in a fine, combustible mist. In the dry, bright air, static sparks or friction-heat ignite it. The eruption is a contained, industrial event: a blowtorch flare of blue-white fire that shoots hundreds of feet into the sky, accompanied by the roar of a foundry furnace. These flare-stacks dot the horizon, burning day and night, refining the atmosphere. They are the world's exhaust vents, belching out the ozone and the thick, bitter smoke that settles in the cool upper altitudes. The fire does not kill the plant; it is a symbiotic pressure-release, cauterizing the wound and triggering a localized spasm of new growth.

The turbulence is absolute. This is a weather system made of living tissue. The canopy breathes in chaotic, hyperactive spasms. When a continental leaf-plate shifts, it displaces billions of tons of air, generating violent downdrafts and wind-shears that rip across the surface. You have to brace against the wind, which shifts direction without warning, carrying a blizzard of debris: sawdust, fractured bark, the hollow exoskeletons of millions of smaller, chitinous fauna that are crushed in the endless mechanical shifting of the green gears. 

It is an anthill of planetary dimensions, a Solaris-like churn of creation and destruction where the plant builds its own architecture, scales its own production, and operates its own mechanized slaughter. The space between the major continental shelves is sparse but terrifying—deep, bright chasms where the vertical root-walls drop away into the dry, glowing depths. Light refracts through the translucent sap-veins in these canyon walls, casting the voids in a sickly, radioactive chartreuse. 

There is no silence here. There is no stillness. You are standing on the superstructure of an engine that does not know it is an engine, burning fuel it cannot drink, turning gears it cannot see, roaring as it drags the entire dry world forward into the blinding light.
