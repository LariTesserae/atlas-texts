Beneath the surface of the earth, there is a vast, subterranean cathedral of living wood. It is a place that has not yet learned the meaning of age—a landscape trapped in the throes of a perpetual, embryonic genesis. Erosion is a concept that has never touched this place; nothing is worn down, nothing is carved by time. Instead, the space is raw, swollen with the pressure of becoming, pressing outward against the damp, dark soil that encases it.

The terrain is not made of stone or dirt, but of a continuous, unbroken biomass. This is a pure-plant ecosystem where vegetation is not merely a resident but the very substrate of reality—a living architecture that fills the space entirely. Great buttress roots, thick as ancient columns, interlock with massive, horizontally sprawling rhizomes to form the ground underfoot. There is no bare earth; to walk is to tread upon the crowded apices of root-caps and the spongy, fibrous mats of ever-creeping stolons. The density is staggering. Every cubic inch of this regional expanse is occupied, layered in a near-touching friction of cellular expansion. Massive fern-fronds, lacking the space to unfurl, are pressed tightly against the striated bark of pale, eyeless trees, creating a compressed verticality that forces the horizon to vanish. The canopy above is not a distant ceiling but a suffocating, continuous mat of photosynthetic tissue, a churning ceiling of green and ochre that stretches far beyond the limits of sight.

And yet, it is not dark. The light here is blinding, a paradox of the deep earth. The overwhelming heat—a stagnant, wet fever that hangs in the air—has driven the flora to an extreme of metabolic desperation. Every surface, from the interlocking ground to the vaulted roof of roots, glows. Bioluminescence here is not a rare sparkle but a full-spectrum radiance, a biological substitute for a sun that has never been seen. Thick, fleshy leaves are translucent, pumping with luminous sap that bleeds an intense, yellow-white glare through the cellular walls. The glare reflects off the glossy, condensation-slicked surfaces of seed-pods and bark, creating a disorienting, sourceless brilliance. There are no shadows; the light emanates from the walls, the floor, the very air itself, steeping the cavern in an oppressive, blinding noon.

The atmosphere is a steam bath. Water exists here as an intermittent, suffocating presence. It does not flow in rivers or pool in lakes; rather, it is squeezed from the environment itself. The relentless heat causes the dense plant-mass to transpire exponentially, pushing heavy curtains of vapor into the saturated air. Moisture beads on every surface, swelling into heavy droplets that slide down the smooth bark, collecting in the deep crevasses of the root-network before dropping into the occasional shallow, warm puddles that occupy the slight depressions in the living floor. It rains indoors—a slow, constant drip of condensation falling from the glowing canopy to the glowing ground, a closed hydrological loop of damp, gasping breath.

The only sound is the sub-audible, relentless hum of growth—a wet, creaking stretch of cellulose fibers pulling against one another under immense turgor pressure, and the soft, rhythmic patter of transpired water falling from the leaves above.
