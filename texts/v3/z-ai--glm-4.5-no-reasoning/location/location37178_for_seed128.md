You descend into the throat of the world. The entrance—a seam in the bedrock of a high, warm plateau—gives way to a manufactured chasm that swallows you whole. This is the Cistern, a pocket ecosystem embedded deep within the crust, a hidden room at the top of the world. 

There is no natural stone here. Everything is made. The walls, the floor, the dripping ceiling—all of it is a continuous, unbroken construct. There is no raw earth, no natural substrate, only the ancient, engineered body of a machine so far beyond modern comprehension that it functions more like a biological organ than a structure. The material is a dark, cerametallic alloy, warm to the touch, running with internal capillaries that pulse with faint, sluggish heat. 

Despite its immense age, the Cistern is in perfect balance. It has weathered millennia not by degrading, but by settling into its own operational rhythm. The erosion is a planned obsolescence that never quite completes—a cycle of nanoscale shedding and regrowth that leaves the surfaces smooth, porous, and etched with microscopic grooves, like the worn bed of a river that has flowed since the continents were young. 

And water is everywhere. It does not roar; it weeps. The atmosphere is choked with warm mist, condensed against the cool, porous alloys. Moisture beads on every surface, running in rivulets that converge into trickling seams. The walls literally cry. Water emerges from micro-apertures in the cerametal, pooling in shallow, engineered grooves that cross the floor in a dense, intersecting grid. It is a landscape of near-surface aquifers and perpetual, dripping saturation, existing in a state of endless, humid exhalation.

The scale is intimate, claustrophobic. The Cistern is a single chamber, perhaps the size of a grand cathedral, but segmented and shrunk by the density of its contents. There is no horizon, no sky—only the looming, drip-streaked walls curving inward. The space is packed to absolute capacity. Every cubic meter is occupied by infrastructure, moisture, or life. It is a high-saturation environment where nothing is wasted, and every inch is near-touching.

And the life is overwhelming. Vegetation is virtually nonexistent—there is no soil, no sunlight for photosynthesis—but fauna thrives in terrifying abundance. The dim, bioluminescent glow of the place comes entirely from them. The Cistern swarms with eyeless, pale arthropods and blind, eel-like amphibians that have evolved to feed directly on the machine’s exhausts and the mineral-rich weeping water. They clamber over the pipes, swim in the shallow grid-channels, and cling to the condensing ceiling. The air is a living fog of winged, thumb-sized isopods that drift in the warm updrafts, their carapines clicking against the alloy walls. It is an ecosystem in constant, frantic motion—a churning, writhing predator-prey engine playing out in the dark, where something is always hunting, always shedding, always consuming.

To stand here is to be deafened. The sound is absolute, a physical pressure against the skull. It is not a single noise, but a layered, roaring cacophony. At the base is the low, tectonic hum of the machine’s deep thermal pumps. Over that, the deafening, rhythmic percussive drum of millions of gallons of water forcing through nanoscale pipes. And over that, the shrill, abrasive chittering and scraping of the dense fauna, vibrating the very air. You cannot hear your own breathing. You cannot hear your footsteps. The sound is the voice of the Cistern itself, a ceaseless, deafening roar of water, biology, and unfathomable engineering, locked together in a warm, wet, hidden pocket at the edge of the world.
