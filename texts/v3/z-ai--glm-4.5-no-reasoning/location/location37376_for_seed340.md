To enter this place is to descend into a throat.

There is no sky here, not really. There is only the ceiling—a living ceiling, sagging under its own terrible weight, composed entirely of interlocking rhizome-bundles and fungal mantles that have long since swallowed whatever mineral geology once existed. You are below the surface, perhaps hundreds of meters down, yet it is not dark. Normal light permeates this chthonic vault, generated by the plant-matter itself. The ceiling and walls pulse with a sullen, amber bioluminescence, like sunlight filtered through closed eyelids, or the glow of a low fever. It is warm—uncomfortably, mammalian-warm, the heat of compost and respiration pressed in from all sides. 

Water is a memory that this ecosystem has evolved to forget. There are no rivers, no dew, no rain. Instead, hydration is an internal, closed-loop alchemy. The substrate beneath your feet—if you can call it that—is a dry, granular humus, like coffee grounds mixed with pulverized bone. It crunches softly, but the sound is instantly swallowed by the deafening roar of the place. This is a world of non-aqueous chemistry, where the flora circulates thick, sap-like lymphs—high-viscosity fluids of ethanol and complex alkaloids—through vast, translucent veins that ridge the walls like the arteries of a god.

And the plants are the architecture. This is a pure-plant ecosystem; there is no stone, no dirt, only vegetal matter in endless recursion. Fungal shelves the size of city blocks jut from the walls, supporting cascading gardens of fibrous, barbed flora. Great pylons of hardened heartwood, calcified by mineral salts drawn from the deep, rise in colonnades, but they are still growing, still stretching. This is an embryonic world, caught in the violent throes of its own formation. The geology is just coming into being; the landscape is raw, unconsolidated, erupting and collapsing in real-time. You can watch hills being born as a massive rhizome bulges outward, splitting the dry substrate, pushing up a mound of pale, unpigmented root-matter that reaches for the ceiling before it is inevitably chewed apart.

Because the fauna is overwhelming. The air is not air; it is a dense, choking aerosol of spores, chitin-shards, and wing-beats. Every square inch of visibility is thick with life. Arthropodal things—multi-legged, eyeless, possessing mouthparts like industrial shears—swarm in seething, iridescent carpets over the vegetal surfaces. They are the excavators, the builders, the destroyers. They burrow into the soft wood, laying eggs that hatch into larval grubs the size of human torsos. The grubs, in turn, secrete a hardening resin that shapes the fungal shelves into terraces. It is a turbulent, Solaris-like cycle of creation and consumption: a fungal shelf grows over a week, is parasitized by a swarm of black, clicking wasps the size of thumbnails, collapses into a rotting heap, and is immediately consumed and rebuilt by the next wave of burrowers. The dynamic energy is anthill-like, raised to the power of an earthquake. The entire space heaves with this hyperactive, churning metabolism.

The sound is unimaginable. It is a physical pressure, a vibration that rattles your teeth and blurs your vision. It is the collective, rhythmic clicking of a trillion mandibles, the sub-bass groan of massive heartwood pylons shifting under their own tons of lymph, the tearing, ripping crackle of dry substrate being excavated, and the shrill, harmonic drone of wing-cases clattering together in mating swarms the density of smoke. It does not cease; it is the heartbeat of the space itself.

Amidst this overwhelming, suffocating biological riot, there are marks. Scattered, primitive, almost pathetic. Deep in the folds of the root-walls, where the amber bioluminescence fades to a bruised ochre, you might find a shard of obsidian, flaked into a crude hand-axe, stuck fast in the dried lymph of a healing scar. Or a single structure—a cairn of hard-shelled seed-pods, stacked with deliberate, deliberate care in a hollow that the swarming fauna have temporarily abandoned. A length of coarse, hand-spun twine made from the silk of the giant grubs, knotted around a wooden stake to mark a path that is overgrown within hours. Someone, or something, is here. They are pre-metal, pre-writing, surviving by the skin of their teeth and the sharpness of their knapped stone. They live as parasites on the margins of this plant-as-architecture world, hiding in the brief pockets of stillness between the churning cycles of growth and collapse, carving out a fleeting existence in a cathedral of raw, deafening meat.
