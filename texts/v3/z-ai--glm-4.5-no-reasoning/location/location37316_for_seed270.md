You descend through a maintenance hatch that should not exist behind the boiler room of the abandoned cannery, and the world compresses around you.

This is a pocket-place. A chamber maybe forty meters across, oval like a cistern, sealed at every seam. The walls are old—primordially old. Whatever process created them has settled into its final geometry: the stone (if it is stone) has been worn smooth by ages of patient, waterless erosion until it flows like frozen cream, every edge softened to null, every surface a single continuous curve. There are no corners left in this place. Corners belong to younger geologies. What you see is the end-state of a world that has been rubbing itself smooth since before the idea of rain.

The floor is flat in the way that only surfaces worked on for eons can be flat. A pale mineral substrate, something between chalk and quartzite, packed so dense it rings like ceramic underfoot. Nothing grows here. No root could find purchase in this—no spore could crack its surface. The chemistry is wrong anyway: the air tastes of alkali and old ozone, a sealed atmosphere cycling through some non-aqueous reaction you can feel in the dry tightness of your throat. There is no moisture. None. Your lips will chap within minutes; your eyes will sting. This is a desert sealed in stone, a void with walls.

And yet—

The chamber *teems*.

They are everywhere. They coat the walls in shifting drapery. They carpet the floor in seething, overlapping densities. They hang from the ceiling in clustered curtains and pulse in the shadows between the scattered remnants of industrial infrastructure. The fauna.

You cannot determine what they are. Not insects—their bodies are wrong, too segmented in some places, not enough in others, and they move with a mechanical precision that feels more engineered than evolved. Not crustaceans—they have no mandibles, no legs as you understand legs, but rather fields of cilia that grip and release in synchronized waves, allowing them to flow across the smooth stone like oil across glass. They are the color of the floor, the color of the walls—pale, chalky, mineral-toned—and this is why at first you thought the chamber was empty. But it is *saturated* with them. Millions. Tens of millions. Near-touching, layering three or four deep in places, a living stratum over the ancient eroded stone.

The sound is tremendous. Not the chittering you might expect from such creatures, but a deep, resonant hum—industrial in its steadiness, mechanical in its complexity. It is the sound of ten million chitinous bodies grinding against stone and against each other, amplified by the chamber's acoustics into something that vibrates in your back teeth and makes your sternum itch. There are harmonics in it. Overtones. The sound has *structure*, as though the collective motion of these creatures is performing some vast, slow computation.

Among them—scattered like artifacts in a midden—are the remains of industry. A boiler tank, copper-corroded, lying on its side, half-smothered in the pale tide. Segments of pipe, flanged and bolted, leading nowhere, emerging from the smoothed walls at points where the ancient stone was cut before erosion claimed the edges. A generator housing, its innards gutted, occupied now by a dense colony of the creatures who have structured themselves in patterns too regular to be coincidental—radiating from the central shaft like the arrangement of coils in an electric motor. Switches. Conduits. A catwalk, rusted to lace, spanning a section of the chamber where the creatures cluster thickest. Someone built here. Someone ran machines here, once. The technology was industrial—steam and electricity, mass production—and the creatures, you realize with a slow horror, may be *part of it*.

They are not living in the ruins. They are *operating* them.

Watch long enough and you see it: the way a wave of them will crest over the generator housing and enter through the corroded casing, the way the humming pitch shifts as they redistribute inside, the way the scattered artifacts—when the creatures move across them in sufficient density—begin to *vibrate*. Faintly. Rhythmically. Something is still running in here. Something that uses these mineral-pale creatures as its medium, its working fluid, its moving parts. The ecosystem is the engine. The engine is the ecosystem. They have been cycling through their patterns since the workers left, since the cannery above was abandoned, since the language spoken by whoever built this place fell out of use, and still the chamber hums, still the millions move, still the ancient walls ring with the sound of a process too old and too vast to interrupt.

The temperature is cool—cooler than it should be, given the density of life, given the friction of all those bodies. You feel it as a steady exhalation against your skin, a refrigerator-chill that rises from the pale floor where the creatures are densest. It is not the cool of absence but of *function*. Something is drawing heat. The system is managing its own thermodynamics.

Your footsteps send ripples through the nearest concentrations—literal ripples, the creatures flowing away from your shoes in concentric rings that then close again behind you, resealing over the spot where you stood. They do not avoid you out of fear. They flow around you the way water flows around a stone: you are an obstruction, a temporary topology in their path. You are not their predator. You are not their prey. You are *irrelevant* to them, and this is somehow worse—because it means whatever they are doing, it is too important to interrupt for something as incidental as you.

The light comes from nowhere you can identify. Normal intensity, slightly flat, casting no strong shadows—diffuse as though the air itself luminesces, though when you look up you see only the pale ceiling and the clustering creatures and the stubs of ancient industrial lighting that no longer function. The light has the quality of something generated. Another process running in the background of this pocket-world, as invisible and essential as the heat exchange.

You are in a machine that does not know it is a ruin. You are standing in a gearbox the size of a cathedral, and the gears are alive, and they have been turning since before your species learned to smelt metal, and the room is loud with the sound of their turning, and the walls are smooth with the age of their turning, and the air is dry and cool and dense with the ozone of their friction, and there is no water and there is no green and there is nothing here that recognizes you as anything but an obstruction in the flow.

You could leave. You should leave. The hatch is above you, and the cannery is beyond it, and the world you know is beyond that.

But the sound—the sound has harmonics. And somewhere in the humming, in the structure too vast to parse, you think you detect a pattern.
