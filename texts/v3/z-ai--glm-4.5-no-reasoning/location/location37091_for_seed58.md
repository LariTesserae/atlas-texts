Descend. The entrance is a wound in the flat earth, a jagged crack in sun-baked clay that breathes out air thick as a wet towel. You drop below the surface into the Thrum—a subterranean ward, a cathedral-sized cavity in the bedrock where the city’s guts have spilled and fossilized into a neighborhood.

The heat hits first. It is a pervasive, crushing heat, 2.9 on the scale of infernal tolerance, radiating from the dense, dark basalt walls and rising from the grated floors. This is a young place, geologically speaking—recently excavated, freshly bored into the earth by engines that have since moved on. The stone is sharp-edged, unweathered. Chisel marks still gleam in the corners, and the tunnels haven't yet had the time to slump or smooth. The rock is jagged, unforgiving, cutting into the meat of the infrastructure that clings to it.

Despite the heat, there is moisture. The air is heavy with intermittent damp, a condensing hell. Water doesn't flow here; it weeps. It beads on the cold-water pipes and drips from iron rivets, pooling in shallow, rust-colored puddles in the hollows of the stone. Occasionally, a fine, hot mist drifts down from the ventilation stacks above, coating everything in a thin, alkaline slick. You taste metal and damp dust on the back of your tongue.

The scale is intimate yet claustrophobic—cathedral-sized, but broken. The space is dense, saturated with presence, yet utterly devoid of life. There is no fauna. No rats scurry in the walls; no insects click in the shadows. The ecology is entirely mechanical. The walls are layered with a near-touching density of urban construction: a three-dimensional lattice of cast-iron walkways, copper piping, and steam conduits. Brick tenements are bolted directly into the rock face, their windows dark, their doors shut. It is a settlement-scale maze, a district built for thousands, but populated only by machines. Every square inch is claimed by infrastructure—valves, pistons, gauges, and flywheels, all shoehorned together with industrial brutality. 

It is dim. Light comes from sickly, glass-encased sodium bulbs tucked into wire cages, casting a bruised yellow-orange glow that only makes the shadows deeper. The illumination catches the slick of condensation on brass fittings, making them gleam like wet eyes in the dark. In this half-light, the weirdness of the place emerges. The layout defies human logic. Walkways double back on themselves; stairs lead to blank rock faces; doors open onto tangles of hissing pipes. It feels as if the city built itself in a fever, following the logic of pressure and heat rather than the needs of people.

And the place is active. It breathes and shifts, not with the pacing of living things, but with the steady, unstoppable dynamic of industrial process. The sound is mostly quiet—a relative term here. It is a background thrumming, a low-frequency vibration you feel in your molars and the soles of your feet. Above this baseline, the soundscape is sparse but distinct: the sudden, hollow *clack-clack* of a timing chain slipping; the hiss of a pressure release valve venting steam into a cistern; the slow, rhythmic groan of a subterranean fan turning in its housing, moving air that never seems to cool. 

Vegetation is nearly absent, save for a few tenacious, pale fibers—a fungal or lichen-like growth that isn't quite plant—that cling to the mortar between bricks, dying and reviving with the cycles of the hot mist. They are the only soft things in a world of iron and stone.

The rules of this world are written in pressure and heat. Gravity feels heavier in the deep; the air resists motion. Doors require force. The damp guarantees rust, and the rust guarantees decay, yet the young stone refuses to erode. It is a place of sharp contrasts: the hardness of fresh rock against the rotting of old iron; the suffocating heat against the weeping walls; the absolute silence of abandoned human spaces against the tireless, clicking life of the machines that inherited them.
