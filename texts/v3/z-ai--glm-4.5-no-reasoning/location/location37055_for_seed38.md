The air is a lung. It breathes in and out with a rhythm so slow that a human lifetime would only catch a single exhale. This is the High Peat—a continental shelf of living moss and root-mass that has subsided, over ages beyond reckoning, into its own final, flattened form. The elevation registers as high on the old tectonic maps, but there are no peaks here, no ridges, no crags. The mountains that once stood were devoured. Over eons, root and rhizome and fungal hyphae dissolved the stone, ate the granite, and replaced it with a living substrate hundreds of meters deep. What remains is a primordial plane, a planetary tabletop of vegetal flesh, resting where the sky-thighs once were.

It is warm. The heat does not come from the sun, which is a dim, pale smear behind an eternal ceiling of copper-colored vapor. The warmth comes from below—the slow, patient composting of the entire continent. Fermentation on a geological scale. The ground yields slightly underfoot, a springy, waterlogged mass, but it does not squelch. There is no free-flowing water here, no streams or lakes. Instead, water is held in a state of total intimacy with the plant-matter. The atmosphere condenses continuously, weeping a fine, warm mist that never quite falls as rain. The air is saturated to its limit; breathing feels like drinking. Every surface is slick, every pore is full.

The landscape is total vegetation, but it is not a forest. There are no boles, no canopies, no leaf-litter beneath. Instead, the space is filled with an architecture of reed and vine and aerophyte, layered densely from the yielding ground up through the dim, misty air. Towering clubmoss—ancient beyond the fossil record—stands in colonies like the pillars of a sunless cathedral, their trunks furred with a down of silver-green lichen that drips constant, thread-thin streams of condensation. Between them, strap-leaved epiphytes grow in helices, catching the mist, their roots dangling like rigging in a ship with no deck. There is no sky, only a ceiling of tangled grey-green tendrils fading into the humid obscurity above. Light barely penetrates; it is a permanent twilight of verdant shadow, an ambient glow of bioluminescent foxfire that pulses in the deep grooves of bark and the undersides of shelf-fungi. It is just bright enough to see the next cluster of pillars, the next curtain of hanging root.

The soundscape is nearly absent. The overwhelming density of wet vegetable matter absorbs all echo, all footfall, all wind. The air is too thick for wind to move quickly; it only settles into slow, patient currents. What sound exists is the sound of water resolving: the tick of a droplet falling from a tendril to a lower leaf, the imperceptible hiss of mist condensing on waxy cuticles. Once an hour, perhaps, a low, resonant groan rolls through the peat—the continent shifting its weight, a massive root-arm unlocking after a century of tension. It is a quiet that feels held, muffled by the green meat of the world.

Fauna is abundant, but it is fauna of the veil and the crevice. Nothing walks on legs larger than a finger. The ecosystem forbids it; the ground would not support weight, and there is no open space to run. Instead, the air is thick with motes of life: clouds of translucent gnats that drift like living fog, spiral-winged moths that navigate by the foxfire's glow, and slender, limbless newts that swim through the saturated moss as if it were water. The top of the food chain is a creature barely the size of a thumb—a bright, carapaced mite that hunts in the condensation-traps of pitcher-plants, diving into the viscous dew to pull out drowned insects. There are millions of them. Life presses close, saturating every cubic centimeter, but it is a life of the minute, the hidden, the interstitial.

Scattered throughout this endless, living plain are the ruins. Not ruins of stone—stone is long gone—but ruins of chitin and old wood. The remains of ancient beetles the size of barges, their hollowed thoraxes now colonized by forests of minute fungi. The collapsed hulls of what might have been wooden structures, perhaps built by something half-remembered, now just ridges of lignin barely distinguishable from the root-heaves around them. A single artifact might be found in a thousand leagues: a shard of obsidian, flawlessly sharp, embedded in the bark of a clubmoss like a fossilized eye. It is the only hard, dead thing. No one knows how it got there. The plants have tried to digest it for millennia, and failed. Around it, the bark grows in tight, angry whorls.

This is a world at the end of its history. Erosion has won. Not the erosion of wind and rain, but the erosion of life itself, which is the most patient and thorough of all forces. The land has been eaten down to its essential, stable state: a warm, wet, breathing plane, where the boundary between organism and environment has dissolved entirely. Here, the plant is the continent. The root is the bedrock. And the slow, dim cycle of condensation and decay rolls on, unmarked, in the quiet green dark.
