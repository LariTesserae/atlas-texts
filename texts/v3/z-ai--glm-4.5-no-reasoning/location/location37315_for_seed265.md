The darkness is not an absence but a substance—thick, wet, pressing against the skin like a membrane. This is a lowland that has only recently remembered it exists. The earth emerged from something a geological breath ago, and it has not yet settled into the patience of old stone. The ground is sharp-ridden, raw-edged, fractured into freshly-exposed strata that haven't endured enough rain to round their shoulders or soften their teeth. You feel this underfoot even if you cannot see it: the crunch and scrape of new mineral, the way the terrain refuses to be walked upon comfortably, as if it resents the weight of anything living before it has finished becoming itself.

And yet the water. The water is everywhere, and it has arrived before the land was ready. Not pooled in generous basins or flowing in established channels—those would require mature topography, gravity's long work. Instead the water seeps, weeps, condenses. It beads on the undersides of overhanging shelves where stone still hangs in fresh-cleaved angles. It trickles from weeping faces of raw clay and compacted ash, making mineral tears that taste of iron and something else, something that has no name yet because the chemistry here is still improvising. The atmosphere is saturated past the point of comfort; you breathe water as much as air, and every exhalation hangs visible, though there is no light to see it by. The ground does not absorb what falls—it has not developed the soil profile for that, the organic loam, the percolation paths. Water strikes the young surface and spreads, finding the sharp new cracks, pooling in the angular depressions between recently-uplifted blocks, forming a patchwork of shallow black mirrors that reflect nothing because there is nothing to reflect.

The scale of this place is difficult to grasp in the dark, but your ears tell you it extends far. The rare sounds that do emerge carry across the open flat without competition: the drip-drip-splat of water finding new paths down raw faces, the deeper gurgle of somewhere a proper flow has begun to cut, the occasional crack and groan of the young land still shifting, still settling into its new configuration. These sounds are sparse and they do not layer. Each arrives alone, distinct, given the space of the whole dark expanse to propagate until it fades. It is mostly quiet in the way that a place with no history is quiet—nothing has accumulated enough to create a sustained soundscape. The silence has texture, though. It is a wet silence, a mineral silence. It rings faintly, like a struck bell that has been buried.

Against this near-silence, there is the breathing. The fauna.

Something lives here in abundance, and it has adapted to the dark and the wet and the raw new world with a speed that defies expectation. You hear them more than you sense them otherwise: small skitterings in the water films, the splash-patter of something moving through the shallows between the sharp rocks, the occasional chirp or click that seems to bounce off surfaces you cannot locate. The density of life is high for a place so young and so sparse in vegetation—there should not be this much breathing, this many bodies. But the water is here, and the water has brought something with it, or recalled something from dormancy, or generated something spontaneously from the mineral-rich soup that runs across the raw terrain. The fauna do not behave like creatures that evolved here over millennia. They behave like creatures that arrived all at once, fully-formed, already knowing how to navigate the dark and the sharp edges and the water that will not drain. In the absolute blackness, you feel them passing near—displacements of the thick air, faint disturbances in the water films that cover every horizontal surface. They are not afraid of you. They flow around you like a current.

The vegetation is sparse but present, and what grows here grows in the water or because of it. Not rooted in any proper soil—there isn't any, not really, just raw parent material beginning its slow conversation with the atmosphere. Instead, fibrous pale things cling to the weeping rock faces where the mineral tears flow, drawing sustenance directly from the dissolved chemistry. They are pale because they have never needed to photosynthesize; there is no light. They are thin and rope-like, forming vague nets across the wetter surfaces, and they seem to tremble even when there is no movement of air to cause it. In the flatter areas where water pools in the angular depressions, there are mats of something that might be algal, might be something else entirely—slick, dark, faintly warm to the touch if you reach down. The vegetation does not crowd or compete. It simply occupies the wet surfaces with a patience that matches the slow dynamic of the place. Nothing happens quickly here. The water drips. The rock weeps. The pale fibers lengthen by imperceptible degrees. Change is measured in the slow accumulation of mineral deposits, the gradual widening of cracks, the patient thickening of the organic mats in the pools. Years pass and the sharp edges soften only slightly.

Then there are the structures. Scattered, small, partial. They appear in the dark as sudden geometries—too regular, too angular, too intentional to be natural, though the young land has not yet established what "natural" looks like here, so the distinction feels less stable than it should. The structures are built of the same raw material as the surrounding terrain, as if whatever made them simply reshaped what was already present rather than bringing anything new. Low walls, waist-high, their courses following lines that suggest purpose without revealing it. Partial shelters, roofless, open to the thick dark sky. Occasional pillars or pylons rising from the shallow water, their bases mineral-crusted, their tops vanishing into the blackness above. They are scattered at intervals that feel deliberate but irregular, as if laid out according to a logic that does not correspond to any familiar scheme of habitation or industry.

And yet industry is what they suggest. Not the industry of human habitation—no chimneys, no clear process, no comfortable scale—but something more fundamental. The faint marks on the stone faces near the structures are not natural weathering; they are grooves, channels, intentional cuts that direct the weeping water along specific paths, guiding it into basins that feed into other basins, creating a slow cascade through the structures that seems to serve some purpose invisible in the dark. The larger shelters—those that still retain partial ceilings—contain the ghosts of machinery: corroded housings, mounting points for something long removed or long decayed, the negative space where engines or pumps or generators once sat. You feel the residual warmth in some of these, a faint thermal signature that has not fully dissipated even though the structures appear abandoned. Whatever ran here ran recently, or is running still, in some slow way that does not require visible motion.

The electrification is the strangest part. You cannot see it, but you feel it—a faint tingle across the surface of any water you touch, a prickle at the base of your skull when you stand too long near the larger pylons. Something is current in this place, something is flowing, though whether through wires buried in the young stone or through the stone itself or through the water that covers everything in thin films, you cannot determine. The fauna seem aware of it; they avoid certain zones and cluster in others, their movements responding to gradients you cannot perceive. Occasionally, in the deeper dark, there is a sound that is not the water and not the rock and not the breathing of creatures: a low hum, felt more than heard, that rises from somewhere beneath the fresh surface and resonates in the shallow pools, making their surfaces shiver with standing waves that persist for seconds after the sound has faded. The electricity— if that is what it is—does not behave like electricity should. It does not seek ground in the expected way. It pools. It accumulates in the angular depressions where water pools. It seems to have preferences.

This is where the reality begins to soften.

It starts subtly, as it always does. The darkness itself is the first sign—too complete, too uniform, too absolute for any natural night. There should be variation, should be stars or ambient glow or the faint luminance of atmospheric scattering, but there is only the black, and in the black, your eyes begin to invent. Not hallucinations exactly, but suggestions—shapes that almost resolve, patterns that almost cohere, depths that shift when you try to measure them. The distance between structures seems to vary depending on the direction you approach from. The sound of your footsteps returns to you at odd intervals, as if the acoustic space is folding, creating pockets where time and distance have come uncoupled.

The water is the medium of breakdown. Where it pools in those angular fresh-cut depressions, it does not simply reflect—it *absorbs* and *contains*. Looking down into the black water (if you can see at all, if you have some small light) you do not see the bottom. You see other terrain, other configurations of the same raw edges and pale growths, as if each pool is a window into a version of this place that is slightly offset, slightly different in the arrangement of its scattered structures or the angle of its fresh-cut strata. The weeping rock faces sometimes weep not water but something thinner, something that evaporates almost instantly and leaves a faint residue that stings the skin and makes the air shimmer in a way that has nothing to do with heat. The pale rope-like vegetation trembles because it is responding to movements in spaces adjacent to this one, fluctuations that propagate through whatever membrane separates here from elsewhere.

The fauna know. They move through the soft places with an ease that suggests they are not confined to a single layer of reality. You watch a displacement in the water film—something the size of a large rat, moving with purpose—and then it is gone, not hidden, not retreated, simply *absent*, as if it stepped sideways into a pool that was not quite this pool. Their sounds, when they come, sometimes arrive from directions that do not exist, or arrive before the motion that caused them, or arrive multiplied, as if one action in one place produces echoes in several.

The structures, with their channels and basins and slow cascades of mineral water, seem designed to manage not just fluid flow but something else—concentration, perhaps, or *coherence*. The electricity that pools where water pools may be an attempt to stabilize something that wants to disperse, or to energize something that wants to be dormant. The residual warmth in the abandoned machinery housings suggests that whatever process these structures were built to serve is still running, still consuming, still producing—but at a pace so slow, so patient, so matched to the geological timescale of the young land that it is invisible to anything that measures time in heartbeats. The dynamic of this place is glacial, tectonic, gradual beyond easy perception. But something is happening. The weeping rocks are weeping more. The pale vegetation is spreading by millimeters per year, its fibers reaching toward new wet surfaces, threading into new cracks. The fauna are multiplying—or perhaps they are just becoming more visible, more willing to inhabit this layer rather than the adjacent ones. The reality breakdown is not a crisis here; it is a condition, a feature of the landscape as fundamental as the water or the new stone. Something is being built in the dark, at the intersection of geology and industry and whatever else leaks through the pools, and it has been building since this land emerged, and it is not finished, and it will not be finished for a very long time, and when it is finished it will be something that has no name yet because nothing like it has existed before.

The dark holds all of this. The wet dark, the warm dark, the dark that tastes of iron and dissolved strangeness. You stand in it and feel the sharp new ground beneath you and the water filming over everything and the breathing of abundant unseen life and the faint electrical tingle and the slow slow slow accumulation of whatever this place is becoming, and you understand that you are standing on something that is still being born, and birth is wet and raw and dark and it takes a long time and it is not comfortable and it is not quiet, not really—the sounds are sparse but each one is significant, each drip and groan and click is a small announcement of change, of growth, of the patient emergence of something that will eventually be enormous. But not yet. Not now. Now it is still young, still sharp, still wet, still dark, still becoming. And you are briefly here, briefly standing in the birth, feeling it happen around you, before you move on and leave it to its slow and terrible and wonderful work.
