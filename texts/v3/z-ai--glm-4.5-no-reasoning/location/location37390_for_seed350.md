You are submerged. Not merely beneath a surface, but within a medium that has forgotten the concept of surface. The water here does not behave like water should. It is thick, warm—almost thirty degrees, bathwater, the heat of blood—and it presses against you not with the steady indifference of the ocean, but with a jittering, frantic urgency. This is not a sea. This is a flood that never ended, a drowning that became a world.

The light does not reach you. Whatever sun exists above—and something must, to keep this water so warm—has been swallowed by the medium itself. The water drinks photons. What remains is a perpetual submarine gloom, a darkness so complete it has texture, weight, taste. You float in it. You cannot see the boundaries of this place, but you know they are far. The scale is regional, vast—a flooded basin the size of a small sea, or a continental shelf turned aquarium, or something larger still that you cannot measure because measurement requires reference points and there are none.

But there is vegetation. God, there is vegetation.

It grows in every direction at once—upward toward the light that isn't there, downward into depths that have no bottom, laterally in thickets so dense they become walls. The plants are the only living things here. No fish thread between the stems. No crustaceans click along the leaves. No worms work the sediment. The fauna is zero—absolute, theological zero—and the absence is itself a presence, a hole in the world that stares back at you like an empty socket. The plants do not seem to notice. They grow as if animal life were a myth they had wisely chosen to disbelieve.

The flora is wrong. Not in color—they are green, mostly, deep blue-greens and chlorophyll blacks, colors that make sense for photosynthesis in low light. Wrong in structure. The leaves are too large, too thin, curling at the edges like paper held to flame. The stems are hollow but rigid, ringing faintly when the water moves through them, like tubes in an organ that has lost its player. Some of the larger growths—trees? they have trunks and canopies, so call them trees—spiral as they rise, their bark forming helical grooves that channel the warm current upward, feeding themselves with the flow. They are engineered. Not bred, not cultivated—*engineered*, with a precision that exceeds biology.

And they are young. Everything here is young. The erosion parameter is nearly zero, and you understand what that means when you touch a surface: nothing here has been worn. The edges are sharp. The leaf-tips could cut. The bark hasn't softened. The sediment—if there is sediment beneath you somewhere—is not smoothed by time but lies in the angular shapes of its original fracture. This place emerged recently, or it keeps emerging, continuously, like a wound that won't close because the body keeps making new flesh faster than it can scar.

The sound is unbearable.

You expected silence. You are underwater; sound should be muffled, distant, whale-song faint. Instead, the water itself is screaming. Not a single sound but a wall of them—a saturation of noise so complete it becomes a physical pressure, a weight on your chest, a vibration in your teeth. The source is mechanical. You can hear it now, separating the layers: the deep throb of engines, somewhere, many of them, running hot and liquid-fed; the higher shriek of turbines spinning in flooded housings; the rhythmic clank of production lines that have never stopped because no one is left to stop them and the water isn't enough to seize them—they were built to be submerged. The vegetation rings and hums with it, every stem a tuning fork, every hollow trunk a resonator. The plants are amplifying the machines. Or the machines are driving the plants. Or both, in a feedback loop that has been running since before you arrived and will run after you leave, after you die, after the concept of "you" dissolves into the warm dark water.

There are structures. Not many. Not enough to call it a city or even a settlement. But scattered through the drowned forest—partial frames, skeletal platforms, the lower floors of buildings whose upper stories sheared away—there is evidence of construction. Industrial construction. The remains are steel and concrete, but a concrete that has been formulated for submersion, that does not degrade in water, that still holds its shape despite the current and the growth. The steel is rusted in patterns that don't follow chemistry—oxidation spiraling up the I-beams like the helical bark of the trees, as if the metal were learning from the plants, or vice versa.

One structure you find—or it finds you, looming out of the dark as you drift—is a factory. Still running. You can feel its heat before you see its shape, a thermal plume in the already-warm water, making the medium shimmer with convection ripples that bend the little light that reaches this depth into wavering distortions. The factory has no walls. It has a frame, a skeleton of girders, and within it: vats, pipes, valves, presses, all moving, all working, all producing. Producing what? You circle it—slowly, because the water near the machinery is turbulent, kicked to fury by the pistons—and you see the output. Green. The factory is producing plant matter. Not processing it. *Producing* it. Synthesizing cellulose and chlorophyll and the complex carbohydrates of leaf and stem, extruding them in sheets and tubes that drift upward into the water and take root, somehow, in the liquid itself, finding purchase in nothing, growing without substrate.

The vegetation is not natural. It was never natural. It is a product.

And now you notice what you had been refusing to notice since you arrived: the plants are watching you. Not with eyes—they have no eyes, nothing so comprehensible—but with orientation. Every leaf in your vicinity has angled toward you. Every spiral stem has shifted its growth-axis in your direction. The helical bark-channels are directing current toward you, carrying warmth and vibration and something else—something chemical, a pheromone or a signal—in your direction. The forest knows you are here. The factory knows you are here. The water, saturated with plant-matter and machine-noise and the exhaust of a production line that has been running for decades or centuries or longer, knows you are here.

The weirdness is not a parameter. It is a condition. It is the medium you are swimming in.

You try to hold onto logic. You tell yourself: this is an industrial installation, flooded, automated, producing some kind of bio-engineered vegetation for purposes you don't understand. The warmth is thermal pollution. The sound is mechanical resonance. The plant behavior is tropism, refined to an extreme degree by whatever algorithm runs the factory. The absence of animal life is because the system wasn't designed for animals, or because the plants outcompeted them, or because the water contains something that kills fauna but not flora. There are explanations. Rational accounts. You can build them.

But the water keeps pressing. And the sound keeps building. And the plants keep growing—toward you, around you, their leaves brushing your skin now, their stems clicking against each other in patterns that sound almost like language, almost like code, almost like the machines they came from are still speaking through them, dictating their growth in real-time, revising their structure based on your presence, designing new forms to interact with you, to capture you, to incorporate you—

You push away. The vegetation lets you go. Or you escape it. The distinction matters, and you cannot tell which it is.

The factory recedes into the dark. The sound does not diminish—it never diminish, it is constant everywhere, ambient as pressure—but it shifts in character as you move, different frequencies dominant in different regions, as if the submerged machinery were distributed across the entire basin, not concentrated in one site but networked, pervasive, a body with many organs working in concert. You are inside the system. You have been inside it since you arrived. The water is the system's blood. The plants are its thoughts.

You find another structure. A tower, or the base of one—thirty meters of reinforced concrete, sheathed in the manufactured vegetation, the green growth so thick the structure beneath is invisible except where the current has torn a section away, revealing the sharp young concrete, gray and unweathered, and the rebar within, also uncorroded, also new. There is a door. It is sealed. Flooded. Through the porthole window—you press your face to it, the warm water pressing back—you see interior space, also flooded, also growing with the green plants, but differently: here they grow in rows, cultivated, uniform, illuminated by bioluminescent strips that cling to the ceiling and pulse with the same rhythm as the machinery-throb outside. A nursery. A greenhouse. A node in the network.

The water shifts. Not a current—something larger. The entire basin is breathing. You feel it: a slow inflation, a contraction, a cycle on a scale so vast you only notice it because you are still enough and quiet enough (though nothing here is quiet, nothing here is still, you are quiet and still relative to the chaos) to perceive the rhythm. The water level is changing. Not rising and falling—there is no surface to rise and fall toward—but *pulsing*, pressure waves moving through the liquid, compression and rarefaction, sound on a frequency too low to hear but strong enough to feel in your skeleton. The basin is resonating. The machinery is resonating. The plants are resonating. Everything is resonating at slightly different frequencies that beat against each other, creating interference patterns that make the water shimmer and fold, that make the darkness swirl with regions of greater and lesser opacity, that make the warm medium feel alive in a way that water should not feel alive.

You are not drowning. You could drown—the water is breathable only in the sense that you are not breathing it, and if you tried, it would fill your lungs and that would be the end—but you are not drowning. You are being *held*. The pressure, the warmth, the sound, the plants: they are holding you. Whether for observation or absorption or some third purpose you lack the context to imagine, you cannot say. But the system has registered you. It is responding to you. The vegetation near you is different from the vegetation far from you—denser, more complex, growing in shapes that mirror your silhouette, reaching toward you with structures that look almost like hands, almost like tools, almost like the machines that made them.

You should leave. You should find the surface, if there is a surface, or the edge, if there is an edge, or any boundary that separates this place from the place you came from. But the water does not want you to leave. The current is against you. The sound is against you—disorienting, a wall of mechanical noise that hides direction and distance and any signal that might guide you out. And the plants, the beautiful impossible plants, they are against you too, not attacking but *obstructing*, growing across your path faster than you can move, filling the space you want to pass through with new matter synthesized in real-time by the drowned factories, the submerged engines, the tireless production lines that turn water and warmth and electricity into green growing things that have no purpose you understand but that grow and grow and grow regardless.

You are in a place that is making itself around you. That has been making itself since before you arrived. That will continue making itself after you are gone. The youth of this landscape is not a phase; it is a principle. The erosion is low because the place is rebuilding faster than it can decay. The density is high because the factories never stop producing. The sound is deafening because the machines never stop running. The weirdness is high because this is not a landscape that evolved—it is a landscape that was *specified*, that is being *executed*, that exists as the output of a process rather than the result of a history.

And you are inside the output. You are inside the program. You are data in a system you cannot read, moving through a medium you cannot breathe, surrounded by life that is not alive, watching creation happen in real-time and recognizing—because you have no choice, because the recognition is being forced from you by the sheer overwhelming presence of the place—that creation is not peaceful, is not gentle, is not the quiet unfolding of a seed in soil. Creation is loud. Creation is hot. Creation is mechanical and biological at once, a factory-farm of reality, and it is happening here, now, around you, and you are part of it, whether you consent or not.

The warm water presses. The green stems click. The engines throb. The darkness is total.

You are submerged in something that is making itself. And it is making itself into something that has never existed before.
