# The Drowned Mesa

The first thing to understand: the water does not fall here. It *occupies*. It stands at heights that defy the logic of basins and sea levels, held in place by something older than hydrology — a saturation so complete that the air itself has surrendered, that the boundary between atmosphere and ocean is a negotiation, not a line.

---

## The Surface of the Water

From above — if you could get above — you would see it as a vast and shallow inland sea, perhaps forty miles across, stretched over what must once have been a plateau. But the water is not shallow. It varies. In places it laps at nothing, only itself, three feet deep over hard stone. Elsewhere it drops into drowned valleys whose floors have never been mapped. The surface rarely moves. No wind reaches it with any conviction; the surrounding heights break the weather before it arrives. What motion there is comes from below — slow upwellings, the breath of the deep, which raise the center of the lake by inches over the course of hours and then let it subside. The water darkens and brightens in patches for no visible reason. It is warm. Not bathwater-warm, but warmer than it should be at this altitude, warmer than the thin air above it can explain.

Rain occurs, but the rain falls upward as often as not. The air is so loaded with vapor that droplets condense out of it and drift in whatever direction the slightest thermal gradient suggests. You will see rain ascending from the surface in silver threads, rejoining the cloud base. The clouds themselves are low and dense and seem to be the same cloud, always, unmoving, a lid of gray-white light that diffuses the sun into a sourceless glow. It is never fully dark. It is never fully bright. The light level at midday is what other places would call heavy overcast, and it stays that way for twenty hours, then dims slightly for four, and calls that night.

## The City Beneath

The structures are below the waterline.

They were built — are still being built — on the shoulders of the mesa, where the stone shelves out in wide steps before dropping to the valley floor. The construction is hand-laid stone, unmortared, fitted with a precision that suggests centuries of accumulated craft and no theoretical geometry at all. The walls are thick. The rooms are small and connected by corridors that would force a human to crawl. Whoever built them — whatever built them — was smaller than a person, and did not value straight lines.

The city extends across the southern and eastern rims of the mesa, sprawling across three distinct tiers of stone, and it goes down. The uppermost structures breach the surface during the long low tides — which occur not with the moon but with some subterranean rhythm of the water itself, perhaps tidal, perhaps thermal, on a cycle of eleven days. During the low tide you can see the highest towers: squat stone pillars, no more than eight feet tall, crowned with flat caps of darker rock. They look like mushrooms. They look like knuckles. Below them, the walls step down into the water, and the water takes them.

The stone underwater is clean. No silt accumulates. The water is too still and too warm to carry sediment for long; it drops everything it picks up within a few yards. So the city is visible, if you submerge yourself and open your eyes, extending down the face of the mesa in a dense lattice of chambers and passages and platforms, all fitted tight, all drystone, all the same gray-brown as the cliff itself. It was not carved from the rock. It was built onto the rock. But the distinction is academic; after enough centuries, the moss makes them one surface.

## The Vegetation

The moss is the key to everything.

It is not moss. It occupies the ecological niche of moss, and it looks like moss in the way that a whale looks like a fish — a convergence of form that obscures a total divergence of origin. It grows in every crevice of the stonework, on every horizontal surface, on the cliff faces between the built tiers, on the undersides of the stone caps where the water has eroded small hollows. It is deep green, nearly black in the ambient light, and it is always damp. It does not photosynthesize. Not in any way that chlorophyll would recognize. It grows toward warmth, not light, and the warmth comes from the water, so the moss grows downward, extending filament by filament into the shallows, colonizing the submerged walls with a fur that thickens over years into a carpet, over decades into a mattress, over centuries into something you could sink your arm into up to the shoulder.

It fruits. Periodically — and no one has determined the schedule — the moss produces small pale nodules at the boundary between air and water. They emerge overnight, pale green-white, the size and shape of a child's tooth, soft as fruit flesh. They last two days and dissolve back into the surface, leaving a faint phosphorescence in the water that fades over hours. Whether this is reproduction or communication or feeding or something without a terrestrial analogue is not known. The builders harvest them. There are stone basins set into the upper towers, just above the waterline, where the nodules are collected and left to dissolve in fresh water. The resulting liquid is thick and faintly luminous and is used — for what, exactly, is unclear. It stains the stone in places. It may be consumed.

Below the moss, in the deeper water, there are larger plants. Not many, but some — long ribbons of something kelp-like that grow from the drowned lower walls and sway in the slow currents with a patience that makes them seem geological. They are not rooted in the stone. They are rooted in the moss. They grow from the deepest mats of it, sending down holdfasts that are themselves moss, so that the kelp and the moss are the same organism at different stages of expression. The kelp-fronds are copper-colored and translucent. They do not reach toward light. They hang vertically and move only when the water moves, which is seldom and slowly, so that they seem to be a kind of architecture — another tier of the city, built from living material instead of stone.

## The Builders

They are present. They are rare. You will not see them on any given day in the city; you might spend a week on the upper towers during low tide and catch only the evidence of recent work — a new course of stone on a half-finished wall, a basin cleaned and repositioned, a passage that was open last month now sealed. They work underwater. They work in the warm dark.

What they are: small, air-breathing, stone-working, moss-cultivating. They surface during the night-tide to breathe and to harvest the nodules and to collect stone from the exposed quarries on the mesa top. They do not use fire. They do not use metal. They have stone tools of remarkable specificity — each one shaped for a single task, a single joint in the stonework, and discarded when the joint is done. The mesa top is littered with discarded tools in patterns that suggest long use and no sentimentality.

They are not seen clearly. They move at the edge of visibility, in the dim light, in the mist, and they are the color of wet stone. A watcher on the tower might see, at the limit of sight, a shape emerge from the water, collect something from a basin, and submerge again. The shape is the size of a large cat. It moves on four limbs but can stand on two. It has a head that is broad and flat and carries the eyes far apart, which would suggest a need for peripheral vision, or a life in narrow spaces where the forward view is always obstructed. Beyond this, nothing is certain. They do not approach. They do not retreat. They simply absent themselves from the vicinity of observers, with a thoroughness that suggests either extreme shyness or a comprehension that observation is, itself, a kind of contact they prefer to avoid.

Their population is not known. Given the extent of the city and the rate of its expansion — which is slow, perhaps one new chamber per decade — the builders may number only a few dozen. Or they may number in the hundreds, working in rotations so long that each one surfaces only once a year. There is no way to tell from the outside. The city grows, which means they are alive, which means the project continues, which means whatever they are building is not yet finished.

## The Sound

The quiet is not silence. The quiet is the sound of water at rest, which is a low and constant note — not a hum, not a drone, but a presence in the ear, like the sound of your own blood, externalized. Below that, and only audible when you submerge yourself, is the creak of the moss, which expands and contracts with the warmth of the water in cycles so slow that each contraction takes a full minute and each expansion takes another. It produces a sound like a wooden door opening somewhere far away, in a house that does not exist, repeated without rhythm. The kelp-fronds make no sound at all. The stone makes no sound. The builders, when they work, make a clicking that carries well through water — stone on stone, fitted and set — but the clicks are infrequent, and the intervals between them are long enough that you begin to doubt you heard the last one.

Above the water, in the air, the dominant sound is the rain, which falls and rises and drifts and makes a sound like someone breathing through cloth. During the dim hours, when the light is at its lowest, the rain-threads brighten slightly and the sound intensifies, and then falls away again. There is no wind to carry distant sounds. There is nothing distant. The mesa is the world, and the world is the water and the stone and the moss, and they are all very quiet.

## The Rules

Time moves differently here. Not faster or slower, but *deeper* — each hour seems to contain more duration than it should, as if the saturation of the air with water has thickened time itself, made it a medium rather than a line. A day spent on the upper towers feels like three days. The builders may experience this more acutely; their cycles of work and rest may be calibrated to a duration that has no equivalent on the surface.

The water does not cool. This is the central strangeness. At this altitude, in this cloud-cover, standing water should be cold. It is warm. It is warm at the surface and warm at depth, and it does not stratify into thermal layers the way standing water should. It is warm all the way down, uniformly, as if heated from within — but there are no vents, no volcanic indicators, no chemical signatures that explain it. The warmth is simply a property of this place, like the light, like the quiet, like the moss that grows downward toward the heat and fruits at the boundary where the warm water meets the warm air and cannot seem to decide which medium it belongs to.

Things dissolve slowly here. Not quickly enough to notice, but quickly enough to measure. A stone tool left on the mesa top loses its edge over a year. A piece of wood left in the water softens and comes apart over a decade. The air itself carries something — not acid, not alkali, but a solvent quality that is, perhaps, simply the property of being so thoroughly dissolved in water. Everything here is in the process of becoming water. The stone resists. The moss uses it. The builders work against it. But the tendency is there, patient and slow, and the city is a kind of argument with dissolution, a statement that structure can persist even in a medium that wants to unmake it.

The city will never be finished. This is evident from its design — or rather, from the absence of design, the way it grows organically outward and downward, adding chambers and passages in response to needs that are never resolved, only accommodated. The builders are not constructing a building. They are constructing a *relationship* with the water, with the moss, with the warm dark below. Each new chamber is a new word in a sentence that has no period. Each sealed passage is a decision not to go somewhere, which is itself a kind of construction. The city is a text being written in stone, and the water is the page, and the moss is the ink, and the builders are the authors, and they have been writing for a very long time, and they are not done.

---

## Being There

You stand on the highest tower at low tide. The water is six inches below the capstone. The stone is warm from the water below. The air is thick and dim and tastes of minerals and something sweet that might be the moss-fruit dissolving. You can see the tiers of the city descending into the water, each level darker than the last, until the moss obscures everything and there is only the green-black suggestion of more, much more, extending down the face of the mesa into the warm deep where the builders are working now, fitting stone to stone in the dark, clicking softly, building the next chamber of a city that has no end.

The rain rises around you. The light does not change. The quiet is enormous and full.

You are being observed. You will not see them. But you can feel the attention — mild, incurious, patient — the way you might watch a leaf fall into a pool. Not with concern. Not with interest, exactly. But with an awareness that the leaf is there, that it is not water, that it will dissolve eventually, and that there is no reason to hurry it.
