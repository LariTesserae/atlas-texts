A vast plain of manufactured material stretches to every horizon, unbroken by any natural contour. There is no bedrock beneath, no soil, no sediment—only an unbroken continuum of made-substance, a planet-scale construction so total that no original substrate remains. The surface is not metal, not ceramic, not polymer, but something that shares properties with all three—a material that was extruded or grown rather than assembled, marked by shallow ripples and flow-lines as if it set or cured while still in motion. The color shifts through dirty ambers and dull orange-yellows, warm as old bone, and it holds heat with an awful fidelity.

The heat is immense. It radiates upward from the surface in visible shimmy, and presses down from above through air that feels thickened, sluggish, barely willing to move. The temperature is the dominant fact of existence here. Even the shadows—where they exist—are hot, merely *less* hot by a degree that feels precious. The material absorbs the day's heat and refuses to release it, so the cooling at night is marginal, a brief respite before the temperature climbs again.

And yet water moves through this place. Not in abundance, but with persistence. The surface is not perfectly flat—over continental distances, it develops shallow basins, long swales, slight declivities that only become apparent when they fill. Moisture arrives in unpredictable events: a thin rain that falls from clouds the construction somehow generates, condensation that beads on the material when the temperature shifts past some threshold, fog that forms in the lower corridors and leaves dark stains of wetness as it burns off. The water has nowhere to go. No soil to seep into, no river channels to follow. It sits in the shallow depressions until the heat takes it back. The stain of wetness darkens the amber surface to something nearly umber, and when the water evaporates, it leaves faint mineral traces—pale rings, delicate salt-lines that map the boundaries of each fleeting pool. These marks accumulate. Over time, they form a sparse calligraphy across the surface, the only record that water was ever here.

The erosion is nascent, but it is happening. The construction is not complete, or rather, it is *completing*—still in the act of becoming what it will be. In places, the surface is not yet fully formed: the material is softer, almost tender, with a skin that hasn't fully set. Foot-thick ripples rise and freeze mid-wave. Low ridges, no more than knee-high, run for kilometers in directions that seem arbitrary until you walk them long enough to sense a pattern emerging from the apparent chaos. In some basins, the surface has a wrinkled, puckered quality, as if it shrank as it cured and hasn't yet been smoothed by whatever process will eventually finish it. There are cracks, but they are not the cracks of age and decay—they are the cracks of formation, of material separating as it sets, joints opening where the construction hasn't yet knit itself together. The edges of these cracks are sharp, fresh, not yet rounded by any wear. Dust—ground from the material itself by the minimal friction of wind and the chemical action of the intermittent water—gathers in these crevices. It is the only loose material in this world: a powder the color of the surface, indistinguishable from it until it moves.

There is nothing alive here. No animals, no insects, no birds. No vegetation anchors the dust or softens the horizon. The only processes are mechanical and chemical: the slow curing of the material, the cycling of heat, the arrival and departure of water. And yet the place does not feel dead. It feels *embryonic*. The immense scale of the construction and the visible evidence of ongoing formation create a sense of something vast coming into being. The heat is the heat of a body growing. The moisture that appears and vanishes is like respiration in something that hasn't yet opened its eyes.

The sound is nearly nothing. This is the quietest place that still moves. Wind crosses the surface at intervals—there are weather cycles, generated somehow by the heat differentials across the construction's continental reach—but the wind carries no sound of vegetation, no rustling, no animal cries. It makes only a faint dry whisper as it lifts dust from the crevices and moves it in low, sluggish curtains across the surface. When rain falls, there is a brief soft percussion of droplets on the material, and then the hiss of water beginning to vaporize almost immediately on contact. Then silence again, settling like dust. The silence has texture here. It is not the ringing silence of vacuum, but the dense, occupied silence of a space that is almost entirely empty but still contains the faint tremor of heat and the slow settling of a world still forming.

The light is ordinary, the light of a sun—though whether a real sun or some equivalent source, there is no way to know. It casts short, hard shadows from the forming ridges and the curing ripples. The surface is too vast for any curve to be visible, but walking it, you feel the curvature in other ways: in the way clouds form at certain distances, in the way the air thickens toward the horizons, in the way the weather arrives in systems that take days to pass. There is enough space here for weather of its own.

The sparse water, the sparse dust, the sparse mineral traces left by evaporation—these are the only interruptions in the continuum of made-material. Walking across this surface, you would find nothing else for hours, days, longer. The distances between features are measured in kilometers, in tens of kilometers. A shallow basin that holds rain might be a day's walk across, and the ridge that defines it might be so gradual you only notice it by the fact that water pools on one side and not the other. The low points are where the action is: the condensation, the pooling, the mineral deposition, the most active formation. The high points—the ridges, the swells—are drier, harder, further along in their curing. They are the oldest parts of the construction, or the parts that set first.

Nothing lives here, but something is *happening* here, at a pace that makes a human lifetime feel like a blink. The construction is making itself. The material is emerging from some process below or within the surface, cooling and curing and cracking and reforming. The heat drives it. The water, however sparse, shapes it—deposits minerals that will become part of the surface, smooths the fresh cracks with its condensation, leaves traces that mark the stages of formation. The wind moves the dust, which fills the cracks, which becomes substrate for whatever comes next, if anything comes next. The place feels like a foundation. Like the ground floor of something that will eventually support structures, or ecosystems, or purposes that cannot yet be imagined. Or like the surface of a thing that has no purpose beyond its own completion—a planet being manufactured from the outside in, or the inside out, by processes that do not require witnesses.

Stand still long enough and you can feel it: not vibration, not sound, but a faint warmth that rises from the material with a persistence that suggests intention. The heat does not merely radiate; it *insists*. And the surface, in all its continental emptiness, in all its sparse and intermittent activity, goes on becoming itself.
