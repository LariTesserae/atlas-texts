The sky does not exist. Above, there is only a low, suffocating ceiling of iron-gray vapor, so dense it presses down on the land like a lid on a crucible. This is a primordial place—not primordial in the sense of brontosaurian ferns and young volcanoes, but primordial at the end of time. It is a landscape that has settled into its absolute final form, eroded down to a near-flat, sprawling plain of damp, dark grooves and basins. It is ancient beyond reckoning, stripped of all geological ambition, scraped flat by eons of processes that have long since forgotten what they were grinding away.

And yet, it is packed. There is no empty space here. Every centimeter of the low, sluggish terrain is saturated with presence. 

There are no plants. Not a single blade of grass, not a fossil of a leaf. This is a mineral world, a sludgy, weeping lithosphere. The ground is a mosaic of porous scoria, volcanic glass frosted with the fatigue of deep ages, and beds of metallic sand that hold water like a bruised spleen. Water is everywhere—2.1 parts to a scale of 3. It does not flow; it seeps, it bleeds, it condenses. The atmosphere is so saturated that droplets of cold, metallic-tasting water form constantly on the black rocks, rolling down their sides in a perpetual, silent weeping. Pools of still, tannin-dark water sit in the shallowest depressions, their surfaces dimpled by the endless drip. The ground squelches underfoot, a cold sponge of rust and silica. 

The temperature sits just above freezing, a deep, structural cold that doesn't bite so much as it aches, seeping into the marrow of the world. In this dim half-light—where shadows don't exist because the light is too weak to cast them, only varying densities of murk—the water and stone seem to merge into a single gelid medium.

But this dead, wet mineral world is not still. It is deafening. The sound is overwhelming, a physical pressure on the eardrums, yet it is not the sound of nature. There are no winds, no storms. The roar comes from below. 

The land is a skin stretched over an industrial nightmare. Technology is here, scaled up to a geological magnitude, but it is no one's technology. There are no built structures, no factories with walls, no smokestacks, no control rooms, no artifacts left by hands. Instead, the crust itself is the machine. For miles in every direction, the packed terrain is broken by massive, exposed valve-flutes and iron bore-holes that rise like jagged teeth from the black mire. They are grown from the planet's own ore, extruded over millions of years by some blind, thermodynamic imperative. Pistons the size of town squares churn slowly in the viscous mud, driven by geothermal pressure and the sheer weight of the overlying damp. Massive, exposed gear-strata grind against each other at the bottom of weeping trenches, spitting up gouts of cold, oily water. The rhythmic, thunderous exhalations of subterranean engines shake the puddles, sending perfect concentric ripples expanding to their muddy shores. Sparks shower from friction-welded fault lines, providing the only flashes of light—sudden, blinding blue strobes that reveal the expanse for a fraction of a second before plunging it back into deep twilight. 

Amidst this grinding, roaring, piston-driven hellscape, there is life. Abundant, teeming life. But the biology here has never heard of carbon as God.

The fauna are not animals in any recognizable sense. They are cybernetic-fungal entities, chitinous and fungal-or-simpler, evolved to feed on the electrical potentials and kinetic bleed of the world-mechanism. They carpet the spaces between the massive gears. The ground crawls with them. There are herds of sightless, multi-pedalled grazers the size of hippos, their backs coated in thick layers of condensation and rust-fungi. They press their porous, filtration-maws against the weeping rocks, inhaling the heavy water to extract the minute currents within. 

Above them, in the suffocating mist, flocks of skeletal gliders circle—creatures of tense wire and lichen-parchment, riding the updrafts of hot exhaust venting from the planet's iron lungs. They screech, but their voices are mechanical, sounding like tearing sheet metal stripped to its resonant frequency. 

Predator and prey dynamics play out in the shadows, driven by hunger and electrical discharge. A pack of needle-limbed stalkers—eyeless, navigating by the electromagnetic fields radiating from the fault lines—descends upon a grazing herd. The stalkers do not bite; they pierce the thick dermal casings of the grazers and discharge massive voltaic shocks, stunning them before draining their fluid-circuits. The fight is a brief, violent cascade of sparking carapaces and boiling mud, over in seconds, swallowed instantly by the roaring din of the engines.

And then, the breakdown.

Reality here is a compromised host. The parameters of existence are suggestions, poorly enforced by a universe too distracted by the mechanical roar to maintain its own laws. In the deepest, most saturated pools—where the water is oldest and the sound of the subterranean engines resonates at a frequency that shatters geometry—the water flows uphill. It clings to the black glass rocks and climbs, defying gravity, weeping upward into the mist. 

Sometimes, a grazing beast, heavy with ingested water and electrical charge, steps onto a patch of saturated mud and simply flattens. Not crushed—just reduced, its three-dimensional mass pressed flawlessly into two dimensions, a perfect, writhing silhouette in the muck that continues to graze on the flat microbial paints of the surface, unaware it has been dimensionally collapsed. 

The sky—the low, iron ceiling—sometimes drips downward. Columns of vapor condense and fall, but halfway to the ground, they reverse, becoming solid, floating spheres of gray steam that drift horizontally into the grinding gear-strata to be silently pulverized. 

At the edge of sight, where the flat plain meets the dim limit of perception, the landscape stutters. It loops. A ridge of weeping stones and a trench of churning pistons will repeat three times, perfectly, like a texture failing to render, before snapping back into a single, agonizingly dense point. 

This is the world at the end of its tether. A cold, flat, packed expanse of wet stone and screaming engines, crawling with electric-flesh, where the weight of deep time and the volume of deafening sound have finally snapped the logic of space. It holds together only through momentum—engine-speed, predator-speed, the relentless drip of water—hurtling forward because it has forgotten how to stop, grinding on into the dim, damp infinity of a broken rule set.
