The plateau goes on forever.

It is a continent of dark, compressed slate—vast beyond any human comprehension of distance, stretching toward horizons that never seem to arrive. The elevation is immense; you feel it in the thinness of the air, in the way the dim, cloud-choked sky presses down like a weight, though the land itself is relentlessly flat. This is the top of the world, a tableland so ancient it has worn itself smooth. The erosion here is not a process but a completed fact. The stone has been weathered into its final, in-its-own-state form. There are no jagged edges left, no crumbling ridges—just an infinite expanse of low, rolling swales and shallow basins, all hewn from the same unyielding, iron-banded rock. It is a landscape in perfect, patient equilibrium, changing only on timescales that make geology seem frantic.

Moisture is a rumor here. It arrives not as rain, but as a persistent, clinging oppression. In the perpetual cold—just above freezing, never quite thawing—the air holds a dampness that never resolves into precipitation. Condensation weeps from the stone in the deepest hours of the dim twilight, pooling in the microscopic fractures and the shallow, pan-like depressions of the slate. These fleeting, shallow mirrors of water are gone by midday, evaporating into the gray haze before they can gather into streams. There is no hydrology here, no runoff, no rivers—only this exhausting, intermittent dampness that keeps the stone perpetually slick and dark.

And from the stone, the engines rise.

There is no architecture here, no foundations, no walls or doors—only industry stripped to its most brutal, geologic essence. The mechanisms are vast, cast from the same iron and carbon as the bedrock itself, and they are embedded directly into the slate. They erupt from the ground in thickets of corroded pistons and camshafts taller than redwoods, their surfaces pitted by ages of the plateau's damp exhalations. You cannot tell where the stone ends and the iron begins; the roots of the machines delve so deep into the crust that they seem native to it, as if the planet itself had calcified into these mechanical organs. 

They are powered by the slow geothermal sighs of the deep earth, by the faint pressure differentials of the continental wind, and by the sprawling, tarry reservoirs of hydrocarbons that bleed from the cracks. The production they serve is incomprehensible, scaled to the vastness of the plateau itself. Entire sections of the continent are dedicated to the extrusion of a single, continuous metal girder that spools out over the slate for miles before cooling and snapping under its own weight. There are bore-holes miles across, ringing with the subsonic thrum of air being pulled down into the furnace-chambers below. The electricity they generate does not light the plateau; it flows away through massive, pylon-like extrusions that march into the fog, feeding purposes entirely alien to this desolate roof of the world.

The dimness is absolute. The sky is a perpetual dusk, choked with the exhaust of a million slow-boiling refineries, the particulate mixing with the low, perpetual clouds to snuff out the sun. It casts the world in a bruised, twilight gray, deepening the shadows in the gouges where the machines sit. 

In this dimness, the sparse vegetation clings to the margins. It is not green; chlorophyll abandoned this place long ago. Instead, the flora is black and rust-colored—resinous, low-growing lichen that feeds on the sulfur and iron of the stone, and occasional, skeletal stalks of a crystalline plant that chimes silently in the unfelt wind. They are spaced widely apart, isolated sentinels across the miles of bare slate, their presence so thin they only emphasize the dominating emptiness.

But the emptiness is a lie. The plateau is overwhelmed by fauna.

They are the machines' children, and the machines' parasites. The fauna here has evolved to exploit the warmth of the slow-moving pistons and the nutrient-rich sludge that leaks from the pressure valves. There are the Boilerbacks—legless, slug-like grazers the size of shipping containers, their backs crusted with thermal-symbiotic ores, sliding in endless, silent processions from one heat-vent to the next. There are the Wire-wolves—lupine forms of calcified sinew and sharpened ferrous bone that pack in the thousands, hunting the oily runoff that pools in the slate basins. Above them, blocking out what little light filters down, swarm the Soot-rats—leathery, winged vermin with the wingspan of eagles, spiraling silently on the thermal updrafts from the exhaust stacks. 

There are so many of them that they are the landscape. From a distance, the shifting, dark masses of the grazers look like dunes of flesh and ore. The plateau is a living carpet of specialized, industrialized biology, a biome so saturated with creatures that the slate itself is rarely visible beneath the crush of bodies and the trails of viscous fluid they leave behind. 

Yet, for all this overwhelming abundance of life and mechanized industry, the plateau is submerged in a profound and terrifying quiet. The scale of the place swallows sound. The pistons move so slowly—taking minutes to complete a single stroke—that their movement is perceived as a glacial shifting of shadows rather than a mechanical roar. The legions of fauna are soundless; the Boilerbacks secrete mucus that silences their friction against the stone, the Wire-wolves pad on calluses of compressed ash, and the swarms above glide on wings thick with oil-dampened down. The only sound is the occasional, deeply subsonic thrum of a bore-hole shifting the air, felt in the marrow rather than heard by the ear. It is a silence of total saturation—a heavy, pressurized quiet born of millions of organisms breathing in perfect, mechanized unison.

This is a world that has settled into its own bizarre physics. It is a place of slow gradients and patient cycles, where biology and industry have fused into a single, weathered geologic fact. There is no history here, only the endless, dim present—the damp, the cold, the silent shuffle of iron-clad beasts beneath the slow, breathing stroke of the engines.
