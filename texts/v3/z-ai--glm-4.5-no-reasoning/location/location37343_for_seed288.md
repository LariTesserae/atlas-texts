# The Comburan Deep

Below the surface of the world, where the crust folds inward and the sky becomes a memory, there exists a city that has never known rain.

It is called a city because that is the nearest word, though it predates the concept. The structures began as something grown—coraloids, the old scholars called them, though scholarship itself has gone quiet now. Massive load-bearing columns of chitin and mineralized cellulose rise from the cavern floor and branch overhead, interlocking, fusing where they meet, forming vaulted ceilings that stretch away into haze. There are streets. There are tiers. There is the clear evidence of intention, of design, of *inhabitation*—but no tools were ever used, no hands ever shaped the arches. The coraloids grew toward each other over millennia, guided by something that was not intelligence but was not entirely random either. Tropism on an architectural scale. The city is a fossil of behavior.

---

The floor of the Deep is flat. Flatter than flat—planed by time. This is what three billion years of stability looks like: every edge surrendered, every peak ground to its essential horizontal. The mineral substrate is a pale, dense evaporite, something between halite and a mineral that has no terrestrial name because it formed in conditions that no longer exist anywhere in the universe. It crunches underfoot with a sound like old ice, though there has never been ice here. The surface is fine-grained, almost polished, and it tastes of copper and absence.

This is the oldest stable state. The Deep has not changed in any meaningful way since before the oxygen catastrophe, since before the first organism considered the possibility of movement. The erosion parameter is not a process here—it is a *condition*. The land has arrived at its final form. What remains is what persists.

---

There is no water.

This must be understood not as a lack but as a *constitution*. The Deep is not dry the way a desert is dry—deserts remember water, expect it, arrange themselves around its absence. The Deep has never been otherwise. The chemistry here runs on other solvents: supercritical carbon dioxide in the fissures, ammoniacal fluids in the biological tissues, something viscous and amber that seeps from the coraloid joints and is neither oil nor sap but serves the functions of both. When you breathe here—and you would need apparatus—what enters your lungs is a gas mix heavy in nitrogen and argon, with traces of sulfur compounds that the local life treats as unremarkable.

The air is cool. Not cold—there is no windchill, no convection, no meaningful air movement at all in the lower tiers. But the rock breathes a slow chill that seeps through boot soles, through the pads of feet, through whatever you stand on. The temperature has not varied by more than two degrees in a hundred million years. Heat sources are buried deep and distant; the cavern is a refrigerator the size of a mountain range, and it has been running since the Archean.

---

The vegetation is the first thing that strikes you, and the last thing you stop thinking about.

It is lush. That word feels wrong here—lush implies moisture, softness, the give of soil beneath roots—but there is no other word for the sheer *abundance* of biological matter. Every surface that is not actively hostile to attachment is claimed. The coraloid columns are sheathed in layered growth: at the base, thick mats of rubbery dark maroon *something* that might be a lichen-analogue or might be the juvenile form of something much larger. Above that, trailing sheets of pale filamentous growth, almost white, that hang in curtains and move in the faint thermal gradients. Higher still, where the light is even dimmer, the surfaces erupt with bulbous formations—gas-bladders, you realize, not leaves—translucent, faintly luminescent, filled with whatever metabolic gases the flora here metabolizes.

Nothing is green. Chlorophyll is a water-based molecule's solution to a water-based problem. Here, the photosynthetic analogues run on retinal-based chemistry, on carotenoid derivatives, on pigments that absorb in the infrared and fluoresce in the deep red. The dominant color is a dark, oxidized purple, shot through with veins of rust-orange where the ammoniacal sap runs close to the surface. In the dimness, the colors read as almost monochrome—dark masses against darker stone—but when your eyes fully adapt, you see the purple is alive with variation, with pattern, with the sort of endless differentiation that only three billion years of stable competition can produce.

And it is competition, even now. The surfaces of the coraloids are battlefields, centimeters thick with layered claims. Nothing decays quickly here—the dry cold preserves, the mineral substrate does not support rapid decomposition—so each generation grows on the compressed remains of its ancestors. The vegetation you see is only the skin of a biological formation that may be tens of meters deep in places. The lushness is a surface tension on an ocean of compressed time.

---

The fauna is what makes the Deep *loud*.

The silence that you expect in underground places does not exist here. The sound is constant, layered, oppressive—a wall of acoustic presence that makes conversation impossible and thought difficult. The sources are numerous and mostly invisible.

The largest contributors are the *resonators*: barrel-shaped fauna, somewhere between arthropod and annelid in their body plan, that anchor themselves to the coraloid surfaces and rhythmically contract gas-bladders in their abdomens. Each pulse produces a low, resonant boom that carries for hundreds of meters through the dense air. They contract in patterns—territorial displays, mate-attractions, warnings—and there are millions of them across the Deep, each with its own frequency and rhythm. The combined effect is a basso profundo drone that you feel in your chest more than hear with your ears, punctuated by individual strikes that surface and submerge like breaching whales.

Above that foundation, the *scuttlers* contribute a higher register. Thousands of species, most no larger than a hand, moving across every surface in every direction on legs numbering from six to forty-two. Their chitin legs on the polished mineral floor produce a sound like static, like rain on a tin roof, like all the small noises of a forest floor compressed and amplified. They do not stop. They do not rest. The cold slows them only slightly; their metabolisms run on different chemistry, and they are always feeding, always moving, always clicking and scraping and producing the granular texture that fills the mid-range of the Deep's soundscape.

And then the *callers*: things that vocalize. Not with air through vocal cords—they have no lungs, no air to push—but with specialized structures that vibrate the substrate itself, transmitting through the mineral floor and the coraloid framework. Each call is a localized earthquake, a trembling that you feel through your feet and that the other fauna feel through specialized receptors in their undersides. The calls are periodic, cyclical, tied to metabolic rhythms that have no connection to day or night (there is neither here) but that create, in aggregate, a pattern of peaks and valleys in the noise level that takes approximately forty-seven hours to complete. The Deep's residents do not measure time in hours. They measure it in cycles of loud and slightly-less-loud.

---

The city—the *structure*—extends beyond sight in every direction.

That is the scale of the place. You stand at an intersection of three broad avenues, each wide enough for twenty abreast, and the avenues extend away into the purple-gray haze until perspective collapses them into nothing. The tiers rise above you—how many?—disappearing into a ceiling that is either very far away or very dim, the distinction meaningless in this light. The coraloid columns are spaced at intervals that feel deliberate but follow no geometric system you can discern, creating spaces that are sometimes intimate, sometimes cathedral-vast, sometimes both as you walk through them.

The density is sparse. For all the structures, for all the architecture, the place feels *empty*. Not abandoned—the fauna is too abundant, the vegetation too vigorous, for abandonment—but uninhabited by whatever the city was built for. The coraloids form rooms that have no doors, corridors that lead to walls, chambers that open onto other chambers in configurations that suggest a purpose you cannot reconstruct. The spaces between the structures are vast, and they are not vacant—they are full of life, full of growth, full of sound—but they are empty of *meaning* as you understand it. Whatever intelligence guided the coraloids' growth, whatever pattern determined the city's layout, it has not been present for a very long time. What remains is the skeleton of an intention, colonized by organisms that have no concept of architecture.

You walk for an hour and encounter no one. The sounds never change. The vegetation thickens and thins according to rules written in nutrient gradients and light availability and the chemistry of the substrate, but the *city* does not change. The same arches, the same vaults, the same branching columns, repeated with endless variation across a distance that could swallow a surface-world nation whole. This is a place that has arrived at its final form and will remain in it until the heat death of the planet.

---

The light is the final strangeness, or the first one, depending on how you enter.

There is no sun. There is no sky. The light in the Deep is biological in origin, produced by the gas-bladders of the higher vegetation and by specialized organs on perhaps ten percent of the fauna species. It is dim—it is always dim—and it is deeply, thoroughly red. The luminescent organs produce photons in the 650-700 nanometer range, the longest wavelengths that still qualify as visible light, and they produce them in quantities that barely exceed the threshold of human perception.

Your eyes adapt, and then they adapt further, and then they reach the limit of what rhodopsin can do. In the resulting gray-on-deep-red darkness, you navigate by sound as much as sight—the resonators' calls give you distance and direction, the scuttlers' movements give you texture and proximity. You develop a new sense of the space around you, constructed from acoustics and air movement and the faint thermal signatures that your skin can detect when the temperature differential is just large enough.

And in that darkness, the Deep continues. The coraloids stand. The vegetation grows its slow millimeters per century. The fauna sounds its unending oratorio of clicks and booms and substrate-tremors. The mineral floor crunches underfoot, unchanged since the eon when it first settled out of whatever ancient sea or magma chamber produced it.

This is a place at the end of time, and it is not quiet, and it is not dead, and it is not empty. It is full of a life that has had three billion years to perfect itself, in a city that was grown by processes no one remembers, in a darkness that the inhabitants have never found remarkable, making sounds that have never known silence.
