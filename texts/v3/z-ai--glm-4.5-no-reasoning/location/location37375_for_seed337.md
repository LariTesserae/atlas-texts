To descend into this place is to enter a city that is still becoming. It is below the surface of the earth, nestled in a wide, regional trough of exposed bedrock, where the ceiling of the world is a low shelf of slate and packed clay. The scale is municipal—an entire district of emerging streets—but it possesses none of the finished assurance of an inhabited realm. This is a city in its embryonic state, still half-chiseled from the raw geology, saturated with the presence of construction and the immense, packed density of stone yet to be cleared.

The terrain is a labyrinth of half-carved blocks and emerging walls. There is no empty space here; the density is absolute. Every corridor is a tight squeeze between the bulk of a building-to-be and the solid matrix of the earth it sits in. The "streets" are merely the negative space where the rock has been removed, trenches and channels carved out of the monolithic bedrock, still bearing the coarse, parallel grooves of stone picks. The walls do not rise; they are revealed. The architecture is subtractive, dug out of a single massive deposit of compressed ash and sedimentary mudstone. Doorways are rough arches chipped into the stone, and ceilings are supported not by pillars, but by the stubborn refusal of the rock overhead to fracture. It is an urban place, organized with the logic of a town—grids emerge, intersections align, and the footprint of a central plaza is distinguishable—but it is a town frozen in the act of excavation, a packed, urban skeleton sheathed in the womb of the earth.

The temperature sits in the bones. It is cool here, a steady, damp chill that seeps through roughspun cloth and settles in the joints. The moisture is intermittent but inescapable. It does not flow; it weeps. Water seeps slowly through the micro-fissures of the stone ceiling, collecting into glossy, mineral-thick droplets that hang in the silence before falling. Pockets of water gather in the uneven hollows of the floor, black and perfectly still, reflecting the dim, normal light of tallow lamps in inverted smears of amber. The air smells of wet dust, clay, and the metallic tang of struck stone. It is a riverine dampness without a river, a humidity born of the earth breathing slowly through its pores.

Vegetation is virtually nonexistent, save for a sparse, powdery bloom of white calcite that crusts along the deeper fissures where the water runs heaviest. It looks less like plant life and more like the stone itself sweating out a mineral frost. Fauna is similarly rare—life here is too raw, too unfinished. Occasionally, a pale, eyeless cave beetle scuttles across the groove of a chisel mark, seeking the damp crevices, but it is a solitary creature in a vast, packed emptiness. 

The technology that shaped this place is rudimentary, leaving its mark on every surface. This is a city built by hand, with simple tools and immense, grinding labor. The walls are scored with the diagonal slashes of mattocks, the circular bore-marks of hand-driven augers used to split the rock, and the blackened, shallow craters where small, controlled fires were lit to heat the stone before it was quenched with cold water to crack it. There are no metal fixtures, no finished carpentry, no refined industry—only the patient, primitive violence of early tools against ancient stone.

And yet, for all its embryonic stillness, the place is remarkably active. It hums with a low, aggregate dynamic. The sounds are quiet, but numerous, layering into a dense, subterranean rhythm: the persistent, overlapping *tak-tak-tak* of stone hammers echoing from deep corridors; the sudden, basso *crack* of a boulder splitting along a fault line; the rhythmic sloshing of water being bailed from a freshly dug trench; the scrape of wicker baskets dragging loose tailings along the carved roads. It is the sound of a town-sized organism slowly digesting itself into existence.

In the normal, diffuse light of whale-oil lamps and smudge pots, the shadows are long and deep, pooling in the unexcavated corners. The light catches the damp sheen on the walls, making the rock gleam like the skin of something alive. There is no weirdness here, no occult geometries or alien presences—only the profound, almost mundane strangeness of a place caught in the awkward, breathless pause between being a mountain and being a home. The rules of the world are the rules of geology: patience, pressure, and the slow, manual removal of the unnecessary.
