A place where no rain has ever fallen, and no river will ever cut. 

You stand in the Amber Intestine—one of the deep metabolic corridors of a world that was entirely made, and then abandoned by its makers so long ago that the making itself has become the planet's geology. There is no natural substrate here. No bedrock, no soil, no sand. Everything beneath your feet, around your walls, and stretching up to the distant ceiling is fabricated. The ground is a contiguous mosaic of tessellated ceramic tiles, each the size of a human palm, fitted together with primitive, painstaking precision. There is no mortar; the tiles interlock via carved grooves, a technology of hands and patience, of simple tools and immense time. 

It is warm here, suffocatingly so. Heat radiates up from the dense latticework of the floor, the residual breath of some profound, slow-burning reaction occurring in the bowels of the structure far below. The corridor is low and narrow—cathedral in its proportions, perhaps three meters wide and ten tall, pressing in on you with a claustrophobic intimacy. The scale is small, a single convoluted passage, yet it is packed to the point of asphyxiation. 

Because the walls are alive. 

Lush vegetation clambers over every manufactured surface, but this is flora that has never known a drop of water. Instead, the roots—pale, thick, and rubbery—suckle at the seams between the ceramic tiles. They drink the condensation of the ambient heat, or perhaps they feed directly on the unknown dry chemistry that seeps from the structure's pores. The leaves are waxy, deep burgundy and oily green, overlapping like the scales of a leviathan. They cluster so densely that the underlying architecture is nearly devoured; you are walking through a throat of ceramic and flesh-like foliage, the air thick with the scent of heated resin and dry sap. 

The fauna is equally abundant, and equally divorced from aqueous biology. Millipedes the length of your forearm, their chitin translucent and amber, navigate the root-mats with ceaseless, patient articulation. They have no moisture to shed; their bodies are dry mechanisms of jointed plate and internal saline sacs, moving like clockwork tractors through the undergrowth. Above them, pale geometric moths cling to the ceiling tiles, sipping the mineral exhaust that vents from tiny, purpose-drilled apertures in the masonry. 

And here, the architecture begins to fail. 

The erosion is young. This corridor was not worn down by wind or rain—there is none—but by the sheer, slow violence of the vegetation pushing outward. The ceramic tiles are freshly cracked, their edges sharp as broken glass. Whole sections of wall bulge outward, fractured into jagged teeth by the slow, relentless swell of the root-balls behind them. It is a young erosion, an early-formation catastrophe of splitting and heaving, exposing the hollow, dry veins of the structure's interior where the primitive makers once ran their cables of twisted copper and dried gut. 

But it is the quiet that reveals the breakdown. 

The sound here is almost nonexistent, a muted, padded hush. The dense leaves absorb the scuttling of the millipedes; the waxy petals silence the air. Yet, if you stand still, you realize the quiet is not empty. It is the sound of reality being digested. You hear the infinitesimal crunch of a tile yielding to a root's expansion—a sound like a bone breaking in deep water. You hear the low, sub-harmonic thrum of the structure’s deep heat, vibrating at a frequency felt in the sockets of your eyes. 

And then, the geometry goes wrong. 

The weirdness is absolute. Because the makers are gone, their primitive logic has lost its anchor, and the made-world is dreaming. You look down the corridor, and the parallel lines of the walls do not converge. They weave, braiding together and apart like strands of muscle, yet they never intersect. A tile on the floor will suddenly have seven sides, then five, its angles shifting when you blink, though the physical edge beneath your boot remains sharp and unforgiving. A cluster of dry-feeding moths will take flight, and for a suspended, nauseating moment, they leave shadows on the wall that belong to hands—human hands—reaching for a tool that no longer exists. 

The plants grow in fractal spirals that reverse their chirality depending on which eye you use to observe them. Space feels laminated. You can reach out and touch the opposite wall, but walking toward it takes an hour, then a second, then three steps. Time thickens in the root-dense pockets. You are in a place of total artifice, built by simple hands, that has outlived its creators and is now, in the patient, grinding silence, waking up into something that biology and geometry cannot share.
