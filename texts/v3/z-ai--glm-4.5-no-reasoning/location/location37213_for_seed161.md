# The Undergrove of Kael Thar

It is warm here, and close, and old beyond reckoning.

---

You descend into it. There is no other way to arrive. The last staircase—a thing of fitted stone, each step worn to a bowl by the passage of feet that stopped coming centuries ago—ends at a gap in the bedrock where the roots have shouldered the rock apart. You squeeze through. And then you are inside.

The Undergrove occupies the space of a cathedral, or rather the space that a cathedral would occupy if a cathedral were grown rather than built, and if it had never been designed by any mind but instead had slowly, over geological time, become what it is. Which is to say: a chamber, roughly dome-shaped, perhaps sixty meters across at its widest, perhaps thirty at its highest point—which is not very high, and yet feels immense because the ceiling comes down on you like a held breath, like the sky deciding to be intimate. The stone overhead is visible in patches between the root-mass, and it is ancient stone, sedimentary stone laid down in some sea that existed before the seas that anyone remembers. The layers tilt at angles that speak of tectonic events of stupendous age, but the stone itself is settled now, has been settled for longer than the word *settled* conveys. It has arrived at its final configuration. It will not move again before the sun fails.

The roots—this is the first thing you must understand—are not roots in the way you think of roots. They are not the underground organs of trees that stand above. There are no trees above. There is no *above* above, not in any meaningful sense. The Undergrove lies beneath something, some thickness of rock and dead earth and older rubble, and what grows here grows *here*, self-contained, a closed system that has been closed so long that it has forgotten it was ever open. The roots belong to the things that grow in the grove, which are not trees exactly but occupy the ecological niche of trees and do so with a kind of patient, lightless ambition that has had eons to refine itself.

---

**There is no water.**

This takes time to perceive because the place feels wet. The air is humid—warm and close and heavy with respiration, with the exhalations of a hundred thousand organisms doing the slow chemistry of staying alive. Your skin prickles with it. Condensation beads on the stone, on the bark, on your forearms. And yet there are no streams. No pools. No drip of water from the ceiling, though the stone above is slick with moisture. The humidity comes from the life itself, from transpiration and breath and the wet surfaces of living things, and it is enough—has been enough for whatever span of time this place has been sealed—to sustain everything here without a single drop of free water ever pooling or flowing.

What the organisms drink, they drink from the air. What moisture exists exists in suspension, in vapor, in the boundary layer that clings to every surface like a second skin. The large root-structures—there are seven of them that dominate the chamber, each as thick as a man's torso, each branching into a canopy of lesser roots that interlock and weave and form the architecture of the space—they draw their hydration from the stone itself, from micropockets of bound water trapped in the mineral matrix when the rock was laid down. You can see where they have eaten into the bedrock: shallow excavations, not violent but digestive, the stone rounded and pitted where the root-tips have pressed and secreted and slowly dissolved their way inward over millennia. The roots are eating the mountain. They have been eating it since before the mountain was a mountain. They will finish it eventually, but *eventually* is a word that does different work here than it does above.

---

The vegetation is not green.

Or rather—the chlorophyll-based organisms are green, but they are the minority, the understory, the small soft things that carpet the rock where the roots do not and that form a spongy mat underfoot that muffles your steps and smells of loam and something else, something sharper, almost metallic, which is the scent of the other kind. The dominant growth is a deep reddish-brown, the color of dried blood or old brick, and it is not a plant at all but something that occupies the kingdom next to plants, or the kingdom between plants and fungi, or a kingdom that has no name because no one has been here to name it and the thing itself does not care about names.

These are the column-growths. They are what take the place of trees. There are perhaps forty of them mature enough to reach the ceiling, and their trunks are not round but fluted, deeply grooved, with a texture like the inside of a bellows or the throat of something that breathes. They are warm to the touch. You realize this when you press your palm against one: there is a heat in them, not the ambient heat of the chamber but something more, an endogenous warmth, as though they are doing something inside that generates energy. And they are—they are *digesting*. The column-growths are heterotrophs, or partly heterotrophs, or something for which the distinction has never been clean. They photosynthesize, but they photosynthesize in a spectrum you cannot see, the long slow infrared radiating from the stone itself, from the ambient heat of the enclosed space, from the bodies of the fauna that move among them. They eat light you cannot perceive. They eat heat. They eat the chemical gradients that form where root meets rock. They have had long enough to learn to eat everything that is here, and so they do.

From their trunks, the shelf-growths emerge. These are the canopy: broad horizontal surfaces, layered like shingles, overlapping, each shelf perhaps a meter across and a few centimeters thick, reaching out from the trunk in all directions and interlocking with the shelves of neighboring columns until the ceiling of the chamber is more shelf than open space, more biology than geology. It is these shelves that give the Undergrove its structure, its rooms, its passages. The space beneath the lowest shelves is the main floor—open, relatively, if you consider a space thick with trunks and root-cables and hanging growths to be *open*. Above that, between the shelf-layers, are the interstitial spaces: narrow gaps, half a meter to a meter high, extending laterally through the chamber like the floors of a building that was never designed but simply grew, each shelf adding a level, each level filling with its own community of smaller organisms, its own light-conditions, its own microclimate. The whole thing is dense. Dense the way a city is dense, the way a reef is dense—every surface occupied, every volume claimed, the life layered on itself with an efficiency that speaks of fierce slow competition conducted over spans too long for urgency to be relevant.

---

**The light is a problem.**

Or rather—it is not a problem for anything here, but it is a problem for you, because you are a creature of sight and there is almost nothing to see by. The light that exists is bioluminescent, and it is dim, and it is not meant for you.

The glows come from the fauna. From the threads that hang between the shelves. From the patches of slime-mold analogue that colonize the root-surfaces. From the things that move. The light is blue-green, mostly, with occasional pulses of amber, and it is intermittent—not flashing but breathing, brightening and dimming on cycles that seem to correspond to nothing you can measure. The effect is of being underwater, of being in a place where light comes from life and life has its own schedule for radiance.

You let your eyes adjust. It takes a long time. When they do, the Undergrove resolves into a space of shadows and surfaces, of depth implied more than seen, of forms that suggest more than they reveal. The column-trunks stand like pillars in a temple that has no god and no congregation and no purpose except persistence. The shelves overhead are darkness against darkness, their undersides faintly visible where the bioluminescence of the creatures above them leaks down through cracks and gaps. The root-cables cross and recross the space like the rigging of a ship that will never sail, and where they touch they have grown together, forming knots and junctions that are structurally sound and also, you realize, *communication*—the organisms connected through these grafted points, sharing something, chemical or electrical or something else, a network of exchange that has been growing since before your species learned to speak.

The floor beneath your feet is not flat. It is hummocked and ridged, the root-mass pushing up the loam-layer, creating micro-elevations, little watersheds of condensation where the humidity collects and runs in films rather than streams. You walk carefully. The footing is treacherous not because it is slippery but because it is *irregular*, every step finding a different angle, a different texture, a different degree of give in the substrate.

---

**The fauna is abundant, and it is loud.**

Not loud the way a city is loud, or a battlefield—loud the way a forest is loud when every insect is singing at once, except the singers here are not insects, or not only insects, and their chorus has a quality that you cannot immediately categorize.

There are the clickers. Small—finger-length—many-legged, moving in quick darting paths across the shelf-surfaces and trunk-flutes and sometimes across your boot. Their shells are dark and smooth and they make sound by snapping their tail-segments against the substrate: *click-click-click*, a rapid series, each click sharp and distinct, overlapping with the clicks of ten thousand others until the sound becomes a texture, a crackling, like the burning of the world's largest hearth. This is the baseline sound of the Undergrove, the noise-floor, the thing that never stops.

On top of that: the thrums. The thrummers are larger—forearm-length—and they cling to the undersides of the shelves in dense clusters, their bodies pressed together in masses of dozens, their limbs folded, their mouths—because they have mouths, complex mouthparts, not for eating but for *resonating*—held against the shelf-surface. They vibrate. The vibration passes through the shelf and amplifies it, and the shelf itself becomes a speaker, a membrane, radiating sound into the space below. Each thrummer produces a single low note, and each cluster produces a chord, and the clusters are distributed across the chamber in a pattern that you begin to suspect is not random, because the chords do not clash, they harmonize, they form a music that is not music in any aesthetic sense but is music in a structural sense, a signaling system of extraordinary complexity, and it is *loud*, the thrumming, loud enough to feel in your sternum, in your teeth, in the fluid of your inner ear where it competes with your own sense of balance.

And over that, under that, through that: the calls. Longer sounds, more variable, produced by the things you cannot see clearly in the deeper shadows. There are grazers—you hear them before you see them, the sound of their feeding, a moist rhythmic scraping as they crop the ground-cover with mouthparts that are apparently made of something harder than the vegetation they consume. There are things that move through the shelf-layers above, their weight causing the shelves to flex and creak, the sound of a living architecture under load. There are things that call to each other across the chamber, sounds that rise and fall, that have syntax, that are not random but are not meant for you and so remain opaque, a language being spoken all around you that you will never learn.

The overall effect is of being inside an organism. The sound is the sound of metabolism, of process, of a closed system cycling its materials through the bodies of its inhabitants and back again, the click and thrum and call the acoustic signature of a place that is alive in every cubic centimeter and knows it and does not care that you are here.

---

**The built structures are old and partly absorbed.**

You find them near the base of the eastern wall, where the stone is close to the surface and the shelf-canopy is thinnest. Walls, or the remains of walls—low constructions of fitted stone, mortared with something that has long since become indistinguishable from the rock itself, the joints mineralized, the whole thing more geological than architectural. There are perhaps six structures visible, or the traces of six structures, none taller than your shoulder, none larger than a small room. They are not buildings in any useful sense. They are the fossils of buildings, the shapes that buildings leave when the buildings themselves have been gone for ten thousand years and only their foundations remain, and the roots have grown through the foundations and the shelf-growths have colonized their walls and the loam has filled their interiors, and what is left is the *idea* of a wall more than the wall itself.

Someone built here. Someone came down the staircase—or they built the staircase—and they made these walls, and they lived here, or tried to, or did something here that required shelter and division and the definition of space that walls provide. They used stone because stone was what they had. They fitted it by hand—the tooling marks are still visible if you look closely, chisel-dimples, the signature of a hand working a piece of metal against a piece of rock, over and over, until the rock was the shape the hand wanted. The metal was simple. Bronze, perhaps, or something like bronze, or iron of the earliest kind, soft and quick to dull, requiring patience that the builder clearly had.

Who they were is unreadable. What they did here is gone. The Undergrove has consumed their works the way it consumes everything—not violently, not with destruction, but with the slow patient incorporation that is the only mode of change available to a place where everything happens at geological pace. Their walls are part of the ecosystem now. The column-growths root against them. The fauna shelters in their remnants. The moisture cycles through their stone. The builders themselves—there is no trace of them at all, unless they became part of the loam you walk on, unless their calcium and their carbon and their iron were taken up by the roots and incorporated into the shelves and eaten by the clickers and thrummed by the thrummers and are still here, still circulating, still part of the closed system, still present in the warm humid dark that does not remember them and does not forget.

---

You stand in the Undergrove of Kael Thar and you feel the thrumming in your bones and you watch the bioluminescent blue-green pulse and fade and pulse again and you listen to the clicking that never stops and you feel the warmth rising from the column-trunks and the moisture beading on your skin and you understand, slowly, because everything here happens slowly, that you are in a place that has reached its end-state. Not its death—its *completion*. This is what a place looks like when it has had long enough to become perfectly itself. Every organism here is adapted to every other organism. Every niche is filled. Every surface is occupied. The competition has been resolved, not through victory and defeat but through accommodation, through the endless slow adjustment of boundaries until everyone's boundary is everyone else's boundary and the whole system is one system, one organism, one body breathing in its cathedral of root and stone.

It will change eventually. The rock will finish digesting. The shelf-growths will grow too heavy for their columns and fall, and the gap in the canopy will alter the light, and the light will alter the community, and the community will alter the sound, and the Undergrove will become something else. But *eventually* is measured here in the spans that mountains use for their arithmetic. For any duration you can comprehend, this place is what it is, and what it is is *finished*, and the finishing is not stillness but the opposite—a dense, loud, warm, humid, living stasis in which everything happens and nothing changes and the thrumming goes on and the clicking goes on and the blue-green light pulses and fades and pulses again in the dark.
