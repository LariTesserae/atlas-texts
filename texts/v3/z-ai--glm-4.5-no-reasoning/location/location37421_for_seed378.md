The air does not move. It hangs, dense and saturated, a membrane of heated vapor that coats the lungs with every drawn breath. This is a landscape of profound, oppressive quiet, where the ambient roar of industry and the hiss of superheated water have been swallowed by the acoustic dampening of the terrain itself. Sound dies here. Footfalls on the obsidian grit make no noise; the shouts of a distant work crew are flattened into a dull, leaden vibration felt in the sternum rather than heard by the ear.

You stand on a high, vast plateau, elevated a mile above some unseen planetary baseline. The sky above is not blue but a bleached, agonizing white, pierced by the blinding, magnesium-flare of a sun that seems too close. The light is absolute—it burns the shadows out of the crevices and makes the air shimmer with thick, torpid mirages. It is a landscape baked to the consistency of fired ceramic, yet it is profoundly, disruptively wet.

There is no mud. There is no soil. Vegetation is entirely absent—not a single blade of grass, not a moss, not a lichen survives in this caustic heat. Instead, the plateau is a mineral world of dark, vesicular basalt and rusted iron-strata, weathered over millennia into smooth, undulating forms. The water does not fall from the sky; it exhales from the earth. Thermal vents, fissures, and weeping pores dot the expanse, forcing up superheated groundwater that hits the scorching air and instantly flashes into rolling banks of condensation. The landscape is perpetually post-rain, perpetually slick, though the water never truly cools. It runs in thin, scalding rivulets down the hollows of the stone, pooling in expansive, shallow terraces of steaming, mineral-saturated broth that reflect the blinding white sky like fractured mirrors.

Amidst this sparse, sweeping geology rises the city—an organism of iron and brick that seems to have been grown rather than built, seeded into the rock by some ancient industrial mandate. The construction is dense and layered near the plateau's center, a sprawling labyrinth of smokestacks, retorts, and distillation columns. But as it stretches outward toward the edges, it frays into a sparse scatter of isolated processing units, standing like metallic monoliths in the empty, steaming expanse. The machines are running; the pistons stroke and the gears turn, yet they operate in a vacuum of noise, their mechanical exhalations absorbed by the wet, heavy air and the porous stone. The infrastructure does not process crops or wood—there are none. It processes the water itself, separating the boiling mineral broth into heavy water, metallic salts, and volatile alcohols that vent from copper pipes as shimmering, toxic plumes. 

The true strangeness of the place reveals itself in its ecology, if it can be called that. It is a fungal, post-vegetal world. Where plants would root, vast mycelial networks have colonized the damp interstices of the basalt, threading through the weeping rocks in thick, leathery chords of grey and sickly phosphorescent pink. They thrive on the heat and the chemical soup, erupting from the steam vents in bulbous, fleshy brackets that drip hot condensation back into the pools. 

And where there is moisture and fungus, the fauna teem, abundant and bizarre. The skies—though blinding—criss-cross with the silhouettes of thermophilic manta-rays, skin-winged grazers with translucent, vein-mapped bellies, riding the violent updrafts of the thermal vents. On the ground, the scalding pools wriggle with dense shoals of blind, limbless salamanders, their skin the exact obsidian black of the rock, making the water appear to churn with living shards of stone. Between the iron legs of the industrial infrastructure, armored isopods the size of hounds scuttle in absolute silence, their mandibles clicking in a frequency swallowed by the damp air, harvesting the fungal blooms and dragging them down into the cool, wet dark beneath the factory floors.

It is a world in motion, yet moving like a creature in a fever dream. The processes are relentless: the steady exhalation of steam, the slow crawl of the fungal creep over rusting pipes, the boiling migration of the blind salamanders from one thermal pool to the next. But it all unfolds under the crushing weight of the heat and the deep, oceanic silence. The landscape breathes, sweats, and digests, a vast, urbanized digestive tract built on a high and scalding plain, enduring in a state of weathered, corrosive balance.
