A railway spur, dead-ended and half-reclaimed, passes through a gap in a living wall of wood and leaf. Beyond the tracks lies the district they call the Sapwood Ward—a small, bounded parcel of low, damp ground where the city's industrial logic was long ago subsumed by a total, patient botanical presence. 

The terrain is flat, a low basin just inches above the slow water table. Underfoot, the ground is not dirt or gravel but a continuous, spongy mat of root-felt and decayed bark, springy and waterlogged. Dampness here is not a standing flood, but a pervasive seep. Moisture beads on the underside of every leaf, condenses on cool metal, and weeps from the pores of thick, corky bark. It rains intermittently—not from the sky, which is obscured by the dense canopy above, but from above the pathways, where the broad leaves of structural canopy trees trap the evening mist and release it in sudden, heavy drops throughout the cool mornings.

There is almost nothing here that is not plant. The vegetation is the substrate, the architecture, and the infrastructure. The ward is a grove-room, barely a city block in scale, bounded by the ancient bulwarks of grafted boles that form an unbroken wall of wood around its perimeter. Inside, the "buildings" are massive, hollowed root-cages and living trunk-houses, their interiors shaped by generations of skilled arborists who coaxed the cambium to grow around empty space. Floors are woven from thick, living taproots; walls are overlapping shelves of shelf-fungus and bark; roofs are dense, interlocked canopies of evergreen fronds.

Yet this is no primitive forest. The industrial era laid its bones here before the green tide swallowed it, and those bones remain, functioning. Cast-iron pipes, corroded to the color of dried blood, snake up through the trunks, carrying steam and condensed water to feed the ward's hydroponic vats. A few small gas engines—converted now to run on wood-gas generated from the ward's own deadfall—chug quietly in the hollow of a massive, hollowed-out banyan-style bole. Their sound is muffled to a deep, rhythmic thudding, felt in the root-felt beneath the feet rather than heard in the air. Copper wire, green with verdigris, is woven like vine-work through the branches, carrying low, steady currents that power the incandescent bulbs dangling like strange, glowing fruit in the perpetual dusk of the interior. The light is normal, adequate—a warm, artificial amber that pushes back the shadows just enough to see the dripping pipes and the slow pulse of the sap-presses.

It is a sparse place. The living architecture is massive, but there are only a few of these structures, separated by wide expanses of open, root-tangled floor. The emptiness is deliberate, necessary to allow the great structural roots to breathe and to capture the faint light that filters down. You can stand in the middle of the ward and see the perimeter wall on all sides, the space echoing with a profound, wet quiet.

The fauna is scarce to the point of absence—a few pale, eyeless woodlice clicking in the deepest crevices of the bark, a single species of moisture-draining moth that flutters near the hot pipes. Instead, the animalistic is subsumed into the botanical. The ecology is in constant, visible motion, fulfilling the role of a bustling menagerie. Vines contract like slow-tensing muscles, pulling dead leaves into the composting pits at the base of the trunks. Stomata breathe open and shut with audible, wet sighs. The fungal shelves that serve as balconies and walkways slowly extrude their ridges over the course of hours, shifting the geometry of a walkway by a centimeter a day. Sap-presses, driven by the natural pressure differentials of the towering thermo-flora, wheeze and click, squeezing nutrient-rich xylem into the iron gutters.

The weather here is a closed loop. Warmth rises from the iron pipes, meeting the cool air drifting in from the lowlands beyond the wooden wall. The condensation is constant. It mists, it drips, it mists again. The air smells of cut copper, diesel fumes, and the thick, sweet scent of rotting cellulose.

There is an unusual rule to this place, a quirk of its total integration. The city and the plant have not merely coexisted; they have synchronized. The electricity's vibration corresponds exactly to the frequency at which the local guard-vines grow, causing them to gently resonate, keeping their tendrils slack and open along the pathways. If the generators fail, the vines cease their vibration and contract—slowly, but with immense force—closing the pathways like a city locking its gates. The ward is a living machine, quiet and damp, ticking over in the cool dimness, every process—mechanical, electrical, and biological—locked in a delicate, slow-moving circuit of seep, steam, and green.
