A vast plain of constructed matter stretches to every horizon, low and hot beneath a dim, condensing sky. There is no natural substrate here—no underlying geology, no primordial stone. Everything is made. The ground beneath your feet, the air above, the moisture that perpetually beads and weeps from every surface: all of it is fabricated, assembled by hands wielding simple tools, or by processes that mimic crude, manual craft on an incomprehensible scale. It is a world that is entirely built, yet built without sophistication—hammered, stamped, riveted, and extruded on a planetary scale, using techniques no more advanced than a blacksmith’s forge or a potter’s wheel, scaled up to the size of continents.

The terrain is low, almost bereft of elevation, an endless flat expanse of heavy plates and interlocking slabs. The materials are dense, metallic-ceramic composites and opaque, vitreous substances that hold heat like closed fists. The temperature is oppressive, a deep, radiant heat that rises from the iron-dark ground and presses down from the atmosphere. There is no shade. The light is incredibly dim—a bruised, twilight luminescence that seems to emanate from the moist air itself rather than any sun. You can see for hundreds of miles only because the plain is so flat and vast, but the distance is swallowed in a permanent, dreary twilight, a saturation of shadow and heavy, humid air.

And the wetness. It is everywhere. Water does not flow here in rivers or pool in lakes; it materializes. The atmosphere is perpetually at the point of condensation. Heavy droplets form spontaneously out of the thick air, clinging to the rivet-heads and flanges of the constructed ground. The world weeps. Every vertical surface runs with condensation; every horizontal plate is slick with a thin, hot film of water. There are shallow, geometric channels stamped into the plates, meant to guide the weeping water away, but they overflow, merging into a shallow, steaming sheet that covers the world in a hot, ankle-deep flood. The water tastes of metal and fabrication, of oil and slag.

There is no life here—no vegetation, no animal, nothing that breathes or grows. The ecology is purely mineral and structural, yet the world is violently active. It is a place of embryonic formation and sudden, catastrophic collapse. The erosion parameter is nearly zero; this is not a landscape wearing away, but one violently coming into being, constantly under construction. But the construction is flawed, or the physics themselves are unstable. The dynamic is turbulent in the extreme. From the dim, sweating distance, you hear the constant, bass-heavy groaning of metal plates warping under thermal stress. The ground shudders. Great sections of the plain—square miles of riveted metal—buckle upwards with the grinding shriek of simple hinges pushed past their limits, rising into new, primitive ridges. Elsewhere, the ground drops away in sudden, square-cut sinkholes, revealing fresh, raw layers of construction beneath, still gleaming with the heat of their recent assembly.

It is an anthill of activity without any ants. The world is building and unbuilding itself at a ferocious pace, using only the crudest of mechanisms. You see levers the size of cathedrals pivoting slowly in the dim light, triggering cascades of interlocking stamps that punch new geometric shapes into the raw, steaming crust. The soundscape is sparse but terrifying: the deep, rhythmic thud of monstrous hammers, the hissing pop of hot rivets being driven into new seams by invisible forces, the low, rushing sound of hot water sluicing through newly forged channels. Mostly, though, it is quiet—a heavy, expectant silence between the paroxysms of creation, broken only by the steady *plink, plink, plink* of condensation falling from the undercarriage of the world.

And then, the weirdness sets in. The reality breakdown is not a localized glitch; it is the weather of this place. The turbulent creation and destruction outpaces logical physics. You walk across a plain of heavy iron plates, and up ahead, the ground is still soft—not mud, but soft metal, glowing a dull, wet crimson, behaving like clay beneath the colossal, invisible stamping presses that are just now retreating into the condensation. A ridge of newly extruded material rises into the dimness, but its geometry is wrong. The angles are irrational. A massive cubic strut, freshly erected, leans at an angle that should bring it crashing down, yet it hangs there, suspended, the rivets along its seam weeping streams of hot water that fall upwards into the dark sky, defying gravity, only to condense into new, heavy plates that slowly crystallize out of the humid air above.

The construction follows a primitive logic—hammered sheets, simple pins, brute-force extrusion—but it is applied to a space that cannot hold it. The landscape loops. You walk for days across the sparse, hot, weeping plates, only to find yourself stepping onto the same colossal hinge you passed a week ago, though now it is partially melted, or shifted three degrees into a color spectrum that shouldn't exist in a world of metal and water. The water itself is subject to the breakdown. You watch a bead of condensation form on the edge of a steel flange, swell, and drop—but as it falls, it slows, freezes in mid-air, and then shatters into a dozen tiny, geometrically perfect cubes of ice that hover at your eye level before sublimating into hot, metallic steam.

This is a world at the forge, a planetary-scale foundry of primitive manufacture, trapped in a recursive loop of emergence and collapse. It is a hell of simple tools and vast, empty spaces, where the only god is a blind, hammering mechanism, and the only element is the hot, weeping water that lubricates the endless, impossible assembly of a reality that refuses to hold together.
