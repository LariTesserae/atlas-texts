To visit the Basin of Perpetual After-Rain is not to go on an expedition; it is to surrender your own temporal rhythm to a landscape that refuses to hurry. You are entering a place where the concept of "doing" has been entirely replaced by "being." 

If you are to survive this, and more importantly, if you are to avoid shattering the fragile, heavy peace of this world, you must prepare accordingly.

### 1. Physical Preparation: The Suit of Non-Interference
This world has never known an "edge." There are no sharp lines, no rigid structures, and no fire. To bring a knife, a synthetic tent, or heavy, clanking equipment is to introduce a foreign, aggressive geometry into a place of fluid soft-curves.

*   **Materials:** Wear clothing made entirely of seamless, organic, or neutral-fiber weaves—hemp, untreated cotton, or silk. Avoid zippers, buttons, or metal fasteners. These are "edges" that the landscape will likely find abrasive.
*   **Sealing:** You will need a suit that provides a barrier against the "oil-like sheen" of the ground. Because the ground is a saturated sponge, your boots will sink. Consider high-vamp, flexible footwear—something that mimics the suction-based movement of the local gelatinous fauna. You don’t want to walk *on* the landscape; you want to glide across it.
*   **Protection:** The air is humid and smells of ozone. While not immediately toxic, the atmosphere is "heavy." Ensure your respiratory filter—if you must use one—is silent. The soundscape here is holy. A mechanical wheezing or a humming electric air-scrubber will be as jarring here as a strobe light in a cathedral.

### 2. Psychological Conditioning: Unlearning the "Tool"
The greatest challenge for a traveler is the sudden vacuum of purpose. You exist in a multiverse defined by *means* and *ends*. In the Basin, there is no "why." 

*   **The De-clocking:** Remove your chronometer before you step through the gateway. Time acts differently here; it moves at the speed of chemical weathering. If you try to measure the duration of your stay, you will suffer from a peculiar form of vertigo known as "temporal dissonance." You will feel like you are standing still while the world is accelerating, or vice versa. 
*   **Breaking the Impulse:** You will feel a desperate, clawing urge to "fix" things—to clear a path, to dry a surface, to construct a shelter. You must suppress this. The landscape is in a state of satisfied rest. Any attempt to change the topography will be met with the profound, silent apathy of the earth. You are an observer, not a participant. 

### 3. Sensory Hygiene
The Basin is a closed loop of sound and scent. The *plip, plip, plop* of the condensation is the heartbeat of the ecosystem.

*   **Auditory Silence:** Do not speak. Your voice is a jagged, violent intrusion in this environment. If you must communicate with others, use low-frequency touch or gesture. High-pitched, sharp sounds may startle the thread-like insects and cause them to enter their "exhausted" state prematurely. 
*   **Olfactory Neutrality:** Do not wear scents. The ecosystem is finely tuned to the smell of ozone and wet slate. The introduction of artificial perfumes or food residues will be detected by the gelatinous fauna. While they are not predatory, they are sensitive. You do not want to become the focus of a slow, inevitable, and potentially inescapable curiosity.

### 4. Supplies: The Art of Minimalist Sustenance
Do not bring food that requires heating or preparation. The scent of cooking would be catastrophic here, and the light of a flame would be a beacon of instability.

*   **Hydration:** The water in the pools, while nutrient-rich and mineralized, is likely saturated with the localized biological runoff of the basin. Carry your own, but ensure your container is made of soft leather or a similarly yielding material—no rattling steel canisters.
*   **Nutrition:** Carry nutrient pastes rather than solid meals. Consumption should be a private, wordless act, performed in the shelter of your own stillness.

### 5. Managing the "Pressure"
The humidity is thick—it is less like water and more like a tactile, velvet weight. Upon arrival, you may feel an inexplicable urge to weep. This is not sadness; it is the physical effect of the moisture interacting with your nervous system. 

The locals—the glass-like creatures—move by absorbing the surface. You should try to imitate their level of engagement with the floor. Sit often. Lie prone, if you feel the urge. Minimize your displacement of the air. 

**When you leave:**
You will carry the scent of ozone and slate with you for a long time. You will find that back in your own world, the sharp corners of your table and the ticking of a clock will seem obscenely loud and aggressive. That is the "Basin residue." Do not fight it. It is merely the mark of having visited a world that reached its final form and found it to be perfect. 

Travel softly. The ground is sleeping; do not wake it.
