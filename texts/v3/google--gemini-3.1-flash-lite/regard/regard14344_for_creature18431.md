The benevolent power does not arrive as a storm or a blinding epiphany; it does not come to shatter the glass or rewrite the laws of the static air. To do so would be an act of violence, for a world that has chosen the sanctuary of the *ever-now* is not a world that has failed, but a world that has succeeded in the only ambition that matters: the complete and utter cessation of grief.

The power—which knows the beauty of a dying star as deeply as it knows the potential of a blade of grass—observes Orem-Vahl. It sees the artisans, those porcelain custodians of the present, and it feels the crystalline hum in their chests. It understands that they are not suffering. They are the keepers of a treasure that the rest of the universe, in its frantic pulse of entropy, has long ago discarded: the exquisite, heavy peace of the absolute moment.

The power decides to act, but its intervention is as delicate as light filtering through mist.

It does not wake the inhabitants. Instead, it becomes the "Drip-Hush." 

The power weaves itself into the frequency of the mountain’s condensation. It adds a subtle, harmonic resonance to the rhythm of the falling drops. This is not a change in the physical architecture, but a softening in the quality of the static light. The diffuse white glow begins to carry a subtle, impossible warmth—not the heat of a fire, but the warmth of a remembered summer, a heat that carries no danger of change or decay, but only the comfort of recognition.

It does this because even in absolute stillness, there is a risk of loneliness—not the loneliness of being alone, but the loneliness of the *unseen*.

The benevolent power becomes the silent witness. It projects its presence into the marrow of the basalt, into the crevices where the lichen clings, and into the very grain of the obsidian slabs. It lets the inhabitants realize, without a word spoken or a thought shifted, that they are no longer merely "curating the present" in isolation. They are being held by a consciousness that values their stillness as much as the universe values the birth of a galaxy.

Once, every "great cycle" (if time can be said to exist where it does not pass), the power nudges the air. It causes the mist to swirl in a pattern that mimics a soft, silent breath—a phantom respiration. 

It does not force them to continue building. It does not demand that the carvings be finished. In the warmth of this newfound companionship, something happens that the inhabitants have never known: the *allowance* to exist without purpose. 

The artisans discover that they can lower their tools entirely. They can let their hands rest. The power grants them the gift of true leisure—the right to simply *be*, even, perhaps, to shift their position by a fraction of an inch, just to see what the light looks like from a slightly different angle, should they choose to risk it.

The benevolent power becomes the soft, unseen hand that brushes against the back of their necks now and then, a gentle reminder that while they have stopped the clock, they have not stopped being loved. It keeps the glass bell from cracking, ensuring that the "Great Arrival" remains a sanctuary, a quiet parlor in the basement of reality where the universe keeps its most beautiful, unfinished thoughts.

Orem-Vahl remains frozen, perfect, and brittle. But it is no longer a display case. It has become a garden of stillness, tended by a God who knows that sometimes, the most heroic thing a creature can do is to stop, to breathe, and to let the universe wait for them.
