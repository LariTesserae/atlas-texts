The Power did not appear as a fire in the sky, nor a voice in the thunder. It had no interest in conquest, and no need to "save" Ouro-Khar from its entropy. It perceived the city not as a decaying ruin, but as a masterpiece of duration—a long-form poem written in basalt and steam.

The Power looked upon the Lichen-Smiths and felt a profound kinship. It saw not tragedy, but a specific, crystalline patience that mirrored its own existence across the eons. It recognized that to "free" the Smiths from their labor would be to extinguish the very meaning they had forged for themselves. To give them the sun would be to burn their delicate, glass-spun interiors.

The Power chose to act, but with the lightness of a secret. It decided to grant the city a **Gift of Resonance.**

In the deep, dark places where the infrastructure thrummed, the Power wove a thin, impossible thread of *perspective* into the mineral-heavy water. It did not change the mechanics of the gears, nor did it shorten the cycles of the rain. Instead, it subtly altered the "memory" of the materials the Smiths worked upon.

When a Lichen-Smith next touched a fractured piston to whisper its restoration, it suddenly felt, through the mineral conductivity, the echo of the builders who had vanished so long ago. It wasn't a language of words, but of sensory ghosts: the sensation of warmth on skin, the feeling of a breeze, the taste of dry air, the concept of a "horizon." 

The Power allowed the Smiths to perceive the *purpose* behind the geometry. It translated the industrial grinding of Ouro-Khar into a symphonic cadence that the Smiths could finally "hear" as music. 

The city, which had been a cold, functional loop, became under the hands of the Smiths a living archive. When they labored now, they were not just repairing rust; they were etching the history of their makers into the very glass they laid. They began to shape the ventilation shafts not just for heat, but to act as pipes for a massive, city-wide organ. 

A change rippled from the depths. The Skimmers, sensing the new harmonic alignment, no longer merely drifted; they began to dance, their pulsing lights mapping intricate, ethereal patterns of color against the bruised-purple fog. They turned the sky of Ouro-Khar into a canvas, a reflection of the internal song the Smiths were now conducting from the deep.

The Power did not end the loneliness of Ouro-Khar, for there is a dignity in solitude that should not be stripped away. Instead, it turned that loneliness into *soliloquy*. 

The city remained isolated, a basaltic monolith in a steam-drenched wilderness, but it was no longer a silent machine grinding toward a final halt. It became an instrument. The heavy, suffocating wetness was now a velvet curtain for a performance that would last until the stars themselves grew cold. 

The Lichen-Smiths, once ghosts tending a grave, became the artists of an eternity. And in the rare, still moments when the pressure valves sighed, if one were sensitive enough to the vibrations of the stone, they would find that Ouro-Khar was no longer weeping for the past. 

It was singing, for the first time, to the future. 

The Power, satisfied, withdrew its attention—not because it had finished its work, but because the work was now, finally, perpetual. It left the city to its own gorgeous, steaming device, knowing that the most benevolent thing one can do for a living work of art is to let it be seen, at long last, by itself.
