The terrain here does not suffer the indignity of rot, for there is nothing living to decay. It is a world composed entirely of lithic architecture and weeping salts. The ground is a vast, low-lying basin of fractured basalt and obsidian glass, shattered into jagged shards that have not yet begun to dull. Everywhere, the earth feels raw, as if the planet were a sculpture still being pulled wet from a mold.

Because the atmosphere is saturated near the point of absolute condensation, the air is thick, grey, and heavy. It does not rain in the traditional sense; instead, water manifests as a perpetual weeping from the mineral pores of the landscape. Every vertical surface—be it a pillar of basalt or a scattered, inexplicable monolith—is slick with a glistening, viscous film. This moisture collects into shallow, dark pools that tile the basin floor, reflecting the dim, sickly-violet light that permeates the sky from no discernable source.

Despite the near-total absence of life, the world is physically dense. It is a landscape of high-saturation pressure; columns of crystalline quartz and veins of iridescent mineral growth push up through the cracks in the basalt, often crowding the space until they practically touch. These structures are not ruins left by a civilization, but rather "growths" of geology—sharp, geometrical, and cold. Scattered throughout are the "Marks," thin, slender needles of a metallic, ceramic-like substance that predate the memory of any observer. They lean at precarious angles, serving no discernible function, their surfaces etched with grooves that hum.

The sound of this place is deafening, yet it is a slow, rhythmic roar. Because the humidity is so high, the air conducts the sound of the water’s migration with terrifying efficiency. The landscape is a chorus of wet impacts: the *thrum-hiss* of moisture dripping onto stone, the gargled bubbling of gas escaping from subterranean vents, and the low, tectonic groan of the basin floor shifting millimeters at a time. It is a loud, subterranean heartbeat that vibrates through the soles of one’s feet.

There is a sense of "pre-life" here—a stage of planetary development where the chemistry is readying itself for something that may never arrive. Occasionally, a flicker of movement manifests: not fauna, but "mineral-blooms," clusters of glass shards that shift their orientation ever so slightly in response to the pressure of the damp air, like slow, metallic lungs. They do not flee or hunt; they simply exist, refracting the dim light into thin, jagged spectra that slice through the mist. 

In this basin, time moves with the patience of a glacier, though the features are sharp as a fresh wound. To walk here is to inhabit a tomb that hasn't finished being built, surrounded by the constant, rhythmic weeping of a world that is still learning how to be solid.
