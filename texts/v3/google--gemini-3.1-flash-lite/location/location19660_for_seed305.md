This is the Plateau of Latent Friction. It is a vast, high-altitude massif that sits far above the cloud layer, bathed in an eternal, punishingly bright light that turns the atmosphere into a prism.

The temperature is locked in a brittle, crystalline cold—the kind that makes metal groan and breath shatter—yet the landscape is densely, claustrophobically packed with life. It is not an organic fecundity, but an industrial one. Here, the "fauna" and "flora" are indistinguishable from mechanisms. Imagine a mountain range where the rock face is not granite or basalt, but a seamless, unoxidized alloy, cold to the touch and shivering with the hum of high-voltage transmission. 

The vegetation consists of sprawling, metallic kelp forests and rigid, gear-toothed ferns that protrude from the jagged, young fissures of the plateau. These "plants" do not photosynthesize; they capture the intense, ionizing light, converting it into raw potential energy that vibrates through the ground. The sound here is a constant, monolithic thrum—a low-frequency roar like a thousand hydro-electric dams operating at maximum capacity, interrupted only by the sharp, electric *snap* of static discharges jumping between monolithic pillars of stone-steel.

Because the erosion is in its infancy, the terrain is agonizingly sharp. There are no soft curves or rolling hills here. The landscape is composed of vertical, needle-like crags and deep, razor-thin chasms that cut miles into the crust. 

Water exists in a state of suspended condensation. It does not flow in rivers; it clings to the sharp edges of the terrain as super-cooled, gelatinous beads. These droplets act as lenses, magnifying the brilliant light into searing pinpoints that cauterize the surface of anything they touch. When the intermittent, heavy mist sweeps across the plateau—a rare, slow-moving weather system that spans the entire continent—these beads merge, sliding down the metallic slopes in slow, viscous sheets that hiss as they make contact with the electrified ground.

The density of this place is absolute. There is no empty space. Every square inch of the cliffside is occupied by interlocking, semi-sentient mechanical growths. Some resemble sprawling, brass-colored corals; others look like intricate, multi-limbed engines that expand by only a few millimeters per century. Despite this "slow" dynamic, there is a frantic, overwhelming sense of presence. Creatures—if they can be called that—scuttle through the cracks: faceted, clockwork organisms that hum with the same frequency as the mountains themselves, their movements so fluid and densely clustered that they create a visual shimmer, blurring the distinction between environment and inhabitant.

There is no sign of a builder. No ruins, no foundations, no footprints of intent. It is an industrial landscape without an industry—a perfected, autonomous factory of cold metal and blinding light that has been running for eons, slowly rearranging its own geometry, waiting for a prompt that may have been forgotten before the mountains were clawed into existence from the frozen tectonic plates below. Everything is electrified, everything is humming, everything is cold, and everything is waiting.
