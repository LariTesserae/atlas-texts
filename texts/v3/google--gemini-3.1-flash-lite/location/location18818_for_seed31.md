This is a land of suspended hydraulics, a skeletal city clinging to the jagged spine of a high-altitude massif. Here, the geography is impossibly young; the mountains have not yet been softened by the long grind of erosion. They thrust upward in blades of shale and basalt, their edges sharp enough to snag the low-hanging, saturated clouds that never quite break into rain, but instead drape the world in a perpetual, humid condensation.

The structures that occupy this space are not so much built as they are coaxed from the native stone. Everything here is constructed of polished obsidian and mortar made from crushed volcanic ash. The architecture consists of terraced, tiered platforms that step down the mountainsides like a staircase for giants, interconnected by flumes and overflowing cisterns. It is a city of hydro-logic. Rainwater is channeled via gravity-fed stone arteries, constantly weeping from the edges of carved gargoyles and spilling into vast, still basins. 

The atmosphere is thick and warm, heavy with the scent of wet stone and clean, odorless mineral salts. Because the world exists in a state of absolute, frozen equilibrium, the water does not flow in a rush; it hangs in the air as a fine, persistent mist, and the water level in the stone basins remains identical from one century to the next—never overflowing, never evaporating. 

There is a profound, meditative vacancy to the place. The vegetation is lush, consisting of broad-leafed, translucent succulents that cling to the shady sides of the obsidian terraces, their roots drinking directly from the damp, porous rock. These plants do not grow, nor do they wither. They are permanent, vibrant sculptures of jade and lime, their leaves beaded with perfectly stationary droplets of moisture.

The most striking feature of the city is its silence. There are no birds to call from the ridges, no insects to drone in the heat, no wind to rattle the wide leaves. Even the water, which moves constantly through the intricate stone plumbing, makes no sound. It slides over the basalt surfaces with the quiet, frictionless quality of a dream. 

Life is absent here—not extinct, but simply never manifest. The city appears as if the builders have just stepped away to rest, leaving their clay bowls and woven fiber mats beside the cisterns, though there is no sign of decay or abandonment. The construction is primitive but elegant; there is no metal, only the masterful use of leverage, stone-shaping, and gravity. 

In this elevated realm, the light is clear and unwavering. It casts long, hard-edged shadows that never shift, pinning the city to the peaks. It is a place of static perfection, a landscape held in the amber of a single, warm, humid moment, where the architecture of the earth and the architecture of the hand have reached an eternal, quiet agreement.
