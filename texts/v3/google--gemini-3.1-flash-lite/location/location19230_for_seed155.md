At these high, thin altitudes, the world does not end in dirt or stone. It ends in cellulose. 

You stand upon a sprawling, tiered architectural continent composed entirely of a singular, hyper-evolved organism—a petrified-yet-living lattice of gray-green, bone-like xylem branches reaching kilometers into the sky. This is the **Vascular Sprawl**. It has been growing for eons, long enough that the organic structures have slumped into a state of "ancient erosion," where the weight of the city creates valleys out of the plant's own calcified limbs.

**The Architecture of Living Matter**
The city is built—or grown—into the skeleton of this mega-flora. Huge, translucent bladders the size of town squares hang from gargantuan boughs, filled with a viscous, nutrient-rich fluid that slowly pulses under external mechanical pressure. This is the industrial heart of the place. Steam-driven bronze pistons, reinforced with woven vines instead of steel, chug rhythmically within the cavities of the plant, pumping sap through iron-clad conduits. The air is never silent; it is a cacophony of metallic grinding, the rhythmic *thrum-hiss* of steam release systems, and the wet, biological *thump* of the plant’s own circulation.

**Dampness and Light**
Water is scarce here, held only in the condensation that beads against the undersides of leaves as large as city blocks. Every hour, the high-altitude air cools, and the leaves weep. This creates "weeping districts"—narrow alleyways between vertical trunks where the humidity is constant and a fine, iridescent mist fills the air. The light is normal, but it is filtered through miles of canopy, casting a pale, soft-green pallor over the rusty, industrial scaffolding that climbs the trunks.

**The Reality Breakdown**
There is a profound wrongness here. Because the architecture *is* the biology, the distinction between machine and cell has vanished. You might see a factory chimney that suddenly sprouts delicate, frantic humming-flowers, or a set of rusted cog-wheels that, when they turn, leak blue ichor instead of oil. At the edges of your vision, the geometry of the branches seems to fold back into itself in defiance of Euclidean rules—a spiral that leads into a deeper past than existence should allow.

**Sparse Life**
The ecosystem is hauntingly empty. Large, multi-limbed creatures with shells like polished amber drift through the voids between branches, feeding on the airborne spores and the discarded steam exhaust, but they are rare. There are no birds. There are only the rhythmic clatter of the automated sap-casks sliding along vine-rails and the distant, grinding tectonic shift of the plant itself, adjusting its posture to chase the sun.

**The Experience of the Sprawl**
You walk along a bridge made of compressed, bleached bark. Below you, thousands of meters of "root-foundation" stretch into the gloom. The ground is never solid; it has a slight, sickening give, a heartbeat that rises from the depths of the structure. It is a place that feels finished—an evolutionary or industrial cul-de-sac where nothing new is expected to arrive. 

You hear the machinery screaming up ahead—another pumping station straining under the weight of the growth. You are in a city that is simultaneously a cathedral, a logging operation, and a dying god. It is cold, it is damp, and it is grinding itself into a peaceful, pulverized compost, one factory-thump at a time.
