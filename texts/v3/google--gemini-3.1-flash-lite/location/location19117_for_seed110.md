Tucked within a singular, jagged hollow of a cavern wall, high upon a nameless mountain peak, exists a pocket of perpetual, humid stasis. It is a chamber no larger than a cathedral's nave, sealed away from the frost of the heights by nothing more than a thick, velvet curtain of woven moss-fiber.

The air here is heavy, vibrating with a warmth that feels like breath against the skin. It does not circulate; it hangs, saturated to the point of liquid. The walls are not stone in the traditional sense, but a raw, embryonic geological sludge—a cooling, shifting mineral paste that has yet to harden into rock. It is a landscape under construction, a place where the earth is still deciding how to be firm. Because the geology is so unfinished, the walls weep constantly, slow translucent beads of mineral-rich water sliding down the soft, grey-violet clay in paths that have never known the cut of wind or the grinding of glaciers.

There is no light here. The darkness is absolute, a thick, tactile medium one must push through to move. Yet, in this gloom, density thrives. The space is crowded with the texture of things that haven't quite become living creatures, nor quite remaining inanimate. Tiny, crystalline structures—the "flora" of this dark—cluster in dense, finger-like colonies along the weeping seams of the walls. They are sparse in variety but abundant in presence, huddled together in glowing, pulsating colonies of fungal slime that emit no light visible to the human eye, but exert a rhythmic, low-frequency hum that can be felt in the marrow of one's teeth.

A human presence has been here, though they left no record of their arrival. A single structure sits in the center: a low, dome-shaped hearth built from sun-baked, unrefined clay and bound with tough, fibrous vines. Inside the hearth, there is a small pile of obsidian tools—crude, chipped flakes that have never known fire or blood. They sit on a bed of damp, rotting palm-fiber that does not decompose, held in a state of suspended decay by the world’s internal equilibrium.

There is no wind, no gradient of temperature, no shifting of the tides. Time does not pass here in the conventional sense; the water drips at a fixed, unchanging interval, a metronome for a world that has refused the cruelty of change. The occasional fauna—a pale, blind organism resembling a segmented ribbon of silk—drifts through the humid air not by swimming, but by pulsing its body in harmony with the cavern’s subtle hum. It avoids the tools. It avoids the moss-curtain at the entrance. It exists only to maintain the density of the room.

To enter this pocket is to step into the womb of the planet, a space where the earth is still soft enough to be reshaped, and where the silence is so heavy it acts as a physical weight, pinning the observer against the damp, weeping walls. Nothing evolves, nothing dies, and the raw rock of the world remains forever in its state of embryonic, warm potentiality.
