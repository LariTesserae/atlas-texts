The horizon does not exist here as a line; it exists as a gradient of suffocating charcoal haze. This is a continental plateau, suspended at an altitude where the air should be thin and freezing, yet it remains stubbornly, humidly warm. It is a place of long-settled erosion, where the bedrock—a porous, calcified shale—has been carved over aeons into sweeping, rounded basins and gentle, rolling ridges that never quite sharpen into peaks.

There is no natural sky. Above, the atmosphere is a thick, cyclonic soup of heavy gases and soot. Down here, the light is near-extinct, a persistent 0.2-scale gloom that renders everything in shades of oil-slicked obsidian and rusted iron. 

Dominating this vastness are the Engines. They are not buildings, for they have no walls and no intentional geometry; they are immense, autonomous industrial processes that have anchored themselves to the geology. Great, rhythmic piston-columns, miles long, pulse silently into the earth, drawing up heat from the mantle and venting it as a warm, ionizing mist. These machines are indistinguishable from the landscape’s own erosion—they are oxidized, fused to the stone, and covered in the condensation that beads everywhere. 

Water here is a ghost. It does not flow in rivers, but clings to the surfaces of the great machines in shivering, localized patches. It pools in the depressed "valleys" of the shale, not as water, but as oily, hyper-mineralized slicks that vibrate in sympathy with the deep-earth drilling. The vegetation is an afterthought, a sparse, parasitic struggle of bioluminescent mosses and pale, brittle filaments that sprout only where the static electricity from the machinery arcs against the damp rock. These plants pulse with a dim, rhythmic blue, mirroring the slow, mechanical heartbeat of the continent.

Despite the emptiness, the density of life is staggering. But it is not a life of flight or song. It is a life of soft, pressurized movement. Great, chitinous hexapods—the size of derelict ships—drift through the fog on spindly, multi-jointed legs, their carapaces etched with the same ancient erosion patterns as the rocks. They feed on the electrical residue and the condensed vapors, moving with an eerie, calculated silence. There are no vocalizations here; the soundscape is dominated by the low-frequency thrum of the subterranean pistons and the occasional, sharp *hiss* of pressure equalizing in a pocket of trapped air.

This world operates on a physics of exhaustion and inertia. Nothing is being built; everything is being processed. The ecosystem is in a permanent state of circular consumption—the machines refine the minerals, the flora feeds on the waste, the megafauna grazes on the flora, and in their passing, the animals deposit biological sludge that the heavy, damp weather beats back into the shale. 

It is a place of immense, heavy solitude. To walk here is to feel the weight of deep time pressing down through the gloom. There is no urgency, only the rhythmic, warm respiration of a planet that has long ago forgotten the sun, replaced by the tireless, dark industry of its own core. You are small, damp, and muffled, moving through a space that is as vast as an ocean and as quiet as a grave, watched over by machines that have become mountains and beasts that have become gods of the rust.
