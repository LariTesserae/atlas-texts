The city is a gargantuan, vertical lung, breathing in the mist and exhaling the scent of wet iron. It occupies a high-altitude crater—an ancient, jagged caldera that has been completely paved over, leveled by centuries of frantic, layered architecture. There is no earth here; there is only "structural substrate," a dense, fossilized network of heavy plates, conduits, and riveted girders that stack so tightly they resemble the grain of petrified wood.

The atmosphere is perpetually weeping. Cool, dense clouds cling to the upper tiers, condensing against the freezing metal surfaces to create a continuous, rhythmic drip. Every square millimeter of the city is occupied; there are no parks, no plazas, no empty streets. Instead, the space is a three-dimensional tapestry of rusted catwalks, steam-venting shafts, and crowded, low-ceilinged hab-units. It is a place of absolute density—a hive-mind of brass and soot where even the stairwells have been converted into secondary production lines.

Nature has not been invited, yet it persists through the cracks. Mosses—thick, velvet-green, and bioluminescent—choke the rusted intake valves, and pale, translucent ferns sprout from the condensation-slicked seams where two massive steel plates meet. This is the "lushness" of the biome: not forests, but a creeping, aggressive encroachment of lichen and fungi that feeds on the humidity and the ambient heat of the machinery. 

The environment is in a state of violent, productive flux. Massive, unseen engines deep within the foundation churn with a low-frequency vibration that shakes the marrow of one’s bones. This is a factory-world—a colossal, automated organism. Pistons the size of houses rise and fall in the distance, rhythmically ejecting clouds of tepid white water vapor into the dim, bruised-purple sky. Huge cogs turn with agonizing slowness, grinding against one another, their edges worn smooth by eons of friction. 

The light is dim, filtered through high-altitude smog and the constant spray of mist. It is a world of charcoal greys, oxidation-oranges, and the sickly, neon-chartreuse glow of the moss clinging to the venting pipes. 

For all the mechanical scale, it is surprisingly quiet. The machinery is so established, so perfectly calibrated, that it has passed the point of harsh clanging and entered into a state of deep, mechanical humming. There are almost no living creatures to be found; human presence is implied only by the flickering of distant yellow lights in the hab-blocks and the faint, recurring scent of ozone and synthetic oil.

Everything is moving. The city is a masterpiece of turbulent maintenance. Panels slide aside to reveal new, churning gear-teeth, while other sections are seemingly dismantled by robotic appendages the moment their work is finished, only to be reconfigured into slightly different geometries. It is an anthill of gargantuan proportions, a high-altitude crucible of perpetual refinement where the environment itself is the product. Every inch is built, every inch is wet, and every inch is working toward a purpose that vanished from historical record long ago, leaving behind a self-sustaining, rusting, weeping monument to industry.
