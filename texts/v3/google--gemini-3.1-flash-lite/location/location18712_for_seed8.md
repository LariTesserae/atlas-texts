The land here has long since finished its arguments with gravity. It is a vast, low-slung basin, worn down by eons of soft weather until the terrain exists merely as a series of gentle, undulating swells—a landscape that has slumped into a permanent, satisfied rest.

It is a world of permanent "after-rain." The air is heavy, thick with a lingering, humid warmth that clings to the skin like a damp shroud. There is no standing water in the sense of a lake or a river; rather, the earth itself seems to be saturated through, a sponge of dark, fine-grained silt that shimmers with a glossy, oil-like sheen. Every slight depression in the ground holds a shallow, translucent pool, and from these, a continuous, low-pitched symphony of liquid movement emanates: the *plip, plip, plop* of condensation falling from the slick faces of moss-covered, rounded stones.

The sound is pervasive, bordering on deafening in its rhythmic monotony. It is the sound of a world that is leaking.

Vegetation here is a suggestion rather than a presence. There are no forests, only thin, spindly stalks of translucent flora—long, pale reeds that bend perpetually in the heavy air, their surfaces weeping a thick, clear sap that smells faintly of ozone and crushed wet slate. They are spaced far apart, standing like sparse, lonely sentinels in the mist, allowing the eye to trace the gentle, flattened curves of the horizon for miles. There is no sense of urgency here; the growth is patient, glacial, moving at the speed of chemical weathering.

Despite the sparsity of the flora, the basin breathes with life. The fauna here is not hidden, but scattered in broad, slow configurations. Large, gelatinous creatures—flat and translucent as clouded glass—slide across the wet ground with a wet, suctioning sound. They are slow, methodical, moving only to press their bodies into the silt to absorb the nutrient-rich minerals brought to the surface by the endless cycle of condensation. Their movements are so sluggish they are almost imperceptible, part of the landscape’s slow-motion rhythm. Occasionally, a cluster of thin, thread-like insects will lift from a pool of water, humming with a frantic, high-pitched energy that contrasts sharply with the deep, bass-heavy stillness of the earth, before collapsing back into the marshy film as if physically exhausted by the moisture of the air.

There is no sign of stone-carving, no notched wood, no spark of fire. The landscape has never been touched by an edge. It is a quiet, primordial basin where the concept of a "tool" has no purchase, as everything required for survival—the water, the slow nutrients, the warmth—is already pressed into the skin of the surface.

To stand here is to feel the weight of deep time. The light is soft and diffused, muffled by the constant evaporation that creates a localized, hazy fog. It is a world that has reached its final form, a place that is no longer becoming but simply *is*: a wet, warm, breathing expanse, whispering to itself in the steady, rhythmic dripping of a thousand thousand stones.
