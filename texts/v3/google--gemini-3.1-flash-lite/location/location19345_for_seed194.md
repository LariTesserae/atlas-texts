The world is a singular, calcified chamber, a hollowed-out sphere no larger than a human bedroom, encased within the heart of an ancient, petrified seed the size of a mountain. 

There is no water here. The "air" is a viscous, chilled sap—a fragrant, amber-colored resin that moves with the sluggish resistance of honey. It does not nourish; it preserves.

The ecosystem is a closed loop of absolute botanical dominance. The floor, walls, and ceiling are not composed of stone or earth, but of a singular, monolithic organism: a gargantuan, ancient vascular system. This is an urban construct of xylem and phloem, where the infrastructure is living tissue. Streets are deep, smooth grooves worn into the wood by centuries of slow, patient expansion; buildings are nodules and bulbous excrescences that have hardened into dark, polished mahogany-like shells. 

Because life here is entirely vegetable and fixed, there is no respiration, no heartbeat, no skittering. There is only the constant, aggressive *noise* of the plant—the structural groan of cellular division. It is loud, a rhythmic, deep-frequency clicking and snapping that reverberates through the resinous air, like a giant house settling in the frost. It is the sound of an ecosystem undergoing a process so ancient and slow that it has reached a state of almost perfect equilibrium. It is the sound of a city grown rather than built, its architecture finalized eons ago.

Technology here is tactile and primitive. The inhabitants—if they can be called that—are the "Needles," long, thin filaments of calcified cellulose that move with agonizing slowness, scraping the walls with hand-carved obsidian shards to harvest the dried resin that flakes away. Their movements are so subtle, so governed by the slow metabolism of the chamber, that to a human observer, they would appear as still as the walls themselves. They perform their maintenance through touch alone, guided by the vibrations in the wood.

Light does not exist. The chamber is shrouded in a profound, velvety darkness. The Needles navigate by the intensity of the wood’s rhythmic humming; they perceive the city through the topography of its erosion. Because the erosion is so deep—because the tissue has been polished by the passage of these filaments for geological ages—the surfaces are worn down to a beautiful, soft, satin-like finish. Everything is smooth, rounded, and reclaimed by the very thing that grew it.

There is no flux here. The temperature remains a brittle, biting chill, kept constant by the thermal insulation of the seed-husk. It is a lonely, hollowed-out urbanity, a city of one continuous, infinite plant, standing in the dark, waiting for a cycle that might never complete. It is the end-state of a garden that forgot the sun, existing only to sustain its own architectural memory.
