The air here is not merely cold; it is a heavy, pressurized dampness that clings to the skin like a wet wool shroud. You are in a "drip-hollow"—a vast, subterranean or low-lying basin carved into ancient, light-absorbent basalt. The scale is deceptively claustrophobic. Though the basin is technically of moderate size, the density is so near-zero that it feels as if you are the only occupant of a cathedral built for ghosts.

The darkness is absolute, a physical weight that pushes against your eyes. You cannot see your hand in front of your face, yet the environment is not silent. It is, in fact, deafening.

The sound is a rhythmic, percussive assault. High above, the ceiling of the basin leaks. Water—cold, mineral-rich, and sluggish—forms heavy beads on jagged, dark-green moss-mats. When they release, they fall twenty feet, striking the polished, obsidian-slick floor with the force of a hammer on an anvil. *THUM. THUM. CLACK-THUM.* The acoustic properties of the basin amplify each drop into a booming, resonant chime that vibrates in your chest cavity. It is a slow, patient, and maddeningly rhythmic percussion that has persisted for eons, eroding the singular floor into a series of perfectly round, basin-like indentations that hold stagnant, obsidian-black pools.

Vegetation here does not grow toward light; it grows toward the humidity. Thick, leathery ferns with bioluminescent veins—dim, sickly, and infrequent—cling to the vertical walls, their roots deep in the weathered cracks of the rock. They are slow-growing, ancient things that seem to pulse in time with the water-drops.  

Scattered throughout this hollow are the "Marks." They are not ruins in the traditional sense, but remnants of a primitive, manual stewardship. Here and there, bolted into the basalt, are small iron rings—rusted to the point of turning into a porous, orange paste—and occasional low platforms fashioned from river stone and sea-smoothed wood. They are the artifacts of a forgotten culture that once used this hollow as a sanctuary or a laboratory, though the *why* has been surrendered to the erosion of time. There are no people left. 

The ecology is sparse, almost meditative. If you stand incredibly still, you might hear the wet, rhythmic *slap* of something moving in the dark—a soft-bodied cephalopod-like creature, perhaps, that pulls itself between the pools, navigating the echo-chamber by reading the vibrations of the falling water. It is a rare, elusive presence that refuses to reveal itself to the listener.

There is no wind. There is no warmth. There is only the long, grinding patience of water against stone, the rhythmic booming that fills the void, and the cold, damp dark that swallows every movement. It is a place that has finished its evolution; it is precisely what it was always meant to be, a hollowed-out room of rock and water, waiting for a clock that no longer keeps time.
