Deep beneath the crust, in a hollowed-out mantle-sphere that never saw the sun, lies the City of Cellulose. There is no water here; the chemical exchanges that sustain life are driven by a viscous, amber-colored sap that cycles through the architecture like hydraulic fluid in a machine. 

The air is cool, kept at a constant, brisk temperature by the rhythmic respiration of the walls. It smells of crushed mint, ozone, and dry, curing resin. The landscape is not built upon rock, but woven from the calcified skeletons of titanic, subterranean mosses and fungal-steel. The "buildings" are not constructed; they are grown, then braced with forged-iron scaffolding and pressurized steam pipes.

Because the environment is almost entirely plant-based, the urban geometry is fluid and organic. Towering, bulbous spires made of interlocking woody nodes reach toward the ceiling of the cavern. These spires are interconnected by translucent, fibrous bridges that pulse with a soft, bioluminescent light. The light is not generated by electricity in the traditional sense, but by the friction of ionized gases forced through the porous veins of the plant-substrate.

The city is sparse. Great, echoing gaps of empty, breathable space separate the districts, allowing the cool air to circulate in slow, majestic currents. You can walk for miles across a swaying terrace of leathery, interconnected leaves, seeing nothing but the distant, flickering amber glow of a factory-bloom miles away.

The industry here is terrifyingly quiet. Great mechanized organs, built from polished hardwood and tempered brass, sit in the centers of the communal nodes. They rhythmically harvest the excess resin, boiling it in glass alembics to produce the fuels that power the city’s life-support. Pistons move with the muffled thud of a heartbeat, driving the circulation of nutrients through the massive, root-like cables that crisscross the floor.

Fauna exists here, but they are specialized and strange. There are no winged creatures, as the air currents are too slow, but there are six-legged, armored grazers—part-crustacean, part-tuber—that wander the scaffolding, rasping at the bark-walls to feed on mineral deposits. They move with a deliberate, clockwork lethargy, their carapaces clicking softly against the wood. Occasionally, one finds a "weaver-drone," a delicate, spindly creature that maintains the tension of the fibrous bridges, its long, needle-like appendages clicking and tapping in a silent, hypnotic rhythm.

Everything is in a state of embryonic expansion. New walls are perpetually budding from the old, pushing against the brass-and-iron braces that contain them. It is a world under construction, a living, breathing engine that is slowly turning its own cavern into a singular, gargantuan organism. 

There is no erosion because there is no wind, no rain, and no weathering—only the slow, relentless pressure of growth and the persistent, ticking activity of the industrial heart. It is a city that feels as if it were dreamt into existence by a sleeping forest, currently in the process of deciding what it wants to become.
