The world is a singular, glowing sphere of brass and polished silicate, no wider than a grand cathedral, embedded deep within a pressurized mantle of warm, saline fluid. You are inside the "Pump-Heart," a sub-aquatic clockwork chamber that exists entirely to churn a relentless, rhythmic deluge.

Because the environment is fully built, there is no bedrock or soil to anchor the landscape. Instead, the floor is an intricate lattice of interlocking gears and copper struts, perpetually slick and vibrating. The temperature is balmy, held at a constant, feverish warmth by the friction of the machine and the thermal output of the pressurized liquid pressing against the transparent hull.

This is a place of loud, cyclical violence. Every few seconds, the primary piston—a massive, hexagonal pillar of dull iron—retracts with a screech, and a high-pressure jet of filtered saline sprays across the interior volume. It hits the "leafs," thin, radial vanes of copper and translucent resin that spin and shudder under the impact. These are the only vegetation here: mechanical ferns that do not photosynthesize but instead convert the kinetic energy of the spray into a faint, amber bioluminescence. There are no animals; the "ecology" is purely hydraulic.

The atmosphere is thick, not with air, but with a fine, aerosol mist that hangs suspended in the bright, clinical light of the overhead conduits. The light is aggressive, a persistent, unblinking white that turns every droplet into a frantic prism. Because the erosion is young, the edges of the machinery are sharp—knife-edge bevels, needle-pointed decorative struts, and freshly milled contact patches that have yet to be smoothed by the grit of the water.

Everything here is tactile. You can feel the vibration in your marrow: a *thrum-thrum-thrum* that dictates the pulse of the room. It is a lonely, intimate space. There is no natural substrate, no chaos of roots or stones, just the cold, rational logic of the construct. The sound is the landscape’s dominant feature: a deafening roar of cascading liquid, the metallic *clack* of falling valves, and a constant, low-frequency hum of pressurized steam traveling through copper veins.

It is a world of total order locked in a state of perpetual, drowning activity. The water cycles, the brass-work rotates, and the light washes it all in a sterile, golden hue. There is no decay here, only the relentless, recurring motion of a machine that forgot its purpose and decided to become its own tide.
