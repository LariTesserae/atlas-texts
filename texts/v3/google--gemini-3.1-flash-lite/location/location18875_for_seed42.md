The city of Oulo is a vertical archipelago suspended within a permanent, slow-moving cyclone. It is not an ocean of salt water, but a dense, atmospheric slurry—a high-altitude supersaturation of heavy mist and bioluminescent condensation that exerts a gentle, buoyancy-defying pressure. You do not walk in Oulo; you drift, breaststroking through the cool, thickened air, navigating the interstices of a gargantuan, ancient flora.

The terrain is entirely biological. The city does not sit *on* the landscape; the landscape is the architecture. Titanic, rubbery stalks—the *Lumen-Oaks*—pierce the sky like moss-covered pillars, their girth spanning the width of a modest village street. The inhabitants have not cut into these stalks; they have coaxed them. Over untold centuries, the growth has been guided by cord-tension and gentle pruning into hollowed-out chambers, spiraling staircases of tough, waxy leaf-growth, and rounded dwellings that bulge from the trunk like giant, hardened blisters.

Everything is damp. The walls sweat a nutrient-rich dew that sustains the moss-carpets coating every surface, turning the city into a soft, textured quilt of emerald, teal, and bruised gold. 

Because the density is sparse, the city feels like a collection of hanging grottoes connected by long, graceful suspension bridges made of woven vine-silk. One dwells in a "room" of calcified bark and translucent leaf-veining, looking out across a chasm of swirling, pearlescent mist to the next neighborhood, fifty yards away. The silence is impossible here; the sound is constant and overwhelming. It is the rhythmic, booming thrum of the atmosphere moving through the massive hollows of the trees—a low-frequency vibration that you feel in your marrow, punctuated by the shrill, melodic whistles of the *Cloud-Skimmers*, creatures that resemble flat, kite-like jellyfish, undulating through the mist to harvest water-borne particulates.

There is no metal in Oulo. The built infrastructure is entirely hand-crafted: chiseled stone weights for ballast, knotted fiber-cables, and polished resins harvested from the tree-secretion. The inhabitants are lean, pale-skinned beings with long, dexterous fingers designed for gripping slick stalks and weaving silk-cables. They move with the patient, deliberate cadence of the environment. In a world of slow dynamics, haste is considered a physical deformity.

Light enters as a diffused, greenish glow. It does not originate from a sun, which remains hidden behind the infinite density of the water-wall, but from the trees themselves. At every joint where one branch meets another, the moss glows with a soft, flickering pulse, syncing with the vibration of the wind. 

Rules of the world are simple: keep your seals tight, ensure your tether-knot is sound, and always orient your movement with the slow, clockwise drift of the mist. If you lose your grip, you do not fall; you simply wander, drifting upward into the higher, thinner strata of the canopy, becoming one of the many drifting shadows that the locals call the *Lost Guests*. 

It is a place of settled peace. The erosion of time is visible only in the smooth, rounded edges of the dwellings—the way the bark has contoured itself to the stone thresholds over millennia. It is a cathedral of living wood, a slow, damp lung, breathing in the humidity and exhaling a quiet, rhythmic life.
