High in the altitudes of a nameless range, the air is thin, biting, and tastes of ozone and crushed chlorophyll. Here, the temperature hovers perpetually near freezing, pinning the landscape in a state of suspended rigor.

There is no soil. There is no stone. The world is a colossal, vertiginous architecture of biological mass—a vast, organic sprawl that has grown for eons, layering itself into a self-sustaining mountain of living matter. This is a forest that has forgotten the ground exists, for the forest *is* the ground.

The "terrain" is a chaotic tapestry of moss-fused cartilage, woody rib-cages, and vast, weeping fungal plates that serve as the tectonic plates of this region. It is a dense, high-saturation world; every cubic inch is occupied by the twitching, hyper-active nerves of the vegetation. The plant life here does not grow so much as it churns, a turbulent, Solaris-like state of constant, frenetic reconfiguration. Great, fibrous stalks—glistening with a cold, sticky dew—shift their orientation within minutes, twisting and uncoiling to reach for the pale mountain light, their movements creating the soft, wet sound of a slow-motion crush.

Disturbingly, this ecosystem is not merely botanical. It is host to a cold, rusted haunting. Embedded deep within the pulsing, green substrate are the relics of a long-dead industrial epoch. Ribbons of blackened copper piping snake through the fleshy stems like veins; serrated gears, fused and immovable, are half-swallowed by the encroaching, velvet-soft lichen. High-pressure steam intermittently vents from hidden fissures in the vegetation—not a product of the plants, but a residual, automated belch from gargantuan, buried machinery that refuses to power down. These bursts of moisture—scalding steam turning to instant rime-ice in the thin, frigid air—provide the only water source, creating rhythmic cycles of condensation that coat the living architecture in a silvery, frosted slick.

The atmosphere is one of profound, heavy silence, broken only by the clockwork "thrum" of internal hydraulics buried miles beneath the surface and the sudden, violent wet *snap* of a plant structure rearranging itself. 

There is no fauna save for the occasional, translucent mite that navigates the forest-skin, searching for the warmth of the leaking pipes. Everything is in a state of rapid, turbulent flux; a bridge of braided vines might be shredded by a localized botanical surge, only to be woven anew by the following tide of growth. 

It is a place where the distinction between machine and cell has been eroded by time. The industry has become the skeleton, and the plant-mass has become the flesh—a cold, indifferent, and perpetually shifting mountain of life, clinging to the heights of a world that no longer remembers why it was built, yet refuses to stop operating.
