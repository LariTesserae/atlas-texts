The world exists in a state of perpetual, stuttering genesis. It is a vertical needle of a planet, a spire of rock piercing a thick, hypoxic atmosphere that holds the heat of a buried furnace. Gravity here is a localized rumor, inconsistent and shifting, allowing for impossible geometries of basalt and obsidian to jut thousands of miles into the void.

There is no water. Moisture is a chemical impossibility in this chemistry; instead, the ground is a slick, crystalline crust of dehydrated sulfides and magnesium salts. It crunches like crushed glass under the weight of nothing, for nothing lives here to walk upon it.

Everything is under construction. The landscape is not ancient; it is raw, tectonic, and hyperactive. Every few minutes, a subterranean thrum builds—a low-frequency vibration that rattles the marrow of the stone—and a new shard of mountain erupts from the dust. These aren't volcanic flows of lava, but rapid accretions of mineral geometry, jagged pillars of obsidian and pyrite clawing their way toward the upper reaches of the atmosphere. They rise with the violence of a breaking wave, then stabilize, frozen in their chaotic, needle-like poses.

The light is suffocating. The sky is a thick, impenetrable charcoal, lit only by the bioluminescent pulsing of deep-seated mineral reactions—slow-burning gas flares trapped within the rock that throw long, rhythmic shadows across the salt-fields. Because there is so little light, the scale of the world is deceptive. You might see a pillar sixty miles away and perceive it as a pebble, only to realize, as the luminescence shifts, that it is a cathedral-sized spike of razor-sharp quartz.

The atmosphere is heavy, smelling of ozone and singed flint. There is a "built" aspect to this place, though it is not the result of hands. Scattered across the highest peaks are "marks"—perfectly symmetrical circular depressions and geometric grooves etched into the rock by nothing more than the persistent, electric scouring of the air currents. They look like ruins, like the footings of some impossible tower, but they are merely the result of the world’s turbulent, static-heavy weather carving patterns into the embryonic crust.

Silence is the baseline, but it is a restless silence. It is frequently shattered by the "clatter"—the sudden, high-pitched ringing sound of a new mineral pillar snapping into existence or a fracture racing along a salt-plain. 

Nothing breathes. Nothing moves of its own accord. Yet, the world is violently alive, shifting in a frantic cycle of growth and reconfiguration. It is a crucible of raw, unshaped matter, building itself into a form that will never quite be finished, perpetually discarding the last shape to make room for the next, turning under the heat of its own tectonic friction, waiting for a biology that cannot survive in its dry, jagged, lightless embrace.
