The sky here is a bruised, eternal velvet, deep and unlit, yet the world is far from dark. It is illuminated by the rhythmic, sickly pulse of bioluminescent silt and the incessant, high-intensity flickering of industrial infrastructure that has fused with the biology of the highland marsh.

This is the **High-Sump Plateau**, a continental expanse of saturated, ancient peat and weeping basalt that stretches toward a horizon you can never actually see. Gravity feels heavy, anchored by the sheer density of a place that has been eroding for aeons; the mountains are not jagged, but slumped into rounded, gargantuan pillows of moss-covered ruin. They are "settled" forms, ancient ridges worn smooth by a cycle of rainfall that never truly ceases.

The atmosphere is a warm, thick soup—a condensate of humidity, machine oil, and biological spores. Because the plateau is so vast, it generates its own localized weather, a churning convection of steam released by the underground geothermal engines. These engines, colossal and rusted, are buried miles deep, their rhythmic thrum vibrating through the soles of your feet. They are the heartbeat of the land, pumping warm coolant into the swampy loam, keeping the vegetation perpetually, aggressively verdant.

There is no natural night. Instead, the landscape is crowded with "light-towers"—skeletal, centuries-old iron struts overgrown with glowing, parasitic vines. They cycle through spectra of ultraviolet and amber-gold, signaling in a language no human speaks. Between these towers, the density of life is claustrophobic. Massive, translucent insects with chitinous, clicking gears for mandibles skitter over the wet rocks. They are part-flesh, part-scrap metal, evolved to integrate the scattered, discarded technologies of a civilization that finished its work millennia ago.

Every movement here is loud. The environment acts as an amplifier: the *hiss-snap* of pressurized steam escaping vents in the rock, the rhythmic *thrum-thrum* of the deep-earth pistons, and the constant, cascading sound of water slipping over weeping basalt. The flora is gargantuan; broad, translucent leaves the size of hangars drip condensation onto the muddy floor, creating a soundscape of rhythmic, percussive plinking that merges with the hum of the tectonic engines.

In the scattered clearings, you find the built artifacts: a lone, oxidized iron spire rising from an altar of petrified wood, or a series of copper pipes that weep mineral-rich water into a pool, the liquid glowing a faint, electric blue. These structures are not being maintained; they are being swallowed. The forest is knitting itself into the circuits. Vines wrap around copper wiring to siphon heat, while machines hum to life periodically, shaking off layers of rot, only to go dormant again, submerged in the rising, warm muck.

Everything is in motion. The predators—shimmering, multi-limbed things that track movement through thermal vibration—stalk the edges of the steaming vents. The marsh shifts, the topography rearranging itself over decades as the high-pressure steam hollows out tunnels beneath the mud. 

It is a world of immense, weathered, industrial decay—a place where the factory never finished its shift, and the swamp decided to keep the machines company forever. You do not walk through this landscape; you wade through its heavy, humming, warm-blooded machinery, watched by a thousand glowing eyes that see you only as another source of heat, another spark in the endless, humid dark.
