High in the thin, crystalline air, the world is a sprawling, vertical amphitheater of soaking moss and white-knuckled granite. This is the High Drainage—an immense, mist-crowned basin that functions less like a wilderness and more like a gargantuan, poorly insulated cooling system.

The scale is vast; the valley stretches out in hazy, pale blue tiers, but it feels sparse. The geography is "under construction," a raw, tectonic shelf where the stone is still sharp, unweathered, and jagged, barely softened by the encroachment of the green. The vegetation here is absolute—it is not merely *on* the landscape, it *is* the landscape. Massive, spongy mats of lichen and succulent succulents cling to the vertical faces of cliffs, drinking the constant, heavy condensation that drips from the low-hanging clouds. There is no soil here, only layer upon layer of compressed, hyper-saturated fiber.

The light is blinding. Because of the elevation and the constant mist, the sun refracts through the suspended water droplets until the air itself seems to glow with a brilliant, opaque whiteness. Everything is rimmed in glare.

But the defining feature of the High Drainage is the sound. It is a deafening, rhythmic thrum—the roar of industrial-scale water processing. Scattered across the vast, open slopes are the "Condensers": colossal, rusted iron funnels and sprawling arrays of copper-piped turbines. They are sparse, single-building operations separated by miles of slick, weeping moss. They do not appear to be managed by human hands; they are slow, patient machines that groan and whine as they strain liquid from the saturated clouds. The sound is an unrelenting, low-frequency vibration that rattles the sternum, a mechanical hum that blends with the constant *splat-hiss* of runoff hitting the metallic grates.

These machines are not thriving; they are barely holding on. They are stained with oxidized runoff and choked by thick, encroaching vines that move with such agonizing slowness you can only track their progress by checking back after a week. There is very little animal life here—perhaps the occasional pale, moisture-slicked insect crawling along the rusted girders—but the silence of fauna is drowned out by the mechanical labor.

The place feels like a factory abandoned in the middle of a forest, yet the factory is the only thing keeping the forest from sliding away. The water is everywhere—beading on your skin, pooling in the indentations of the giant moss-mats, cascading in erratic, unchanneled sheets down the raw, embryonic cliffs. It is a landscape of cold, wet, industrial labor, where the sun reflects off the steam and the pipes, and the earth is just beginning to figure out how to be anything other than a wet rock reaching for the sky.
