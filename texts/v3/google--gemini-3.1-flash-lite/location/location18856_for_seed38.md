This is a world that has forgotten the hard edge of stone. At this elevation, where the atmosphere is thick with a perpetual, pearlescent condensation, the mountains have long since surrendered their jaggedness. They are not made of granite or basalt, but of accumulated aeons of mulch, peat-mass, and fibrous root-work, worn down by a soft, ancient rainfall into rounded, tumulus-like hummocks that stretch across the horizon. It is a continent of moss-architecture, slow and stable, where the ground itself pulses with a heartbeat that takes hours to complete.

The air is perpetually warm and tastes of bruised sap and wet earth. Because the vegetation acts as the sole substrate, the landscape possesses a strange, spongy elasticity. One does not walk upon the ground so much as over the top of a living, layered carpet that is hundreds of meters thick. The light is dim, filtered through a continuous canopy of broad, translucent fronds that bleed a low, amber luminescence into the mist. It is a world without horizon lines; there is only the transition from the dense, saturated velvet of the floor to the darker, tangled galleries of the giants above.

In this vast, primordial stability, built things are rare and fragile. Occasionally, one finds the "remnants"—hollowed-out, calcified heartwood chambers shaped like inverted bells, left behind by some long-departed, non-human agency. They are not structures in any mechanical sense but biological vacancies, coated in a shimmering, iridescent fungus that glows with a faint, rhythmic pulse. They offer shelter, though they are usually claimed by thick, ropey vines that weave themselves through the portals, sealing them shut over the centuries.

Sound here is an anomaly. The water—drawn from the mist itself—does not run in rivers. It collects in vast, suspended bladders that hang from the underside of the canopy like silver fruit, dripping with a slow, hypnotic regularity. The *plip... plop...* of these droplets is the primary metronome of the continent. 

The ecology is entirely symbiotic. The fauna, small and soft-bodied, seem to be expressions of the flora rather than independent entities. One sees translucent, lung-like creatures that drift through the humid air, trailing filaments that graft into the tree-trunks to draw nourishment, only to detach and drift away when the vine shifts its chemistry. There is no urgency. There is no violence. Everything is in a state of high-density saturation, a slow-motion consumption of time.

This landscape is the result of a planet that has finished its geological life and entered its biological fruition. It is a place where the concepts of "surface" and "inhabitant" have blurred. As you move through the warm, damp gloom, you realize that the silence isn't an absence of life—it is the silence of something so vast, so deeply settled into its own form, that it no longer needs to communicate with anything else. It is finished. It is breathing in its sleep.
