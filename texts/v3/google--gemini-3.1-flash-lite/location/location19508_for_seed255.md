The world is a vast, subterranean clockwork heart, a hollowed-out sphere of interlocking geometric plates that moan as they shift. There is no ground here, only tiers—thousands of square miles of tiered, brass-plated obsidian shelves, stacked with such rhythmic density that one can rarely see more than twenty feet before a wall of ticking iron blocks the horizon.

It is a world of total construction. Every square inch of the landscape is a machine part. There is no soil, no rock, no organic decay. Instead, the floor beneath your feet is a shimmering, cold grid of interlocking copper tiles, damp with a rhythmic weeping. A grey, viscous humidity clings to the air—a heavy, metallic dew that beads on the cold surfaces, only to be scraped away by the passing of heavy, grinding tectonic gears. It smells of ozone, oxidized pipe-sweat, and the bitter tang of wet flint.

The light is nonexistent. Where it exists, it is trapped and flickering: a periodic discharge of blue-white static that crawls along the copper veins of the walls, illuminating the space for a heartbeat before plunging it back into heavy, suffocating shadow.

There is no vegetation, but the world is not sterile. It is infested with "The Weaving"—a self-replicating, fungal-like electrical filament that clings to the brass structures. These glowing, obsidian-colored hairs pulse in time with the shifting floor, drawing power from the friction of the grinding plates. They are simpler than life, crystalline and predatory, snapping at the occasional moisture droplets that condense on the colder vents. 

The sound is punishing, absolute. The landscape is a symphony of industrial labor. It is a continuous, deafening *clack-hiss-thrum*—the sound of million-ton gears grinding against one another, of high-pressure steam venting through porous iron grilles, and the sharp, rhythmic ringing of brass striking brass. It is the sound of a vast, primitive engine that has forgotten its purpose, continuing to refine itself in the dark.

The elevation is confusing; you are always in a canyon of machinery, buried far beneath the original crust. The geography is "emerging"—constantly being re-forged by the slow, grinding movement of the panels. A path that exists in the morning may be crushed into a new, complex relief by noon, as the internal geometry of the world rearranges its own geography.

Fauna is rare, but present. They are not animals, but 'Construct-Lice'—multi-limbed, hand-tooled brass scuttlers, no larger than a child’s arm, that move with erratic, sudden movements across the wet walls. They operate via simple, clockwork instincts: find moisture, scavenge the frayed filament-growth, and avoid the crushing jaws of the shifting floor plates. They do not communicate; they only tick.

There is a sense of "pre-life" or "post-life" here—a feeling that this is a factory for reality itself, currently running a test cycle that will never conclude. The dampness is the only thing that feels living, a cold, persistent fluid that lubricates the gears and prevents the world from seizing. To walk here is to navigate a landscape under construction, a dense, dark, and thunderous cathedral of cold iron, where the only thing that grows is the friction, and the only thing that thrives is the noise.
