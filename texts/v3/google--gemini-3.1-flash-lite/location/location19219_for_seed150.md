This is a city built entirely of *Cordy-quartz*: a glass-hard, translucent vascular moss that has calcified into rigid, structural walls. It clings to the needle-spires of a high-altitude mountain range, occupying the narrow, frozen crevices where the clouds snag and tear themselves apart.

Because the temperature remains perpetually just above freezing, the city does not dry. Every surface—the living walls, the spiraling staircases, the vaulted ceilings of the residential nodes—is slicked with a thin, shimmering film of condensation. Water does not flow in channels here; it beads and crawls along the interconnected veins of the plant-architecture, thickening into gel-like globules that cling to the frost-coated edges of doorways.

The scale is intimate and suffocating. You are never in an open square; you are always in a hallway, a gallery, or a vertical shaft. The “buildings” are formed by the overlapping, folded architecture of giant, prehistoric ferns that have hardened over centuries. There is no wood here, only cold, cartilaginous stems and translucent, membrane-light dividers that pulse with a faint, bioluminescent indigo.

Because of the high density, the soundscape is muffled. The air is too heavy with moisture and thick vegetal matter to carry echoes. When you walk, your footsteps are absorbed instantly by the spongy, living floors that shift slightly under your weight—a tactile, organic rebound that mimics the feeling of walking on a congested, swampy lung.

There is a rhythm to the city, but it is not mechanical. It is the rhythmic swelling of the primary water-veins within the walls. Every few hours, the temperature drops, the condensation freezes into a delicate, needle-like frost, and the city “breathes”—the stalks constrict, shifting the geometry of a room, narrowing a corridor, or forcing a passage shut. If you are not in the right chamber at the right time, you are shifted into the next compartment by the city's glacial, biological peristalsis.

Fauna is nearly non-existent. Occasionally, a pale, translucent gastropod—a slug the size of a human palm—will traverse a wall, leaving a trail of hyper-slick, luminous mucus that acts as a signpost for others. These creatures move with agonizing slowness, their internal organs visible through their skins, digesting the mossy substrate in silence. They are the only engineers here, grazing the architecture into existence, pruning dead calcified stalks, and keeping the pathways clear through the dense, tangled thickets of the mid-levels.

The light is a bruised, eternal twilight. The sun rarely penetrates the thick ceiling of storm clouds above the peaks, so the light is filtered through meters of chlorophyll and ice. Everything is cast in a submerged, deep-sea green, punctuated only by the occasional, rhythmic blink of a dormant fungal node.

It is a claustrophobic, quiet, and profoundly solitary architecture. There is no furniture, no art, no history carved into the walls—because the walls are the history. They are the record of every moisture cycle, every winter, and every slow, creeping expansion of the colony. To live here is to be a guest of a slow-witted, gargantuan organism that does not recognize you as anything more than a fleeting impurity in its circulation.
