This environment is not a place of horizons, but of interiority. It is embedded deep within the basaltic mantle of a rogue wandering planetoid, a vast "gallery" of raw, pressurized geology that has never seen a star. It is the scale of a cathedral, yet compressed into a pocket of space no larger than a heavy-machinery hangar.

The atmosphere is a swirling, caustic haze of ionized sulfur and particulate silica—a dry, mineral soup that vibrates with the low-frequency hum of tectonic friction. There is no water here; the chemical interactions are governed by liquid-metal conduits and thermal sublimation.

The heat is absolute, a steady 2.7 on the scale, radiating from the bedrock itself. The landscape is "emerging"—this is the pressure-forging stage of planetary construction. It is a world of raw, unpolished geology. There are no plants, but the rock is alive with what looks like calcified, bioluminescent nerve endings—filamentous mineral growths that pulse with a faint, rhythmic heat-throb.

Every inch of this space is occupied. It is a "packed" environment, a high-density cluster of activity where jagged obsidian spires press against one another, shivering in the heat. It feels like the interior of an engine that has just begun its million-year cycle. 

The "tech" parameter—the industrial hum—emerges from the geology itself. Because the density is so extreme, the tectonic plate shifts function precisely like a clockwork movement: grinding, shrieking, and cycling with the mechanical predictability of an assembly line. This is "Natural Industry." Massive slabs of basalt, forced together by subterranean pressure, act as pistons. The sound is deafening—a continuous, grinding roar that fills the skull, the sound of mountains being compacted into diamonds.

Though it is a place of darkness, it is not empty. The fauna here are lithovores—scuttling, segmented things that look like iron filings animated by magnetism. They move in frantic, precise swarms across the shifting rock faces, feeding on the electrical discharges created by the grinding stone. They have no eyes; they navigate by the density of the hum and the heat gradients of the strata.

There is no "built" structure, yet the world functions like a factory. The environment is under construction, a self-assembling machine of heat and heavy, jagged matter. Change is excruciatingly slow—the movement across the chamber is a geological crawl, measured in millimeters per decade. 

It is a world of crushing closeness, where the very air you breathe is the grinding vibration of the earth itself, a claustrophobic, high-pressure machine-chamber, eternally dark, eternally hot, and perfectly, violently, occupied.
