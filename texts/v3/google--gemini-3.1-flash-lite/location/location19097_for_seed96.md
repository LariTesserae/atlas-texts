There is no soil here, nor dirt, nor stone weathered by time. The world is a vast, high-altitude lattice of interlocking ceramic plates and extruded metal struts, a sprawling architecture of geometry that exists for no apparent inhabitant. It is a place of total construction; if a surface exists, it was cast, polished, or bolted into place by hands that have long since folded away.

The atmosphere is thick and humid, clinging to the skin with a heavy, cool persistence. Because of the elevation, the light is blinding—a relentless, high-altitude radiance that reflects off the wet, glassy surfaces, turning the entire valley into a distorted, luminous mirror. Water is everywhere, but it does not flow in rivers. Instead, it beads along the seams of the great plates, weeping from the joints of the hexagonal tiles as if the architecture itself were sweating. Every crevice is filled with a shallow, stagnant sheen of moisture. It rolls in tiny, slow-moving droplets down the vertical pillars, gathering in the corner-joints of the lattice to create miniature, crystalline pools.

There is no vegetation—no root finds purchase here, for there is no substrate to pierce. There is no decay, for there is nothing that has ever lived. The silence is absolute, broken only by the rhythmic *drip-clink, drip-clink* of condensation falling in sequence from one metal strut to another. These sounds travel far, echoing across the open, packed landscape; in the stillness, the sound of a single water droplet hitting a strut a hundred yards away rings out like a bell.

The landscape feels young, though not in the sense of a forest newly grown. It is young because it is unfinished. The edges of the struts are impossibly sharp, the bevels crisp, the intersections precise. There is no dust, yet there is a sense of immense density. Every square inch of the basin is occupied by a structural necessity—pipes, conduits, and load-bearing scaffolds stacked into a vertical labyrinth. Space is at a premium here, despite the scale of the valley; the floor is covered in a tight-knit tiling of slate-colored composite panels, each recessed with channels to direct the constant weeping of the air.

Navigation is an exercise in cautious, tactile movement. The technology feel is primitive—no glowing screens or humming coils—just heavy, manual leverage. Massive brass-hinged plates can be shifted by hand, their mechanisms slow and ponderous, requiring the weight of a body to coax them into new configurations. There are no machines to assist; if you wish to see how far the architecture extends, you must climb the struts, your hands slick with the cool, persistent moisture that coats every surface.

There is no fauna, no skittering motion, no breath. The world exists in a state of patient, patient equilibrium. It is a high-altitude machine that does nothing but hold itself together, shivering under the weight of its own moisture, waiting for a purpose that never arrives. The light, bouncing off the millions of wet, manufactured facets, creates a sort of visual hum—a brightness so complete it masks the horizon, leaving you suspended in a bright, dripping, metal infinity.
