The world here exists within a jagged, vertical throat of obsidian and pale, chalky quartz, rising so high that the air has forgotten how to be anything but thin and biting. It is a cathedral-sized chimney of rock, an enclosed chasm where the light—unfiltered and blindingly bright—tumbles down from a sliver of sky to strike the mineral walls like a hammer on an anvil.

There is no life here. There is no green, no pulse of warm blood, no breath of decay. The environment is entirely a matter of chemistry and thermal stress. Because the scale is tight—a cluster of interconnected chambers and narrow, spiraling shelves—every sound is magnified: the sharp, metallic *clink* of stone shards shifting, or the rhythmic drip of condensation that provides the only "water" in this parched, desperate geography.

The moisture does not pool; it clings. It gathers in the microscopic pores of the translucent, glassy rock faces as a dense, silver-beaded frost, only to melt under the midday glare into singular, heavy drops. These droplets act as lenses, magnifying the textures of the stone beneath them. They slide down long, weathered gullies etched into the obsidian, creating a slow, rhythmic weeping that marks the passage of hours. When a drop falls, it makes a sound like a small bell striking glass in a tomb.

The terrain is weathered, ancient, and settled. It does not feel like a place that is being eroded by the elements; rather, it feels like it has finished being eroded. It is in a state of crystalline stasis. The density of the rubble—heaps of razor-sharp shale and smooth, wind-polished quartz—is high; every surface is layered, pressing against the next, creating a labyrinth of miniature canyons and scree slopes that one could navigate only by hand and knee.

Scattered amidst this geological debris are the "Artifacts." They are not ruins of a city, but marks of necessity: singular, primitive tools carved from the local basalt. There are concave bowls fashioned to trap the condensation, and crude, hand-scored notches in the rock walls that served as handholds for some long-departed, non-biological visitor. These marks are faint, smoothed over by aeons of wind and thermal expansion, and they offer no clue as to the nature of their makers.

The cold is a physical intrusion. Even when the light is at its brightest, turning the quartz veins into searing, white-hot needles, the ambient temperature remains tethered to the freezing point. The rocks hold the chill deep within their cores, creating a paradoxical atmosphere of blinding radiance and numbing frost. 

Occasionally, a process occurs: the "Cycling." Because of the extreme elevation and the shifting angle of the sun, the narrow chimney periodically undergoes a thermal snap. The basalt expands, the quartz contracts, and the entire space emits a low, groaning hum—a tectonic sigh that vibrates through the bones of the architecture. Dust, fine as flour, dances in the sudden agitation of the air, and for a few minutes, the silence is broken by the sound of a thousand tiny collisions. 

Then, the mountain settles again. The drops begin their slow, calculated descent. The bright, sterile light resumes its work, polishing the glass, etching the shadows, and maintaining this frozen, mineral silence. It is a place that feels less like a landscape and more like an unfinished thought, carved in stone, waiting in the cold for a witness that will never arrive.
