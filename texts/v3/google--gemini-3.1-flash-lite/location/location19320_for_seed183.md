At the razor-thin edge of the troposphere, the world is a series of vertigo-inducing shelves made of translucent, calcified salt and obsidian. Here, at an elevation where the air is pulled thin as parchment, the temperature hovers at an exhausting coolness—the kind that settles deep into the marrow but leaves the surface of the skin stinging and alert.

There is no soil. There is no chlorophyll. The geometry of the landscape is dominated by verticality; thousands of thin, needle-like crags rise from the shelves like broken teeth, polished smooth by an eon of relentless, scouring winds. They are not volcanic, nor are they strictly geological; they feel grown, a crystal-lattice architecture that seems to have finished its work eons ago.

Water is the ghost that haunts these heights. It does not rain; instead, the very air is a thick, frigid suspension of super-cooled mist. It clings to every micron of the crags, forming a slick, weeping sheen that coats the world in a perpetual, freezing sweat. Every surface is saturated. Small, dark reservoirs of heavy, hyper-saline fluid sit pooled in the depressions of the stone, unmoved by any wind, acting as perfect, ink-black mirrors that reflect the blindingly bright, white-gold sky.

The brightness is absolute. Because the air is so thin and the surfaces so reflective, there is no place to hide from the light. It bounces off the weeping rock and the obsidian mirrors until shadows are not black voids, but bruised, iridescent smudges of violet and neon-blue. 

It is a loud place. The sound is constant, not from the movement of creatures—for there is nothing here that breathes, scuttles, or flies—but from the *physics of the medium*. The moisture, as it condenses and slips down the weeping crags, produces a sound like a million glass needles shattering against a floor of velvet. It is a persistent, harmonic hum, vibrating at a frequency that makes the teeth ache. 

Occasionally, the reality of the place fractures. A ridge of obsidian might suddenly liquefy, flowing uphill in a slow, viscous ribbon of dark geometry before snapping back into a rigid, solid state with a sound like a closing vault. These "glitches" occur with the rhythm of weather, a predictable instability born from the proximity of the sky.

Scattered across these ledges are the "marks." They are not ruins, for they were never inhabited. They are simple, hand-wrought artifacts: cairns of pumice bonded by a primitive, clay-like resin, or long, singular tethering-cables made of woven, dried mineral-fiber that stretch from one crag to another, anchored for reasons lost to time. They possess the tactile geometry of human endeavor, yet there is no history to account for them. They are merely *there*, integrated into the cliffside as if they were as natural as the weeping stone itself.

To stand here is to occupy a state of high-saturation. The density of the landscape is suffocating. One feels the weight of the water-logged air, the pressure of the brightness, and the proximity of the weird, shivering geometry as it rearranges itself just peripheral to one’s direct line of sight. It is a world that has reached a static, crystalline perfection, operating in a loop of condensation and sound, indifferent to the fact that it is a masterpiece with no audience.
