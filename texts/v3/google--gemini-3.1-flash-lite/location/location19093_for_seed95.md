The plateau rests at an impossible height, a table of stone so ancient that the concept of a "peak" has been eroded by a billion years of patience. There is no crag here, no sharp geological teeth; there is only a vast, undulating expanse of bedrock ground down to the condition of polished bone. 

It is a warm, still country. The air holds a heaviness—a thick, clinging humidity that refuses to coalesce into a proper downpour, instead clinging to every surface as a constant, slick glaze of condensation. Because the earth is so deeply aged and near-flat, water does not flow away. It sits in shallow, hexagonal depressions in the stone, perfect geometric basins filled with tepid, mineral-heavy droplets that shimmer in the milky, mid-range light.

The terrain is a tapestry of high-density biology. Though vegetation is sparse in terms of height—one finds no trees, only a carpet of moss-like lichen that feels like crushed velvet and smells of wet ozone—the fauna is staggering. The plateau is thick with bodies. Small, translucent creatures, neither insect nor mollusk, cluster in the millions. They are the size of fingertips, shaped like teardrops, and they cling to one another in dense, pulsating patches that cover the bedrock like a second skin. 

There is a strange, rhythmic quiet here. The silence is not an absence of sound, but an absence of *noise*. Occasionally, a creature will release a long, vibrating hum—a frequency so low it rattles in the marrow of one’s teeth—and the entire neighborhood of creatures will ripple in response, a slow-motion wave of movement that takes minutes to dissipate. It is a world of gentle, patient flux. 

Scattered across this living, pulsating mosaic are the remnants of things that were never meant to be moved by human hands. There are rings of basalt, worn smooth until they are nearly flush with the ground, and strange, spiraling cairns of calcified bone that serve no architectural purpose. They are not ruins; they are artifacts of a biological intent that has long since transitioned into stillness. The stone itself seems to have absorbed these marks, as if the earth were slowly swallowing them, folding the built world back into the primordial ledger of its own geology.

You walk—or rather, you traverse—across this landscape, your boots finding little purchase on the slick, weeping stone. Every step feels like an intrusion into a long-held secret. The heat of the air matches the heat of the creatures pulsing beneath you. There is no horizon in the traditional sense, for the world curves away into the haze, a vast, saturated open-space where the boundary between the ground and what dwells upon it has become blurred by time. 

Nothing changes quickly here. The sun crawls, the clouds mist, the patches of life expand by millimeters in the solar cycle. To exist here is to become patient; to watch the water bead on a lichen-spore is to witness a major event. It is a slow, warm, heavy kingdom, settled into a final form that has no intention of ever finishing.
