The city of Ouro-Khar sits upon a high, basaltic plateau, a sprawling necropolis of industry where the air is never dry and the heat never relents. It is a place of heavy, suffocating wetness; the atmosphere is essentially a pressurized soup of steam and mineral dust that clings to the skin like a second, lukewarm shroud.

There is no green here. The landscape—if it can be called that—is composed entirely of obsidian, rusted iron, and architectural glass hardened into geometric obsidian-like shards. Nothing photosynthesizes. Instead, the geometry of the city performs a kind of alchemy. Long, flute-like spires of volcanic glass rise from the plateau, designed to catch the wandering thermal updrafts. They vibrate with a low, sub-audible thrum, condensing the thick water vapor into black, mineral-heavy rain that perpetually slickens the streets.

The city is immense, yet it feels profoundly empty. Its urban density is structural rather than kinetic. There are endless miles of elevated walkways, arched bridges, and vaulted industrial bays, yet the ratio of built space to occupant is staggering. You can walk for hours through the grid-work of the Central Tier and encounter only the rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* of water dripping from an iron overhang into a subterranean collection basin. 

Everything here is weathered but functional. The machinery—enormous, slow-turning gears beneath the street grates, and massive, soot-stained pistons that groan every few minutes—has been grinding for eons. It does not seem to be controlled by any living hand. It is an industrial self-sustaining loop, a machine that repairs its own glass panes with volcanic resin and flushes its own gutters with the condensation it harvests from the clouds.

Light is sparse, held back by a perpetual, bruised-purple shroud of sulfurous fog that hugs the skyline. The sun is a ghost, a pale orange smear behind the haze that never truly pierces the gloom. Because of this, the shadows are not dark, but rather deep, thick, and viscous.

Life exists, but it is rare and ignores the rules of biology you might expect. Occasionally, you may see a "Skimmer"—a drifting, tentacled construct of silicate and pulsing light that moves not by flight, but by manipulating the localized air pressure. They do not eat; they appear to feed on the static friction generated by the city’s vast, unlubricated turning gears. They move silently, like ghosts in a house where the doors have been forgotten.

The soundscape is defined by this quietude. It is a hallowed, heavy silence, broken only by the hydraulic sigh of the city’s lungs and the occasional, rhythmic *clack-hiss* of a pressure valve releasing excess heat into the mist. It is a landscape that has long since achieved a state of lonely perfection. It is a city that has outlasted its builders, a vast, hot, dripping monument to a way of life that no longer requires the living to function. 

To walk through Ouro-Khar is to feel the weight of a clock that measures geological time rather than minutes. There is no urgency here, only the infinite, circular maintenance of the stone and the steam. It is grand, it is stifling, and it is entirely, utterly alone.
