The horizon here does not curve; it simply stops, shearing off into a haze of static. This is the High Mesas of the Ferrum-Bloom, a landscape that defies the basic biology of decay and the physics of hydration. 

There is no water. Not a drop of dew, not a trace of humidity in a wind that carries the scent of ionized copper and ozone. Yet, the land is lush. Stretching out across a series of tiered, plate-like highlands, forests of metallic-fibrous stalks rise hundreds of feet into the air. They do not photosynthesize; they vibrate. These "plants"—if the word can be applied to static, mineral-based architectures of silicon and heavy conductive alloys—excrete long, gossamer filaments of silver thread that catch the normal, bright sunlight, turning the entire high-altitude valley into a shimmering, reflective grid.

It is an industrial garden, though no hand planted it. The "vegetation" is self-replicating circuitry, growing in geometric patterns that suggest a factoryfloor’s schematic rather than a meadow’s chaos. The stalks are rigid, warm to the touch, and pulse with a faint, low-frequency hum—the sound of electricity moving through conductive veins. Because the erosion is zero—the land is still "fresh," as if the tectonic plates were locked into place by a celestial architect just moments ago—the rock beneath the silver trees is raw, jagged, and obsidian-dark, devoid of soil, sand, or dust.

There is no animal life. There is no decay. Because the rules of this world are governed by static equilibrium, nothing ever falls. A leaf of silver foil that detaches from a stalk does not hit the ground; it hangs suspended in the stagnant, gravity-warped air, caught in a perpetual drift that never changes its angle or height. 

The environment is vast and terrifyingly empty. Walking through the forest of towers, you see nothing for miles. The density is near-vacuum between the pillars. You are walking through the interior of a non-functional engine. Everything looks as if it were calibrated to produce something—a vast output of energy, a cascade of data, a manufactured reality—yet the machine is silent. 

It is "under construction" in the sense that the geometry feels incomplete, a preliminary sketch made of matter rather than pencil. The atmosphere is warm, blanketed by the radiation of the metallic leaves. If you stand perfectly still, the silence is absolute, save for the hum of the pillars. You feel the weight of a world that is perfectly maintained, perfectly preserved, and utterly indifferent to the fact that it is a masterpiece of un-living, un-changing, high-altitude production. There is no wind, no weather, no history here—only the shimmering, electrified potential of a machine that forgot to start.
