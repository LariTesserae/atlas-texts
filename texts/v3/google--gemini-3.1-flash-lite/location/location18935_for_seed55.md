The world is a vast, submerged lattice, a continent-scale coral of hammered lead and dark, oxidized copper. There is no natural ground here; beneath the miles of crushing, frigid brine, there is only the *Fabrication*—an ancient, total architecture that has long since ceased to be a building and has become a planetary substrate.

Because the water is so dense and perpetually near-freezing, sound behaves differently here. It does not travel so much as it haunts. A chime struck against a copper strut a mile away arrives as a dull, thrumming vibration in the marrow of one’s bones, long after the physical event has faded. This is a quiet place. The water is heavy, viscous as oil, and carries the bitter, metallic taste of an epoch spent dissolving the world’s edges.

Because the erosion is absolute, the structures are smooth. There are no sharp corners left in this realm. Every pillar, beam, and interlocking gear-tooth has been worn down by the slow, grinding patience of the deep-sea currents over aeons. The geometry is primordial, settled into a final, rounded state of grace. It is a landscape of soft, bulbous hills made of riveted metal, cascading down into infinite abyssal canyons where the light of the sun has never reached.

The vegetation is not biological, but *lithic-growth*. Great, sprawling mats of calcified moss and crystalline fibers cling to the copper pillars. They are the only "living" things, if one defines life by the slow, chemical bloom of mineral deposits. They pulse with a faint, bioluminescent dimness—a sickly, pale violet glow that barely penetrates the thick, dark water. These forests of crystal ferns reach out like frozen static, layered so densely that they form tangled, impassable thickets of porous stone. 

The tech is primitive: everything is held together by heavy, manual rivets and hand-forged tension joints. There is no electricity, no circuitry, no flow of information—only the blunt, stubborn mechanics of a world that was finished long ago. To move through this space is to navigate a colossal, derelict machine that has forgotten its purpose. 

There are no animals. There are no eyes to watch the shifting of the moss. There is only the immense, cold pressure of the water pressing against the walls, the slight, tectonic groaning of the metal plates as they shift by a fraction of an inch every century, and the long, slow darkness. It is a world of total construction that has been reclaimed by the silence of the deep. It is finished; it is cold; it is eternal. You drift through the corridors of a tomb that was built not for the dead, but for the sake of the building itself.
