The air here is a cool, silver-filmed mist that hangs eternally in the low basins of the Basin-Cities. It is not "weather"—it is a byproduct of the cooling pipes, a constant, industrial exhalation that keeps the terrain perpetually damp.

The world is constructed entirely of hematite, dark oxidized iron, and weeping basalt. There is no green, no cellulose, no chlorophyll. In the absence of organic life, the "fauna" has claimed the entire architectural grid as its own. Thousands upon thousands of brass-carapaced, multi-jointed arthropods—some the size of dinner plates, others no larger than a grain of sand—scuttle across every surface. They are not animals in the biological sense; they are reactive, assembly-line-produced scavenger-constructs, vibrating with a high-pitched, rhythmic hum that never softens.

The landscape is a settled, low-lying sprawl of interconnected, rusted conduits and terraced platforms, weathered by aeons of mechanical friction. Everything is "in-balance" because the erosion here is not caused by wind or water, but by the relentless, circular grinding of limbs against metal. The paths are polished smooth by the millions of claws that have traversed them for centuries.

There is no change here. The light is a flat, consistent, high-noon gray, bleeding down from unseen overhead grids. No dawn, no dusk, no wilting, no growth. 

Consider the "Cathedral of Pulleys": a wide, open-air chamber formed by the intersection of three massive, rusted irrigation ducts. The moisture, trapped by the cool temperature, beads on the underside of these ducts and falls in steady, rhythmic droplets onto the floor below. Every second, a drop strikes a gear. *Clink.* A thousand gears, a thousand drops. The sound is a deafening, precise symphony of metal-on-metal percussion.

If you stand in the center of a plaza, you are surrounded by the frantic, silent-seeming industry of the swarm. They ignore you, provided you do not obstruct a trajectory. They are cleaning, repairing, and re-sorting the oxidized scrap that defines the world’s geology. Walls are layered with fused, discarded clockwork, creating a strange, topographical relief—it is a geography of pure industry. 

There is no horizon, only the edge of the next enclosure. You are in a city that is simultaneously a factory and a graveyard, where the inhabitants are the machinery itself, performing long-forgotten, repetitive tasks with an intensity that borders on the religious. Nothing grows, nothing dies, and nothing concludes. The hum is the only pulse, a steady-state kinetic vibration that settles in the marrow of your bones, leaving you convinced that the world has been grinding like this since the beginning of duration, and will grind exactly this way until the metal itself turns to dust.
