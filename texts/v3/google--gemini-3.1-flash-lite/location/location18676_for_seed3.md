At the zenith of the world, where the air is too thin to hold warmth, lies the city of **Ouroboros-on-the-Spine**. It is not built upon the mountain; it is grafted onto the raw, pulsing tectonic nerves of a peak that has yet to decide what it wants to be. 

The geography here is "embryonic." Great, jagged protrusions of obsidian and unrefined ore thrust upward like teeth breaking through a gum line, still shedding flakes of primordial dust. There is no erosion here—the wind doesn't scour these surfaces so much as it waits for them to finish cooling, to stop sighing in their sleep. 

The city itself is a vertical labyrinth of damp, oxidized iron and riveted copper. Because the temperature hovers perpetually near freezing, the city does not see rain so much as a constant, sweating condensation. Water exists in pockets: trapped in the concave hollows of industrial cooling vats, dripping from the undersides of sky-bridges, and weeping from the porous, moss-slicked stone of the subterranean foundations. This moisture fuels a paradoxical lushness. Bio-luminescent fungal forests cling to the girders—spongy, indigo-colored polyps that pulse in sync with the city’s massive, rusted turbines.

The sound is a constant, grinding low-frequency thrum, punctuated by the high, metallic shrieks of heavy machinery. It is loud, but a slow, rhythmic loudness—the turning of a gear the size of a cathedral, the laboring of steam-pressure valves that sound like the groaning of a tectonic plate. 

It is a place of reality breakdown. Because the elevation is so extreme, the atmosphere—or perhaps the physics of the site itself—is thin and brittle. Occasionally, the space between buildings stretches; a pedestrian reaching for a handrail might find their arm elongated by several feet, or a factory worker might look out a window to see the same street corner repeated infinitely in a vertiginous spiral of rust and pipework. Things are not anchored. Sometimes, the heavy industrial soot falling from the stacks freezes mid-air, hanging suspended in the dim, twilight gloom like constellations of black snow.

The fauna here is as strange as the architecture. Abundant, but sparse in its distribution—you might go hours without seeing a living thing, and then turn a corner to find a flock of flightless, metallic-feathered scavengers nesting in a high-voltage conduit, their beaks sparking with static as they feed on the residual electricity. These creatures seem to know the "rules" of the city; they move with the same maddeningly slow, patient deliberation as the tectonic plates beneath the foundations.

There is no sky to speak of, only an oppressive layer of heavy, swirling vapor that traps the dim, sepia-toned light. Below, the mountain is still growing, shifting, and sharpening. Above, the city creaks and moans, held together by chains of light and the wet, clinging growth of moss that feeds on the factory exhaust. It is a lonely, industrial lung—breathing in the cold, thin air of the heights and exhaling a slow, rhythmic reality that is perpetually fraying at the edges. Everything here is in a state of coming-into-being, a city built on the promise of a mountain that refuses to finish its work.
