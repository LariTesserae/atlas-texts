The world does not move. It is a portrait of a mountain range caught in a perpetual, freezing inhale. At an elevation where the air is too thin to carry sound normally, the environment compensates by generating a deafening, discordant ring—a constant, low-frequency hum that vibrates the very marrow of one’s bones. 

The terrain is a jagged assembly of slate and obsidian, weathered by eons of a strange, non-liquid moisture. It does not rain here in a traditional sense; instead, the air itself becomes heavy with a viscous, metallic dew that clings to the sharp vertical cliffs, forming slick, gelid pockets in the cracks of the rock. These pockets do not evaporate nor do they flow; they sit within the stone like cloudy, frozen cataracts, reflecting nothing.

The landscape is defined by its emptiness. Miles of basalt plateaus stretch toward darkened, violet horizons, punctuated only by a rare, wiry lichen—gray, skeletal strands that cling to the rock faces. They do not grow; they simply persist, having reached their final, hardened form ten thousand years ago. 

This place is governed by a breakdown of sensory consistency. If you trace the silhouette of a peak against the dim, bruise-colored sky, your gaze might slip, finding that the mountain’s edge does not terminate where it should, but rather repeats its own geometry in a flickering, overlapping echo. At times, a shadow cast on the ground will behave as if it were a physical object, resisting the weight of a boot or humming with the same aggressive frequency as the windless air.

There is biological life, though it is rare and seemingly accidental. Creatures of translucent, brittle chitin—thin as parchment and spindly as clockwork hands—drift through the crags. They do not eat or hunt; they are static entities, occasionally found fused to the side of a monolith, their elongated limbs serving as the only marker of their existence. 

Scattered across this desolate expanse are the "remnants." They are not ruins of human architecture, but rather strange, calcified structures that resemble half-formed ribcages or pillars of fused, white marrow, protruding from the stone slopes. There are no doors, no foundations, and no signs of chisel or tool. They seem to have erupted from the earth in a moment of geological error.

The light is a flat, unyielding charcoal. It offers no shadows of warmth, only a pervasive gloom that renders the entire region into a monochrome study. Everything here is in a state of final, locked resolution. You could stand for a century in the lee of a jagged obsidian spire, and not a single particle of dust would shift, not a single drop of the metallic dew would slide, and the deafening, hollow ring of the atmosphere would remain the heartbeat of a world that has already decided it is finished.
